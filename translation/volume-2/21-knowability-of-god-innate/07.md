## 7.

This, however, shows only one side of the truth. There is another side that is no less important. It cannot be denied that, just as objectively the light of the sun, so subjectively the eye is necessary for seeing. That human beings actually learn and acquire knowledge from their environment is certain, but it presupposes that they bring with them a capacity, aptitude, and inclination to learn. Language is taught to us by the people among whom we are born, but it presupposes in every human being a disposition and an inclination to speak. So it is in every domain: religion, art, morality, law, science, and so forth. The _semina scientiarum_ lie in man by nature. All science proceeds from general _principia_ that stand firm by and of themselves. All knowledge rests in faith. Every proof ultimately presupposes an ἀρχη ἀποδειξεως. There are logical, mathematical, philosophical, ethical, and likewise religious and theological _principia_ that are indeed very general and abstract, but are nevertheless accepted by all human beings and in all ages and bear a character of naturalness and necessity. The laws of thought are the same for all; the doctrine of numbers is everywhere the same; the distinction between good and evil is known to all; there is no people without religion and knowledge of God. This cannot be explained otherwise than by accepting _principia per se nota, κοιναι ἐννοιαι, veritates aeternae_, which are impressed upon the human spirit by nature. In religion, whether one wishes it or not, one must always return to a _semen religionis_, a _sensus divinitatis_, an _instinctus divinus_, a _cognitio insita_. Scripture itself leads us in this. It binds man as strongly as possible to objective revelation in nature and grace, but it acknowledges at the same time that man is God’s image and offspring, that in the νους he possesses a capacity to see God in his works, and that he bears the work of the law written in his heart, Gen. 1:26; Acts 17:27; Rom. 1:19; 2:15. Yet everything depends on understanding properly this originality of the κοιναι ἐννοιαι. It has been designated in various ways, and people have spoken of ἐμφυτος, _ingenitus, insculptus, insitus_, innate, inborn, implanted, and so forth. And no one uses these words in the literal sense. As soon as these expressions are thought through, almost all hasten to explain that they do not mean that these _ideae innatae_ are brought along _fix und fertig_ immediately at birth and are present in consciousness as _species impressae_. In that sense there are no innate concepts. God in no respect causes man to enter the world fully grown but causes him to be born as a helpless and dependent child. And that child would perish if it were not nourished and cared for by its environment. Yet the future man already lies concealed in the child. So it is in the intellectual, ethical, and religious domains. _Cognitio Dei insita_ does not mean that man is equipped immediately by God himself with a sufficient knowledge and no longer needs revelation. It does not signify that all by himself he is capable of deriving some conscious, clear, and true knowledge of God from his own spirit. But it indicates that man possesses both the _potentia_ (_aptitudo, vis, facultas_) and the _inclinatio_ (_habitus, dispositio_) to arrive, in the normal course of development and amid the environment in which God gave him life, spontaneously and without compulsion, without scientific reasoning and demonstration, ἐμφυτως και ἀδιδακτως, at some firm, certain, indubitable knowledge of God. The words ἐμφυτος, _ingenitus_, innate, and so forth therefore do not intend to express that _with which_ a person is born, but wish only to indicate that the knowledge of God is born from man himself along the natural path, without scientific reasoning. They are not to be conceived as opposites of the doctrine that man is born as a _tabula rasa_, without determinate material content in his consciousness; rather, they stand opposed to the opinion that man is first brought to knowledge of God externally, through a particular revelation, through scientific proof, as it were artificially and by compulsion. These expressions were always intended in this sense in Christian theology. They therefore alternated with ἀδιδακτως, φυσει, _vi insita, sine praevio studio, sine discursu operoso_, and so forth. _Dei cognitio nobis innata dicitur esse, in quantum per principia nobis innata de facili percipere possumus Deum esse_, Thomas in Kleutgen, _Phil. der Vorzeit_ I 348. Locke’s observation is therefore also incorrect that, if by _ideae innatae_ only the capacity for knowledge were understood, then all knowledge could be called innate. For _cognitio Dei_ is called _insita_ or _innata_ because every human being in normal development _must_ arrive at it. Just as a person, opening his eyes, spontaneously beholds the sun and by its light the objects around him, so by virtue of his nature a person must, as soon as he hears that there is a God, that there is a distinction between good and evil, and so forth, give his assent to it. He cannot do otherwise. He accepts these truths spontaneously, without compulsion or proof, because they stand firm by themselves. For this reason the knowledge of God is called innate, and the expression of an innate potency or faculty was unsatisfactory. On the one hand, over against the doctrine of _ideae innatae_, it expresses that the knowledge of God is not brought ready and complete by man but arises mediately in his consciousness through the operation of revelation; and on the other hand, over against empiricism, it indicates that this revelation of God speaks so loudly and powerfully and finds so deep an echo in everyone’s heart that it can be called naturally proper and inborn to man. In this way theology has done full justice not only to Scripture but also to psychology and history. There is revelation from God in all his works, not only outside but especially also within man. Man himself forms the principal part of the nature from which God is known. And from this entire nature, both outside and within him, man receives impressions and sensations that, before all reasoning and proof, cultivate in his consciousness the awareness of a Supreme Being. It is God himself who leaves no person without witness. Cf. I 16, 157 f., 212 f.; and further Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I² 587–792; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 195; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 197; McCosh, _The Intuition of the Mind, Inductively Investigated_, 1860; John Caird, _An Introduction to the Philos. of Religion_, 1880, pp. 39, 160; Lotze, _Mikrok._ III 580 f.
