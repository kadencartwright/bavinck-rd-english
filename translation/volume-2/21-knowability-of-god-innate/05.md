## 5.

Among the Lutherans the good and true element contained in the doctrine of innate concepts could not come into its own. _Theologia naturalis_, both _insita_ and _acquisita_, found little favor with them; cf. already Volume I, p. 222. Through his opposition to the scholastic doctrine _naturalia adhuc esse integra_, Luther allowed himself to be led to the opposite extreme. The image of God was entirely lost. _Ratio sine Spiritu Sancto est simpliciter sine cognitione Dei. Homo in rebus divinis nihil habet quam tenebras._ Properly speaking, man retained only the _aptitudo passiva_, that is, the possibility of being saved. For the rest his understanding, will, and affection are restricted solely to the _civilia_; in spiritual things he is entirely blind and dead. Luther does acknowledge that God still reveals himself in his works—the creation is a _Larve Gottes_—but man no longer knows him from them. Luther sometimes even goes so far as to call sin _essentia hominis_ and to say that man is _nihil quam peccatum_, expressions which certainly may not be unfairly pressed and forced but are nevertheless significant for his conception of the _cognitio Dei insita_ and _acquisita_, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 244 f., 287 f., 367 f. The same negative standpoint is adopted by the Lutheran symbols, ed. Müller⁵ 522, 585, 589, 594; and certain theologians such as Flacius and Chemnitz went so far that they rejected the whole _cognitio Dei naturalis_, Zöckler, _Theol. Natur._, 1860, p. 64. But they soon returned from this one-sidedness. Luther himself often spoke differently and saw and praised a work of God in nature, Zöckler, ibid., 60 f. Melanchthon taught a _cognitio Dei_ both _insita_ and _acquisita_; there are traces of God in all his works, but these would be insufficient _nisi etiam insita esset menti quaedam notitia seu προληψις de Deo_, _Corpus Ref._ XIII 137 sq. This example is followed by the later theologians. Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, _Prooemium_ §17, loc. II, c. 4; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ I 250 sq.; Hollaz, _Exam. Theol._, p. 187 sq.; Calovius, _Isag. ad S. Theol._, c. 4; Buddeus, _De atheismo_, 1767, p. 225 sq.; and others discuss _theologia naturalis_ and expressly defend it against the Socinians. Later it was even treated separately by some, such as Jaeger and others. _Cognitio Dei insita_ was now variously defined by Lutheran theologians as a _facultas_ or _dispositio_ or _habitus_ or _perfectio_ or _lumen congenitum et habitui analogum_. But all agreed that it did not consist in a _species impressa_ that would already be present in man before every use of reason, Hollaz, 189; Quenstedt, 253; Calovius, 77. Descartes’s doctrine of _ideae innatae_ was therefore rejected, Hollaz, 195, and likewise the mystical doctrine of inward light and contemplation, ibid., 198–220. In opposition to these, _theologia insita_ was conceived as a natural capacity and inclination implanted in the _intellectus humanus_ to arrive at the knowledge of God _sine discursu et mentis ratiocinatione_ and to give an undoubted, firm, and certain testimony to this knowledge, Hollaz, 188–190; Quenstedt, 253. _Ante sensualem perceptionem nihil est in intellectu, quoad idealem rerum repraesentationem, est tamen aliquid in intellectu, quoad notitiam habitualem_, Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 193; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. pol._ I 260.

From the beginning the Reformed adopted a friendlier attitude toward _theologia naturalis_. Calvin distinguished between a general and special grace and explained from the former all the good that still remained even in sinful man; cf. my address on _De Algem. Genade_, p. 27 f. In particular, he also accepted that a _sensus divinitatis_ was implanted in the human spirit, and indeed _naturali instinctu_. God implanted _numinis sui intelligentiam_ in all, _cujus memoriam assidue renovans, novas subinde guttas instillat_, _Inst._ I, 3, 1. He also calls this _semen religionis_ and from it explains the universality of religion, ibid. The conviction that there is a God is _naturaliter ingenita_; it can never be eradicated, I, 3, 3. Yet scarcely one in a hundred is found _qui conceptum in suo corde foveat, nullus autem in quo maturescat_, I, 4, 1. And to that _semen religionis_ is now added God’s revelation in his works, so that _aperire oculos nequeant quin aspicere eum cogantur_, I, 5, 1. There is no piece of the world, _nulla mundi particula, in qua non scintillae saltem aliquae gloriae ipsius emicare cernantur_, I, 5, 1. First of all man himself as microcosm is an _officina innumeris Dei operibus nobilis_, I, 5, 3, 4, but then further the whole of nature, which can even piously be called God, I, 5, 5. Cf. II, 2, 18, and the commentary on Pss. 8; 19; Acts 17:27, 28; Rom. 1:19; Heb. 11:3. And so speak all Reformed symbols and theologians. Ursinus, _Tract. Theol._, 1584, says that God reveals himself to man _cum notitiis de se menti hominum impressis, tum creaturis omnibus tanquam speculis et documentis suae divinitatis propositis_, p. 35; and among the _notitiae nobiscum nascentes_ he also counts the sense that there is a God, as the universality of religion proves, p. 39. Zanchius, _Op._ III 636 sq., rejects both Plato and Aristotle and teaches with the Stoa and Cicero that the κοιναι ἐννοιαι _nobiscum nasci, non autem usu comparari_; children immediately know that three is more than two, that there is a distinction between good and evil, and so forth. Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, p. 325, understands by _recta ratio_ the _vera notitia voluntatis et operum Dei, ut et ordinis judiciique divini, a Deo menti humanae inscripta_, whose author is the Logos, whose _forma_ and _norma_ are the _principia naturalia_, and which is increased by the contemplation of God’s works; cf. further P. Martyr, _Loci Comm._, p. 2 sq.; Maresius, _Syst. theol._, p. 41; H. Alting, _Theol. elenctica_, 1654, p. 2 sq.; Leydecker, _Fax Verit._, loc. 3, contr. 1; Alsted, _Theol. polem._, 1620, pp. 185–187; Turretin, _Theol. El._ I, qu. 3, n. 2; Moor, _Comm._ I 41; etc. Yet although _theologia insita_ was so forcefully advocated, Descartes’s doctrine of _ideae innatae_ was decisively rejected by Voetius, _Disp._ V 477–525. He accuses Descartes especially of three things: that he uses the word _idea_ in an unusual sense and thus falsifies it; that he does not clearly say what is to be understood by that _idea Dei innata_, whether it belongs to _theologia insita_ or _acquisita_, whether it is a _facultas_ or _actus_, an _ens reale_ or _ens intentionale_, and so forth; and that he robs the knowledge that comes to us through the senses of its value and certainty. Voetius clearly defines the sense in which _theologia insita_ is to be understood. It is a _facultas seu vis seu aptitudo facultatum rationalium, seu lumen naturale in eo, quod intellectus veritatem principiorum potest sine ullo labore, praevio studio aut ratiocinatione comprehendere, et positis ponendis (cognitione scil. terminorum) de facto sic comprehendit, ex naturali quadam necessitate et insito sibi pondere in hunc veritatis sensum atque assensum dilatus et inclinans_; in the same way that the will through a natural disposition pursues the good and the eye of itself beholds light and the visible, _Disp._ I 141; cf. V 516, 525. The familiar maxim _nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu_ is therefore also acknowledged as true insofar as the world around us, in one respect or another—as direct object, or as product, or as part, or as contrast, and so forth—is necessary to bring us to conscious knowledge, ibid., V 525.
