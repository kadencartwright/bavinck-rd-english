## 3.

In modern philosophy the doctrine of _ideae innatae_ was advanced by Descartes, who was also the first to use this term and thus began to employ the word idea in a sense unusual until that time.
For Descartes, this doctrine was connected with his dualism between soul and body.
The _cognitio intellectualis_ cannot be derived from sensory perception; the latter supplies only the _occasio_ in which our mind forms representations and concepts _per innatam facultatem_, Renati Descartes, _Notae_, p. 185.
The _cognitio_ proceeds from a principle of its own—that is, from _ideae innatae_.
Among these _ideae innatae_, the foremost is the idea of God, which is, as it were, a _nota artificis, operi suo impressa_, _Medit. tertia_, p. 24.
But he understands the innateness of these ideas to mean that the soul by nature possesses the power, the _facultas_, the _dispositio_, to produce them from itself.
The ideas are therefore present in our mind not actually but potentially, _Object. et Respons._, p. 102; _Notae_, pp. 184, 185.
According to Leibniz too, necessary and universal truths do not come to us from outside but arise from within ourselves, such as, for example, those of substance, duration, change, and cause, the mathematical truths, and above all the idea of God.
Together these truths constitute the natural light of reason.
But the innateness of these truths is explained more fully and clearly by Leibniz than by Descartes.
Descartes said only that these ideas were potentially present in our mind.
Leibniz, however, says that they are innate _virtualiter_, _comme des inclinations, des dispositions, des habitudes ou des virtualités naturelles et non pas comme des actions_.
The human mind does not merely have the capacity to know them, for then all knowledge could be called innate; rather, it is the source of these truths and can bring them forth from itself, _les tirer lui-même de son fond_.
The ideas therefore lie, as it were, preformed in the human soul.
And this is possible because there is representation and thought without consciousness.
They become conscious when sensory perception provides the occasion for it.
Sensory perception thereby also brings the ideas virtually present within us actually into our mind; cf. Spruyt, _Proeve van de leer der aangeb. begrippen_, 144; Stöckl, _Gesch. d. n. Phil._ I 426.
Malebranche believed that human learning and knowledge could be explained only by supposing that we see the ideas in God and that God, as the universal and infinite being, is therefore immediately present in our understanding.
Following in his footsteps, the ontologism of Gioberti, Gratry, Ubaghs, and others taught that in our mind we immediately behold God as absolute being and that an intuitive knowledge of God is therefore present in the human being; Stöckl, _Gesch. d. neuern Philos._ II 568 f., 620 f.
Kant significantly modified this doctrine of innate concepts.
Following Wolff's terminology, he spoke of knowledge _a priori_ and _a posteriori_ and now taught not innate concepts but innate forms—namely, forms of intuition, that is, space and time; forms of the understanding, that is, categories; and forms of reason, that is, the ideas of God, virtue, and immortality.
The idealism of Fichte and Hegel carried this doctrine to such an extreme that they constructed from thought not only the knowledge of necessary and universal truths but all knowledge, and even all being, the entire material world.
The grounds on which this doctrine of _ideae innatae_ chiefly rests are these:
Learning, and the ability to learn, presupposes that what is learned is already present in some way in our mind.
Reasoning and proof are built on _principia_ that must stand firm _per se_ and _a priori_.
Experience supplies only _δοξα_, contingent truths; universal and necessary truths can arise only from the human mind itself.
That such universal and necessary truths exist is demonstrated by the _consensus gentium_.
Above all, the contrast between soul and body is such that representations and concepts cannot have their origin in sensory perception; they must be explained either from the human spirit or from the Spirit of God, in whom the human being beholds all ideas.
In opposition, the doctrine of innate concepts was contested by the Socinians, who rejected natural religion, Fock, _Der Socin._ 307 f.; cf. also Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ I, c. 3; Locke, _Essay concerning human understanding_ I, chs. 2–4; Hobbes, _De cive_, c. 14, n. 19; etc.
They appealed to the following considerations:
The doctrine of _ideae innatae_ is entirely superfluous because the origin of these ideas can also be satisfactorily explained in another way.
History teaches that not a single representation or concept is the same among all human beings and peoples.
There are not even any innate moral principles; the greatest possible difference prevails concerning good and evil; and the concept of God is so little innate that there even exist atheistic individuals and peoples.
Children, idiots, and the insane accordingly know nothing of such innate concepts.
All human knowledge originates in sensory perception: _nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu_.
This opposition to the _ideae innatae_ gained much currency in England and France in the previous century.
The materialism of the present century joined it.
The doctrine of innate concepts has been generally abandoned and replaced by the theory of the inheritance of characteristics: Darwin, _Afst. v. d. mensch_, chaps. 3, 4; Spencer, in Spruyt, _Proeve_, 342; Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_¹⁶, S. 344 f.
