## 4.

It is important to know what attitude Christian theology adopted toward this doctrine of _ideae innatae_. Prof. Spruyt finds it strange that the scholastics declared themselves against this doctrine with rare unanimity and suspects that there was a theological reason for this, though he cannot indicate it, _Proeve_, p. 60. This is indeed the case and will become clear in what follows. Although Christian theology generally accepted that there were truths known not through revelation but by nature, and obtained not through deliberate investigation and reflection but as it were involuntarily, it nevertheless decisively rejected the doctrine of innate concepts. Later advocates of _ideae innatae_, such as Thomassinus, _Theol. dogm._ I, c. 4; Staudenmaier, _Chr. Dogm._ II 57 f.; Kuhn, _Gotteslehre_, 2te Aufl., 542 f.; Klee, _Dogm._ II 1–4; and the ontologists such as Malebranche, Gioberti, Ubaghs, and others, did indeed appeal to certain church fathers, but wrongly. Justin Martyr, _Apol._ II 6, does speak of the idea of God as a πραγματος δυσεξηγητου ἐμφυτος τῃ φυσει των ἀνθρωπων δοξα, but does not further say how he understands this ἐμφυτος. Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 1 sq., argues over against the Gnostics that the world was created by God and reveals him and makes him known, but does not mention a _cognitio innata_. Clement of Alexandria also says that the Father and Creator of all things is known by all naturally and without instruction, ἐμφυτως και ἀδιδακτως, but he himself repeatedly explains that this knowledge is obtained from the contemplation of God’s works, _Strom._ V 13, 14. Tertullian places very strong emphasis on the natural knowledge of God. Despite their idolatry, all human beings call upon the one God in danger and need. And they did not learn this from Moses or the prophets; their own soul instructed them in it. _Animae enim a primordio conscientia Dei dos est._ This is the same among all nations, _Adv. Marc._ 10. The soul is Christian by nature, _Apol._ 17. By this Tertullian means nothing other than that certain truths, such as those of God’s existence and unity, are known by nature rather than first through special revelation: _quaedam enim et natura nota sunt, ut immortalitas animae penes plures, ut Deus noster penes omnes_, _De resurr._ 3. With more right one appeals to Augustine, who stood in many respects under Plato’s influence and himself acknowledges this, _De beata vita_ 4. He does indeed attach much more value to thought than to sensory perception. The senses perceive only mutable things, but reason, although distinct in every human being, sees and knows universal, necessary, immutable truths. And this can be explained only by the fact that every person’s reason sees those universal truths in the one universal reason, the one immutable truth, which is God himself. Augustine therefore repeatedly declares that, just as we see sensible objects through the light of the sun, so we behold intelligible truths in the light of God, _De civ._ VIII 7; _De trin._ XII 15. He is nearer to us than creatures and easier to find than they, _De Gen. ad litt._ V 16. He is the _veritas super omnia praesidens_, _Conf._ X 41. He even says, _Deus et Dominus rerum omnium humanis mentibus, nulla natura interposita, praesidet_, _De musica_ VI 1. From this it is clear that Augustine considers it much better and easier to ascend to God by thinking through the eternal truths than by contemplating external nature. Yet it may not be inferred from this that the human soul here on earth already beholds God directly and immediately and in this way then arrives at knowledge of the eternal truths. For elsewhere he clearly says that the _visio Dei_ is reserved for heaven, _De trin._ II 17; that here on earth the _cognitio vespertina_ is allotted to us, _De Gen. ad litt._ IV 32; that the eternal truths acknowledged by man are to be distinguished from God as truth itself, _De lib. arb._ II 13; _Solil._ I 5; and that man gradually ascends from the contemplation of nature, from the knowledge of reason and its laws, to God, _Conf._ VII 10; _De lib. arb._ II, capp. 3–13. Cf. Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorzeit_ I² 756 f.; Schwane, _D. G._ II² 54–67; Gangauf, _Aug. Lehre v. Gott dem Dreieinigen_, 1883, p. 137 f.; Spruyt, _Proeve_, 43–57. Mysticism, however, had a mighty support in Augustine, and it taught that originally man received, besides an _oculus carnis_ and an _oculus rationis_, also an _oculus contemplationis_, which, restored by grace, would behold God already here at moments and perfectly in glory, Bonaventure, _Brevil._ II, c. 12. Bonaventure therefore also opposes the proposition _omnis cognitio sit a sensu_; the soul knows God and itself without the aid of the senses, _Sent._ II, dist. 39, art. 1, qu. 2. Yet mysticism in Bonaventure, although departing from Thomas on this point, remains within bounds. _Quamvis Deus sit praesens, tamen propter caecitatem et caliginem intellectus nostri, in qua sumus, ipsum cognoscimus ut absentem_, _Sent._ II, dist. 10, art. 1, qu. 1. The vision of God is not given to every human being but is solely a gift of grace, which can elevate a Paul to it, _Sent._ II, dist. 23, art. 2, qu. 3. And even with respect to the knowledge of the _prima principia_, this is indeed called innate in Bonaventure, but he explains: _quia lumen illud sufficit ad illa cognoscenda post receptionem specierum sine aliqua persuasione superaddita propter sui evidentiam. . . . Naturale enim habeo lumen, quod sufficit ad cognoscendum quod parentes sunt honorandi, et quod proximi non sunt laedendi, non tamen habeo naturaliter mihi impressam speciem patris vel speciem proximi_, _Sent._ II, dist. 39, art. 1, qu. 2. Although Bonaventure thus also accepts that there are truths we obtain not through sensory perception but through inward intuition and communion with God, neither in him is there any question of innate ideas in the proper sense; cf. the Freiburg edition of the _Breviloquium_, 1881, pp. 79 sq., 148 sq.; Schwane, _D. G._ III 111 f.; Sanseverino, _Philos. Christ._, Naples, III, art. 41. The whole of scholasticism unanimously rejected the doctrine of innate concepts. In distinction from it, scholasticism taught that the essence of things is the proper object of intellectual knowledge. All knowledge begins with sensory perception. But when this perceives things, the understanding has the capacity to abstract the universal from them, and first of all the so-called innate concepts. These are therefore not brought ready-made by the _intellectus_, but in accordance with its nature are abstracted from the perception of sensible things. This also applies to the idea of God. God is not the substance of things but their _causa_, and therefore from his works through perception and thought he can to some extent be known in his existence and perfections. There is innate knowledge only insofar as a natural _habitus_ was created in our understanding to ascend from the finite to the infinite, from the particular to the universal; cf. Volume I, p. 158 f., and further Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 2, art. 1; _C. Gent._ I, capp. 10, 11; _Theol. Wirceb._ III 5 sq.; C. Pesch, _Theol. dogm._ II 10–13; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorzeit_ I² 67 f., 587 f., _Beilagen_, 3tes Heft, pp. 3–47; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher v. d. relig. Erk._ II 28 f. The ontologism of Gioberti and Ubaghs was condemned at Rome on September 18, 1861, and September 21, 1866.
