## 2.

This naturalness, universality, and necessity of religion and the knowledge of God led early on to the thought that these were created in and innate to the human being by nature. The fact remains that from earliest youth all human beings have consciousness not only of a physical world but also of a psychical, spiritual, invisible world. True and false, good and evil, right and wrong, beautiful and ugly are admittedly not quantities that can be weighed and measured and cannot be perceived with the five senses, but they are a reality that stands much more firmly established for our consciousness than that of matter and force. Materialism may reckon only with gravity and heat and electricity, but faith and hope and love are entirely different forces that have governed humanity and kept it from sinking into bestiality. Augustine rightly said that the truth of spiritual things is actually much more certain and firmly established than that of visible things. _Nihil absurdius dici potest, quam ea esse quae oculis videmus, ea vero non esse quae intelligentia cernimus, cum dubitare dementis sit intelligentiam incomparabiliter oculis anteferri_, _De immort. an._ c. 10, n. 17; _De civ._ XIX, c. 18. The truths of mathematics and logic, the fundamental principles of ethics, law, and religion, stand indisputably firm for all. They bear a character of naturalness, universality, and necessity that can be denied by no one. As _ideae innatae_, they seem to be enclosed in human nature and given with birth.

The doctrine of innate concepts is already rooted in Greek philosophy. There it constituted a great problem for thought how learning could be possible at all. For it seemed that one of two things had to be the case: either we already know something, in which case we can no longer learn it, or we do not know it—but then how can it be explained that we strive to learn it? Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ I⁴ 996, II⁴ 823, III 189. Plato solved this problem by his doctrine of recollection: before its union with the body, the soul had beheld the ideas in all their beauty and preserved their images deep in its memory. He proved this especially from mathematics, which we can construct entirely from our own mind without the aid of sensible perception, but otherwise held that all learning presupposed the preexistence of the soul, Zeller, ibid., II⁴ 639, 643 f., 823 f. Aristotle indeed regarded sensible perception as the way to knowledge, but nevertheless judged that reason potentially brought with it certain highest, universal principles that stand firm by themselves, underlie all proofs, and are acknowledged by all, ibid., III 188 f. The Stoics spoke of κοιναι ἐννοιαι, φυσικαι ἐννοιαι, ἐμφυτοι προληψεις—that is, such concepts as, by virtue of the nature of our thinking, are derived by all from perception, ibid., IV³ 74 f., 389 f. Among all these philosophers there is not yet any question of innate ideas in the strict sense. Plato does not restrict recollection to a few innate concepts but extends it to all knowledge; and Aristotle and others indeed speak of universal principles but explicitly teach that these are not given at birth as ideas but are specifically discovered by means of perception and thought, yet in such a way that every normal human being must then also discover them. The doctrine of innate concepts in the proper sense first occurs in Cicero. He speaks of _notiones impressae, cogitationes insitae, innatae_ and assumes a knowledge of all manner of truths prior to all experience and inquiry. According to him there are _semina innata virtutum_, _Tusc._ III 1, 2; _notitiae parvae rerum maximarum_, which nature planted _sine doctrina_ in our soul, _De fin._ V 21, 59; and an innate knowledge of God, _Deos esse natura opinamur_, _Tusc._ I 16, 36; _Nat. D._ I 1, 2; etc.; Zeller, IV³ 659 f.
