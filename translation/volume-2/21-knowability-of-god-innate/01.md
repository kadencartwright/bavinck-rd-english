# § 21. The Knowability of God (_Cognitio Dei insita_)

## 1.

Just as earlier, in connection with religion, vol. I, p. 209 f., so now, in connection with the knowledge of God, it has become evident that this knowledge can have no origin other than revelation.
If God does not become manifest in his creatures, then naturally no knowledge of him is possible either.
But if he displays his perfections in the world of creatures, then the knowability of God can no longer be denied.
Naturally, this does not determine the nature and measure of that knowledge.
All who teach the knowability of God readily concede that this knowledge is wholly distinctive and very limited in extent.
For although God is to some degree made manifest in his creatures, there remains in him an infinite fullness of power and life that does not come to light.
His knowledge and power do not coincide with the world and do not pour themselves out fully into it.
He cannot even reveal himself completely to and in creatures, for the finite does not comprehend the infinite; no one knows the Father except the Son, Matt. 11:27; cf. Deut. 29:29.
Moreover, what God reveals of himself in and through creatures is already so rich and so profound that it can never be known fully by any human being.
In so many respects we do not comprehend the world of created things and at every moment find ourselves surrounded on all sides by riddles and mysteries; how, then, could we understand God’s revelation in all its richness and depth?
But the knowability of God is not thereby nullified.
So little does God’s incomprehensibility nullify this knowability that it instead presupposes and confirms it; the riches of the divine being that surpass all knowledge constitute precisely a necessary and important element of our knowledge of God.
It remains established that God is knowable to us in the same way and to the same degree as he reveals himself to us in creatures.

That there is a revelation of God in the world can hardly be denied.
First of all, Holy Scripture leaves us in no doubt about this for a moment.
It erects no altar to the unknown God but proclaims the God who made the world, Acts 17:23; whose power and divinity can be contemplated from the creatures by the human _νους_, Rom. 1:19; who above all created the human being in his image and likeness, Gen. 1:26, as his offspring, who lives and moves in him, Acts 17:28; who spoke to the human being through prophets and apostles and above all through his Son himself, Heb. 1:1; and who now continually reveals himself to and in the human being through Word and Spirit, Matt. 16:17; John 14:23, and so forth.
According to Holy Scripture, the whole world is a creation and therefore also a revelation of God; in the absolute sense nothing is atheistic.
And this testimony of Scripture is confirmed from every side.
There is no atheistic world, there are no atheistic peoples, and there are no atheistic human beings either.
The world cannot be conceived as _ἀθεος_, for then it could not be the work of God but would have to be the creation of an _ἀντιθεος_.
Dualism, which has repeatedly appeared in religion and philosophy, has indeed viewed matter as a demonic principle, but it has nevertheless always recognized in the world a union of idea and matter, a conflict between light and darkness.
No one can in the absolute sense and consistently deny altogether the knowability of God and thus his revelation in the world.
Agnosticism itself is proof of this, just as skepticism can maintain itself only with the aid of what it opposes.
And precisely because the world cannot be conceived as _ἀθεος_, there are also no atheistic and irreligious peoples.
This has indeed been asserted by Socinus, _Tract. Theol._ c. 2; _de auctor. Script._ c. 2; Locke, _Essay on human understanding_ I, ch. 4, § 8; and by many in this century, for example, Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_¹⁶ 380 f.; Darwin, _Afst. des menschen_, 1884, I 127.
But this opinion has been sufficiently refuted and is now almost universally abandoned, Moor, _Comm._ I 57; M. Vitringa, _Comm._ I 16; Flint, _Antitheïstic theories_, 3 ed., pp. 250-289, 519-532; Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, S. 260; Zöckler, _Das Kreuz Christi_, 1875, S. 417 f.
Cicero’s well-known statement that no people is so barbarous that it does not believe in the gods has been confirmed century after century.
This fact is of great significance.
What all human beings agree upon by virtue of their nature cannot be false, Cicero, _de nat. deor._ I 17.
_Opinionum enim commenta delet dies, naturae judicia confirmat_, ib. II 2.
And so, finally, there are no atheistic human beings either.
There is disagreement not so much about the existence of God as about his being.
There is indeed a practical atheism, a life without God in the world, Ps. 14:1; 53:2; Eph. 2:12.
But conscious theoretical atheism in the absolute sense is rare, if it occurs at all.
The word atheism, however, has frequently been used in a relative sense, not as a denial of deity _überhaupt_ but of a particular deity.
The Greeks accused Socrates of atheism, Xenophon, _Memor._ I 1.
Cicero counted Protagoras and Prodicus among the atheists because they denied the popular gods, _de nat. deor._ I 42.
For this reason Christians were often accused of atheism by the pagans, Suicerus, s.v. _ἀθεος_.
And Christians in turn used that name for those who denied the God of revelation, ib.
Roman Catholics have sometimes numbered Luther, Melanchthon, and Calvin among the atheists, Buddeus, _de Atheismo_, 1747, p. 116 sq.
In more recent times J. G. Fichte was publicly accused of atheism because he regarded the moral world order itself as God.
And even now the name atheist is sometimes given to those who acknowledge no power other than matter, such as Feuerbach, Strauss, Büchner, Haeckel, Czolbe, and Dühring; cf. A. Drews, _Die deutsche Spekulation seit Kant_ II 235 f.
Indeed, if the materialists acknowledge nothing other than matter and transformations of matter, then they are atheists and wish to be regarded as such themselves.
But this almost never occurs.
Taken in the absolute sense, as the denial of an absolute power, atheism is almost inconceivable.
In the end, everyone again acknowledges a power that is revered as God.
Strauss asks for a piety toward his universe like that of the believer toward his God, _Der alte u. d. neue Gl._² 141 f.
Atheism and materialism repeatedly turn into pantheism, Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_¹⁶ 3, 70, 71 f.; Haeckel, _Nat. Schöpfungsgesch._ 20, 32, 64; id., _Der Monismus als Band zw. Rel. u. Wiss._, 1893.
This occurs because the human being cannot escape the acknowledgment of a supreme power.
At the same moment that he denies the true God, he fashions a false god for himself.
Religion is rooted too deeply in human nature for that, and the revelation of God speaks too loudly.
Even when religious indifferentism and skepticism spread deeply and widely in certain periods, as, for example, in the age of Pericles, of Emperor Augustus, of the Renaissance, and also in this century, religion nevertheless always rises again.
The human being would sooner grasp at the crudest superstition than endure naked, cold unbelief in the long run.
But we can go even further.
Not only does atheism in the absolute sense almost never occur, but even in the sense of denying a personal God who has a claim upon our worship it is rare.
Naturalism, hylozoism, and pantheism are without doubt movements that repeatedly appear.
But they are movements not so much in the religious as in the philosophical domain.
They have never arisen of themselves, spontaneously, but owe their origin to criticism of the religious representations of others.
They are not dogmatic but critical, and therefore always last only for a time and within a limited circle.
A people, a society, a church, or a congregation of such naturalists and pantheists is inconceivable and impossible; the pantheists themselves acknowledge that the religious representation is necessary for the common person, while only the philosopher rises to the pure concept.
It follows that belief in a personal God is natural and normal; it arises spontaneously everywhere and in all human beings.
But atheism, even as the denial of a personal God, is the exception.
It is philosophy, not religion.
There is truth in Schopenhauer’s sharp saying: _ein unpersönlicher Gott ist gar kein Gott, sondern bloss ein missbrauchtes Wort, ein Unbegriff, eine contradictio in adjecto, ein Shiboleth für Philosophieprofessoren, welche, nachdem sie die Sache haben aufgeben mussen, mit dem Worte durchzuschleichen bemüht sind_, _Par. u. Parel._ I⁵ 123; cf. _Welt als Wille u. Vorst._ II⁶ 398, 406, 739.
Therefore a certain act of will is also necessary in order not to believe in a God, in a personal God.
_Deum non esse non credit, nisi cui Deum non esse expedit._
There are no _athei_ who are firmly and certainly convinced of their unbelief, even unto martyrdom.
Because it is abnormal and unnatural and rests not on immediate awareness but on mediate proofs and fallible reasonings, atheism is never certain of its case.
The proofs for the existence of a personal God may be weak, but they are nevertheless always stronger than those for its denial.
To prove that there is no God is even impossible; to do so one would have to be omniscient and omnipresent, that is, oneself be God; cf. Voetius, _de atheismo_, _Disp._ I 114-225; G. J. Vossius, _de origine et progressu idol._ I c. 3; Leydecker, _Fax Veritatis_ III, controv. 3; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III qu. 2; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._, 1673, p. 44; Buddeus, _Theses theol. de atheismo et superst._, ed. Lulofs, L. B., 1747; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 198, 242; Flint, _Antith. theories_³, lecture I; Hoekstra, _Des Christens godsvrucht_, p. 6; Doedes, _Inl. tot de leer v. God_, 57 f.
