## 6.

From all the foregoing it becomes clear to us why Christian theology so unanimously rejected the doctrine of _ideae innatae_. It was guided in this by fear of rationalism and mysticism. If at birth the human being already brought with him in his mind a perfectly clear and distinct knowledge, whether of all the ideas (Plato), of God (Descartes), or of being (Gioberti), he thereby became independent of the world; he could produce perfectly pure knowledge from his own mind and was sufficient unto himself; he could even dispense with the revelation God had given in his Word; more perfectly and clearly than in nature and Scripture, he found perfect knowledge in his own mind. More than that, the doctrine of _ideae innatae_ dug an unbridgeable chasm between spirit and matter, between soul and body. The visible world was then not a creation and revelation of God, not an embodiment of divine thoughts; the eternal truths, intellectual knowledge, could not be derived from it. The human being found these only through self-reflection and recollection, when he isolated himself from the world and withdrew into his own inner life. And this does indeed indicate the danger that always threatens from the side of the _ideae innatae_. The dualism of Plato led in Neoplatonism, and through it again in the Christian, specifically the Roman, Church, to a mysticism that at first, on the lower stages of meditation, indeed made use of God’s revelation in nature and Scripture, but then, having ascended higher to contemplation, could dispense with all those external aids and found the inward word, the spiritual light, and the vision and communion of God in the innermost existence of the soul perfectly sufficient. And the dualism revived in modern philosophy by Descartes, together with the related doctrine of the _idea Dei innata_, led first in Leibniz and Wolff and then in Kant, Fichte, and Hegel to a rationalism that constructed the whole world of being from the immanent thought of the human spirit. It is now as clear as day that Scripture will have nothing to do with such an αὐταρκεια of the human being or with such contempt for body and world. It teaches that the human being in soul and body bears the image of God and that through his body he is related and bound to the whole visible world. But this bond is no chain of slavery. On the contrary, the world in which the human being is placed leads him not away from God but upward to God; it is a creation of God, a mirror of his excellencies, a manifestation of his thoughts. In Calvin’s beautiful words, _nulla mundi particula, in qua non scintillae saltem aliquae gloriae ipsius emicare cernantur_, _Inst._ I 5, 1. And because Christian theology understood this, it unanimously rejected the doctrine of innate concepts. To this were added the objections drawn from psychology and history that Locke, among others, raised against this doctrine. Over against mysticism and rationalism, empiricism defended a precious truth. Indeed, when these movements maintained that the essence, the idea of things, could not come to us from sensible perception but could arise only in God (Malebranche), in the soul through ἀναμνησις (Plato), or through thought from the human being’s own spirit (Descartes–Hegel), they too had a good intention. God is indeed the sun of spirits. In his light we see light. The Logos enlightens everyone coming into the world. Nevertheless, it is true that here on earth we do not see face to face, that we walk by faith, and that we see in a mirror, dimly. Only through contemplation of God’s revelation in nature and Scripture do we arrive at the knowledge of God, Rom. 1:19; 1 Cor. 13:22; 2 Cor. 3:18. Here on earth no direct, immediate knowledge of God and his thoughts can be obtained, but only mediated knowledge, _per speculum_ and _in speculo_. The opinion of the mystics, rationalists, and ontologists is therefore not theistic but pantheistic; it confuses the _lumen rationis_ with the _lumen Dei_, the universal truths in us with the ideas in the consciousness of God, our _logos_ with the Λογος του θεου. Over against this, Christian theology set the teaching of Holy Scripture that all our knowledge of God, since his essence in itself is unknowable to us, is obtained through mediated revelation and bears an analogical character. In fact, no human being arrives at the knowledge of the _prima principia_ or at the idea of God apart from the world. The infant, born unconscious, gradually receives all manner of representations and concepts from the environment in which it is raised. With the first human being this may naturally have been different; all who were born after him came to conscious and clear knowledge, both of visible and invisible things, not each on his own and through his own reflection, but through their parents and their sphere of life. There is therefore no knowledge of the invisible except under the symbol of the visible. Whoever lacks a sense also acquires no representation of the phenomena corresponding to it. A blind person does not know what light is and therefore also does not understand that God is light except negatively and by contrast. For this reason so much difference is possible among human beings and peoples in law and morality, in religion and art. This would be inexplicable if the ideas were innate in the proper sense and had been implanted immediately in our minds by God himself. But now we see that all human beings indeed have the capacity for speech yet speak very different languages; that all indeed have an idea of God but clothe it in all manner of representations; that there are human beings who say in their hearts that there is no God; that the distinction between good and evil is known everywhere but the content of each is determined very differently; that opinions concerning right and wrong, beautiful and ugly, diverge very widely. In a word, there is not a single religious or ethical truth that has been acknowledged _ubique, semper et ab omnibus_; a _theologia naturalis_ in an actual sense has never existed, any more than a natural law and a natural morality.
