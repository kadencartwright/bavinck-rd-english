## 12.

From this there also flows an entirely distinctive view of the world. The word _creation_ can mean both the act and the product of creating. As the former is understood, so the view of the latter also changes. Pantheism attempts to explain the world dynamically, materialism mechanically. But both strive to derive everything from one all-governing principle. In the former the world may be a ζωον of which God is the soul, and in the latter a mechanism brought about by the combination and separation of atoms; in both, unconscious, blind fate is enthroned. Both fail to recognize the world’s richness and diversity, erase the boundaries between heaven and earth, matter and spirit, soul and body, human being and animal, understanding and will, eternity and time, Creator and creature, being and nonbeing, and dissolve everything into a lifeless uniformity. Both deny an end and can indicate neither a cause nor a destination for the world’s existence and history. Scripture’s view, however, is entirely different. Heaven and earth are distinct from the beginning. Everything is created with a nature of its own and rests upon ordinances that God has established for it. Sun, moon, and stars have their own task; plant, animal, and human being have distinct natures. There is the richest diversity. But in that diversity there is also the highest unity. The ground of both lies in God. It is he who created all things according to his unsearchable wisdom, who continually maintains them in their distinct natures, who leads and governs them according to the powers and laws created in them, and who, as the highest good and the final end of all things, is pursued and desired by all things in their measure and in their manner. Here is a unity that does not destroy diversity but maintains it, and a diversity that does not detract from unity but unfolds it in its riches. By virtue of that unity the world may in a figurative sense be called an organism, in which all the members are connected with one another and reciprocally affect one another. Heaven and earth, human being and animal, soul and body, truth and life, art and science, religion and morality, state and church, family and society, and so forth, are indeed distinct, but they are not separated. All kinds of relations exist among them; an organic—or, if one prefers, an ethical—bond holds them all together. Scripture expresses this clearly when it not only comprehends the universe under the name of heaven and earth but also designates it as ‎‏עולם‏‎, that is, the hidden, the invisible, the immeasurable; time in the past or future; the age, eternity, the world, Eccl. 1:4; 3:11; and in the New Testament as κοσμος, John 1:10; τα παντα, 1 Cor. 8:6; 15:25 f.; κτισις, Mark 10:16; αἰωνες, Heb. 1:2—duration, lifetime, age, world; cf. _seculum_, derived from _sexus_, human lifetime, world, and our word _wereld_, from _waeralti_, _werolt_, the lifetime of a man or of humanity. The names ‎‏עולם‏‎ and αἰωνες proceed from the thought that the world has a duration, a lifetime, and that within it a history takes place that culminates in a definite end. The Greek κοσμος and the Latin _mundus_, however, place the world’s beauty and harmony in the foreground. And the world is in fact both. Just as Paul compares the church simultaneously to a body and a building and speaks of a growing temple, Eph. 2:21, and Peter calls believers living stones, 1 Pet. 2:5, so the world is at once a history and a work of art. It is a body that grows and a building that is erected. It extends across the breadth of space and advances through the length of time. Neither materialism’s mechanical principle nor pantheism’s dynamic principle is sufficient to explain it, but whatever is true in both is acknowledged in the doctrine of the world according to the Scriptures. The world must be viewed both horizontally and vertically. From the lowest creatures it strives upward, toward the light and life of God, and at the same time it moves forward toward a God-glorifying end. Thus it displays God’s virtues and perfections—in principle already at its beginning, in an ever higher degree as its development proceeds, and one day perfectly at the end of the ages.

Augustine was the church father who understood these thoughts most deeply and worked them out most broadly. In his work _De civitate Dei_ he furnishes a Christian philosophy of history; demonstrates how the Christian worldview finds its truth and proof in history; and portrays the _civitas coelestis_ in its origin and essence, in its development and relation to the _civitas terrena_, and in its end and goal, Biegler, _Die Civitas Dei des h. Aug._, Paderborn, 1894. At the same time, however, he incorporates into it a description of the universe as a wondrously beautiful harmony. For Augustine the world is a unity: _universum ab unitate nomen accepit_, _De Gen. c. Manich._ I 21. But that unity is not uniformity; it is endlessly rich diversity, _De civ._ XI 10. God, namely, is _summum esse, summum verum, summum bonum, summum pulchrum_. And for this reason he created many creatures that participate in different degrees in his being, truth, goodness, and beauty. _Aliis dedit esse amplius et aliis minus, atque ita naturas essentiarum gradibus ordinavit_, _De civ._ XII 2. Appealing to the words from the book of Wisdom 11:21, παντα μετρω και ἀριθμω και σταθμω διεταξας, Augustine says that all things are distinguished by _modus, species, numerus, gradus, ordo_. And precisely thereby they constitute that world, that _universitas_, in which God distributes his _bona_ according to his good pleasure and which is thus a revelation of his virtues, _De div. qu._ 83, qu. 41; _De ord._ I 19; _De Gen. ad litt._ I 9; II 13; _Conf._ XII 9; _De civ._ XI 33. For all that diversity is due to God alone, not to the creature’s merit. _Non est ulla natura etiam in extremis infimisque bestiolis, quam non ille constituit, sine quibus nihil rerum inveniri vel cogitari potest_, _De civ._ XI 15; cf. Scipio, _Des Aur. Aug. Metaphysik_, Leipzig, 1886, pp. 31–80. And this worldview has been that of the whole of Christian theology. The world is one body with many members, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 28, 48. For the church fathers, the world’s unity, order, and harmony were a powerful proof for the existence and unity of God, Athanasius, _Or. c. gentes_, c. 39. God is the center, and all creatures group themselves around him in concentric circles and in a hierarchical order, Pseudo-Dionysius, _De coel._ and _De eccl. hierarchia_. Thomas compares the world to a finely tuned stringed instrument whose harmonies interpret for us the glory and blessedness of the divine life, _S. Th._ I, qu. 25, art. 6. Its parts _inveniuntur ad invicem ordinatae esse quasi partes animalis in toto, quae sibi invicem deserviunt_, Thomas, _Sent._ II, dist. I, qu. I, art. 1. _Nulla est mundi particula_, says Calvin, _in qua non scintillae saltem aliquae gloriae ejus emicare cernantur_, _Inst._ I 5, 1. _Nihil in toto mundo praestantius, nobilius, pulchrius, utilius, divinius ista rerum multarum diversitate, distinctione, ordine, quo una altera nobilior est, et una pendet ab altera, una subest alteri, una obsequium accipit ab altera. Hinc enim totius mundi est ornatus, pulchritudo, praestantia; hinc multiplex usus, utilitas, fructus nobis exoritur. Hinc ipsa Dei bonitas, gloria, sapientia, potentia clarius elucet, illustratur_, Zanchius, _Op._ III 45. And for them all the world is a _theatrum_, a _speculum lucidissimum gloriae divinae_.

Through this worldview Christianity overcame both contempt for nature and the deification of nature. In paganism the human being does not stand in the right relation to God and therefore not to the world either. Likewise, in pantheism and materialism the human being’s relation to nature is falsified in principle. By turns he regards himself as infinitely exalted above nature and supposes that it no longer holds any secrets for him; and then again he feels nature to be an incomprehensible, dark, mysterious power whose riddles he cannot solve and from whose might he cannot wrest himself free. Intellectualism and mysticism alternate; unbelief gives way to superstition; and materialism turns into occultism. But the Christian looks upward and confesses God as the Creator of heaven and earth. He sees in nature and history the unsearchableness of God’s ways and the inscrutability of his judgments, yet he does not despair, for all things stand under the government of an almighty God and a gracious Father and will therefore work together for good to those who love God. Here, then, there is room for love and admiration of nature, but every deification of it is also excluded. Here the human being is placed in the right relation to the world, because he has been placed in the right relation to God. Creation is therefore also the fundamental dogma; it stands in the foreground throughout all Scripture; it is the foundation stone upon which the Old and New Covenants rest. And this doctrine therefore finally also excludes an egoistic theology and a false optimism. Certainly, there is truth in the claim that all things exist for the human being—or rather, for humanity, for the church of Christ, 1 Cor. 3:21–23; Rom. 8:28. But that humanity, together with all creatures, finds its final end in the glorification of God. Everything is subordinate to this. Everything, including sin and suffering, contributes to this. And with this in view, the world is purposively ordered. In scholasticism the question was also discussed, _an Deus possit aliquid facere melius quam fecit_. Abelard said no, because God’s goodness must always will the best and would otherwise be selfish, _Introd. ad theol._ III, c. 5, and Leibniz later reasoned in exactly the same spirit. But in God no uncertainty and no choosing can be conceived. He did not choose the best from among many possible worlds. His will is eternally determined. A creature can in itself always be conceived as better, greater, and more beautiful than it actually is, because a creature is contingent and capable of development and perfection. Indeed, even the universe, as contingent, can be conceived by us human beings as different and better. Thomas did say: _universum non potest esse melius propter decentissimum ordinem his rebus attributum a Deo, in quo bonum universi consistit; quorum si unum aliquod esset melius, corrumperetur proportio ordinis, sicut si una chorda plus debito intenderet, corrumperetur citharae melodia_. But he added: _Posset tamen Deus alias res facere vel alias addere istis rebus factis, et esset aliud universum melius_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 25, art. 6, ad 3; cf. Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 44; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, qu. 44, art. 1, qu. 1–3; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacr._ II, c. 22; Voetius, _Disp._ I 553 sq.; Mastricht III 6, 11; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ VI 21. The creature’s nature entails that both in its being and in its mode of being it can be conceived only as contingent. But for God this question does not exist. This world is good because it answers to the end determined by him. It is neither the best nor the worst, but it is good because God has called it such. It is good because it serves not the individual human being but the revelation of God’s virtues. And for the one who views it in this way, it is also good because it enables him to know God, whom to know is eternal life. Lactantius therefore said truly: _idcirco mundus factus est ut nascamur; ideo nascimur ut agnoscamus factorem mundi ac nostri, Deum; ideo agnoscimus ut colamus; ideo colimus, ut immortalitatem pro laborum mercede capiamus, quoniam maximis laboribus cultus Dei constat; ideo praemio immortalitatis afficimur, ut similes Angelis effecti, summo Patri ac Domino in perpetuum serviamus et simus aeternum Dei regnum. Haec summa rerum est; hoc arcanum Dei; hoc mysterium mundi_, _Inst. div._ VII 6.
