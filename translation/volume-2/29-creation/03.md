## 3.

Now it deserves notice first of all that both pantheism and materialism are not results of exact inquiry but philosophy, worldview, systems of faith, not of knowledge in the strict sense. Materialism indeed likes to present itself as exact natural science, but it can easily be demonstrated that both historically and logically it is a fruit of human thought and a matter of the human heart as well as the head. The origin and end of things, after all, lie outside the boundaries of observation and inquiry; science presupposes being and rests on the foundation of the created. To that extent pantheism and materialism are in the same condition as theism, which confesses a mystery in the origin of things. The only question, therefore, is whether pantheism and materialism are able to replace this mystery with an intelligible explanation. This demand may be made of both, since they reject the doctrine of creation precisely because of its incomprehensibility and see in it a cross for the understanding. Is it indeed true that pantheism and materialism satisfy thought better than theism and therefore deserve preference? But both systems have so repeatedly arisen and been abandoned again in human history; they have so frequently been subjected to serious, conclusive criticism, that no one can accept them solely because they satisfy thought. Other motives are decisive here. If the world did not arise through creation, it must nevertheless be explained in some other way. And then, not to speak of dualism, only two paths present themselves: namely, to explain either matter from spirit or spirit from matter. Pantheism and materialism are not pure opposites; they are rather two sides of the same matter; they repeatedly pass over into each other; they approach the same problem only from a different side; both encounter the same objections. Pantheism stands before the transition from thought to being, from idea to actuality, from substance to modes, and has furnished nothing resembling a solution. It has indeed assumed different forms and designated that transition under different names. It understands the relation of God to the world as that of ἑν και παν, _natura naturans_ and _naturata_, substance and modes, being and appearance, the universal and the particular, species and specimens, whole and parts, idea and objectification, ocean and waves, and so forth, but with all these words it says nothing to explain that relation. From the pantheistic standpoint it cannot be understood how being proceeded from thought, multiplicity from unity, matter from spirit. This emerged especially clearly in the systems of Schelling and Hegel. There was no lack of words here. The idea assumes a form, a body; it objectifies itself, passes over into its other-being, divides and differentiates itself; it freely resolves to _sich zu entlassen_, _zu entäussern_, to turn into its opposite, and so forth, Schelling, _Werke_ I 2, 62 f.; 4, 223 f., 257 f.; Hegel, _Werke_ VI 413 f.; VII 23 f. But both found in this so little a satisfying solution that they repeatedly spoke of a _fall_ from the absolute through which the world arose, Schelling, ibid., 6, 38 f.; Hegel, ibid., XII 177. No wonder, therefore, that in his second period Schelling, and likewise Schopenhauer, von Hartmann, and others, accorded primacy to the will and understood the absolute first of all as nature, as will, as urge; cf. above, p. 203 f. The pantheistic identity of thought and being proved to be an error, all the more because substance, the idea, the one, the all, or whatever pantheism may call the absolute, is not a fullness of being but a pure potency, a contentless abstraction, sheer nothing. And from this the riches of the world, the multiplicity of being, could be explained! Let whoever can believe it! Kleutgen therefore says very rightly: _Darin aber besteht die Verschiedenheit der pantheistischen Speculation von der theistischen, dass jene, von ebenso dunkeln als unerweislichen Annahmen über das göttliche Sein beginnend, in offenen Widersprüchen endet, diese aber von sicheren Erkenntnissen der endlichen Dinge ausgehend, immer höhere Aufschlüsse gewinnt, bis sie vor dem Unbegreiflichen steht, nicht irre werdend, dass Der, welchen sie als den ewigen und unwandelbaren Urheber aller Dinge erkennt, in seinem Sein und Wirken über unser Denken erhaben sei_, _Phil. der Vorz._ II² 884. The origin of things remains just as unexplained in materialism. While pantheism causes the universe to proceed from one ultimate principle that coincides with the world, materialism assumes a multiplicity of _principia_. But according to materialism these ultimate _principia_ of all things are nothing other than indivisible particles of matter. Now if the advocates of this worldview remained faithful to this fundamental proposition of theirs, they would be unable to ascribe a single metaphysical, transcendent predicate to these atoms. Properly considered, it is impermissible from the standpoint of materialism to speak of the eternity, uncreatedness, or indestructibility of atoms, or even of matter _and_ force. If one says that the world arose from material atoms, one must remain faithful to this as well. For atoms, because they are elements of the empirical world, can bear only empirical and no metaphysical attributes. The concept of atom by no means includes that it is by nature and _per se_ eternal and indestructible. Whoever regards atoms as the ultimate _principia_ of all being cuts off the road to speculation and metaphysics and must therefore explain the world from those empirical atoms alone along the empirical path. The materialist can say only that experience teaches that atoms do not arise and do not perish; but he may not speak of a metaphysical nature and metaphysical attributes of atoms. Natural science, to which the materialist always appeals, deals as such with the finite, the relative, with nature and its phenomena. It always proceeds from nature, assumes it as given, and cannot penetrate to what lies behind it. As soon as it does so, it ceases to be physics and becomes metaphysics. But materialism does not remain faithful to itself and immediately ascribes all kinds of attributes to the atoms that are not enclosed in the concept itself and are not taught by experience. Therefore materialism is no exact science, no fruit of strictly scientific inquiry, but a philosophy built upon the denial of all philosophy. It suffers from an internal contradiction; it denies all that is absolute and makes the atoms absolute; it denies God’s existence and deifies matter. One can speak even more strongly: if materialism wishes to explain all things from matter, it lacks every right to speak of atoms. Atoms have never been perceived; no one has ever seen them; empirical inquiry did not bring them to light. From the outset they bear a metaphysical nature and should therefore already be contraband for materialism. And as metaphysical substances they stand in an antinomy that no one has yet resolved. They are material and yet at the same time are supposed to be indivisible, immutable, infinite in number, eternal, and indestructible. And if, after all this, matter itself, which is assumed as the explanatory principle of the universe, were only known and intelligible! But precisely the being and nature of matter are the most mysterious of all and entirely withdraw themselves from our knowledge. We can still better conceive and represent to ourselves what spirit is than what matter is. Matter is a name, a word, but we do not know what is to be understood by it. Here we stand before a mystery, in its kind just as great as the existence of spirit, which materialism rejects because of its incomprehensibility. But granted that there are atoms and that they are eternal and immutable, nothing has yet thereby been achieved toward explaining the world. How did the world arise from those atoms? If the presently existing world, or even a preceding one, had a beginning, there must be a cause by which the atoms came into motion, and specifically into such a motion as had the present world for its result. But motion cannot be explained from matter, for all matter is by nature inert and comes into motion only through an impulse from outside. A _primum movens_ outside matter cannot, however, be assumed by materialism. And so nothing else remains than to make motion, change, and time, like the atoms—or with Czolbe even this existing world—absolute and eternal. Materialism entangles itself in ever greater contradictions. It confuses the physical and metaphysical, becoming and being, change and immutability, time and eternity, and speaks of infinite space, infinite time, infinite world, as though it were nothing and the most absurd contradiction did not lie in it. Finally, it has repeatedly and from various sides been demonstrated that materialism fails to explain from the mechanical combination of merely material and thus unconscious, lifeless, unfree, purposeless atoms the entire spiritual world of life, consciousness, purpose, religion, morality, and so forth, which presses itself upon our inner awareness with no less force than the physical world upon our senses. And it appears that this criticism is gradually beginning to make some impression upon materialists themselves. Materialism in this century, which arose from pantheism, increasingly returns to pantheism and even includes all kinds of mystical elements within itself, _Wetensch. Bladen_, May 1896. The long-rejected vital force now again finds defenders. Atoms are represented as living, ensouled. Haeckel again speaks of a “_Geist in allen Dingen_,” a “_göttliche Kraft_,” a “_bewegender Geist_,” a “_Weltseele_” that dwells in all things, and seeks in this pantheistic monism the bond between religion and science.
