## 2.

The doctrine of creation is known only from revelation; it is understood by faith, Heb. 11:3.
Roman Catholics indeed teach that it can also be discovered from nature by reason, Thomas, _Sent._ II dist. 1 qu. 1 art. 2; _S. c. Gent._ II 15, 16; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorz._ II² 795 f.; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 5, 6, and the Vatican Council even elevated this to a dogma, _De fide_ c. 2 can. 2.
But the history of religions and of philosophy does not support this.
Islam indeed teaches creation from nothing but borrowed this from Judaism and Christianity, Zöckler, _Gesch. der Beziehungen zw. Theol. und Naturw._, 1877, I 426 f.
The pagan cosmogonies are all polytheistic and at the same time theogonies; they all assume a primordial matter, an _Urstoff_, whether this is conceived as a chaos, as a personal principle, as an egg, or something similar; and finally they are either more emanationist, so that the world is an efflux of God, or more evolutionist, so that the world becomes increasingly deified, or more dualistic, so that the world is a product of two hostile principles, Wuttke, _Die Kosmogonien der heidn. Völker vor der Zeit Jesu und der Apostel_, The Hague, 1850; Lenormant, _Les origines de l’histoire d’après la Bible et les traditions des peuples orientaux_, Paris, 1880; Zöckler, in Herzog² 13, 631 f.
The Chaldean Genesis, which otherwise offers remarkable parallels with that of the Old Testament, is no exception to this.
It is at the same time a theogony and has Bel form the world from Tiamat, who chaotically conceals everything within herself, Saussaye, _Lehrb. der Rel._ I 341 f.
Greek philosophy seeks the origin of things either materialistically in a material element (the Ionian school, the Atomists), pantheistically in the one, eternal, immutable being (Elea), or in eternal becoming (Heraclitus, the Stoa).
Even Anaxagoras, Plato, and Aristotle did not rise above a dualism of spirit and matter; God is not a creator but at most a fashioner of the world (δημιουργος).
The scholastics indeed maintained that Plato and Aristotle taught creation from nothing, but this was rightly disputed by others, including Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 1 p. 1 art. 1 qu. 1.
The Greeks knew a φυσις (_natura_) and κοσμος (_mundus_) but no κτισις (_creatura_).
Christianity gained the victory over this pagan theogony and cosmogony in the struggle against Gnosticism, which for the explanation of sin assumed alongside the supreme God a lower god or an eternal ὑλη.
But the pagan explanations of the origin of things nevertheless repeatedly resurfaced in the Christian centuries as well.
Already in the book of Wisdom 11:17 it is said that God’s almighty hand created the world from ἀμορφος ὑλη, and the same expression also occurs in Justin Martyr, _Apol._ I 10, 59.
But Justin has in view here what was later called _creatio secunda_ and elsewhere expressly teaches the creation of matter as well, _Dial. c. Tr._ 5; _Coh. ad Gr._ 23; cf. Semisch, _Justin der M._ II 336.
As Gnosticism arose in the second century, so after Nicaea Manichaeism especially arose, which likewise for the explanation of sin assumed alongside the good God an originally evil being; Augustine, in many writings, _C. epist. Manich._, _C. Faustum_, _C. Fortunatum_, and so forth; Flügel, _Mani, seine Lehre und seine Schriften_, Leipzig, 1862; Kessler, in Herzog² 9, 233 f.
This dualism found wide diffusion in Christendom, even among the Priscillianists in Spain, and reappeared in many sects in the Middle Ages, such as the Bogomils and Cathars.
But besides dualism, pantheism also found its interpreters.
Under the influence of Neoplatonism, Pseudo-Dionysius taught that the ideas and exemplars of all things existed in God from eternity and that his overflowing goodness moved him to give these ideas reality and to communicate himself to his creatures, _De div. nom._ c. 4 § 10.
In creatures God has, as it were, emerged from his unity, multiplied himself, and poured himself out, _De div. nom._ c. 2, 11, so that God is universal being, _De div. nom._ c. 5, 4, the very being of things, _De coel. hier._ c. 4, 1.
But he nevertheless adds that God maintains himself in his unity, _De div. nom._ c. 2, 11, and that God is therefore all in all because he is the cause of everything, _De coel. hier._ c. 5, 8.
The same thoughts recur in Erigena.
He repeatedly teaches expressly a creation from nothing, _De div. nat._ III 15; V 24, 33, but the relation in which he places the four natures to one another makes his system pantheistic.
The first nature, which creates and is not created, that is, God, by thinking brings forth from nothing—that is, from himself—the ideas and forms of all things in the divine Word, ibid., III 14, 17.
This is the second nature, which is created and creates.
This second nature is _natura creata_ insofar as it is brought forth by God; it is _creatrix_ insofar as it is itself the cause and potentiality of the actual world.
For this second nature is not essentially and substantially distinguished from the third nature, that is, the world of appearance, which is created and does not create; the former is the cause, the latter the effect; it is the same world considered at one time _in aeternitate verbi Dei_, then _in temporalitate mundi_, III 8.
It is God himself who first creates himself in the ideas, then descends into creatures and becomes all in all, in order finally to return to himself in the fourth nature, which does not create and is not created, III 4, 20.
And the cause of this process in God is God’s goodness, III 2, 4, 9, his striving to become everything, _appetitus quo vult omnia fieri_, I 12.
Outside the Christian world, pantheism was taught by the philosophers Avicenna 1036 and Averroës 1198; by Sufism, which saw in the universe an emanation of God; and among the Jews by the Kabbalah, Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II 28, 92, 181, 237.
And toward the end of the Middle Ages and at the beginning of the modern age, all these pantheistic, dualistic, emanationist ideas became intermingled among mystics, theosophists, and Anabaptists such as Floris, Amalric of Bena, the Brethren of the Free Spirit, the Libertines, Eckhart, Tauler, Servetus, Franck, Schwenckfeld, Bruno, Paracelsus, Fludd, Weigel, and Böhme; even Socinianism taught only a creation from an ἀμορφος ὑλη, _materia informis_, in Fock, _Der Socin._, S. 482; the finite and the infinite were placed too abstractly alongside and over against each other for the former to be able to have its cause in the latter.
But pantheism as a philosophical system was first restored to honor by Spinoza.
He saw in the one substance the eternal and necessary _causa efficiens_ and _immanens_ of the world; the world is the explication of the divine being, and particular things are the _modi_ by which the divine attributes of thought and extension are determined in a particular manner, _Eth._ I.
Toward the end of the previous century this philosophy found increasing agreement and was elevated by Schelling and Hegel into the system of the nineteenth century.
The biblical doctrine of creation was rejected entirely.
Fichte said: _die Annahme einer Schöpfung ist der Grundirthum aller falschen Metaphysik und Religionslehre und insbesondere das Urprinzip des Juden- und Heidenthums_, _Anweisung zum seligen Leben_, 1806, S. 160.
Schelling called creation from nothing _das Kreuz des Verstandes_ and decisively opposed it, _Werke_ I 2, S. 44 f.; 8, S. 62 f.
In his first period he taught the absolute identity of God and the world; they are related as being and form; considered from different points of view, they are the same; God is not the cause of the All but the All itself, and therefore the All is not becoming but an eternally existent being, ἑν και παν, _Werke_ I 4, S. 117 f.; 5, S. 24 f., 365 f., 373 f.; 6, S. 174 f.
But in his later period, through Baader he came under the influence of Böhme and thus of the Kabbalah and Neoplatonism, and now seeks the ground of the world in the dark nature of God.
Theogony and cosmogony are most closely related.
Just as God raises himself out of his indifference through the opposition of the _principia_—nature (_Urgrund, Ungrund_, darkness) and understanding (word, light)—to Spirit, love, personality, so these three are at the same time the _Potenzen_ of the world.
The dark nature in God is the blind, unordered principle, the matter and ground of the created world insofar as it is chaos and bears a chaotic character.
But in that world the _Potenz_ of the divine understanding also works and brings light, order, and regularity into it.
Finally, in the human spirit God himself reveals himself as Spirit and becomes full personality, _Werke_ I 7, S. 359 f.; II 2, S. 108 f.; 3, S. 262 f.; cf. above, p. 305.
And Hegel likewise openly confessed pantheism—not indeed the pantheism that regards finite things themselves as God, but nevertheless that pantheism which sees in the finite and contingent the appearance of the Absolute, the petrified idea, frozen intelligence, _Werke_ VII 23 f.
From philosophy this pantheism passed into theology.
Schleiermacher rejected the separation of creation and preservation and considered the question of the temporality or eternity of the world indifferent, provided that the absolute dependence of all things upon God was maintained, _Glaub._ §§ 36, 41; Bovon, _Dogm. Chrét._ I 289.
And likewise God is only the eternal immanent cause and ground of the world in Strauss, _Glaub._ I 656 f.; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ § 649 f.; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ § 71; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss der chr. Gl. u. Sittenlehre_ § 84; Scholten, _Dogm. christ. initia_, ed. 2, p. 111; Hoekstra, _Wijsg. Godsd._ II 174 f.
Alongside this pantheism, materialism also arose, which seeks the ultimate elements of all being in eternal, uncreated, and indestructible material atoms and attempts to explain from their mechanical and chemical separation and combination, taking place according to fixed laws, all phenomena, the whole world.
It was already prepared in Greek philosophy, was reintroduced into the modern age by Gassendi and Descartes, and was promoted by the English and French philosophy of the previous century.
And in this century it arose not as the fruit of scientific investigation but as the product of philosophical reflection in Feuerbach, who can be called the father of materialism in Germany, and then especially after 1850 found acceptance among practitioners of natural science through all kinds of incidental causes, such as Vogt, Büchner, Moleschott, Czolbe, Haeckel, Strauss, and others; Lange, _Gesch. des Materialismus_, 4th ed., 1882.
