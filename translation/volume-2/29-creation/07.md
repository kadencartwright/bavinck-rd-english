## 7.

Nevertheless, although creation is a work of the entire Trinity, it cannot be denied that in Scripture it still stands in a distinctive relation to the Son, one that deserves separate discussion.
The Old Testament frequently says that God created all things through the word, Gen. 1:3; Pss. 33:6; 148:5; Isa. 48:13, that through wisdom he founded the earth and through understanding prepared the heavens, Ps. 104:24; Prov. 3:19; Jer. 10:12; 51:15.
But this wisdom is also presented personally as counselor and master worker of creation.
God acquired and possessed her, ordered her and searched her out, so that through her, as the firstfruits of his way and the beginning of his works, he would create and order the world.
And thus she was already with him before creation, and during creation she worked with him and rejoiced in the works of God’s hands, above all in the children of humanity, Prov. 8:22-31; Job 28:23-27.
In the New Testament this doctrine is developed further.
There we read not only that God created everything through the Son, John 1:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 1:15-17; Heb. 1:3, but Christ is also called πρωτοτοκος πασης κτισεως, Col. 1:15; ἀρχη της κτισεως του θεου, Rev. 3:14; the Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end of all things, Rev. 1:17; 21:6; 22:6; the one unto whom everything is created, Col. 1:17, in order to be gathered together again under him as Head, Eph. 1:10.
In all these passages Christ has not only a soteriological but also a cosmological significance.
He is Mediator not only of re-creation but also of creation.
The Apologists did not yet know what to do with these thoughts of Scripture.
Standing under Platonic influence, they often saw in the Logos little more than the κοσμος νοητος; they connected him most closely with the world, regarded his generation as motivated by creation, and did not yet make a sufficient distinction between the birth of the Son and the creation of the world; they were still wrestling with the Gnostic thought that the Father is properly the hidden, invisible Godhead and first becomes manifest through the Logos; cf. above, p. 251 f.
Although this Gnostic element was banished from theology by the church fathers, especially Athanasius and Augustine, it nevertheless repeatedly returned within it.
The root from which this thought springs is always a certain dualism, a more or less sharp opposition between spirit and matter, between God and the world.
God is invisible, inaccessible, hidden; the world is, if not anti-divine, nevertheless undivine, godless, _athée_.
To reconcile this fundamental opposition, an intermediate being is needed, and that is the Logos.
In relation to God he is the world-idea, the world-image, the κοσμος νοητος, and in relation to the world he is the proper Creator, the principle of the possibility that a world comes into existence.
Among the Moravians this even led to the Father receding entirely and Christ being the proper Creator; re-creation devours creation, grace destroys nature.
Various mediating theologians, _Vermittelungstheologen_, teach that the Logos is the world according to its idea and that it therefore _gehört zum Wesen des Sohnes, sein Leben nicht mehr im Vater sondern auch in der Welt zu haben; als das Herz des Vaters ist er zugleich das ewige Weltherz, der ewige Weltlogos_, Martensen, _Dogm._ § 125; Meyer on Col. 1:16.
This conception then leads of itself to the doctrine of incarnation apart from sin.
The world is profane in itself; creation is properly speaking no essentially divine work.
If God is to be able to create, if the world and humanity are to be capable of pleasing him, then he must see them in Christ.
Christ is the one in whom and for whose sake alone the world can be willed by God; in whom, as Head and _Centralindividuum_, humanity alone can be well pleasing to God; the incarnation is necessary for God’s revelation and communication, and the God-man is the highest end of creation; cf. Dorner, _Entw. der Lehre von der Person Christi_ III 1227-1262; Nitzsch, _Syst. der chr. Lehre_, S. 204; Lange, _Chr. Dogm._ II 215; and especially also Keerl, _Der Mensch, das Ebenbild Gottes_ II 1 f.
Finally, this chain of thought reaches completion in the doctrine that creation is necessary for God himself; God _an sich_ is indeed nature, _Urgrund_, βυθος, σιγη, but in order himself to become personality, spirit, he needs creation.
Creation is the history of God; cosmogony is theogony, Schelling, _Werke_ II 2, S. 109.

This Gnosticism can be overcome in principle only when every dualism between God and the world is cut off at the root.
Creating is no lower and less divine work than re-creating; nature does not stand beneath grace; the world is not profane in itself.
And thus no lower divine being was needed to enable the Father to create the world.
The Christian church believes in the Father, the Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth.
Creation is by no means more the work of the Son than of the Father.
All things are from God.
And the Christian church confesses of the Son that he is not less than the Father and does not stand nearer to creatures, but that he is of one essence with the Father and with the Spirit, and that together they are the one, eternal, true God, Creator of heaven and earth.
But the Son does indeed occupy a place of his own in the work of creation.
Augustine especially brought this to light.
Although he did not identify the ideas of things with the Logos, as the Apologists had done, he nevertheless had to connect them with the Logos.
The world was indeed not eternal, but its idea was nevertheless eternal in the consciousness of God.
The Father speaks all his thoughts, his whole being, in the one personal Word, and therefore the world-idea is also contained in the Logos; the Logos can therefore be called _forma quaedam, forma non formata sed forma omnium formatorum_, _Serm._ 117; cf. _De lib. art._ III 16 and 17; _De trin._ XI 10; XV 14; cf. Anselm, _Monol._ 34; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 34 art. 3; cf. 44 art. 3.
This enables the significance of the Son for creation to be established.
First, it is the Father from whom the initiative of creation proceeds, who thinks the world-idea, but everything the Father is and has and thinks he communicates to and expresses in the Son.
In him the Father beholds the very idea of the world, not as though it were identical with the Son, but such that in the Son, in whom his entire fullness dwells, it stands before him and meets him.
In the divine wisdom is also enclosed, as a part, as an epitome, that wisdom which will be realized in creatures.
He is the Logos through whom the Father creates all things; the whole world is a realized thought of God, a book with large and small letters from which his wisdom can be known.
But he is not only _causa exemplaris_ but also ἀρχη δημιουργικη.
The word that God speaks is no sound without content; it is powerful and living.
The word, the world-idea, that the Father expresses in the Son is a _ratio seminalis, forma principalis_ of the world itself; therefore the Son is called the ἀρχη, πρωτοτοκος, ἀρχη της κτισεως, the firstborn who bears creation, in whom it rests, from whom it arises as its cause and exemplar; and therefore that word which the Father speaks at creation and through which he calls things out of nothing into being is also powerful, for it is spoken in and through the Son.
And finally, the Son is also in a certain sense the _causa finalis_ of the world.
Because it has its foundation and exemplar in him, it is also created unto him, not indeed as the ultimate final end but nevertheless as Head, Lord, and Heir of all things, Col. 1:16.
Comprehended in the Son and gathered under him as Head, all creatures return again to the Father, from whom are all things.
And thus the world has its eternal idea, its ἀρχη, and its τελος in the triune being of God.
The word that the Father speaks in the Son is the full expression of the divine being and thus also of all that which through that word will exist as creature outside the divine being.
And the spiration through which Father and Son are the _principium_ of the Spirit also contains within itself the willing of that world whose idea is comprehended in the divine Wisdom, Kleutgen, _Philos._ II 870.
Therefore creation proceeds from the Father through the Son in the Spirit, in order that in the Spirit through the Son it may return again to the Father.
