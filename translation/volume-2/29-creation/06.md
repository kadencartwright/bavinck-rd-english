## 6.

Correspondingly, Holy Scripture also teaches that the triune God is the author of creation. Scripture knows no intermediate beings. The Jews thought of the angels in connection with the plural in Gen. 1:26, Weber, _Syst._ 170. The Gnostics caused a series of aeons that acted creatively to proceed from God. The Arians made the Son an intermediate being between Creator and creature, one who, though created, nevertheless in turn himself created. In the Middle Ages many were not disinclined to assume the possibility of the creature’s cooperation in creation. They arrived at this because in the church forgiveness of sins and distribution of grace were inherent in the office, and in the mass a priest could change the bread into the body of Christ and thus become a _creator sui creatoris_ (Biel). Hence Lombard says in the doctrine of the sacraments that God also _posset per aliquem creare aliqua, non per eum tanquam auctorem sed ministrum cum quo et in quo operaretur_, _Sent._ IV, dist. 5, n. 3. Followed by a few, such as Durandus, Suárez, and Bellarmine, he was nevertheless contradicted by others such as Thomas, Scotus, Bonaventure, Richard of St. Victor, and others, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 45, art. 5; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorzeit_ II² 849 f. With this the Reformed also agreed, who resisted even more than Roman Catholics and Lutherans every commingling of Creator and creature, Voetius, _Disp._ I 556 sq.; _Synopsis_ X 14; Turretin, _Th. El._ V, qu. 2; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ VI 14; Mastricht III 5, 20; M. Vitringa II 81–82. Scripture ascribes creation exclusively to God, Gen. 1:1; Isa. 40:12 f.; 44:24; 45:12; Job 9:5–10; 38:2 f. By this he is distinguished from the idols, Ps. 96:5; Isa. 37:16; Jer. 10:11, 12. Creating is a divine work, an act of infinite power, and therefore communicable neither in nature nor in grace to any creature, whatever it may be. But all the more unanimously did Christian theology ascribe the work of creation to all three persons in the Trinity. Scripture left no doubt about this either. God created all things through the Son, Ps. 33:6; Prov. 8:22; John 1:3; 5:17; 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 1:15–17; Heb. 1:3, and through the Spirit, Gen. 1:2; Ps. 33:6; Job 26:13; 33:4; Ps. 104:30; Isa. 40:13; Luke 1:35. And in this the Son and Spirit are conceived not as original powers but as independent _principia_, as _auctores_, who with the Father bring about the work of creation, just as with him they are also the one true God. In the Christian church this doctrine of Scripture did not immediately come into its own. At first the Logos was understood too much as an intermediate being who effected the transition between God and the world, and the person and work of the Holy Spirit still receded entirely into the background, above, p. 251 f. Yet Irenaeus already said that God needed no alien instruments in creation and did not use the angels in it, but that he has his own hands, the Logos and the Holy Spirit, through whom and in whom he created everything, _Adv. haer._ IV 20. The doctrine of creation as the work of the entire Trinity was clearly developed by Athanasius and the three Cappadocians in the East and by Augustine in the West. No creature, says Athanasius, can be ποιητικον αἰτιον of creation. If the Son therefore creates the world with the Father, he cannot be an extra-divine, created demiurge, as Arius thinks, but must be the Father’s own Son, ἰδιον γεννημα της οὐσιας αὐτου, _C. Ar._ II 21 sq. But where the Logos is, there is also the Spirit, and therefore ὁ πατηρ δια του λογου ἐν τῳ πνευματι κτιζει τα παντα, _Ad Serap._ III 5. Augustine speaks even more strongly: _ab hac summe et aequaliter et immutabiliter bona trinitate creata sunt omnia_, _Enchir._ 10, so that the whole creation is a _vestigium trinitatis_, _De trin._ VI 10; _De civ._ XI 24; _Conf._ XIII 11. Thus this doctrine became the common possession of all Christian theology, John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 45, art. 6; Luther, _Art. Smalc._ I 1; Calvin, _Inst._ I 14, 20, and also of the various confessions, Denzinger, _Enchir. Symb._, nos. 202, 227, 231, 232, 355, 367, 598; Müller, _Die symb. Bücher der ev. luth. K._, 5th ed., pp. 38, 299; Niemeyer, _Coll. conf. eccl. Ref._, pp. 87, 331, 341; etc. This doctrine found opposition only among those who also reject the ecclesial dogma of the Trinity and at most wish to know only of a creation by the Father through the Son, but in no case acknowledge in creation a common work of the three divine persons, such as the Arians, Socinians, Remonstrants, rationalists, and in more recent times Martensen, _Dogm._ §61; van Oosterzee, _Dogm._ §56, 5; and especially Doedes, _Ned. Gel._ 121 f. The two dogmas stand and fall together. The confession of the unity of being of the three persons naturally entails that all _opera ad extra_ are common and indivisible; conversely, opposition to the Trinitarian work of creation is evidence of deviation in the doctrine of the Trinity. What matters here is only to make, with Scripture and with church fathers such as Athanasius, the strictest distinction between Creator and creature and to guard against every Gnostic commingling. If Son and Spirit appear in Scripture as independent _principia_, as _auctores_ of creation, then they also partake of the divine being; and if they are true God, they also share in the work of creation. The Arian doctrine, by contrast, entangles itself in insoluble difficulties. It cannot be denied that Scripture teaches creation as a work of the Father through the Son. If the Son is now regarded as a person outside the divine being, there is reason for the question: no sense can be attached to the Father’s creating through the Son; Scripture says it, but what can it mean? Did the Father assign creation to the Son? Then the Son would be the proper Creator. Did Father and Son create everything together? But then it is not creation through the Son, Doedes, at the place cited, p. 128. The doctrine of the Trinity causes the true light to arise here. Just as God is one in being and distinct in persons, so the work of creation is one and undivided and yet in that unity rich in diversity. It is one God who creates all things, and therefore the world is a unity, just as conversely the unity of the world proves the unity of God. But in that one divine being are three persons, each of whom fulfills a task of his own in the one work of creation. This is not in the sense that creation belongs _principaliter_ to the Father and _minus principaliter_ to the Son and the Spirit, or that the three persons work independently alongside one another, supplement one another’s labor, and are three separate _causae efficientes_ of creation. The manner of speaking of _tres causae sociae_ among some theologians therefore met with objection among many, Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bezieh._ I 621 f., 679 f. There is indeed cooperation but no division of labor. All things are simultaneously from the Father through the Son in the Spirit. The Father is the first cause; from him the initiative proceeds; creation is therefore in an economic sense ascribed particularly to him. The Son is not the instrument but the personal wisdom, the Logos through whom everything is created; everything rests and has its system in him, Col. 1:17, and is created unto him, Col. 1:16, not as final end but as Head and Lord of all creatures, Eph. 1:10. And the Holy Spirit is the personal, immanent cause through whom all things live and move and have their being in God, receive their form and shape, and are led to their destination. Cf. above, p. 417 f.; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I 328 f.; Kuyper, _Het werk van den H. Geest_ I 20 f.
