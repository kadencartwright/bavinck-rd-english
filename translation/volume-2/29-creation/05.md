## 5.

This doctrine of Scripture finds its sharpest expression in the words _from nothing_, and from the beginning it was thus understood and expressed by Christian theology, Hermas, _Pastor_ I 1; Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ II 4; Tertullian, _De praescr._ 13; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 10; etc. But among Gnostics and Manichaeans, theosophists and naturalists, pantheists and materialists, this doctrine has at all times encountered opposition. Especially the maxim derived from Aristotle, _ex nihilo nihil fit_, has been brought forward against it. Yet this objection is wholly without ground. First, this rule of Aristotle is far from being as simple as it appears; at every moment we confront phenomena that cannot be reduced to the factors at hand; history is not a calculation; life is not a product of chemical compounds; genius is still something other and something more than the child of its time, and every personality is something original. But apart from this, and understood _cum grano salis_, this rule of Aristotle meets with no contradiction. Theology has never taught that nonbeing was the father, the source, the _principium_ of being. Moreover, it repeatedly added that the expression _ex nihilo_ did not designate a preexistent matter from which the world was formed; rather, it indicated only that what now exists once did not exist and that it was called into being solely by God’s almighty power. The expression _ex nihilo_ is thus equivalent to _post nihilum_; _particula ex non designat sed excludit materiam_; the world has its cause not in itself but in God alone, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 14; Augustine, _Conf._ XI 5; XII 7; _De Gen. ad litt._ I 1; Anselm, _Monol._ c. 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 45, art. 1; etc. The expression was so readily retained in Christian theology only because it was particularly suited to cutting all kinds of error off at the root. First, it served to oppose the pagan doctrine of an ἀμορφος ὑλη, above which even Plato and Aristotle had been unable to rise. In paganism the human being is bound to matter, subjected to sensuality and the worship of nature; he cannot grasp the thought that spirit stands free over against and above matter, and still less that God is absolutely sovereign, determined by nothing but his own being. Over against this, creation from nothing teaches God’s absolute sovereignty, his complete independence; if even a single particle of matter had not been created from nothing, God would not be God. In the second place, this expression excludes every emanation, every identity of essence between God and the world. The scholastics did indeed repeatedly speak of an _emanatio_ or _processio totius entis a causa universali_, and also of the creature’s participation in the being and life of God. But by this they did not mean emanation in the proper sense, as though the divine being flowed out into creatures and unfolded itself within them, as a genus does in its species. Rather, they intended only to say that God is _ens per essentiam_, but the creature _ens per participationem_; creatures do indeed have a being of their own, but the divine being is its _causa efficiens_ and _exemplaris_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 45, art. 1; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorz._ II 828 f., 899 f. Creation from nothing maintains that there is an essential distinction between God and the world. Creation consists neither in a transition of the world from a being in God to a being outside God, nor in a transition from a being without God to a being through God, but in a transition from nonbeing to being. The world is certainly no αντιθεος; it does not exist independently; it is and remains in God as its continual _causa immanens_, as must later be demonstrated in treating preservation over against Manichaeism and deism. But according to the doctrine of Scripture, it is neither a part nor an efflux of the being of God. It has an essence and being of its own, another essence and being distinct from the _essentia Dei_. And this is expressed by _ex nihilo_. Yet philosophy has also misused this term. Just as Plato understood by μη ὀν an eternal unformed matter, so Erigena even designated God as _nihilum_, insofar as he is exalted above all categories and determinations, above all being and essence: _dum ergo incomprehensibilis intelligitur, per excellentiam nihilum non immerito vocitatur_, _De div. nat._ III 19. And when he brings everything forth from nothing, this means that he _de sua videlicet superessentialitate producit essentias, de supervitalitate vitas_, etc., _De div. nat._ III 20. Hegel treated this concept even more strangely in his _Logik_, when by nothing he understood a _Nicht-seyn, das zugleich Seyn, und ein Seyn, das zugleich Nicht-seyn ist_; a nothing that at the same time is everything, namely, everything _in potentia_ and nothing determinate _in concreto_, _Werke_ III 64, 73 f. Christian theology stands directly opposed to this philosophical confusion of concepts; it understands by nothing a pure _nihil negativum_ and rejects every emanation. Yet emanation also contains a true idea, which is maintained precisely by the biblical doctrine of creation without violating the being of God, far better than philosophy does. The doctrine of creation from nothing, finally, gives Christian theology a place between Gnosticism and Arianism, that is, between pantheism and deism. Gnosticism knows no creation but only emanation and therefore makes the world into the Son, the wisdom, the image of God in an adequate sense; Arianism knows no emanation but only creation and therefore makes the Son into a creature; there the world is deified; here God is reduced to the level of the world. But Scripture, and Christian theology in accordance with it, knows both emanation and creation: a twofold communication of God, one within and one outside the divine being; one to the Son, who in the beginning was with God and was himself God, and one to the creatures, which came into existence in time; one from eternity and one in time; one from the divine being and one through God’s will. The first is called generation, the second creation. Through generation the adequate image of God is eternally communicated to the Son; through creation only a weak, faint likeness of God is communicated to the creature. Yet the two are connected. Without generation, creation would not be possible. If God could not communicate himself absolutely to the Son, much less could he communicate himself in a relative sense to his creature. If God did not exist in a trinitarian manner, creation would not be possible, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 12; II 56, 78.
