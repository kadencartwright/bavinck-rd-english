## 4.

Over against all these movements, the Christian church unanimously maintained the confession: _credo in Deum patrem, omnipotentem, creatorem coeli et terrae_; and by creation it understood that act of God by which, according to his sovereign will, he brings the whole world from nonbeing into a being that is distinct from his own being.
And this is indeed the teaching of Holy Scripture.
The word ברא originally means to split, divide, cut (Josh. 17:15, 18, in the Piel, of cutting down forests), and hence to form, bring forth, create.
It does not in itself yet express that something is brought forth from nothing, for it is also frequently used of the works of preservation, Isa. 40:28; 45:7; Jer. 31:22; Amos 4:13.
It is synonymous and alternates with עשׂה, יצר, חֹדֶּשׁ, Ps. 104:30.
Yet it differs from these in that it is always used of divine making and never of human doing; that it never has with it an accusative of the material from which something is made; and that precisely for this reason it everywhere expresses the greatness and power of divine working; Delitzsch, _Neuer Comm. zur Gen._, p. 48.
And the same is true of the New Testament words κτιζειν, Mark 13:19; ποιειν, Matt. 19:4; θεμελιουν, Heb. 1:10; καταρτιζειν, Rom. 9:12; κατασκευαζειν, Heb. 3:4; πλασσειν, Rom. 9:20, which likewise do not in themselves express a creation from nothing.
The term creation from nothing is therefore not derived literally from Scripture but first occurs in 2 Macc. 7:28, where it is said that God made heaven and earth and humanity ἐξ οὐκ ὀντων ἐποιησεν, Vulgate: _fecit ex nihilo_.
It has been disputed that this expression may be understood in the strict sense, and it has been given a Platonic interpretation.
Nevertheless, it deserves attention that the writer speaks not of μη ὀντα, that is, a _nihilum privativum_, a matter without quality and form, but of οὐκ ὀντα, that is, a _nihilum negativum_.
Further, it is not even certain that the author of the book of Wisdom in cap. 11:18 taught the eternity of a formless matter; the passage can very well be understood of the _creatio secunda_, as was later also the case with Justin Martyr.
But however this may be, Scripture leaves no doubt in this respect.
It does not use the term, but it clearly teaches the thing itself.
It has indeed been thought that Gen. 1:1–3 also actually proceeded from an original, uncreated chaos.
Because בְרֵאשִׁית is in form a _status constr._, verses 1–3 would then have to be translated as follows: in the beginning, when God created heaven and earth—the earth now was waste and void, and so forth—then God said, let there be light.
In verse 2, the waste and void earth would then be presupposed in God’s creating; so Ewald, Bunsen, Schrader, and others; cf. Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴, p. 570 f.
But this translation is unacceptable.
First, the sentence then acquires a periodic length that occurs little in Hebrew, is wholly unexpected here immediately at the beginning and according to the style of writing in Gen. 1, and also lays far too strong an emphasis on the creation of light.
Next, the _status constr._ of בראשׁית does not require this translation, because in the same form without a suffix or genitive it also occurs in Isa. 46:10; cf. Lev. 2:12; Deut. 33:21.
And third, it would be strange that, while the protasis would say that God still had to create heaven and earth, the parenthetical clause would already designate the chaos by the name earth and would make no mention at all of the original state of heaven.
And there is the further fact that this translation, even if it were correct, would by no means teach the eternity of the waste earth but would at most leave this undecided.
That, however, conflicts with the entire spirit of the creation narrative.
In Gen. 1 Elohim is not presented as a world-former who, in human fashion, makes a work of art from material at hand, but as one who, merely by speaking, by a bare word of power, calls all things into existence.
And all of Scripture agrees with this.
God is the Almighty, who stands infinitely far above every creature and does with all creatures according to his sovereign good pleasure; the absolute possessor, כֹנֵה, of heaven and earth, Gen. 14:19, 22, who does all that pleases him and whose power is nowhere assigned a limit.
He speaks and it is; he commands and it stands, Gen. 1:3; Ps. 33:9; Isa. 48:13; Rom. 4:17.
Further, all things are repeatedly described in Scripture as made by God and absolutely dependent upon him.
He made everything—heaven, earth, the sea, with all that is upon and within them, Ex. 20:11; Neh. 9:6, and so forth.
Everything was created through him, Col. 1:16, 17; exists only through his will, Rev. 4:11; and is from and through and to God, Rom. 11:36.
Next, never and nowhere is even the slightest mention made of an eternal, unformed matter.
God alone is the Eternal, the Incorruptible; he alone is exalted above becoming and change.
By contrast, things have a beginning and an end and are subject to change.
This is expressed in an anthropomorphic manner.
God existed before the mountains were born, and his years do not end, Ps. 90:2; Prov. 8:25, 26; his choosing and loving took place προ καταβολης κοσμου, Eph. 1:4; John 17:24; cf. Matt. 13:35; 25:34; Luke 11:50; John 17:5; Heb. 4:3; 9:26; 1 Pet. 1:20; Rev. 13:8; 17:8.
And finally, Rom. 4:17, even though it does not specifically speak of creation, teaches that God calls and commands τα μη ὀντα, that which possibly does not yet exist, ὡς οντα, as though it existed; being or nonbeing makes no difference to him.
Heb. 11:3 states even more clearly that the world was so made by God that what is seen did not come into being ἐκ φαινομενων, from what appears before the eyes.
A formless matter is thereby entirely excluded; the visible world did not arise from the visible but rests in God, who by his Word called everything into existence.
