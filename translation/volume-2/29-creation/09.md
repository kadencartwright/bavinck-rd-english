## 9.

If now this world, which arose and exists in time, is essentially distinguished from God’s eternal and immutable being, the question presses itself upon us with all the more force: What moved God to call this world into existence? Scripture repeatedly traces all the being and particular being of creatures back to God’s will, Pss. 33:6; 115:3; 135:6; Isa. 46:10; Matt. 11:25; Rom. 9:15 f.; Eph. 1:4; Rev. 4:11; etc. For us that is the final ground, the end of all contradiction. _Dei voluntas suprema lex. Voluntas conditoris rei cujusque natura est_, Augustine, _De civ._ XXI 8. To the question why things exist and are as they are, there is no other and deeper answer than because God willed it. Whoever then asks further, “Why did God will it?” _majus aliquid quaerit quam est voluntas Dei; nihil autem majus inveniri potest_, _De Gen. c. Manich._ I 2; cf. above, p. 208 f. This has been the position of the whole Christian church and theology. But pantheism is not satisfied with this and seeks a deeper ground. It then attempts especially in two ways to explain the world from God’s being. Either it presents that being as so overflowing in riches that the world flows forth from it of itself and, as it moves further away from him, approaches μη ὀν and congeals into sensory matter. This is the doctrine of emanation, which arose in the East, found circulation especially in India and Persia, and then penetrated into the West in the systems of Gnosticism and Neoplatonism. Or it attempts to explain the world not from the πλουτος but from the πενια του θεου. God is so poor, so wretched, that he needs the world for his own development; in himself he is sheer _potentia_, which is nothing but can become everything; he must objectify himself and, through the opposition with the world, become spirit, become personality, in humanity. _An sich_ God is not yet the absolute; he first becomes this through the world-process. First _Deus implicitus_, he gradually becomes _Deus explicitus_. The world is therefore necessary for God; it is a necessary moment of development in his being. _Ohne Welt ist Gott kein Gott._ Over against this pantheism, which nullifies God’s personality and deifies the world, theism maintains the doctrine that creation is an act of God’s will. But arbitrariness is not intended by this. God’s will was indeed understood this way in Muslim theology and also among the nominalists, Socinians, and Cartesians. The world is a product of sheer arbitrariness; it exists but could just as well not have existed or have been entirely different. Christian theology, however, usually guarded itself against this extreme and taught that although God’s will in creation was supremely free and all compulsion and necessity are excluded, that will nevertheless had its motives and God had his high and holy intentions with his outward works, above, pp. 208–216. And so the question was asked what moved God to create this world, that is, what end he set himself with creation. The answers to this have varied. Many saw in God’s goodness and love a sufficient motive to explain the world. Scripture also spoke repeatedly of God’s being good, of his goodness appearing in all his works, and of his loving all his creatures and willing their blessedness. God also could not be conceived as needing anything; he could not have created the world in order to receive something, but only in order to give and communicate himself. His goodness was therefore the reason for creation. Already Plato, _Timaeus_, p. 29 D; Philo, in Herzog² 11, 643; and Seneca, _Epist._ 95, spoke in that spirit; and Christian theologians also said repeatedly that God created the world not from need but from goodness, not for himself but for human beings. _Deus mundum non sibi sed homini fecit_, Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ I 43; _Adv. Prax._ 5. _Bona facere si non posset, nulla esset potentia; si autem posset nec faceret, magna esset invidia_, Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ IV 16. Ὁ των ὁλων θεος ἀγαθος και ὑπερκαλος την φυσιν ἐστι, διο και φιλανθρωπος ἐστιν. Ἀγαθω γαρ περι οὐδενος ἀν γενοιτο φθονος, ὁθεν οὐδε το εἰναι τινι φθονει ἀλλα παντας εἰναι βουλεται, ἱνα και φιλανθρωπευεσθαι δυνηται, Athanasius, _Or. c. gentes_ 41; cf. also John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ II 2; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 19, art. 2; Voetius, _Disp._ I 558; etc. These statements, however, repeatedly alternated with others in which God himself and his honor were called the cause and end of creation. But humanism placed the human being in the foreground; Socinianism sought humanity’s essence not in fellowship with God but in dominion over the earth; the doctrine of natural law, natural morality, and natural religion made the human being autonomous and independent of God. Leibniz taught that through his goodness, wisdom, and power God was morally bound to choose the best of many possible worlds and bring it into existence. Kant summoned God to aid through the _praktische Vernunft_ only to provide the human being hereafter with the blessedness to which he had a claim according to his virtue. And so in the rationalism of the previous century the human being became the most interesting creature; everything existed for him and was serviceable to his perfection; the human being was _Selbstzweck_, and everything else, God himself included, was a means, Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._ 442 f.; idem, _Dogm._ I 669; Wegscheider, _Dogm._ §95. Even now many still teach that God must give actuality to the world-idea that he necessarily thinks, because otherwise he would be selfish and not the highest love. Because he is good, he wills not only to be blessed but establishes a kingdom of love and seeks the blessedness of his creatures; this is for him the final end, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ §§40, 41; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ I 448–459; Martensen, _Dogm._ §59; Hofmann, _Schriftbew._ I² 205 f.; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 428; Müller, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ II⁵ 187 f.; Schoeberlein, _Prinzip u. Syst. der Dogm._ 628; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 144; James Orr, _Christian View of God_ 155; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 259 f.; and also Hermes and Günther, in Kleutgen, _Theol._ I² 642 f. This doctrine of the human being as _Selbstzweck_ is, however, unacceptable from the Christian standpoint. Naturally God’s goodness is indeed also revealed in creation; Scripture repeatedly says this. Yet it is not correct to say that his goodness requires creation because otherwise he would be selfish. God is, after all, the All-Good, perfect love, complete blessedness in himself, and therefore also does not need the world in order to develop his goodness or love, any more than he needs it to attain self-consciousness and personality. Next, it lies in the nature of the case that God does not exist for humanity but humanity can exist only for God. For although the human being may in a certain sense be called _Selbstzweck_, insofar as, as a rational, moral being, he can and may never be reduced to a will-less instrument, he is nevertheless most deeply dependent upon God and has nothing he did not receive. God alone is Creator; the human being is creature; for this reason alone he cannot be the end of creation. Since he has his origin in God alone, he can also have his end in him alone. Finally, the doctrine that creation has its motive in God’s goodness and its final end in human blessedness also conflicts with reality. The universe is certainly not exhausted in the service of humanity and must nevertheless have another end than to be useful to humanity. The crude utilitarianism and selfish teleology of the previous century have been sufficiently refuted. The suffering and pain poured out over humanity cannot be explained from God’s goodness alone. And the outcome of world history, which speaks not only of the blessedness of the elect but also of an eternal triumph over the wicked, brings entirely other virtues of God to revelation than his goodness and love alone.
