## 10.

Scripture therefore adopts another standpoint and indicates a higher end. It says that all nature is a revelation of God’s virtues and a proclaimer of his praise, Ps. 19:1; Rom. 1:19. God created humanity in his image and for his honor, Gen. 1:26; Isa. 43:7. He glorifies himself in Pharaoh, Ex. 14:17, and in the man born blind, John 9:3. He creates the wicked for the day of evil, Prov. 16:4; Rom. 9:22. Christ came to glorify him, John 17:4. He grants all the benefits of grace for his name’s sake—deliverance, forgiveness, sanctification, and so forth, Pss. 105:8; 78:9; Isa. 43:25; 48:11; 60:21; 61:3; Rom. 9:23; Eph. 1:6 f. He gives his honor to no other, Isa. 42:8. The final end is that all kingdoms be subjected to him and every creature bow before him, Dan. 7:27; Isa. 2:3–13; Mal. 1:11; 1 Cor. 15:24 f. Already here honor is brought to him by all his people, Ps. 115:1; Matt. 6:13. One day God alone will be great, Isa. 2:3–13, and receive glory from all his creatures, Rev. 4:11; 19:6. He is the First and the Last, the Alpha and the Omega, Isa. 44:6; 48:12; Rev. 1:8; 22:13. From and through and to him are all things, Rom. 11:36. Resting on this, Christian theology taught almost unanimously that the _gloria Dei_ was the final end of all God’s works. Although in the earliest period God’s goodness is named especially as the motive of creation, God’s honor as the final end of all things is nevertheless not absent. Thus Athenagoras says that God _propter se ipsum et elucentem in omnibus ipsius operibus bonitatem et sapientiam adductum fuisse, ut hominem faceret_, _De resurr. mort._ 12. Tertullian says that God created the whole world _in ornamentum majestatis suae_, _Apolog._ 17. Especially in medieval theology did this _gloria Dei_ come more fully into its own, in Anselm, who makes God’s honor the principle of his doctrine of incarnation and satisfaction, _Cur Deus homo_, c. 11, but then further also in Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 44, art. 4; qu. 66, art. 2; qu. 103, art. 2; _S. c. Gent._ III 17, 18; _Sent._ II, dist. 1, qu. 2, art. 2; Bonaventure II, dist. 1, p. 2; etc. We find this same doctrine among later Roman Catholics, Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 31 f.; Simar, _Dogm._, p. 234 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorz._ I² 640–692; Schwetz, _Theol. dogm._ I 396 sq.; Jansen, _Pract. dogm._ II 319 sq.; then among the Lutherans, Gerhard, _Loci theol._, loc. V, c. 5; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 418; Hollaz, _Examen_, p. 360; etc.; and finally and especially also among the Reformed, cf. above all Edwards, _The End in Creation_, _Works_ II 193–257. The difference between them and the Lutherans and Roman Catholics does not consist in the Reformed making God’s honor and the latter making the human being the final end, but in this: that they made this honor of God the principle of all doctrine and life, of dogmatics and morals, of family, society, and state, of science and art. Nowhere was this _gloria Dei_ applied so universally as among the confessors of the Reformed religion. A double objection has, however, been raised against this final end of all creatures. First, that God thereby becomes selfish, seeks himself, and reduces creatures, particularly human beings, to means. This was already discussed earlier, p. 178, and it was demonstrated that God as the perfectly good can rest in nothing but himself and may be satisfied with nothing less than himself. He cannot do other than seek his own honor. Just as a father in his family and a sovereign in his kingdom must demand and seek the honor due them as such, so it is also with the Lord our God. Now a human being can ask for this honor due him only in God’s name and for the office assigned to him by God, but God asks and seeks that honor in his own name and for his own being. Because he is the highest and only good, perfection itself, it is therefore the highest righteousness that in all creatures he seeks his own honor. This seeking of his own honor has so little in common with our selfishness that God instead exacts that honor, where it is wrongfully withheld from him, in the way of right and righteousness. Willingly or unwillingly, every creature will one day bow the knee before him. Obedience in love or compelled submission is the final destiny of every creature. The other objection is that God, in thus seeking his honor, nevertheless needs the creature. The world serves for his glorification; something is therefore lacking in his perfection and blessedness; creation supplies a need of God and contributes to his perfection, Strauss, _Gl._ I 633. This objection seems irrefutable, and yet an analogy can also be observed in various kinds of human labor that can clarify God’s creating for us. At a lower level the human being labors because he must; need or compulsion drives him forward. But the nobler the labor becomes, the less place there is for need or compulsion. An artist creates his work of art not from need or compulsion but according to the free impulse of his genius. “I pour out my heart like the finch among the poplars; I sing and know no other aim” (Bilderdijk). The devout person serves God not from compulsion or from hope of reward but from free love. So too in God there is a good pleasure infinitely exalted above both need and compulsion, above πενια and πλουτος, which embodies God’s artistic thoughts in creation and finds delight in it. Indeed, what in human beings is only a weak analogy is present in God in absolute originality. The creature has no independence outside and over against God, as does an artist’s creation. God therefore never seeks the creature as though it could give him something he lacks or take from him something he possesses. He does not seek the creature, but through the creature he seeks himself. He is and always remains his own end, cf. above, p. 207. His willing is always, also in and through creatures, absolute self-enjoyment, perfect blessedness. Therefore the world did not proceed from a need of God, from his poverty and wretchedness, for in the creature he is concerned not with that creature but with himself. Nor does its origin owe itself to an irresistible _pleroma_ in God, for he uses every creature for his own glorification and makes it serviceable to the proclamation of his virtues.
