## 8.

This also casts some light on the difficult problem of creation and time.
Scripture says simply and humanly that things had a beginning.
It speaks of a time before the mountains were born, before the foundation of the world, before the times of the ages, Gen. 1:1; Ps. 90:2; Prov. 8:22; John 1:1; Eph. 1:4; 2 Tim. 1:9.
And in our thinking and speaking we too can never rid ourselves of this form of time.
In fact, all the objections that repeatedly arise against a creation in time are born from this human limitation.
Going backward in our thoughts, we finally arrive at the first moment in which every thing has its beginning.
Before that there is nothing but the profound silence of eternity.
But questions at once multiply in our heart.
With what shall we fill that eternity, and what activity can there be if all work of creation and preservation is thought away?
The doctrine of the Trinity and of the decrees gives us some answer here; but detached from the world, they no longer offer any content to our thought.
What, then, did God do before creation—he who cannot be conceived as a _Deus otiosus_ but is always ἐργαζεται, John 5:17?
Did he then change, passing from idleness to activity, from rest to labor?
How can creation, the transition to the act of creating, be reconciled with God’s immutability?
And why did he first proceed to that creation after an eternity had already sped by?
How can a moment be found in that eternity, which is exalted above all time, in which God passed from not creating to creating?
And why did he choose precisely that moment; why did creation not begin centuries earlier?
All these questions elicited various answers.
Pantheism attempted to offer a solution by teaching that in God being and working were one, that God did not become Creator but creation itself is eternal; the world had no beginning, for it is God’s eternal self-revelation; and God does not precede the world in duration but only in the logical sense, insofar as he is the cause of all things; the _natura naturans_ cannot be conceived without the _natura naturata_, substance not without _attributa_ and _modi_, the idea not without appearance; Erigena, _De div. nat._ I 73, 74; III 8, 9, 17, and so forth; Spinoza, _Cogit. metaph._ II c. 10; Hegel, _Werke_ VII 25 f.
Related to this is the solution Origen gave.
He did indeed reject the eternity of matter and taught that everything was created from nothing through the Logos, _De princ._ II 1; but God cannot be conceived as idle, his omnipotence is eternal as he himself is, and therefore he also began to create from eternity.
The present world is indeed not eternal, _De princ._ III 5, but this world was preceded by innumerable worlds, just as it will also be followed by many, _De princ._ I 2; III 5.
This view, which actually originated with the Stoa, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ IV³ 153 f., was condemned by the church at the Council of Nicaea but was nevertheless repeatedly renewed, even in this century by Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 50 f.; Ulrici, _Gott u. die Natur_, 1866, p. 671 f.; Martensen, _Chr. Dogm._ §§ 65, 66; Dorner, _Gl._ I 466 f.; G. Wetzel, “Die Zeit der Weltschöpfung,” _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._, 1875, pp. 582–588, and so forth.
Here mention should also be made of the question, much discussed in scholasticism, whether the world could not have been eternal.
In defense of Aristotle, who taught the eternity of the world, Zeller III³ 357 f., this question was answered affirmatively by some, including Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 46 arts. 1, 2; _S. c. G._ II 31–37; Durandus, Occam, Biel, Cajetanus; cf. Esser, _Die Lehre des h. Th. v. Aq. über die Möglichkeit eener anfangslosen Schöpfung_, Münster, 1895; Rolfes, _Philos. Jahrb._ X, 1897, issue 1.
By others, however, it was decidedly denied, as by Bonaventure, _Sent._ I dist. 44 art. 1 qu. 4; Albertus Magnus, Henry of Ghent, Richard, Valentia, Toletus; cf. Petavius, _De Deo_ III c. 5, 6; and likewise by the Lutherans, Quenstedt I 421; Hollaz, p. 358; and the Reformed, Zanchius, _Op._ III 22 sq.; Voetius, _Disp._ I 568; Leydecker, _Fax Verit._, 140; Coccejus, _S. Theol._ c. 15; Moor II 179; M. Vitringa II 83; Turretin, _Th. El._ V qu. 3; only one theologian considered an eternal creation possible, Burmannus, _Synopsis_ I 41, 24.
All these answers, however, give no satisfaction to thought.
Naturally, there is no disagreement about the fact that at this moment the world could already have existed for millions of centuries instead of thousands of years.
No one denies this in the abstract.
But it is an entirely different question whether the world could have been eternal in the same sense in which God is eternal.
This is impossible, for eternity and time differ essentially.
Kant saw an insoluble antinomy in the fact that, on the one hand, the world must have had a beginning because an infinitely elapsed time was inconceivable, and yet, on the other hand, it could have no beginning because an empty time is inconceivable; _Kr. d. r. Vern._, ed. Kirschmann, 5th ed., 360 f.
Only the latter member of this antinomy has no right to exist; with the world, time too disappears of itself; there then remains no time and therefore no empty time either; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ III 8; Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 29, 58; Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 3; _Adv. Hermog._ 4; Augustine, _De civ._ XI 6.
That we cannot conceive this and always need such an auxiliary representation of a time before time is irrelevant here and arises solely from the fact that our thinking exists in the form of time; to think time away, thought would have to think itself away, which is impossible.
Thus only the first member of Kant’s antinomy remains, namely, that the world must have had a beginning.
However endlessly prolonged, time remains time and never becomes eternity.
There is an essential difference between the two; above, p. 131.
The world cannot be conceived without time; time is a form of existence of all that is finite and created; the predicate of eternity in the strict sense can never belong to what exists in the form of time.
Likewise, the question whether God could not then have created from eternity rests upon the identification of eternity and time.
There is no earlier or later in eternity.
God _did_ create the world eternally; that is, also at the moment when the world received existence, God was and remained the Eternal and created as the Eternal.
Even if the world had existed for an endless series of centuries and millions of worlds had preceded this one, it would remain temporal, finite, limited, and therefore would have had a beginning.
Origen’s hypothesis offers not the least solution; the issue remains entirely the same and is merely shifted by some millions of years.
Still more groundless is the question of what God did before creation.
It was answered by Augustine, _Conf._ XI 12; Luther; Calvin, _Inst._ I 14, 1, in accordance with Prov. 26:5.
It proceeds from the assumption that God exists in time and that creation and preservation are his strenuous daily labor.
But God dwells in eternity; he is _actus purissimus_, an infinite fullness of life, blessed in himself, neither idle without creation nor wearied by it.
_Non itaque in ejus vacatione cogitetur ignavia, desidia, inertia, sicut nec in ejus opere labor, conatus, industria. Novit quiescens agere et agens quiescere_, Augustine, _De civ._ XII 17.
And similarly with pantheism: it is not as superficial as Socinianism and materialism, which simply change eternity into a time endlessly extended forward and backward and do not know the difference between endless and infinite; it does not say that God is all things and all things are God; it distinguishes between being and becoming, _natura naturans_ and _naturata_, substance and _modi_, the All and all things, the idea and appearance, that is, between eternity and time.
But to the questions—in what, then, does that distinction consist; what relation is there between the two; how does eternity pass over into time?—pantheism fails to provide an answer.
It gives words and images enough, but nothing can be conceived by them.
Theism, however, regards eternity and time as two incommensurable magnitudes.
Neither can nor may be neglected by us; both press themselves upon our consciousness and thought.
But we are unable to comprehend the relation between them; living and thinking in time, we stand in wonder before the mystery of eternal, uncreated being.
On the one hand, it is established that God is the Eternal; in him there is no past or future, no becoming or change; and all that he is is eternal—his thought, his will, his decree.
The world-idea that he thinks and expresses in the Son is also eternal in him; the decree to create the world is eternal in him; the will that created the world in time is eternal in him; the act of creating, as an act of God, as _actio interna et immanens_, is also eternal, Augustine, _De civ._ XII 17; Lombard, _Sent._ II dist. 1 n. 2; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 1 art. 1 qu. 2; Thomas, _C. Gent._ I 82; Petavius, _De Deo_ V 9, 9 and 13, 5; Voetius, _Disp._ I 565; Turretin, _Th. El._ V qu. 3, 16; for he did not _become_ Creator, so that for a time he would first not have created and then would have created; he is the eternal Creator, as Creator he was the Eternal, and as the Eternal he created.
Creation therefore brought no change in him; it did not emanate from him; it is no part of his being; he is immutably the same eternal God.
And on the other hand, it is established, also for thought, that the world had a beginning and was created in time.
Augustine rightly said: _mundus non est factus in tempore sed cum tempore_, _Conf._ XI 10–13; _De civ._ VII 30; XI 4–6; XII 15–17, just as Plato and Philo and Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 3, had all said this before him and all theologians later repeated it.
An empty time is inconceivable.
There was no time before the world.
Time is the necessary form of existence of the finite.
It was not created separately but was given of itself with the world, concreated just like space.
In a certain sense, therefore, the world has always existed, that is, as long as there was time.
Thus all change lies not in God but in the world.
It is subject to time, that is, to change.
It is continual becoming over against God as eternal and immutable being.
And these two—God and the world, eternity and time—stand in this relation: the world in all its parts is upheld by God’s omnipresent power, and time in all its moments is permeated by the eternal being of our God.
Eternity and time are not two lines, the shorter of which runs parallel for a time with the endlessly extended one; rather, eternity is the immutable center that sends out its rays to the entire circumference of time.
Before the limited eye of the creature, it successively unfolds its infinite content in the breadth of space and the length of time, so that the creature might understand something of the unsearchable greatness of God.
But eternity and time nevertheless remain distinct.
This alone we confess: God’s eternal willing, without ceasing to be eternal, can produce and does produce effects in time, just as his eternal thinking can also have temporal things as its content; Thomas in Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorz._ II 871.
The power of God’s will, which is eternally one, brought into being what did not exist, without any change taking place in him.
God eternally wills what will take place only after centuries or what took place centuries ago.
And at the time when it happens, there is change in things but not in his being.
_Una eademque sempiterna et immutabili voluntate res quas condidit et ut prius non essent egit, quamdiu non fuerunt et ut posterius essent, quando esse coeperunt_, Augustine, _De civ._ XII 17.
