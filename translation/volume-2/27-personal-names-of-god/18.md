## 18.

Now that the doctrine of the Trinity has been set forth in general, the three persons must next be discussed individually. The first person is the Father, and his personal property is _paternitas_, or also ἀγεννησια. This word played a major role in the Arian controversy. It was adopted from ordinary Greek. Plato called the ideas ἀγεννητους; Aristotle so called matter; the Gnostics spoke of God as βυθος ἀγεννητος, _ingenitum_, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I 1. From them Paul of Samosata and the Arians, Aëtius and Eunomius, adopted this usage in order thereby to contest the _homoousia_ of the Son and the Spirit with the Father. In contrast with every creature, ἀγεννησια expressed God’s proper being. The Son, however, is not ἀγεννητος; in Scripture he is called μονογενης, and orthodoxy too calls him generated; therefore he cannot be God but must be a creature. We may not assume δυο ἀγεννητα, that is, δυο θεους, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 31 sq.; _De decr. nic. syn._ 28 sq.; Basil, _Adv. Eunom._ II 25 sq.; etc. In Greek, however, there are two words: γεννητος, from γενναν, _gignere_, _generare_, and γενητος, from γιγνεσθαι, _fieri_. The latter is much broader than the former and designates everything that is produced and has a beginning of existence, whether by creation, generation, or propagation. At first these two words were not always clearly distinguished. It was pointed out only that the word ἀγεννητος or ἀγενητος could be used in different senses and could in the one sense not, and in the other sense indeed, be applied to the Son. But gradually it became customary to distinguish between the two words. All three persons could then, in contrast with every creature, be called ἀγενητος; none of them was produced in the manner of creatures; none of them had a beginning of existence. _Agenesia_ was an attribute of the divine being and common to all three persons. But ἀγεννησια had to be distinguished from it. This was a property of the Father alone. The Son could be called γεννητος, not because he was produced as a creature in time, but because he was generated from eternity from the being of the Father; cf. Athanasius, Basil, Gregory, at the places cited; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I, c. 8; Augustine, _De trin._ V, c. 3; Petavius, _De trin._ V, c. 1–3; Suicerus, s.v. But the church fathers noted at the same time that this property, namely ἀγεννησια, belonged specifically to the person and not to the being. The being is one and the same in the three persons, but _agennesia_ is a relation in the being. Just as Adam, Eve, and Abel partake of the same being although they received it in different ways, so also in God the being is one, although it exists in the three persons in different ways, Athanasius, _De decr. nic. syn._ 8; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat. theol._ V. Added to this is the fact that the name ἀγεννησια is negative and says only that the Father is exalted above generation, but says nothing positive concerning God’s nature. Properly speaking, therefore, it is also not a designation of the person of the Father, for _ἀγεννητον esse_ and being Father are by no means the same, Basil, _Adv. Eun._ I 9, 15; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat. theol._ III; Augustine, _De trin._ V 5. Therefore the name Father is to be preferred to that of ἀγεννητος, Basil, _Adv. Eun._ I 5. The scriptural name Father designates the personal property of the first person much better than does ἀγεννητος. In _paternitas_ a positive relation to the second person is enclosed. The name Father is even more proper to God than the name God, for the latter is a common name, a _nomen dignitatis_, but the name Father in the New Testament, like that of YHWH in the Old Testament, is a _nomen proprium_, a designation of a personal property of God. Whoever denies God the name Father dishonors him even more than one who denies his creation. This name Father is therefore no figurative manner of speech transferred from human beings to God. Rather, the relation is reversed. Fatherhood on earth is a distant, weak likeness of God’s Fatherhood, Eph. 3:15. God is Father in the true and perfect sense. Among human beings a father is in turn the son of another, and a son in turn also becomes a father; among human beings a father alone is not able to produce a son; among human beings fatherhood is temporal and in a certain sense accidental, not essentially connected with being human; it begins only late and soon ceases again, at any rate at death. But in God this is entirely different. He is solely and purely and wholly Father; he is only Father; he is Father by nature; he is Father eternally, without beginning and end. Therefore the generation too must be eternal and the Son as eternal as the Father, for if the Son were not eternal, neither could the Father be eternal. The eternity of Fatherhood entails the eternity of Sonship. Whoever names the Father thereby naturally also names the Son, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 23, 28; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat. theol._ III 5, 17; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I 8; Hilary, _De trin._ XII 24. Because of this relation of the Father to the Son and at the same time to the Holy Spirit, the Father was frequently called αὐτογενης, αὐτογενητος, αὐτοφυης, ἀποιητος, ἀναρχος, _principii expers_, _sui ipse origo_, _suae causa substantiae_, _sui principium_; and further ἀρχη, αἰτια, ριζα, πηγη, _principium_, _causa_, _radix_, _fons_, _origo_, _caput_, and so forth, of the Son and the Spirit or of the whole deity, Petavius, _De trin._ V 5; Suicerus, s.v.
