## 11.

This dogma, however, has at all times met with serious opposition. Not only from outside, from the side of the Jews, Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._ 147 f., and the Muslims, for example Averroes, Stöckl, _Philos. d. M. A._ II 89, against whom Christians then appeared in defense, literature in Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ I 881 sq., 896 sq. But also within the boundaries of Christianity this dogma was resisted by many both before and after its establishment. In the confession of the Trinity beats the heart of the Christian religion; every error is derived from, or upon deeper reflection can be traced back to, a deviation in the doctrine of the Trinity. It is such an essential component of the Christian faith that it still continues to work in the confession of the Unitarians. All who value the name Christian continue to speak of Father, Son, and Spirit, Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 425. All the more, however, has the doctrine of the Trinity in its ecclesial form been contested and repeatedly presented differently. But the history of this dogma clearly shows that the ecclesial form in which this truth is contained is alone capable of maintaining uncorrupted the matter at issue. Now the great problem in this dogma consists in the unity of being not nullifying the threeness of the persons, nor conversely the threeness of the persons abolishing the unity of being. And therefore the danger always threatens of deviating either to the right or to the left and thus falling into Sabellianism or Arianism. Arianism was prepared in the second and third centuries by the Ebionites, the Alogi, Theodotus, Artemon, and Paul of Samosata, who indeed saw in Christ a human being supernaturally born, anointed with the Holy Spirit at baptism, equipped for his work, and exalted to Lord, but who decidedly denied his preexistence and deity. They were adherents of an adoptionist Christology, Harnack, _D. G._ I 160, 616 f. In the fourth century this doctrine was advocated by Lucian and his pupil Arius and then further by Aëtius and Eunomius. According to a writing Θαλεια, fragments of which Athanasius preserved for us in _C. Ar._ I, Arius taught that because God is ἀγεννητος and without beginning, he is absolutely unique. He is ineffable, incomprehensible, cannot have direct fellowship with the finite, and cannot communicate his being, which consists precisely in ἀγεννησια. Everything outside him is therefore created, brought into being by his will. He is not Father from eternity but Father through and of the creatures. But before God proceeded to the creation of the world, he produced for mediation, as a kind of intermediate being, an independent ὑποστασις or οὐσια, who in Scripture bears the name wisdom, son, Logos, image of God, and so forth, and through whom God created all things; and likewise also a third, lower ὑποστασις, namely the Holy Spirit. This Logos was not generated from God’s being and does not have his being in common with the Father, for then there would be two Gods; rather, he was created or born εξ ουκ οντων, is a κτισμα or ποιημα of God, produced θεληματι και βουλη. Thus there was a time when he was not, ἠν ποτε, ὁτε οὐκ ἠν, even though he was created προ χρονων και προ αἰωνων, that is, before the world. This Logos was therefore not ὁμοουσιος with the Father but entirely separated from him, mutable, capable of choosing both evil and good. Yet he was a κτισμα τελειον; he chose the good, thereby became immutable, and became, as it were, a God. This Logos also became human, proclaimed the truth, effected our redemption, and is now worthy of our honor but not our worship, Harnack, _D. G._ I 194 f. Arianism was strong and found many adherents, not least among those who, after the conversion of Emperor Constantine, had gone over to Christianity for all kinds of reasons. Moreover, the writings of Athanasius teach us what formidable weapons they brought into the conflict. First, they appealed to a series of Scripture passages that expressed the unity of God, Deut. 6:4; 32:39; John 17:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; the birth or coming into being of the Son, Prov. 8:22; Col. 1:15; his subordination to the Father, John 14:28; 1 Cor. 15:28; Heb. 3:2; his ignorance, Mark 13:32; John 11:34; his limited power, Matt. 28:18, and goodness, Luke 18:19; his increase in wisdom, Luke 2:52; his suffering, John 12:27; 13:21; Matt. 26:39; 27:46; and his exaltation to Lord and Christ, Acts 2:36; Phil. 2:9; Heb. 1:4; etc. Further, with many quotations they argued that they had many earlier church fathers on their side. Then they borrowed various arguments from Aristotelian philosophy understood nominalistically and thereby argued for the unicity and ἀγεννησια of God. Finally, they pointed out the weaknesses and contradictions adhering to the Christology of Nicaea; among these especially this argument occupied a large place: that if the Son was generated, he was thereby essentially distinguished from God as the unbegotten, ἀγεννητος, and thus had arisen in time.

Sabellianism was prepared in the second and third centuries by Noëtus, Praxeas, Epigonus, and Cleomenes, who taught that in Christ the Father himself had been born, suffered, and died; that Father and Son were therefore names for the same person in different relations, before and in the incarnation, _an sich_ and in his historical appearance; or also that the divine nature in Christ was the Father and the human nature, the σαρξ, was the Son. This monarchianism, patripassianism, or modalism was advocated and further developed in the third century by Sabellius. Father, Son, and Spirit are the same God; they are three names for one and the same being, whom he called υἱοπατωρ, but not simultaneously—rather, in three successive energies or stages. God existed first in the _prosopon_, the appearance, the mode of the Father, namely as Creator and Lawgiver; then in the _prosopon_ of the Son as Redeemer from incarnation to ascension; and finally in the _prosopon_ of the Holy Spirit as Life-Giver. Sabellius appealed here especially to Deut. 6:4; Ex. 20:3; Isa. 44:6; John 10:38. Sabellius thus also included the Holy Spirit in the being of God, placed Son and Spirit on the same level as the Father, and further taught a historical succession in God’s revelations, a becoming in God himself, Hippolytus, _Philos._ IX; Epiphanius, _Haeres._ 62; Origen, _De princ._ I 2; Athanasius, _C. Ar._ IV; Harnack, _D. G._ I 674 f.
