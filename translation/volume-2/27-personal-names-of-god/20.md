## 20.

The third person in the Trinity bears the name Holy Spirit, and his personal property is the ἐκπορευσις, πνοη, _processio_, _spiratio_. In Christian theology the doctrine of the Holy Spirit was always treated only in the wake of the doctrine of the Son. With the second person, the controversy concerned almost exclusively his deity; his personality was generally established. But with the Holy Spirit, the controversy was conducted principally over his personality; if this was acknowledged, his deity followed of itself. With the deity of the Son, that of the Holy Spirit also had to be accepted. The Pneumatomachi of earlier and later times, however, raised all kinds of objections against the personality and deity of the Holy Spirit. They appealed to the fact that the name God is never assigned to the Holy Spirit in Scripture, that there is nowhere any mention of worshiping him, and that he is repeatedly presented as a power and a gift of God. The few passages that speak of him as a person had to be understood as personifications. Gregory of Nazianzus now explained the great difference of opinion that existed in his time concerning the Holy Spirit from the fact that the Old Testament had clearly revealed the Father but less clearly the Son, and that the New Testament had indeed placed the divine nature of the Son in a bright light but had still only obscurely indicated the deity of the Holy Spirit. Now, however, the Holy Spirit dwells among us and makes himself clearly known to us, _Orat._ V. In this lies an unmistakable truth. The personality and deity of the Holy Spirit do not stand objectively outside and over against us as do those of the Father and the Son. The name he bears does not express that personality as do those of Father and Son. The economy of the Holy Spirit, namely sanctification, does not delineate itself as clearly before our consciousness as those of creation, incarnation, and satisfaction. We ourselves live in that economy; the Holy Spirit dwells in and among us, and therefore prayer directs itself less to him than to the Father and the Mediator. He is much more the author than the object of prayer. For this reason the personality, or at least the deity, of the Holy Spirit was disputed in the church for a long time. The religious importance of this doctrine was not felt in the earliest period. The Spirit was usually indeed acknowledged in his personality but regarded only as the Spirit who had performed his work in the past, illuminated the prophets and apostles, and equipped and qualified Christ for his office. The necessity of _gratia interna_ was not yet understood; there was not yet a conscious, living need for an almighty, divine operation of grace in the heart; the _unio mystica_, the fellowship between God and humanity, had not yet been sounded in its depth; the objective revelation of God in Christ seemed sufficient, and a subjective illumination was not yet considered necessary, cf. above, p. 251 f. As soon as the church reflected more deeply upon its own life and attempted to give an account not only of the objective but also of the subjective _principia_ of salvation, it joyfully confessed the personality and deity of the Holy Spirit. And so it has gone through all the centuries. The denial of the personal existence and divine nature of the Holy Spirit always proceeds, consciously or unconsciously, from a rationalistic, Pelagian, deistic principle. It belongs in the circle of Arians, Socinians, Remonstrants, and others. From this it also immediately appears that the confession of the personality and deity of the Holy Spirit arose not from philosophy but from the heart of the Christian religion itself, from the faith of the church. As with the deity of the Son, a profound religious interest is connected with it; the Christian religion itself is at stake in it. On the ground of Scripture it is indisputably established that the Holy Spirit is the subjective principle of all salvation—of regeneration, faith, conversion, sanctification, and so forth; in other words, that there is no fellowship with Father and Son except in and through the Holy Spirit. Now one of two things is true: the Holy Spirit is a creature—whether a power, a gift, or a person—or true God. If he is a creature, then he cannot actually and in truth make us partakers of God himself, the Father and the Son with all their benefits; then he cannot be the _principium_ of the new life in the Christian and in the whole church; then there is no true fellowship of God and humanity; then God remains above and outside us and does not indwell humanity as in his temple. But the Holy Spirit is not and cannot be a creature. For he stands in the same relation to the Son as the Son does to the Father and makes us partakers of the Son and the Father, cf. above, p. 248. He is as closely connected to the Son as the Son is to the Father. He is in the Son, and the Son is in him. He is ἰδιον κατ’ οἰσιαν του υἱου. He is the Spirit of wisdom and truth, of power and glory, the Spirit through whom Christ sanctifies the church and in whom he makes it a partaker of himself and all his benefits, of the θεια φυσις, the υἱοθεσια, the _unio mystica_ with God. He who grants us God himself must himself be true God. To this soteriological interest is added the theological significance of the personality and deity of the Holy Spirit. Without this there is no true unity of Father and Son; whoever denies the deity of the Holy Spirit cannot maintain that of the Son. Only in the divine person of the Holy Spirit is the Trinity completed; only there does the unity of being in the threeness of the persons and the threeness of the persons in the unity of being come to realization. With the deity of the Holy Spirit stands or falls the whole dogma of the Trinity, the mystery of Christianity, the heart of religion, the true, essential fellowship of our souls with God. The church fathers understood this and therefore defended with the deity of the Son also that of the Spirit; cf. Athanasius in his letters to Serapion, Gregory of Nazianzus in his _Oratio theol. de Spiritu Sancto_, Basil in his third book against Eunomius and in his _Liber de Spiritu Sancto_, Gregory of Nyssa especially in his writing to Ablabius, Hilary, _De Trinitate libri XII_; etc. The Constantinopolitan Creed I expressed faith εἰς το πνευμα το ἁγιον, το κυριον, το ζωοποιον, το ἐκ του πατρος ἐκπορευομενον, το συν πατρι και υἱῳ συμπροσκυνουμενον και συνδοξαμενον, το λαλησαν δια των προφητων. And since then all Christendom confesses its faith εἰς τριαδα ὁμοουσιον, Basil in Hahn, _Bibl. der Symbole und Glaubensregeln der alten Kirche_³, 1897, p. 270.
