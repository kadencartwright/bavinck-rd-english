## 21.

The relation in which the Holy Spirit stands to the Father and the Son is made known to some extent by his name το πνευμα ἁγιον and also by many verbs—being given, sent, poured out, breathed, proceeding, descending, and so forth; see above, p. 246.
Christian theology designated it as προβολη, ἐκπορευσις, ἐκφοιτησις, πνοη, προιεναι, προκυπτειν, προχεισθαι, _processio, spiratio_, and so forth.
It was preferably conceived as a πνοη, _spiratio_.
Scripture provided a ground for this when it called the Holy Spirit רוּחַ, πνευμα and repeatedly compared him with breath and wind, Ps. 33:6; Job 33:4; John 3:8; 20:22; Acts 2:2, and so forth.
For the rest, however, theology observed modesty in defining spiration.
Like generation, it had to be conceived as the eternal communication of the same being; it also had to be distinguished from generation, since generation gave the Son life in himself and spiration gave the Holy Spirit life in himself, Athanasius, _Ad Serap._ I 15 sq.; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or. theol._ V 7 sq.; Basil, _De Spir. S._ 46 sq.; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I 8; but the difficulty of further determinations was felt.
Augustine said: _quid autem inter nasci et procedere intersit de illa excellentissima natura loquens, explicare quis potest? Non omne quod procedit, nascitur; quamvis omne procedat quod nascitur. Sicut non omne quod bipes est homo est, quamvis bipes sit omnis qui homo est. Haec scio. Distinguere autem inter illam generationem et hanc processionem nescio, non valeo, non sufficio_, _C. Maxim._ III 14; _De trin._ XV 17, 20; _Tract. 99 in Joann._, and so forth.
Nevertheless, some distinction was sought.
And this was then found in the fact that the Son proceeded from the Father alone but the Holy Spirit from both, Augustine, _De trin._ XV 26, or in the fact that he proceeded from Father and Son _ut datus, non ut natus_, Augustine, _De trin._ V 14; Petavius, _De trin._ VIII c. 13.
It was especially emphasized, however, that the Holy Spirit could not therefore be the Son of the Son, because then the _trias_ would become a πληθος ἀπειρον and there would be no end to the movement of life in the being of God, Basil, _Adv. Eunom._ V.
The Holy Spirit is δι’ ἑαυτου συμπληρουν την πολυυμνητον και μακαριαν τριαδα, Basil, _De Sp. S._ 45.
The Trinity is susceptible of neither increase nor decrease; it is τελεια, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 18.
Augustine does indeed respond once to the Arian objection that the Father was mightier than the Son if the former alone could generate a Son through whom everything was created: _absit autem ut, quomodo putas, ideo sit Pater potentior Filio, quia creatorem genuit Pater, Filius autem non genuit creatorem. Neque enim_ **_non potuit_** _sed_ **_non oportuit_**.
But how this is to be understood is clarified in what immediately follows.
_Immoderata enim esset divina generatio, si genitus Filius nepotem gigneret Patri.... nec impleretur generationis series, si semper alter ex altero nasceretur, nec eam perficeret ullus, si non sufficeret unus_, _C. Maxim. Arian._ III 12.
_In illa vero essentia trinitatis nullo modo alia quaelibet persona ex eadem essentia potest exsistere_, _De trin._ VII 6; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 27 art. 5.
Finally, especially by Thomas and his followers, the distinction between generation and spiration was indicated in such a way that generation took place _per modum intellectus_, spiration _per modum voluntatis_.
This distinction had long been prepared by the fact that generation was compared with thinking and speaking and the Holy Spirit was presented as the love that binds Father and Son to one another.
In medieval and Roman Catholic theology this distinction became almost universal, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 27; _C. Gent._ IV 13, 15 sq.; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ I c. 3; Thomas, Bonaventure, and others on _Sent._ I dist. 13; Petavius, _De trin._ VII c. 13, and so forth.
The Protestant theologians all indeed accepted a certain distinction between generation and spiration, just as between Son and Spirit; in part they also acknowledged the correctness of the distinctions named above, Melanchthon, _Loci Comm._, _De Filio_; Alsted, _Theol. schol._, pp. 137, 145; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ III 28; but many nevertheless did not dare to speak so confidently here and considered this distinction unscriptural and insufficiently modest, Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ I 387 sq.; Hollaz, _Examen theol._, p. 341; Zanchi, _Op._ I 255; Voetius, _Disp._ V 139; _Synopsis pur. theol._ IX 14; Moor I 791.
