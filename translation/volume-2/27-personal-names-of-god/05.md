## 5.

Holy Scripture, however, does not stop at these data; it offers still more and also enables us to know something of the relations in which these three distinct subjects—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—stand to one another.
For this, the name Father must first of all be considered.
In the most general sense, this name designates God as Creator of all his works, especially of the human being, Num. 16:22; Matt. 7:11; Luke 3:28; John 4:21; Acts 17:28; 1 Cor. 8:6; Eph. 3:15; Heb. 12:9.
In the Old Testament this name acquires a theocratic meaning; God is the Father of Israel because he created and preserved it by his wondrous might, Deut. 32:6; Isa. 63:16; 64:8; Mal. 1:6; 2:10; Jer. 3:19; 31:9; Ps. 103:13; Rom. 9:4.
In the New Testament this meaning passes over into the ethical, in which God is the Father of his children, Matt. 6:4, 8, 9; Rom. 8:15, and so forth.
But in a wholly unique, metaphysical sense, God is the Father of the Son.
Jesus always makes an essential distinction between the relation in which he himself stands to the Father and that in which others—the Jews, the disciples—stand to him, Matt. 11:25-27; Luke 22:29; John 2:16; 5:17; 20:17, and so forth.
He called God his own Father, πατερα ἰδιον, John 5:18.
And Scripture clearly indicates that the name Father does not apply in the first place to God in relation to Israel and believers, but, on the contrary, originally to the relation of the Father to the Son, John 14:6-13; 17:26.
God is, in the proper and original sense, Father of the Son; he loves the Son, John 5:19 f.; 10:17; 17:24, 26, and this love passes from the Father through the Son to others, John 16:27; 17:26.
This relation of the Father to the Son did not come into being in time but is from eternity, John 1:14; 8:38; 17:5, 24.
And therefore God is repeatedly called by the apostles, in a special sense, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, Rom. 15:6; 1 Cor. 15:24; 2 Cor. 1:3; Gal. 1:1; Eph. 1:3, and so forth.
His fatherhood in relation to the Son is his special, personal property.
He alone is of himself, the first in the order of existence, John 5:26, and therefore also the Father both in creation and re-creation, _from whom_ are all things, 1 Cor. 8:6.
In both the Old and the New Testament, it is the Father who occupies the first place.
His is the purpose, Acts 4:28; Eph. 1:11; the good pleasure, Matt. 11:26; Eph. 1:9; the initiative in creation and redemption, Ps. 33:6; John 3:16; the ἐξουσια and the δυναμις, Matt. 6:13; Rom. 1:20; Eph. 1:19; the righteousness, Gen. 18:25; Deut. 32:4; John 17:25; Rom. 3:26; 2 Tim. 4:8; the goodness, the wisdom, the immortality, the unapproachable light, Matt. 19:17; Rom. 16:27; 1 Tim. 6:16.
Therefore he too repeatedly bears the name God in a special sense.
He is Elohim, YHWH Elohim, El Elyon, El Shaddai, μονος ἀληθινος θεος, John 17:3; εἱς θεος, 1 Cor. 8:6; 1 Tim. 2:5, who is named as God and Father alongside the Lord Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit, 1 Cor. 12:6; 2 Cor. 13:13; 1 Thess. 1:3; Rev. 1:6.
Christ even calls him not only his Father but also his God, Matt. 27:46; John 20:17; Heb. 1:9; 2:17; 5:1; 10:7, 9, and he himself is called the Christ of God, Luke 9:20; 1 Cor. 3:23; Rev. 12:10.
From this, however, the Arians of earlier and later times wrongly inferred that the Father alone is God and that the Son and the Spirit, although closely related to God, nevertheless stand outside the divine being.
For, first, Scripture, as will become more fully evident shortly, ascribes divine names, attributes, works, and honor to the Son and the Spirit just as much as to the Father.
Further, it deserves notice that Scripture nowhere says that the _Father alone_ is the true God, but does say that the Father is the only true God, something that is fully acknowledged in the church’s doctrine of the Trinity.
Next, none of these passages draws a contrast between the Father on the one hand and the Son and the Spirit on the other, but between the Father, as the one God, and the gods of the Gentiles.
Then it by no means follows from the statements that the Father is the only true God, alone wise, alone good, alone immortal, that the Son and the Spirit are not also of the same divine essence and do not partake of that same unity, wisdom, goodness, and immortality, any more than it follows from 1 Cor. 8:6 that Christ alone, and not God, is our Lord, through whom are all things and we through him.
Finally, the Father can therefore be called alone wise, alone good, and so forth, because he possesses everything from himself, is πηγη θεοτητος, whereas the Son and the Spirit possess the same essence and the same attributes by communication.
The name God, ascribed to the Father in particular, indicates that he is first in the divine economy; it is, as it were, an official title, a designation of his rank and place, just as among human beings, who all partake of the same nature, there is nevertheless a distinction of station and honor, Augustine, _De trin._ I c. 6; VI c. 9; Petavius, _De trin._ II c. 4; III c. 1, 2; Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ II c. 25.
