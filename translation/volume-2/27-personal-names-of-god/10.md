## 10.

In the West, after Tertullian and Cyprian, the doctrine of the Trinity was defended vigorously and proved from Scripture above all by Hilary in his twelve books _De Trinitate_, which, however, contain very little about the Holy Spirit and therefore probably formerly bore the title _De fide contra Arianos_; and then more speculatively and profoundly by Augustine. His fifteen books _De Trinitate_ are the most profound work written about this dogma. In them he not only summarizes what earlier fathers had said on this subject, but also treats it independently and introduces important modifications. First, Augustine proceeds not from the person of the Father but from the one simple being (_essentia_) of God, which excludes all composition, and therefore expresses the absolute unity of the three persons more strongly than had ever been done before him. Each person is as great as the whole Trinity, _De trin._ VIII 1, 2. In each person is the whole, same divine being, so that there are not three Gods, three almighty ones, and so forth, but only one God, one Almighty, and so forth, V 8. The distinction of the persons therefore cannot lie in attributes or accidents that one person would have and another not, but only in their mutual relations. The Father is and is called the first person because he stands in a particular relation to Son and Spirit, and so forth, V 5, just as the designation Lord, Creator, and so forth indicates God’s relation to creatures but brings about no change in him, ibid., 16, 17. Second, Augustine now had to reject every opposition formerly made between the Father and the Son. As true God, the Son is no less hidden and invisible than the Father and is perfectly equal to the Father. Every subordinationism is banished. Augustine goes even further than Athanasius. The latter still allowed a certain subordination, _C. Ar._ I 59, but Augustine overcame every thought that the Father was the proper, original God. He proceeds from the _essentia Dei_, which dwells equally in all three. Although he still uses the expression _fons_ or _principium deitatis_ for the Father, _De trin._ IV 20, it nevertheless has a different sense for him. It does not signify that deity logically first existed in the Father and was communicated by him to the Son and the Spirit; rather, the Father can be called this only because he is Father of the Son not as God but as person. And in this sense the formula of Nicaea, _Deus de Deo_, is also explained by him, _De trin._ VII 2, 3. Augustine therefore also came to another understanding of the theophanies in the Old Testament. Previously people had always and exclusively seen in them revelations of the Logos because the Father was hidden, but Augustine ascribes them also to the Father and the Spirit, who can reveal themselves just as well as the Son and whose revelations, after all, cannot be separated from those of the Son, _De trin._ II and III. Finally, Augustine more than any church father before him sought images, likenesses of the Trinity, _vestigia trinitatis_, and thus brought to light the connection between the doctrine of God and that of the entire cosmos, _De trin._ IX–XV. Cf. Schwane II 173–194; Gangauf, _Des h. Augustinus specul. Lehre v. Gott dem Dreieinigen_, Augsburg, 1883, p. 209 f.; Münscher–von Coelln, _Dogmengesch._ I 245 f.; Harnack, _D. G._ II 294 f.; etc. Thus Augustine completed what Tertullian had begun. In the West there is a different conception of the Trinity from that in the East, and for this reason agreement could not later be reached concerning the _filioque_. The West joined Augustine and adopted his doctrine of the Trinity, elaborating it on various points, often in a subtle manner. The symbol that is wrongly named after Athanasius is largely in his spirit and was never adopted by the East, Harnack, _D. G._ II 298 f.; Karl Müller, _Symbolik_, Erlangen: Deichert, 1896, p. 51 f. The doctrine of the Trinity was treated later in the Western church especially by Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 2; Erigena, _De div. nat._ I 62; II 32, 35; Alcuin, _De fide sanctae et individuae trinitatis_, Migne CI, cols. 9–64; and further by the scholastics: Anselm, _Monologion_, _De incarnatione verbi_, and _De processione Sp. S._; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacramentis_, lib. I, pars 3; Lombard, _Sent._, lib. I, and the commentaries on that work; Richard of St. Victor, _Libri 6 de trinitate_; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 27–43; _C. Gent._ IV, c. 2–26; Bonaventure, _Brev._ I, c. 2–6; etc. And the doctrine of the Trinity rests on the same Augustinian foundation in Roman Catholic theology, Petavius, _De trinitate_, libri VIII; in Lutheran theology, Gerhard, _Loci_, loc. III; etc.; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, p. 87 f.; and in Reformed theology, Calvin, _Inst._ I 13; Zanchius, _De tribus Elohim_, _Op._ I 1–564; Polanus, _Synt. theol._, lib. 3; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._ 80–98. In more recent times the doctrine of the Trinity was indeed usually much simplified but was taught and defended in an ecclesial sense by Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 117–224; Ebrard, _Chr. Dogm._ §§95–154; Böhl, _Dogm._ 113–123; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 462; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 249; Augustus Hopkins Strong, _Systematic Theology_, 3rd ed., New York: Armstrong, 1890, pp. 144–170; etc.
