## 16.

But now the glory of the confession of the Trinity lies precisely in this: that this unity, however absolute, does not exclude but includes diversity.
The being of God is no abstract unity, no concept obtained by abstraction, but a fullness of being, an infinite richness of life, which unfolds the highest unity precisely in diversity.
Those self-distinctions that Holy Scripture makes known to us in the divine being under the names Father, Son, and Spirit are designated in theology by the word _person_.
In the East one first used προσωπον for this, corresponding to the Hebrew פָּנִים, face, outward appearance, role.
But this word was open to misunderstanding; Sabellius said that the one divine οὐσια or ὑποστασις assumed different προσωπα.
Over against this, the church fathers argued that the three προσωπα in the divine being were not merely appearances, forms of revelation, but προσωπα ἐνυποστατα, that they existed ἐν ὑποστασει.
Thus the word προσωπον was replaced by ὑποστασις, a word that first means foundation, substructure, firmness, and then designates that which exists really and not merely in appearance, or also that which exists in itself in distinction from _accidentia_ that inhere in something else; cf. also 2 Cor. 9:4; 11:17; Heb. 1:3; 3:14; 11:1; and Cremer, s.v.
In Latin the word _persona_ was used, which first designated the mask, then the role of an actor, thereafter the _conditio, qualitas, status_ in which someone appears, and thus in legal language made known the _jus standi in judicio_.
This concept too was therefore rather fluid, and in Tertullian it still alternates with all kinds of other words: _nomen, species, forma, gradus, res_.
Nevertheless, this word was retained in Latin even when in the East προσωπον had already been replaced by ὑποστασις, for the word ὑποστασις could not be rendered in Latin by any suitable word; _substantia_ could not be used because it was already in use for the being.
But this difference in expression repeatedly occasioned misunderstanding between East and West.
The Greeks thought of their word προσωπον when they heard the Latin _persona_, and the Latins understood the Greek ὑποστασις in the sense of their _substantia_.
Each side reproached the other for the poverty of its language.
Nevertheless, they taught exactly the same thing in substance, namely, that the three persons were not _modi_ but subsistences.
Thus in ecclesiastical usage subsistence came to be the essential characteristic of the word προσωπον, _persona_: ὑποστασις, _subsistentia, subsistens, suppositum_.
The word ὑποστασις still has this sense in Athanasius and the Cappadocians.
But later another characteristic was added to the word _persona_.
For if _persona_ designated nothing but ὑποστασις, that which exists in itself over against _accidens_, it could also be used of things.
In the Christological conflict, over against Nestorianism and Monophysitism, people were compelled to define nature and person still more sharply; and thus, in the _De duabus naturis et una persona Christi_ attributed to Boethius, the definition of _persona_ arose as _naturae rationabilis individua substantia_.
The word now expressed two things: subsistence and rationality or self-consciousness.
It has this meaning in scholasticism, Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 23; cf. 25, 26; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 29; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I dist. 23 qu. 1 and dist. 25; and further also in the older Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed dogmatics, Petavius, _De trin._ IV c. 9; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, S. 90 f.; Beza, _Tract. theol._ I 646; Zanchi, _Op._ I 13; Maresius, _Syst. theol._ III 7; _Synopsis pur. theol._ VII 8, and so forth.
Only since the previous century did the definition arise that the essence of personality lies not in existing in itself but in self-consciousness and self-determination.
This definition may perhaps be correct for human personality, although even then it remains subject to objection, but it is in any case not a correct description of the word _person_ in the doctrine of the Trinity.
Applied here, it would lead to tritheism, that is, to three personalities, each with a separate self-consciousness and self-determination.
Even the definition of Boethius is much more applicable in the doctrine of Christ than in that of the Trinity.
And this has also repeatedly been felt.
Richard of St. Victor disputed it because it spoke of _individua substantia_ and therefore defined person as _divinae naturae incommunicabilis existentia_, _De trin._ IV 21.
Calvin spoke only of _subsistentia in Dei essentia_, _Inst._ I 13, 6; cf. Alting, _Theol. probl. nova_ III qu. 31.
And all acknowledged the truth of Augustine’s statement: we speak of persons, _non ut illud diceretur, sed ne taceretur_, _De trin._ V 9; VI 10; cf. Anselm, _Monol._ c. 37, 38; Calvin, _Inst._ I 13, 2-4; and further Braun, _Der Begriff Person in seiner Anwendung auf Trinität und Incarnation_, Mainz, 1896.
In the dogma of the Trinity, _person_ designates nothing other than that the three persons in the divine being are not _modi_ but each exists in its own manner.
Even the rational and self-conscious elements in this concept receive no emphasis whatever, for this follows of itself from the fact that they partake of the same being and of all the virtues, and thus also of knowledge and wisdom.
What is expressed in the word _person_, however, is that the unity of the divine being unfolds itself in a threefold existence.
It is a _unitas, ex semetipsa derivans trinitatem_.
The persons are not three modes of revelation of the one divine personality, but the divine being exists in no other way than as tripersonal, precisely because it is absolute, divine personality.
In the human being we have only a weak analogy of this.
Personality in human beings comes into being only because there is a subject that sets itself over against itself as object and then gathers itself together with itself again; three moments constitute the essence of human personality, but in us these are only moments; in God, because in him there is no time or space, no extension and no division, they are three ὑποστασεις, three modes of existence of the one and the same being.
This analogy of human personality must, however, be supplemented in yet another way.
Human nature is too rich to be embodied in a single human being or person; it therefore extends itself in many human beings and first attains its full unfolding in humanity.
So too the divine being unfolds its fullness in three persons; but these are not three individuals alongside and separated from one another, but in and within the divine being the threefold self-distinction that takes up the unfolding of the being into personality and thus makes it tripersonal.
The unfolding of human nature is of two kinds: in the individual human being into personality, and in humanity into many individuals who together once again form a unity, a personality, just as Christ together with the church is one perfect man, 1 Cor. 12:12; Eph. 4:13.
This double unfolding, which cannot be otherwise in humanity, is one in God; the unfolding of his being into personality coincides with that of his being into persons.
The three persons are the one divine personality brought to complete self-unfolding in and from and through and within the being.
