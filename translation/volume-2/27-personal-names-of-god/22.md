## 22.

Yet gradually a considerable difference developed between East and West in the doctrine of the Trinity. In the second century the ontological procession of the Son from the Father was understood as an eternal generation; and so a similar procession also had to be assumed for the Holy Spirit, who after all could not remain isolated alongside Father and Son. The relation of the Spirit to Father and Son had to be established. In this respect Athanasius taught that the Holy Spirit is called both the Spirit of the Father and that of the Son or of Christ, and that he possesses the same ἰδιοτης, ταξις, φυσις in relation to the Son as the Son does to the Father, _Ep. ad Serap._ I 21; III 1. He is said to proceed from the Father, ἐκπορευεσθαι, because he is sent and given by the Logos, παρα του λογου, who is from the Father, ibid. I 20. As the ἐκπορευμα of the Father, the Holy Spirit is always in the hands of the Father, who sends him, and of the Son, who bears him, and cannot be separated from them, _Expos. fidei_ 4; _De sent. Dionys._ 17. He is not the brother or son of the Son but is the Spirit of the Father, just as the Son is the Son of the Father, _Ep. ad Serap._ I 16; IV 3. But although he is not called Son, he is nevertheless not ἐκτος του υἱου, for he is called the Spirit of wisdom and of sonship; when we have the Spirit, we have the Son, and conversely. Why, however, the one is called Son and the other Spirit is incomprehensible, but this is what Scripture teaches, ibid. IV 4, 5. He is εἰκων του υἱου, ibid. I 24, 26; IV 3, united with the Son, ἡνωμενον, as the Son is with the Father, ibid. I 31. Athanasius therefore teaches very clearly a dependence of the Spirit on the Son but nevertheless does not state that he proceeds from the Father and the Son. The three Cappadocians speak in the same spirit; they clearly teach that the Holy Spirit stands to the Son as the Son does to the Father, that in order he follows the Son, that the Spirit grants us the Son and the Father, that he proceeds from the Father and is thought after and with the Son, that he is from the Father through the Son, δια υἱου, and that only ἡ προς ἀλληλα σχεσις gives each of the three persons a name of his own, Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or. theol._ V 9; Basil, _Adv. Eun._ III 1 sq.; _De Sp. S._ V, etc.; but the procession of the Spirit from the Son is expressed by none of them. Nor can it be said that it was denied or opposed by them, for this question had not yet arisen at that time. Hence expressions also occur in Gregory of Nyssa, Epiphanius, Didymus, Cyril, and others that seem to teach the procession of the Holy Spirit also from the Son. They use the prepositions παρα and ἐκ; say that the Holy Spirit receives everything from Christ and that Christ is the source of the Holy Spirit, John 7:38; declare that he is from the being of the Father and of the Son and that he is ἰδιον της οὐσιας υἱου, the image, the mouth, the breath of the Son; acknowledge that he is the third person, existing after the Son and receiving everything from the Father through the Son, and so forth, Petavius, _De trin._ VII, c. 3–7. But the development of the doctrine of the Trinity in the East nevertheless took a different direction than in the West. Over against Nestorius, who made Christ dependent on the Holy Spirit and thus reversed the order of the persons, Cyril taught, entirely in agreement with the Greek fathers, that the Spirit proceeded from the Father through the Son, δια του υἱου, in Schwane, _D. G._ II 204. John of Damascus likewise says that the Spirit is also the Spirit of the Son because he is revealed and communicated through him; that he proceeds from, ἐκ, the Father δια του υἱου; but he expressly rejects that the Spirit is from the Son and has his existence from him. Son and Spirit are traced back to one cause, εἰς μιαν αἰτιαν ἀναφερονται, _De fide orthod._ I 8, 12. And this has remained the doctrine of the Greek church, _Conf. orthod._, qu. 9, 71; Schaff, _Creeds of Christ._ II 282, 350. The East remained with the theology of the fathers. The West, however, went further. Tertullian had already begun to derive the Trinity not from the Father but from the being of God, and he also said, _spiritum non aliunde puto quam a Patre per filium_, _Adv. Prax._, c. 4, 25. Hilary places the Spirit in the same relation to the Son as the Son to the Father and says that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and is sent and distributed by the Son, indeed, that he has the Son as _auctor_, _De trin._ II 4; VIII 20. But Augustine especially went beyond the Greek fathers. He conceives the three persons as relations in the one, simple deity and therefore had to place the Spirit in relation not only to the Father but also to the Son. Augustine accordingly clearly teaches that the Holy Spirit is related, _refertur_, to the Father and to the Son, that he is _ineffabilis quaedam patris filiique communio_. In the names Father and Son, it is true, only their reciprocal relation is expressed and not their relation to the Spirit; our language is too poor for this. But the Spirit is nevertheless called a gift of the Father and of the Son. He has both as _principium_; _fatendum est patrem et filium principium esse spiritus sancti_. But these are not then two _principia_, any more than Father, Son, and Spirit are three _principia_ of creation. No, Father and Son are _unum principium_ of the Spirit. The Son has also received from the Father that he causes the Spirit to proceed from himself as well, for the Son can differ from the Father in nothing except that he is Son, _De trin._ V 11–15; XV 17, 26; _De Symb._ II 9. After Augustine, this doctrine of the procession of the Holy Spirit is then found in the symbol of the Synod of Toledo, ca. 400; in the letter of Leo I to Turribius; in the _Athanasianum_, n. 22; and in the symbol of the third Synod of Toledo, 589, which inserted the _filioque_ into the text of the _Constantinopolitanum_, Denzinger, nn. 98, 113, 136. The church and theology in the West followed Augustine and repeatedly defended the _filioque_ against the East, Denzinger, nn. 224, 242, 294, 355, 382, 385, 586, 598, 868, 873; Alcuin, _De processione Sp. S._; Anselm, _De processione Sp. S._; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 11; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 36; Petavius, _De trin._ VII; and the Reformation aligned itself with this. But despite all attempts at agreement, the East remained at its old standpoint, down even to the conference of the Old Catholics at Bonn in 1875, Harnack, _D. G._ II 275 f., 285 f.; Schwane, _Dogm._ II 198 f.; III 146 f.; Schaff, _Creeds of Christ._ II 545 sq. The fruitlessness of all these attempts is all the more remarkable because the difference seems to be so slight. The Greeks do not teach subordinationism and acknowledge the full consubstantiality of the three persons; they do indeed place the Holy Spirit in a definite relation to Christ, who sends and distributes him; nor do they have any objection to saying that he proceeds from the Father through the Son, δια του υἱου, _per filium_. Conversely, the Western church declared that the procession from Father and Son must not be understood as proceeding from two _principia_ and consisting in two _spirationes_, but as _ex uno principio_ and _unica spiratione_, Denzinger, nn. 382, 586. Pope Leo acknowledged that the inclusion of the expression _filioque_ in the ancient symbol was formally incorrect, Schwane II 209; even the formula _ex patre per filium_ as such met with no objection in the West, Denzinger 586, 868; Schaff, _Creeds_ II 552. And yet no agreement was achieved. The Greeks always retained the one great objection that, if the Holy Spirit also proceeded from the Son, there would be two _principia_, two αἰτιαι, in the Godhead. That points back to a different doctrine of God and to a different practice of godliness. The rejection of the _filioque_ is in the Greek church still the last remnant of subordinationism. However fully the three persons are conceived as one and equal, that unity and equality belong to Son and Spirit only from the Father. He is πηγη και ἀρχη της θεοτητος. Thus, if the Holy Spirit also proceeds from the Son, the latter comes to stand alongside the Father, the principle of unity is broken, and a kind of ditheism is embraced. For the Greeks the unity of being and the root of the Trinity lie not in the divine nature as such but in the person of the Father. He is the sole αἰτια. The three persons are not three relations in the being, not a self-unfolding of the Godhead, but it is the Father who communicates his being to Son and Spirit. But from this it follows that Son and Spirit now also come to stand alongside each other and both in the same way have their αἰτια, their _principium_, in the Father. In both the Father reveals himself. The Son makes him known; the Spirit enables us to enjoy him. The Son does not reveal the Father in and through the Spirit; the Spirit does not lead to the Father through the Son. Rather, both are to a certain extent independent; both open a way to the Father. Orthodoxy and mysticism, intellect and will, stand dualistically alongside one another. And this peculiar relation of orthodoxy and mysticism is the mark of Greek piety. Doctrine stands outside and above life; it serves only the head; it is a suitable object for theological speculation. Alongside it is another source for life in the mysticism of the Spirit; this does not well up from knowledge but has its own origin and nourishes the heart. Head and heart do not stand in the right relation; representation and emotion are totally separated; the ethical connection is lacking. Cf. vol. I 70; Harnack, _D. G._ II 285 f.; Kattenbusch, _Vergl. Confessionskunde_ I 318 f.; Schmid, _Symbolik_, p. 30 f.; K. Müller, _Symbolik_, 220.
