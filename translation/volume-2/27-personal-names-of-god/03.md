## 3.

Although the Wisdom of Jesus Sirach still adheres fairly closely to the canonical literature, in the Wisdom of Solomon, Philo, and Jewish theology an influence of philosophy can be perceived that causes an ever greater departure from the thoughts of the Old Testament. First, there is a difference in principle. The doctrine of intermediate beings in Philo and later Jewish theology was born from the Platonic opposition between God and the world, of which not a trace can yet be observed in the Old Testament books. The word and wisdom in the Old Testament are not intermediate beings mediating between God and the world, but stand entirely on God’s side, belong to him, and are the _principia_ of the created world. In Philo, however, the intermediate beings occupy an impossible position: they are neither God nor creature, neither person nor attribute, neither substance nor power, but partake of both. They erase the boundary line that in the Old Testament always separates creature from Creator and prepare the way for the philosophy of Gnosticism and the Kabbalah. Second, there is a no less significant difference between the character borne by the doctrine of the word and wisdom in Scripture and that borne by the doctrine of intermediate beings in Philo. In Philo, the Logos in its first meaning is equivalent to the reason, thinking, and thought in God and thus, as such, immanent in God, nothing but an attribute. Only in its second meaning does that Logos become a word that proceeds from God and occupies a mediating place between God and the world. In the Old Testament, however, the word is not first God’s reason and thought, much less an ideal image of the world, a κοσμος νοητος, but the spoken word by which he creates and preserves all things. Wisdom too, in Job and Proverbs, is presented not as an attribute of God but personally, as possessed and ordained by God from eternity and consulted and searched out in the creation of all things. Third, the intermediate beings in Philo and the Jewish theologians have no soteriological significance. They indeed serve for illumination and the communication of knowledge, but there is no question of a connection between those intermediate beings and the Messiah. They even push the doctrine of the Messiah as revelation of the truth and acquirer of salvation into the background. Now it is indeed true that in the Old Testament the connection between word and wisdom, servant of God and Messiah, _Malak YHWH_ and son of David, is not yet clearly made. The lines still run alongside one another. But they nevertheless approach one another. Elohim and YHWH is the same God. He who as Elohim creates and preserves the world through Word and Spirit is also the one who as YHWH led Israel through his _Malak_, will save his people through the servant of YHWH, reign eternally through the Messiah from David’s house, and renew and sanctify them all through his Spirit. And these lines, which in the Old Testament approach one another more and more, issue in him who is the Logos, the prophet, priest, and king in whom God comes to his people and dwells among them eternally. For Philo, an incarnation of the Logos would have been an absurd thought. In the New Testament it is precisely the highest revelation of God. Finally, it may be added here that the doctrine of intermediate beings in Philo and others is unfinished and has no boundary. Philo does bring some unity into the divine ideas by comprehending them in the Logos, but what he says of this Logos he also repeatedly says of all the divine ideas. In Jewish theology the number of intermediate beings increases more and more. It is an emanation, as with the aeons in Gnosticism. Dualism takes its revenge to the end. The intermediate beings establish no communion between God and the world, because in fact they are neither. The world still always remains outside and over against God. The significance of God’s Spirit is not understood. In the Old Testament this doctrine of the Spirit occupies a large place. In the apocryphal literature, in Philo, and in Jewish theology it is almost entirely neglected. At most, the Spirit is now only a Spirit of prediction granted to a few, Zeller V 384; Weber 184 f., but no longer the Spirit of the Old Testament who concludes and completes creation and re-creation. For all these reasons there is a principial difference between the development that the Trinitarian thoughts of the Old Testament found in the apocryphal literature, in Philo, and among the Jews, and the development given to them in the New Testament. The New Testament may have some words in common with Philo and others, and may also speak of Christ as λογος, εικων, απαυγασμα, υἱος, θεος, and so forth; the agreement goes no further. The New Testament is written in Greek, in the κοινη διαλεκτος, in the language that existed and was spoken everywhere. It created no new language. The thoughts of God assumed the σαρξ of human language. But into those words God placed a new content. There is agreement in form; the content differs.
