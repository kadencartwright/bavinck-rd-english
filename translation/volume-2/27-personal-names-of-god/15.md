## 15.

To understand the doctrine of the Trinity properly, three questions must be answered: What does the word _being_ signify? What is designated by the word _person_? And what is the relation between being and person and between the persons mutually? As for the concept of being, Aristotle described οὐσια as a substance, ἡ μητε καθ’ ὑποκειμενον τινος λεγεται μητε ἐν ὑποκειμενω τινι ἐστιν, οἱον ὁ τις ἀνθρωπος και ὁ τις ἱππος. And thus the word was also first used in theology and applied both to the three persons and to the one being. But gradually οὐσια was used in another sense and became the designation of the being, nature, _essentia_ of a thing, what Aristotle had called το τι ἠν εἰναι. Thus it became synonymous with φυσις. Some considered this word, because derived from φυναι, just as _natura_ from _nasci_, less suitable for designating the being of God, Gregory of Nazianzus, _Ad Evagr._; Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 2. Yet this word became established in theology, just like the word _nature_, and found support in 2 Pet. 1:4. Οὐσια, φυσις, _substantia_, _essentia_, _natura_ became the constant name for the one being of God, deity in general, considered apart from its ὑπαρξις, _subsistentia_, and from its τροποι ὑπαρξεως, _modi subsistendi_, thus for the divine nature as it is common to all three persons. This being of God is one and simple, essentially distinguished from all creatures, and in possession of all those attributes previously treated. The distinction between this being and the three persons in God finds its analogy in creatures. Among these we distinguish between being and individuals. Paul, John, and Peter all partake of the same human nature but as persons are distinguished from the being and from one another, Basil, _Epist._ 43. But here a double danger immediately threatens. Nominalism understands being, the universal, only as a name, a concept, and thus arrives at tritheism in the doctrine of the Trinity. Excessive realism conceives being as a substance standing behind and above the persons and thus arrives at tetradism or Sabellianism. Even Gregory of Nyssa did not entirely overcome this exaggerated realism. In order to argue that deity is one and that one may not speak of three Gods, he denied the applicability of number even to finite creatures. According to him it was an abuse to speak in the plural of those who partake of one nature, and thus to speak of many human beings, _Ad Ablabium_; cf. Petavius, _De trin._ IV, c. 9; Schwane II 156 f.; Dorner, _Entw._ I 916 f. In this way, however, the distinction between being in God and in creatures is overlooked. Without doubt there is analogy, and on account of that analogy we may also speak of God’s being. But that analogy at the same time presupposes a very important difference. The concept of the being of humanity is a species-concept. Human nature indeed has real existence and is not a _flatus vocis_; it is present, admittedly not outside and above human beings, but nevertheless in every human being. Yet it exists in every human being in an individual, finite manner. Human beings, like the gods in polytheism, are indeed ὁμοι-, but not ὁμο- or μονοουσιοι. Human nature in the different human beings is not _tota_ and not _numero eadem_; human beings are therefore not only _distincti_ but also _divisi_. In God all this is different. The divine nature must not be conceived as an abstract species-concept, nor as a _substantia_ outside, above, or behind the persons. It exists in the persons and in each of them _tota et numero eadem_. The persons are therefore indeed _distincti_ but not _divisi_. They are ὁμο-, μονο-, ταυτοουσιοι. They are separated by no place or time or anything else. They all partake of the same divine nature and perfections. It is one and the same deity that exists in the three persons, in all and in each individually, so that in God there is only one Eternal One, one Almighty, one Omniscient One, one God with one understanding, one will, one power, Athanasian Creed, nos. 9–18. The word _being_ thus maintains the truth that is always placed in the foreground in Scripture, lies enclosed in monotheism, and is also defended by Unitarianism. Whatever distinctions may exist in the divine being, they may and can do no injury to the unity of nature. For in God unity is not defective and limited but perfect and absolute. Among creatures all diversity by the nature of the case also includes more or less separation and division. Everything that is creature necessarily exists in the forms of space and time and thus alongside and after one another. But eternity, omnipresence, omnipotence, goodness, and so forth, by their nature exclude all separation and division. God is absolute unity and simplicity, without composition or division, and this unity is itself not ethical or contractual in nature, as among human beings, but absolute, and thus no accident of the being but one with God’s being itself.
