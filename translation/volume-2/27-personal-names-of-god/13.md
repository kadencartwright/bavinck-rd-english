## 13.

Sabellianism too can appear in different forms. It shares with Arianism the denial of threeness in the divine being, but now seeks to obtain the unity of God not by placing Son and Spirit outside God’s being, but by including both in it in such a way that every distinction between the three persons disappears. According to the church’s doctrine of the Trinity, the distinction between the persons lies in the personal properties, particularly in eternal generation. If these are denied, the persons come to stand loosely alongside one another, and tritheism arises. In antiquity the Monophysites John Askusnages and John Philoponus were accused of this, Baur II 13 f., and in the Middle Ages Roscelin, cf. Anselm, _De fide trinitatis et de incarnatione verbi_. Later a similar accusation arose against Thomas Sherlock because he assumed three infinite spirits in the divine being, Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ I 972; further against Roell because he opposed generation, _Examen v. h. Ontw. v. Toler._, collection VIII, p. 10 f.; Moor I 735, 761–771; and then also against Lampe and Sibelius because they had objections to the formula _per communicationem essentiae_, Moor I 744–755; Ypey and Dermout, _Gesch. der N. H. Kerk_ III 202 and n. 84 f.; _Archief v. Kerk. Gesch._, ed. Kist and Royaards, VIII 419 f. If God’s being is still conceived in the Platonic-realist sense, tritheism passes over into tetradism, which was charged against Damian of Alexandria, Baur II 29 f. Since, however, such a threeness of individual, separate beings, μερικαι οὐσιαι, ἰδικαι φυσεις, cannot be reconciled with the unity of God, that unity can also be maintained in such a way that Father, Son, and Spirit are the same person and the same being; this patripassianism was taught by Praxeas in the second century. Or one can also, with Marcellus of Ancyra and Photinus of Sirmium, regard Son and Spirit as attributes in God that only for the sake of creation and re-creation proceeded from God and became independent and personal. The Logos was indeed eternal as λογος ἐνδιαθετος; the Father was never without Logos, he was λογοπατωρ; but this Logos first became Son, προφορικος, in time. God expands himself in time into Son and Spirit and then returns again into himself, Schwane, _D. G._ II 135–150. From this is naturally born modalistic monarchianism, which sees in the three persons only three modes of revelation of the one divine being. This was the proper doctrine of Sabellius, and later this conception of the dogma of the Trinity repeatedly returns. The speculation concerning the Trinity in Erigena, _De div. nat._ I 13, and in Abelard, _Introd. ad theol._ I 7, is not free from modalism either. But this appears more clearly among the pantheistic sects in the Middle Ages, in Joachim of Fiore, Amalric of Bena, and David of Dinant, who distinguished an age of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit and considered the last near, Reuter, _Gesch. der relig. Aufkl. im M. A._ II 183–249. In the age of the Reformation, Anabaptism came into opposition to the ecclesial doctrine of the Trinity. The true God is the God in us; this is the essential Christ; and the Word, the Spirit in us, is the true God, for example Camillo, in Trechsel II 95 f. David Joris taught that God was one and revealed himself successively as Father, Son, and Spirit in three ages of faith, hope, and love, which began with Moses, Christ, and himself, Trechsel I 44 f.; Cramer, _Ned. Archief v. Kerk. Gesch._ V 1–145; VI 289–368. But Servetus in particular devoted all the power of his thought to this dogma. In three writings he subjected it in its ecclesial form to a sharp criticism and also attempted positively to reconstruct it: _De trinitatis erroribus libri septem_, 1531; _Dialogorum de trinitate libri duo_, 1532; and _Christianismi restitutio_, 1553. Servetus has no words sharp enough to condemn the ecclesial doctrine of the Trinity. In his eyes it is tritheistic, atheistic, a _tricipex monstrum_, _tricipex cerberus_, _tripartitus Deus_. Over against it he proceeds from the proposition that the divine being cannot be divided and that therefore, in order to maintain the deity of Christ and the Holy Spirit, not persons but only dispositions, appearances, modes of God may be assumed. The Father is the whole being of God, the only God. But he makes use of the Logos, who already existed before Christ, not as a person, however, but as word, reason, thought, in order to reveal himself in creation and under the Old Testament and to become human in Christ. The Logos did not assume human nature in Christ but became flesh in him. The man Christ is therefore the true Son of God; God dwells fully in him. Likewise, the Holy Spirit, who is not really distinguished from the Logos but is comprehended in him, is the mode of God’s self-communication. Through him God indwells all creatures and communicates his life to all. At the end of the process the Trinity ceases again, Trechsel I 65 f., 103 f., 120 f. The Gnostic and theosophical elements that here again appeared in the doctrine of the Trinity increased presently in Böhme, Zinzendorf, and Swedenborg. In Böhme, the Trinity is the result of a process whose foundations and factors are the dark nature, the light of the idea, and the will in the deity, Claassen, _Jakob Böhme_ II 25 f. Zinzendorf indeed called himself _trinitariissimus_ but nevertheless really proceeded from a Gnostic conception of God. God _an sich_ was unattainable, hidden, inscrutable, but he reveals himself in Christ. Christ is the proper Creator of all things, the YHWH of the Old Testament, who became flesh and is the object of our worship. In him the Trinity too is revealed, though not with immanent relations of generation, spiration, and so forth, but as a holy family. The first person is the Father, the Holy Spirit is the mother, Christ is the Son, and into that family the individual believer and the church are now received as the bride of the Son, who, just as Eve from Adam’s side, is created in an entirely realistic manner from the side and blood of Christ, Plitt, _Zinzendorffs Theol._ I 211 f.; II 24, 133 f.; III 14 f. Swedenborg opposed the doctrine of the Trinity even more strongly. Like Servetus, he saw in it nothing but tritheism. God is one, but he became manifest in Christ as Father, Son, and Spirit, who are related as soul, body, and the activity proceeding from both, Swedenborg, _Die wahre christl. Religion_, 2nd ed., Stuttgart, 1873, p. 241 f. This theosophy prepared the doctrine of the Trinity in modern philosophy. In Spinoza’s system, with its one immutable substance, there was still no place for it. In Kant the three persons are replaced by three qualities; true religion consists in faith in God as holy lawgiver, good ruler, and righteous judge, _Religion innerhalb_ etc., 168 f. Schleiermacher subjected the dogma of the Trinity to severe criticism and acknowledged in it only this truth: that God is united with humanity both in the person of Christ and in the _Gemeingeist der Kirche_, _Gl._ §§170–172; cf. also Lipsius, _Dogm._ §368; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ §103 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 238. According to Schelling and Hegel, the dogma contained a profound philosophical thought, which they interpreted as follows: God is spirit, thought, idea, and it therefore belongs to his nature to represent, think, objectify himself. The content of that thought, however, cannot be a thought as in a human being but must be reality. God therefore produces himself in thinking, objectifies himself, namely in the world, which is the proper Son of God, and then returns from that _Selbstentäusserung_ through the consciousness of humanity in the Spirit back to himself, Schelling, _Werke_ I 5, p. 294; I 6, p. 28 f.; I 7, p. 56 f.; Hegel, _Werke_ XI 120 f., 129 f.; XII 181 f. The great difference between this speculation and the ecclesial doctrine of the Trinity was indeed recognized by Strauss, _Gl._ I 492 f. Nevertheless, many still delight in a similar philosophical construction, Biedermann §159; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ §122. Others content themselves with distinguishing three potencies, moments, or powers in the one personality of God, Schelling in his later period; Rothe, _Ethik_ §23 f.; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 438–446. Finally, many get no further than a revelational Trinity of God in nature (creation), history (Christ), and conscience (church), Hase, _Hutt. Rediv._ §70; de Wette, _Dogm._³ 71; Kaftan, _Das Wesen der chr. Rel._ 388.
