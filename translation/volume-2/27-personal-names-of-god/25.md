## 25.

Church and theology generally adopted a very reserved attitude toward these philosophical constructions of the doctrine of the Trinity.
At most, they are willing to acknowledge something in proofs for the Trinity only _a posteriori_, for the purpose of clarifying the dogma; but even then many warned against the attempt to seek support for this doctrine in reason.
More than any other, the dogma of the Trinity was a mystery that far transcended nature and reason and could be known only from special revelation.
Like Augustine, Thomas did indeed accept _vestigia trinitatis_ in creation and also attempted to illuminate them _a posteriori_ by reasoning.
But he expressly declared that the Trinity was not knowable by reason, for creation is a work of the entire Trinity and therefore does indeed show the unity of being but not the distinction of the persons.
_Qui autem probare nititur trinitatem personarum naturali ratione, fidei dupliciter derogat_: first, by detracting from the dignity of faith, which has only invisible things as its object, and second, by deterring others from faith when they begin to think that our faith rests on such weak grounds, _S. Theol._ I qu. 32 art. 1; cf. also Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 3 n. 6 and the commentaries on that passage.
Calvin saw little benefit in the analogies and proofs for the Trinity that were adduced from nature and the human being, _Inst._ I 13, 18; I 15, 4; _Comm. in Gen._ 1:26.
And many Reformed and Lutheran theologians judged in the same way, Hyperius, _Meth. theol._, p. 111; Zanchi, _Op._ I 356 sq.; Walaeus, _Op._ I 236; _Synopsis pur. theol._ VII 14; Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ II 24, 21; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ III § 23-32; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I, p. 265 sq.; Hollaz, _Examen theol._, p. 344, and so forth; cf. in modern times Böhl, _Dogm._, 80; Beck, _Glaub._ II 129.

Without doubt, over against all who wish to make the doctrine of the Trinity rest upon rational grounds, it must be maintained that knowledge of this doctrine is owed solely to God’s special revelation.
Scripture is the only and ultimate ground for the doctrine of the Trinity; reason can at most clarify this doctrine somewhat by thought _a posteriori_.
Nevertheless, the reasonings adduced to elucidate the dogma of the Trinity are not devoid of all value.
First, Scripture itself gives us liberty to do so when it says that the whole creation, and especially the human being, is a work of the triune God.
Certainly, all God’s outward works are undivided and common to all three persons; in these works the unity of God therefore stands in the foreground much more than the distinction of the persons.
But diversity cannot be absent from that unity.
And Scripture itself thereby indicates that all creatures will exhibit _vestigia_, and the human being the _imago trinitatis_.
However greatly sin has objectively veiled the revelation of God in his works and subjectively darkened our eye, it cannot be denied _a priori_ that an understanding illumined by revelation can discover in nature traces of that God whom it learned to know in Scripture as triune in his manner of existence and working.
To this must be added that all these reasonings are undoubtedly incapable of proving the dogma of the Trinity.
Not one of them can or may be the ground of our faith.
We would expose the truth to the ridicule of our opponents if we accepted it on such weak grounds as our reason is able to adduce, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 32 art. 1; qu. 46 art. 2; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 9.
But these reasonings are indeed capable of refuting various objections that are raised against the dogma, ibid., _C. Gent._ I c. 9; they indeed have the power to show that what revelation teaches us is neither impossible nor absurd, ibid., _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 1 art. 5 ad 2; and they are able to demonstrate that the belief of the opponents is inadequate and conflicts with reason itself, ibid., _S. Theol._ I qu. 1 art. 8; Voetius, _Disp._ I, p. 1 sq.
The doctrine of the Trinity is not, after all, as absurd as it appeared to a superficial rationalism of earlier and later times.
It is not overthrown by the calculation that one cannot be three and three cannot be one.
Rather, philosophy has repeatedly, and again in this century, returned to the doctrine of the Trinity and has at least to some extent discerned its rich sense and profound significance.
Finally, it must also be added that these reasonings trace and preserve the connection between nature and grace, between creation and re-creation.
It is one and the same God who creates and preserves us and who re-creates us according to his image.
Grace indeed rises above nature but does not conflict with it.
Restoring what has been corrupted in it by sin, grace also illumines and perfects what still remains in it of God’s revelation.
The thinking understanding places the doctrine of the Trinity in the midst of the rich life of nature and humanity.
The Christian’s confession is not an island in the ocean but a high mountain peak from which the whole creation is surveyed.
And it is the task of the Christian theologian to set forth clearly God’s revelation in its connection with and significance for the whole of life.
Christian thought remains unsatisfied until all that exists is traced back to the triune God and the confession of God’s Trinity is placed in the very center of our thought and life.
The analogies and proofs adduced for the Trinity therefore do not serve to demonstrate the dogma, but above all to show the manifold benefit and rich significance contained in this confession for the life and knowledge of creatures.
In the end, they were born not from a desire for vain speculation and inappropriate curiosity but from a deep religious interest.
If God is triune, then that must be of the highest significance, for it is only from and through and unto God that all things exist.

First, then, the Trinity makes God known to us as the truly Living One.
The church fathers already observed that the error of deism and pantheism, of monism and polytheism, was avoided by the Trinity, and that what was true and lay hidden in all those ideas concerning God was taken up into the Trinity.
Deism digs a chasm between God and his creature, abolishes their kinship, and leaves to God nothing but an abstract unity, a pure being, a monotonous and uniform existence; it satisfies neither head nor heart; it is the death of religion.
Pantheism does indeed bring God nearer to us, but makes him equal to the creature, erases the boundary between _creator_ and _creatura_, deprives him of a being and life of his own, and undermines religion.
But the Christian doctrine of the Trinity makes God known as essentially distinct from the world and yet as possessing a blessed life of his own.
God is a fullness of being, πελαγος οὐσιας.
He is not barren, ἀγονος.
He is absolute being, the Eternal One, who is and was and is to come, but in that being the eternally living, the eternally productive one.
People have tried to derive the Trinity from God’s thinking and willing, from his love, goodness, perfection, and so forth.
Intended as philosophical constructions of the doctrine of the Trinity, they leave everything to be desired.
The construction from thought arrives at no distinction of persons, does not make clear the procession of the third person, and with a view to this person must pass over to and supplement itself with the construction from God’s will.
The derivation of the Trinity from love encounters the same objections and does not know how to bring the procession of the Holy Spirit to light.
Nevertheless, it is true that these and all the other attributes first acquire life and reality through the Trinity.
Outside and apart from it they are names, sounds, empty concepts.
As attributes of a triune God they begin to live for head and heart.
Only through the Trinity do we understand that God in himself, even apart from the world, is the independent, the eternal, the omniscient, the all-good, love, holiness, and glory.
The Trinity makes God known to us as the fullness of being, true life, eternal beauty.
In God too there is unity in diversity, diversity in unity.
Indeed, this order and harmony are present in him in an absolute manner.
In creatures there is only a weak analogy of it.
Either unity or diversity fails to come into its own.
Creatures exist in space and time, stand alongside one another, and do not interpenetrate one another.
There is unity only through attraction, through will and affection, a moral unity that is breakable and unstable.
And where a deeper, physical unity exists, for example, between the faculties of one substance, there is a lack of subsistence and the unity takes the distinction back into itself.
But in God both are present: absolute unity and absolute diversity.
It is one and the same being that is borne by three _supposita_.
Here there is the highest communion, communion of the same being, and at the same time the highest diversity, diversity of persons.
Therefore, if God is triune, the three persons cannot be conceived otherwise than as ὁμοουσιοι.
Arianism in its many forms does not think through the being of God and therefore does not satisfy thought.
If there are distinctions not outside but in and within the divine being, then these distinctions, that is, these persons, must all be equal in being.
Nothing can exist in God that would be something other or something less than God.
There is no transition between Creator and creature.
Father, Son, and Spirit all partake of the same being and are true God, or they sink down to the creature.
From the Christian standpoint there is no third possibility.
But in the same way Sabellianism in its different _modi_ is condemned by this.
For the consubstantiality of the three persons has sense and significance only if they are truly and _realiter_ distinguished from one another, as distinct bearers of the same substance.
The diversity of the subjects that appear alongside one another in God’s revelation, in creation and re-creation, arises from the diversity that exists between the three persons in God’s own being.
There could be no distinction outwardly in the unity if it did not exist inwardly, _ad intra_.

In the second place, the Trinity is of the highest importance for creation.
Creation can be maintained only with the confession of a triune God.
For through this alone is it possible to maintain, on the one hand, the connection between God and the world against deism and, on the other hand, their distinction against pantheism.
Creation can be conceived neither as sheer chance nor as a self-development of the divine being.
It must have its foundation in God and yet form no moment in his inner process of life.
How could both be maintained otherwise than through the Trinity of God?
The life of God is divinely rich; it is fruitful; there is action, production, in it.
And therefore the doctrine of the Trinity also speaks of the generation of the Son and the procession of the Holy Spirit.
Both these acts are essentially distinct from creation; the former are immanent relations, the latter is an _opus ad extra_; the former are sufficient in themselves—God does not need creation; he is life, blessedness, and glory in himself.
Yet creation stands in the closest connection with this fruitfulness of God.
For, first, Athanasius rightly observed that if the divine being is barren and cannot communicate itself, neither can there be any question of an outward revelation of God, that is, of a communication of God in and to his creature.
The doctrine of the incommunicability of the divine being through generation and procession to the Son and the Spirit entails that the world stands detached alongside, outside, and over against God.
God is then absolutely hidden, βυθος, σιγη, the unconscious, _Ungrund_; the world does not reveal him; no knowledge of God is possible.
By contrast, the dogma of the Trinity teaches that God can communicate himself, absolutely to the Son and the Spirit, and therefore relatively also to the world.
For the communication that takes place in the divine being is, according to Augustine’s thought, the exemplar, the archetype, of God’s work in creation.
Scripture repeatedly points to the close connection that exists between the Son and the Spirit on the one hand and creation on the other.
The designations Father, Son (word, wisdom), Spirit most certainly indicate immanent relations, but are nevertheless also reflected in the relations that exist among the three persons in God’s outward works.
From the Father are all things; in the Son lie the ideas of all being; in the Spirit are the _principia_ of all life.
Generation and procession in the divine being are the immanent acts of God that make possible an outward creation and revelation of God.
From this, finally, it can be explained that all God’s outward works are adequately known only when their Trinitarian existence is understood.
The examples named above are in part very contrived and in every case nothing more than analogies.
But philosophy from Plato to von Hartmann has repeatedly, consciously or unconsciously, returned to three _principia_ or ἀρχαι from which creation in its whole and in its parts could be explained.
There is a great truth in the fact that creation everywhere exhibits _vestigia trinitatis_ to us.
And because these _vestigia_ manifest themselves most clearly in the human being and he may even be called the _imago trinitatis_, he therefore, as it were by an immanent impulse, traces out these _vestigia_ everywhere.
In the _trias_ the perfection of the creature, the completeness of the system, the harmony of beauty, first come to expression.
The higher something stands in the order of creatures, the more it strives toward the _trias_.
And even in the aberrations of human beings in the religious domain, something of this operation can still be discerned.
Schelling’s attempt also to interpret mythology in a Trinitarian manner was nevertheless something more than an ingenious fantasy.

In the third place, the Trinity is of the highest importance for the Christian religion.
With the confession of God’s Trinity stands or falls the whole of Christianity, the entire special revelation.
It is the core of the Christian faith, the root of all _dogmata_, the substance of the new covenant.
The development of the church’s doctrine of the Trinity therefore also began from this religious, Christian interest.
It was truly concerned not with a metaphysical tenet or philosophical speculation but with the heart and essence of the Christian religion itself.
This is felt so strongly that all who still value the name Christian acknowledge and honor some Trinity.
In every Christian confession and dogmatics the deepest question is this: how can God be one and yet also threefold?
And according to how this question is answered, Christian truth comes more or less into its own in every part of doctrine.
In the doctrine of the Trinity beats the heart of the whole revelation of God for the redemption of humanity.
Prepared in the Old Covenant, it therefore first comes clearly to light in Christ.
Religion can be satisfied with nothing less than God himself.
Now in Christ God himself comes to us, and in the Holy Spirit he communicates himself to us.
The work of re-creation is Trinitarian through and through.
From and through and in God are all things.
It is one divine work from beginning to end and yet distinguished threefold; it is encompassed by the love of the Father, the grace of the Son, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit.
And correspondingly, the Christian’s life of faith points back to three _principia_, just as the Belgic Confession, art. 9, says that we know the doctrine of the Trinity both from the testimonies of Holy Scripture and from the operations of the three persons, especially from those we feel in ourselves.
We know ourselves to be children of the Father, redeemed by the Son, and in fellowship with both through the Holy Spirit.
All salvation and blessing come to us from the triune God.
In that name we are baptized; that name is the summary of our confession; from that name every blessing descends upon us; to that name we bring eternal thanks and honor; in that name we find rest for the heart and peace for our conscience.
The Christian has a God above him and for him and in him.
Therefore blessedness in this life and the life to come is connected with the doctrine of the Trinity, even though we cannot determine the measure of knowledge of this mystery that is required for a sincere faith.
