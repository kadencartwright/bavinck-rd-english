## 6.

Further light is also shed for us on God’s immanent relations by the names that the Son bears in Scripture. These names are very numerous, refer mostly to his historical appearance, and therefore come up for discussion later in the _locus de Christo_. But among them are also names that belong to him before and apart from his incarnation. First, the name Logos comes into consideration. Various reasons have been given why Christ bears this name. The word has been translated by _ratio, sermo, verbum_, and the last has in turn been understood as _verbum interius_ or _exterius_, Petavius, _De trin._ VI, c. 1. The point of departure for the designation, however, undoubtedly lies in the continuous teaching of Scripture that God reveals himself in creation and re-creation through the word. Through the word God creates, sustains, and governs all things, and through the word he also renews and re-creates the world. The gospel is therefore also called the word of God, λογος του θεου. John calls Christ the Logos because he is the one in and through whom God reveals himself, both in creation and in re-creation, John 1:3, 14. In the Old Testament, however, the word through which God reveals himself first appears at creation. The hypostasis and eternal existence of that word are not expressed. In Proverbs 8 wisdom is indeed presented personally and eternally, but it is also brought into close connection with creation; with a view to creation it was prepared, appointed, and searched out by God, vv. 22, 23. From the קָנָנִי of v. 22, LXX εκτισε με, Syr. Targ. בְּרָאנִי, cf. Sirach 1:4, 9; 24:8, the Arians inferred that the Son was not eternally generated but was created before all things. And the church fathers maintained over against them that קָנָנִי had to be rendered by ἐκτησατο, Aquila, Symmachus, or _possedit_, Jerome, or that this word referred not to the being of the Son but to his office and dignity in creation and re-creation. Without doubt, the latter is the case. There is no mention here of an eternal generation, but it is said only that God prepared wisdom, קָנָנִי, and appointed it, נִסַּכְתִּי, that it was born, חִלַּלְתִּי, before and with a view to creation, Petavius, _De trin._ II, c. 1. But the New Testament goes far beyond this. John says not only that he in and through whom God reveals himself is a person, but he expressly declares that this Logos was in the beginning, ἐν ἀρχῃ ἠν ὁ Λογος. He did not _become_ Logos; he was not first prepared and appointed to be Logos at creation. As a person and by nature he was Logos, Logos from eternity. And further, he was himself θεος; he was in fellowship with God, ἠν ἐν ἀρχη προς τον θεον, v. 2, είς τον κολπον του πατρος, v. 18, the object of his eternal love and self-communication, 5:26; 17:24. He could therefore also reveal the Father perfectly because from eternity he shared in his divine nature, his divine life, his divine love, and so forth, and was Logos by nature. God communicated himself to him; therefore he can communicate God to us. The Logos is the absolute revelation of God, for God has eternally communicated himself to him in all his fullness.

Another name is that of Son of God. In the Old Testament this name usually has a theocratic meaning. Israel is called this because it was chosen, called, and adopted by God, Ex. 4:22; 19:5; Deut. 1:31; 8:5; 14:1; 32:6, 18; Isa. 63:8; Jer. 31:9, 20; Hos. 11:1; Mal. 1:6; 2:10. In the New Testament the church takes its place, consisting of υἱοι θεου through adoption or τεκνα θεου through birth. In particular, the title Son of God is also frequently an official name: for the judges, Ps. 82:6; for the angels, Job 38:7; and especially also for the king, 2 Sam. 7:11–14; Ps. 89:27, 28. In Ps. 2:7 YHWH says to the King anointed over Zion, אֲנִי הַיּוֹם יְלִדְתִּיךָ, בְּנִי אַתָּה, LXX γεγεννηκα σε, Vulg. _genui te_; on the day when the Lord anointed and appointed him king, he generated him as Son and gave him the right to dominion over the world. With respect to David, this looks back to God’s decree in 2 Sam. 7, and with respect to the Messiah foreshadowed by David, it is explained in Heb. 1:5; 5:5 of eternity, cf. vv. 2, 3, in which Christ as the Son is generated by the Father, that is, in which he is brought forth as the radiance of God’s glory and the exact imprint of his being. And that he was this has now, according to Acts 13:33; Rom. 1:3, been powerfully demonstrated in the resurrection. A related thought is expressed in Mic. 5:1. The Ruler over Israel who will one day come forth from little Bethlehem has already existed from ancient days. His goings forth as Ruler, from God, are already from the days of eternity. He was Ruler from eternity, he has shown this in the history of Israel, and thus he will one day visibly appear from Bethlehem. The designation Son of God, applied to the Messiah, undoubtedly proceeds from the theocratic meaning of this expression in the Old Testament. It is not probable that the possessed, Matt. 8:29; cf. 4:3; the Jews, Matt. 27:40; the high priest, Matt. 26:63; or even the disciples, at least in the early period, John 1:50; 11:27; Matt. 16:16, understood the full content of this designation. But in Christ this name nevertheless receives a much deeper meaning. He is indeed now and then called God’s Son in a theocratic sense as mediator and king, Luke 1:35, although even then the adoptionist conception—that he is Son by generation according to his divine nature and by adoption according to his human nature, as was maintained by the Socinians, _Remonstr. Conf._, art. 3; _Apol. Conf._, art. 3; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II 17, 10; B. S. Cremer, cf. _Archief v. Kerk. Gesch. v. Ned._ VIII 419–428; Calvin, _Inst._ II 14, 6; Moor I 755 sq.; etc.—finds no support in Scripture. But Christ was not first adopted as Son of God in time as a king in Israel. He is not called God’s Son because of his supernatural birth, as the Socinians taught and Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I 116 f., still attempts to demonstrate. Nor does he bear that name in an ethical sense, as many believe, Weiss, _Bibl. Theol._ §17; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II² 97; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 206; and others. Nor did he first become Son of God through his mediatorship and his resurrection, for which appeal is made to John 10:34–36; Acts 13:32, 33; Rom. 1:4. Rather, he is Son of God in a metaphysical sense, by nature and from eternity. He is highly exalted above angels and prophets, Matt. 13:32; 21:27; 22:2, and stands in an entirely unique relation to God, Matt. 11:27. He is the beloved Son in whom the Father is well pleased, Matt. 3:17; 17:5; Mark 1:11; 9:7; Luke 3:22; 9:35; the only-begotten Son, John 1:18; 3:16; 1 John 4:9 f.; the Father’s own Son, Rom. 8:32; the eternal Son, John 17:5, 24; Heb. 1:5; 5:5, to whom the Father gave ζωην ἐχειν ἐν ἑαυτῳ, John 5:26; who is equal to the Father in knowledge, Matt. 11:27; in honor, John 5:23; in power in creation and re-creation, John 1:3; 5:21, 27; in activity, John 10:30; and in dominion, Matt. 11:27; Luke 10:22; 22:29; John 16:15; 17:10; and who precisely because of this Sonship was condemned to death, John 10:33; Matt. 26:63 f.

In the third place, the name Image of God comes up for discussion. A human being can indeed be called this by analogy, but Christ is the image of God in the absolute sense. Before his incarnation, as Logos, as Son, Rom. 1:3, 4; 8:3; Gal. 4:4, he was εν μορφη θεου, Phil. 2:6; rich, 2 Cor. 8:9; clothed with glory, John 17:5; and he has now returned to this through his resurrection and ascension. Thus he was then and still is now εἰκων του θεου του ἀορατου, Col. 1:15; 2 Cor. 4:4; ἀπαυγασμα της δοξης και χαρακτηρ της ὑποστασεως αὐτου, Heb. 1:3, that is, not the radiation itself, ἀπαυγασμος, but the image of God’s glory effected or produced through radiation, reflection, ἀπαυγασμα, and the expression, the imprint, of the subsistence, of the being of the Father. As such he is πρωτοτοκος πασης κτισεως, Col. 1:15; Rev. 1:14, the one firstborn in comparison with every creature and thus existing before every creature, not made or created like the creatures, πρωτοκτιστος, πρωτοπλαστος, but begotten, πρωτοτοκος, in whom all things were created, ἐκτισθη; and at the same time he is ἀρχη, πρωτοτοκος ἐκ των νεκρων, ἐν πασιν πρωτευων, Col. 1:18; πρωτοτοκος ἐν πολλοις ἀδελφοις, Rom. 8:29, according to whose image believers are renewed, 2 Cor. 3:18; Phil. 3:21. The expression πρωτοτοκος does not include Christ among the creatures but precisely excludes him from them. As the only-begotten and firstborn, as Son and Logos, as the adequate image of God, he stood from eternity in an entirely unique relation to the Father. And Christ is indeed now presented as mediator, dependent on and subordinate to the Father, so that he is one who is sent, a servant, a fulfiller of the Father’s work, obedient unto death, and one day surrendering his kingdom to the Father, but his unity of being is never thereby impaired. In John 14:28 Jesus says that his going to the Father is a cause of joy for the disciples, ὁτι ὁ πατηρ μειζων μου ἐστιν. By this he does not express that the Father is greater in power, which is decisively contradicted by John 10:28–30; rather, he has in view the relation in which he now stands to the Father in his humiliation. Now the Father is greater. But this lesser status of Jesus will cease precisely when he goes to the Father, and therefore his disciples can rejoice at his departure; in being and nature he is equal to the Father, although now in office and station less than the Father. He is not a creature but was and is and remains God, blessed above all forever, John 1:1; 20:28; Rom. 9:5; Heb. 1:8, 9; 2 Pet. 3:18; 1 John 5:20; Rev. 1:8, 11; cf. also perhaps 2 Thess. 1:12; Titus 2:13; 2 Pet. 1:1. The attempt, already made earlier by the Socinians and now by Ritschl, Schultz, Kaftan, Pfleiderer, and others, to understand the name θεος, used of Christ, not as an essential name but as an official name, requires broader discussion later in the doctrine of Christ. For now let it only be noted that this designation is untrue of Christ if he does not in fact also partake of the divine nature, Lipsius, _Theol. Jahresbericht_ X 378.
