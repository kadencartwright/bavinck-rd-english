## 12.

Throughout all the centuries both tendencies have continued to exist within the Christian church, to the right and left of the church’s doctrine of the Trinity.
The essence of Arianism lies in the denial of the consubstantiality of Father and Son, in the assertion that the Father alone and in the absolute sense is the only true God.
The Son is then naturally a lesser and lower being, standing outside the nature of God; but there can be disagreement concerning the place that belongs to the Son between God and the world of creatures; Arianism allows all kinds of latitude.
The distance between God and the world is endless, and at every point along that distance a place can be assigned to the Son, from the place next to God on his throne down to that alongside creatures, angels, or human beings.
Accordingly, Arianism has also appeared in different forms.
First, in the form of subordinationism: the Son is then indeed eternal, generated from the essence of the Father, no creature and not brought forth from nothing, but he is nevertheless less than and subordinate to the Father.
The Father alone is ὁ θεος, πηγη θεοτητος; the Son is θεος and received his nature by communication from the Father.
This was taught by Justin, Tertullian, Clement, Origen, and others; also by the Semi-Arians, Eusebius of Caesarea and Eusebius of Nicomedia, who placed the Son ἐκτος του πατρος and called him ὁμοιοοσιος with the Father; later by the Remonstrants, _Conf._ art. 3; Arminius, _Op. theol._, 1629, p. 232 sq.; Episcopius, _Instit. theol._ IV sect. 2 c. 32; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II c. 17 § 25; by the supernaturalists, Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I⁴ 612 f.; Knapp, _Gl._ I 260; Muntinghe, _Theol. Christ._ pars theor. § 134 sq., and so forth; and by very many theologians in modern times, Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I 207 f.; Beck, _Chr. Gl._ II 123 f., 134 f.; Twesten II 254; Kahnis I 353, 398; Van Oosterzee II § 52; Doedes, _Ned. Gel._, 71 f.
Next, it reappeared in its ancient form, which it had in Arius, among many theologians after the Reformation, especially in England.
Milton, for example, taught that the Son and the Spirit were created by the free will of the Father before creation and bore the name God only on account of their office, like the judges and authorities in the Old Testament, _De doctr. christ._, ed. Sumner, 1827, lib. I c. 5, 6.
The same was taught, with slight modifications, by W. Whiston, whose Arianism gave rise to many rebuttals, Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ I 957 f.; S. Clarke, _The Scripture Doctrine of the Trinity_, 1712; P. Maty, _Lettre d’un théologien à un autre théologien sur le mystère de la Trinité_, 1729; Daniel Whitby; Harwood; many Remonstrants in this country; and in the present age the Groningen theologians, Hofstede de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._, 160 f.
A third form of Arianism arose in Socinianism.
The Father is the only true God.
The Son is a holy human being created immediately by God through supernatural conception, who did not exist before his conception and was brought forth by God for this purpose: to proclaim a new law to human beings.
After fulfilling this commission, he was exalted in heaven and made a participant in divine grace.
The Spirit is nothing other than a power of God, _Cat. Racov._ qu. 94-190; cf. F. Socinus, in _Bibl. patr. Polon._ I 789 sq.; Crell, _De uno Deo Patre libri duo_, and others, in Trechsel, _Protest. Antitrinitarier_ II 221 f., 233 f.
This Socinianism spread from Poland to Germany, the Netherlands, England, and America; found exponents in the latter two countries in John Biddle, Nathaniel Lardner, Theophilus Lindsey, Joseph Priestley, founder of the Unitarian Society, and others; and passed over into Unitarianism.
Socinianism could not sustain for long the supernaturalism it had initially still accepted; Jesus became an ordinary human being, even if an exemplar of piety and morality, and Christianity became completely detached from his person.
This was also taught by rationalism, Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ § 91.
Modern theology occupies the same standpoint.
And Ritschl has in substance done nothing other than revive Socinianism.
Jesus was a human being enabled by God to establish the kingdom of heaven on earth and was afterward exalted to the rank of God and Lord of the church.
In this whole rationalistic view of the doctrine of the Trinity there is naturally still much less need for divine grace, and therefore the Holy Spirit scarcely comes under discussion; his deity and, most of the time, his personality are denied.
