## 17.

From this may be inferred the sense in which the third question mentioned above, concerning the relation between essence and person and among the persons themselves, must be answered.
In Tertullian the three persons are _unius substantiae, unius status, unius potestatis sed unus Deus_; they are three _non statu sed gradu_, _unum_, not _unus_; they are _unus Deus, ex quo et gradus isti et formae et species in nomine Patris, Filii et Spiritus Sancti deputantur_.
There is _distinctio, distributio_, no _diversitas_ and _divisio_.
Athanasius and the Cappadocians described the ὑποστασεις as τροποι ὑπαρξεως and thereby indicated that, one in essence, they nevertheless each had an _existentia_ of their own and differed in mode of existence.
The distinction between essence and person and among the persons themselves thus lay in the reciprocal relation, ἡ προς ἀλληλα σχεσις, in being Father, Son, and Spirit, in the properties of πατροτης (ἀγεννησια), υἱοτης (γεννησις), and ἁγιασμος (ἐκπορευσις).
This was developed more fully by Augustine.
He derives the _trinitas_ not from the Father but from the unity, from the _deitas_, and regards it not as accidental but as essential to God.
It belongs to his essence to be triune.
To this extent, being a person is identical with the essence itself.
_Non enim aliud est Deo esse, aliud personam esse sed omnino idem_, _De trin._ VII 6.
For if being belonged to God in an absolute sense and being a person in a relative sense, the three persons could not be one essence.
Each person is therefore equal to the whole essence and is as much as the other two or as all three together.
This is not so among creatures.
One human being is not as much as three human beings.
_At in Deo non ita est; non enim major essentia est Pater et Filius et Spiritus Sanctus simul quam solus Pater aut solus Filius, sed tres simul illae substantiae sive personae, si ita dicendae sunt, aequales sunt singulis_, _De trin._ VII 6.
_In summa trinitate tantum est una quantum tres simul sunt nec plus aliquid sunt duae quam una, et in se infinita sunt. Ita et singula sunt in singulis et omnia in singulis et singula in omnibus et omnia in omnibus et unum omnia_, ibid., VI 10.
_Ipsa trinitas tam magnum est, quam unaquaeque ibi persona_, ibid., VIII 1.
Therefore the distinction between essence and person and among the persons themselves cannot lie in any substance but only in their mutual relations.
_Quidquid ergo ad seipsum dicitur Deus, et de singulis personis singulariter dicitur, id est, de Patre et Filio et Spiritu Sancto, et simul de ipsa trinitate non pluraliter sed singulariter dicitur_, _De trin._ V 8.
_Quidquid autem proprie singula in eadem trinitate dicuntur, nullo modo ad seipsa sed ad invicem, aut ad creaturam dicuntur et ideo relative, non substantialiter ea dici manifestum est_, ibid., c. 11.
And accordingly later theology said that essence and person differed not _re_ but _ratione_, though not _ratione ratiocinante_—that is, _rationaliter, nominaliter_, as Sabellius thought—but _ratione ratiocinata_.
The distinction consisted not in any substance but only in relation, yet this distinction was nevertheless real, objective, and grounded in God’s revelation.
Essence and person differ not _re_ but nevertheless _realiter_; the difference consists in a _modus subsistendi_ but is nevertheless a real difference.
The person is a mode of existence of the essence; and the persons therefore differ among themselves as one mode of existence differs from another or, according to the usual example, as the open hand differs from the closed hand, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 28 sq.; Petavius, _De trin._ IV, c. 10, 11; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorz._ I² 350 f.; Calvin, _Inst._ I 13, 6, 16–20; Zanchius, _Op._ I 21 sq.; Moor I 713 sq.; Turretin, _Th. El._ III, qu. 27; etc.
If this distinction between essence and person and among the persons themselves must be indicated in a single word, there is indeed not much more to be said about it.
But this distinction nevertheless becomes clearer when we think through the relations themselves by which it comes about within the divine essence.
Although Holy Scripture is strictly monotheistic, it nevertheless attributes divine nature and perfections to the Son and the Spirit and places them on the same level as the Father.
Father, Son, and Spirit are thus distinct subjects in the one and same divine essence.
And as such they therefore also bear different names, have particular personal properties, and always act in a certain fixed order, both _ad intra_ and _ad extra_.
The distinction of the persons thus lies entirely in the so-called ἰδιωματα, ἰδιοτητες, _proprietates_, personal properties—namely, _paternitas_ (ἀγεννησια, _innascibilitas_), _generatio activa, spiratio activa, filiatio_ (_generatio passiva_ and _spiratio activa_), _processio_ (_spiratio passiva_).
These naturally add nothing new, no new substance, to the essence.
A human being who becomes a father does not change in essence but merely enters a relation hitherto foreign to him.
God’s essence is distinguished from being Father, being Son, and being Spirit not materially or substantially but only _ratione, relatione_.
The one and same essence is and is called Father when it is conceived in its relation to that same essence in the relation of the Son.
And the persons therefore differ among themselves only in this: that one is Father, another Son, and the third Spirit.
Among human beings we find only a weak likeness of this, but one that can nevertheless serve as clarification.
Among human beings fatherhood and sonship are indeed nothing but relations, yet this relation presupposes a personal, individual subject that is the bearer of the relation but otherwise also exists in all manner of ways outside that fatherhood and sonship.
Fatherhood is only an accidental property of being human; some human beings never become fathers; one who does become a father was not one for a long time and also gradually ceases to be one again, and so forth.
Being human is therefore by no means exhausted in fatherhood or sonship.
But it is not so in the divine essence.
Being God coincides completely with being a person.
_Quemadmodum hoc illi est esse, quod Deum esse, quod magnum, quod bonum esse, ita hoc illi est esse, quod personam esse_, Augustine, _De trin._ VII 6.
The divine essence therefore, so to speak, coincides completely in each of the three persons with being Father, Son, and Spirit.
The _paternitas, filiatio_, and _processio_ are no properties added to the essence but the essence’s eternal modes of existence, the eternal immanent relations within the essence.
Among human beings the unfolding of the one human nature falls apart: it takes place in part within the individual human being as he becomes a personality; it further takes place in humanity, whose members all represent human nature in a distinctive way; and it finally also comes about in relations of sex and kinship, each of which in turn makes known to us a side of human nature.
Among human beings this threefold unfolding of nature falls apart in space and time; it is essentially extension.
In God, however, there is no separation or division.
The unfolding of his essence into personality at once, immediately, absolutely, and perfectly comprehends within itself the unfolding of his essence into persons and also the unfolding of his essence into the immanent relations expressed by the names Father, Son, and Spirit.
Thus God is the archetype of the human being; what falls apart in humanity, lies alongside other things, and is spread out in the forms of space and time is eternally and simply present in God.
The _processiones_ within his essence simultaneously bring about in God his absolute personality, his triunity, and his immanent relations.
They are the absolute archetypes of all those _processiones_ by which human nature comes to its complete unfolding in the individual human being, in the family, and in humanity.
Therefore the three persons are indeed each another, _alius_, but not another thing, _aliud_; the Trinity is from and in and unto the unity; the unfolding of the essence takes place within the essence and therefore does not detract from the unity and simplicity of the essence.
Further, the three persons, though not _aliud_, are nevertheless _alii_, distinct subjects, ὑποστασεις, _subsistentiae_, who precisely in this way bring God’s essence to absolute unfolding within the essence itself.
And finally, these three persons are mutually related to one another in an absolute manner through generation and spiration; their distinctness as subjects coincides completely with their immanent relations of kinship.
The Father is solely and eternally Father, the Son is solely and eternally Son, the Spirit is solely and eternally Spirit.
And because each is himself in a simple, eternal, absolute manner, the Father is God and the Son is God and the Holy Spirit is God.
The Father is God as Father, the Son as Son, the Holy Spirit as Holy Spirit.
And because all three are God, they partake of one divine nature, and there is therefore one God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, who is to be praised forever.
