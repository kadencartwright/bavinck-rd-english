## 4.

The New Testament contains the pure development of the Trinitarian thoughts of the Old Testament. But these now appear in a much clearer light, not through abstract reasonings about God’s being, but through God’s self-revelation in appearance, word, and deed. The unity of God is expressed just as strongly in the New Testament as in the Old. There is only one being that can be called God, θεος, Elohim, John 17:3; 1 Cor. 8:4, but this one true God reveals himself in the economy of the New Testament, particularly in the facts of incarnation and outpouring, as Father, Son, and Spirit. No absolutely new _principia_ appear in these facts. They are the same as were also active in creation and in the economy of the Old Testament. The Father, who bears this name chiefly in relation to the Son and to his children, is the same who can also be called Father as Creator of all things, Matt. 7:11; Luke 3:38; John 4:21; Acts 17:28; 1 Cor. 8:6; Heb. 12:9; all things are from him, 1 Cor. 8:6. The Son, who is called such above all in his wholly unique relation to God, is the same who as Logos created all things with the Father, John 1:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 1:15–17; Heb. 1:3. And the Holy Spirit, who received his name especially with a view to his work in the church, is the same who with Father and Son adorns and completes all things in creation, Matt. 1:18; 4:1; Mark 1:12; Luke 1:35; 4:1, 14; Rom. 1:4. Further, it is the general teaching of the New Testament writers that these three—Father, Son, and Spirit—are no others than those who also revealed themselves to the fathers in the economy of the Old Testament, in word and deed, prophecy and miracle. The Old Testament name YHWH, inadequately rendered by κυριος, unfolds its content fully in the New Testament name πατηρ, above, p. 113. In the incarnate Son of God is seen the fulfillment of all Old Testament prophecy and shadow: of prophet and king, priest and sacrifice, servant of God and son of David, _Malak YHWH_ and wisdom. And in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit what the Old Testament had promised came to pass, Acts 2:16 f. But although the New Testament connects with the Old, it does not stop there; it goes far beyond it. Much more clearly than in the Old Testament, it now comes to light that the God of the covenant is and must be a triune God, that a threefold principle appears in the work of salvation. Not a few texts but the entire New Testament is Trinitarian in this sense. All salvation, blessing, and blessedness has its threefold cause in God, Father, Son, and Spirit. We see these three appear immediately at the birth of Jesus, Matt. 1:18 f.; Luke 1:35, and at his baptism, Matt. 3:16, 17; Mark 1:10, 11; Luke 3:21, 22. Jesus’ teaching is entirely Trinitarian. He declares the Father to us and describes him as Spirit, who has life in himself, John 4:24; 5:26, and in a wholly unique sense is his Father, Matt. 11:27; John 2:16; 5:17. He distinguishes himself from the Father and yet is his only-begotten, own, dearly beloved Son, Matt. 11:27; 21:37–39; John 3:16; etc., one with him in life, glory, and power, John 1:14; 5:26; 10:30. And he speaks of the Holy Spirit, who himself leads and equips him, Mark 1:12; Luke 4:1, 14; John 3:34, as another Paraclete whom he will send from the Father, John 15:26, and who will convict, teach, guide into the truth, comfort, and remain forever, John 14:16. Before he departs, Jesus draws all this together in το ὀνομα του πατρος και του υἱου και του ἁγιου πνευματος, that is, in the one divine name—το ὀνομα in the singular—in which nevertheless three distinct subjects, ὁ πατηρ, ὁ υἱος, and το πνευμα, all deliberately named with the article, reveal themselves. This teaching is continued and expanded by the apostles; all know and extol a threefold, divine cause of salvation. The good pleasure, foreknowledge, election, power, love, and kingdom are the Father’s, Matt. 6:13; 11:26; John 3:16; Rom. 8:29; Eph. 1:9; 1 Pet. 1:2; etc. The mediation, reconciliation, salvation, grace, wisdom, and righteousness are the Son’s, Matt. 1:21; 1 Cor. 1:30; Eph. 1:10; 1 Tim. 2:5; 1 Pet. 1:2; 1 John 2:2; etc. And regeneration, renewal, sanctification, and fellowship are the Holy Spirit’s, John 3:5; John 14–16; Rom. 5:5; 8:15; 14:17; 2 Cor. 1:21, 22; 1 Pet. 1:2; 1 John 5:6; etc. And just as Jesus finally summarizes his teaching in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, so too the apostles repeatedly place these three alongside one another and on one level, 1 Cor. 8:6; 12:4–6; 2 Cor. 13:13; 1 Pet. 1:2; 1 John 5:4–6; Rev. 1:4–6. Gl._ I 331 f.; Sartorius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 44 f.; Beck, _Chr. Gl._ II 40 f.; etc. Biedermann too, _Chr. Dogm._ II 37, acknowledges that the Trinitarian doctrine of God is rooted in Scripture; cf. Strauss I 409–425; Lipsius, _Dogm._ §§241, 242; F. A. B. Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 426. The text 1 John 5:7 was not mentioned above because of its doubtful authenticity. It is absent from all Greek codices except a few from the sixteenth century, from all Latin codices before the eighth century, and from almost all translations. Further, it is never cited by the Greek fathers, not even in the Arian controversy, nor by the Latin fathers, Hilary, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, and others. _If_ it is cited or presupposed in Tertullian, then it existed around 190. _If_ in Cyprian, then it was known around 220. If it occurred in the _Afra_, according to one manuscript from the fifth and one from the seventh century, then one can go back somewhat further. The _Afra_ arose around 160 in Africa and came to Italy around 250. The text certainly occurs in Vigilius, at the end of the fifth century. In the sixteenth century it was included in the Complutensian edition, Erasmus’s third edition, Stephanus, Beza, and the _textus receptus_. It is not required by the context. An explanation of its omission and disappearance is very difficult. Its authenticity is nevertheless still defended by a few, for example W. Koelling, _Die Echtheit von 1 Joh. 5:7_, Breslau, 1893.
