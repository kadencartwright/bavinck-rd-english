## 19.

The particular property of the second person in the Trinity is _filiatio_. In Scripture he bears various names that designate his relation to the Father, such as word, wisdom, Logos, son, firstborn, only-begotten, only son, image of God, εἰκων, ὑποστασις, χαρακτηρ. Upon these names and upon several texts cited above was built the doctrine of eternal generation, αἰωνιος γεννησις, first called such by Origen. Naturally, with this we speak in a human and therefore imperfect and defective manner, and the thought of this admonishes to modesty, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 28, 6; Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 36; Basil, _Adv. Eun._ II 22, 24; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat._ XX; etc. Yet we may speak this way. For just as by analogy mouth and ear and eye are ascribed to God, so human generation is an analogy and image of that divine act by which the Father gives the Son life in himself. But then everything imperfect and sensory must also be removed from it in thought. The generation of a human being is imperfect and defective; a man needs a woman in order to produce a son; a man can never communicate his full image, his whole being, in one child or even in many children; a father first becomes a father in time and also ceases to be one, and the child soon becomes entirely independent of and self-standing over against the father. But it is not so in God. There is generation also in the divine being. It is a beautiful thought, repeatedly recurring among the church fathers, that God is fruitful. He is no abstract, rigid unity, not μοναδικος, _solitarius_. He is a fullness of life; his nature is a γεννητικη, καρπογονος οὐσια; it is capable of expansion, unfolding, communication. Whoever denies this and denies all production in the divine being does not take seriously that God is infinite, all-blessed life. He retains nothing but an abstract, deistic conception of God or, by way of compensation, pantheistically includes the life of the world in the divine being. Without the Trinity, creation too cannot be understood. If God cannot communicate himself, then he is a dark light, a dry fountain, and how could he communicate himself _ad extra_, to creatures? Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 2; John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ I 8. Nevertheless, that generation must be conceived in a divine manner. Therefore, first, it is spiritual. The Arians raised against it especially the objection that all generation necessarily entails separation, division, τομη, διαιρεσις, and also παθος, ἀπορροια. That would be so if it were bodily, sensory, creaturely. But it is spiritual and divine, and therefore simple, without division or separation; it occurs ἀρρευστως και ἀδιαιρετως, Athanasius, _Expos. fidei_ 2. It brings _distinctio_ and _distributio_, but no _diversitas_ and _divisio_, into the divine being. Ὁ δε θεος ἀμερης ὠν ἀμεριστος ἐστι και ἀπαθης του υἱου πατηρ, οὐτε γαρ ἀπορροη του ἀσωματου ἐστιν, οὐτ’ ἐπιρροη τις εἰς αὐτον γινεται, ὡς ἐπ’ ἀνθρωπων, Athanasius, _De decr. nic. syn._ 11; _C. Ar._ I 16, 28 sq. Generation in God therefore has its most striking analogy in thinking and speaking; Scripture itself points to this when it calls the Son the Logos. Just as the human spirit objectifies itself in the word, so God communicates his whole being in the Logos. But here too there is again a distinction. A human being needs many words to express his thought; those words are sounds and thus sensory and material; they are without independence and have no subsistence in themselves. But when God speaks, then in the one Logos he expresses his whole being and gives him life in himself. Second, generation therefore entails that the Father generates the Son ἐκ της οὐσιας του πατρος, θεον ἐκ θεου, φως ἐκ φωτος, θεον ἀληθινον ἐκ θεου ἀληθινου, γεννηθεντα οὐ ποιηθεντα, ὁμοουσιον τῳ πατρι, as was established at Nicaea. The Arians taught that the Son was produced from nothing by the will of the Father. But this was not generation but creation. To create is το ἐξωθεν και οὐκ ἐκ της οὐσιας του κτιζοντος και ποιουντος γινεσθαι το κτιζομενον και ποιουμενον ἀνομοιον παντελως κατ’ οὐσιαν; but generation is το ἐκ της οὐσιας του γεννωντος προαγεσθαι το γεννωμενον ὁμοιον κατ’ οὐσιαν, John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ I 8; cf. Athanasius, _De decr. nic. syn._ 13, 26; _C. Ar._ I 5, 6. The Son is no creature but God to be praised into all eternity, Rom. 9:5. Therefore he was not produced by the will of the Father, from nothing and in time. Rather, he was generated from the being of the Father, in eternity. Generation is therefore not properly a work, not an ἐνεργεια of the Father, but a φυσις γεννητικη belongs to the Father. This naturally does not mean that generation is an unconscious, involuntary emanation, that it takes place apart from the Father’s will and power. It is not an act of a preceding, deciding will, of a βουλησις προηγουμενη, as is creation; but it belongs so naturally to the Father that his will as _voluntas concomitans_ has its entire good pleasure in it. It is revelation of his φυσεως γνησιοτης και οὐσιας ἰδιοτης, and thus also of his knowledge, will, and power and of all his virtues, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ III 59–67; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or._ III 6 sq.; Cyril of Alexandria, _De trin._ II; Hilary, _De trin._ III 4; Augustine, _De trin._ XV 20; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 6, 7; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 41, art. 2. Third, generation is therefore also confessed by the Christian church as eternal. The Arians said that the Son first did not exist, ἠν ποτε ὁτε οὐκ ἠν; appealed above all to the ἐκτισε, _creavit_, of Prov. 8:22, cf. above, p. 242; and pointed to the antinomy existing between the concepts αἰωνιος and γεννησις. But if Father and Son bear these names in a metaphysical sense, as Scripture incontrovertibly teaches, then generation is thereby also proven to be eternal. If the Son is not eternal, then neither is God eternally Father; he was then God before he was Father and only later became Father in time. The denial of eternal generation therefore detracts not only from the deity of the Son but also from that of the Father. It makes him mutable, deprives him of his divine nature, takes away from him the eternity of Fatherhood, and leaves unexplained how God can truly and rightly be called Father in time if the foundation for this does not lie eternally in his nature, Athanasius, _De decr. nic. syn._ 26 sq.; _De sent. Dionysii_ 14 sq.; _C. Ar._ I 12 sq.; Basil, _Adv. Eun._ II 14 sq.; _De Spir. S._ 14 sq.; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or. theol._ III 3 sq. But that generation must then also be understood in the true sense as eternal. It is not an act once concluded and accomplished in eternity, but an eternal, immutable, and thus at once eternally completed and eternally continuing act of God. Just as the sun radiates light and the fountain pours forth water, so generation belongs to the nature of the Father. The Father never was and never is without generation. He always generates. Οὐχι ἐγεννησεν ὁ πατηρ τον υἱον και ἀπελυσεν αὐτον ὁ πατηρ ἀπο της γενεσεως αὐτου, ἀλλ’ ἀει γεννᾳ αὐτον, Origen, _In Jerem._, hom. IX 4; _De princ._ I 2, 2. God’s generating is speaking, and his speaking is eternal, Θεου αιδιον το γεννημα, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ I 14, 20; IV 12; _De sent. Dion._ 15, 16; John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ I 8; Augustine, _De trin._ VI 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 42, art. 2; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ III, c. 4; Voetius, _Disp._ V 632; _Synopsis pur. theol._ VIII 11; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 207.
