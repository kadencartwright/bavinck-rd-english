# § 27. The Personal Names of God

## 1.

God’s revelation ascends still higher in the _nomina personalia_ than in the essential names, for they make known to us the distinctions that exist within the unity of his essence.
This revelation already begins in the Old Testament.
It does not yet occur there in the full form in which the church fathers and later theologians often, with a failure to appreciate the historical character of revelation, taught that it did; but neither is it correct that it is not yet to be found there at all, as was taught, after the Socinians and Remonstrants, by Semler, Herder, Doederlein, Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I⁴ 565 f.; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I² 90 f.; and others; cf. Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ II 687.
The Old Testament makes God’s trinitarian existence known only obscurely; it is the record of the developing doctrine of the Trinity, Petavius, _De trin._ II, c. 7; Lange, _Dogm._ II 124 f., 148 f.
Nevertheless, it contains, not only in a few isolated texts but especially in the organism of its revelation, elements of the highest significance for the doctrine of the Trinity.
First of all, the name Elohim merits consideration.
It was already noted earlier, p. 104, that this name in its _pluralis_ form is no proof of the Trinity.
It is nevertheless remarkable that among advocates of monotheism this name has never met with objection on account of its form.
This can be explained only by the fact that it contains no reminiscence of polytheism but designates the Deity in the fullness and richness of its life.
The God of revelation is no abstract unity but the living, true God who includes the highest diversity within the infinite fullness of his life.
This becomes evident immediately at creation.
Elohim creates by speaking and by sending out his Spirit.
The word that God speaks is no sound but a power so great that by it he creates and sustains the world; he speaks, and it is there, Gen. 1:3; Pss. 33:6, 9; 147:18; 148:8; Joel 2:11.
That word, spoken by God, proceeding from him and thus distinguished from him, is later hypostatized as wisdom in Job 28:23–27; Prov. 8:22 f.; cf. Prov. 3:19; Jer. 10:12; 51:15.
That wisdom is possessed, prepared, appointed, and searched out by God from eternity, as his nursling and master worker, through whom he creates and sustains all things.
But the work of creation and preservation comes about not only through God’s word and wisdom but also through his Spirit, Gen. 1:2; Pss. 33:6; 104:33; 139:7; Job 26:13; 27:3; 32:8; 33:4; Isa. 40:7, 13; 59:19.
While the word is the mediator through whom God calls all things into existence, it is his Spirit through whom he is immanent in all that is created and makes it all alive and adorns it.
Thus, already according to the teaching of the Old Testament, creation shows that all things owe their origin and continued existence to a threefold _causa_.
Elohim and cosmos do not stand dualistically alongside one another; rather, the world created by God has his Word as its objective principle and his Spirit as its subjective principle.
The world is first thought by God and therefore comes into being through his almighty speaking; and when it has received reality, it does not stand outside and over against him but continues to rest in his Spirit.
This threefold cause comes out still more clearly in the Old Testament in the domain of special revelation, in the work of re-creation.
Then it is no longer Elohim alone but YHWH who reveals himself and makes himself known as the God of the covenant and the oath, of revelation and history.
But even as such he does not reveal himself directly and immediately, Ex. 33:20.
It is once again through his Word that he makes himself known and saves and preserves his people, Ps. 107:20.
And the bearer of that word of the revelation of salvation is the _Malak YHWH_, the Messenger of the Covenant.
Not every occurrence of the expression Angel of God or Angel of the Lord in the Old Testament is to be understood as the _angelus increatus_, as Hengstenberg thought.
In 2 Sam. 24:16 f.; 1 Kings 19:5–7; 2 Kings 19:35; Dan. 3:25, 28; 6:23; 10:13, we must think of an ordinary angel, as also in Matt. 1:20, 28; Luke 1:11; 2:9; Acts 5:19; 8:26; 10:3; 12:7, 23; 27:23; Jude 9; Rev. 12:7.
There can be doubt about other passages, such as Num. 22:22 f.; Josh. 5:13, 14; Judg. 2:1–14; 6:11–24; 13:2–23.
But in the passages named in volume I, p. 250, the subject who speaks and acts in the Angel of the Lord far transcends a created angel.
The church fathers before Augustine unanimously saw in this Angel of the Lord a theophany of the Logos; cf. the citations in Trip, _Die Theophanien in den Geschichtsbüchern des A. T._, Leiden, 1858, S. 20–41.
Frequently, however, this conception was connected with the opinion that the Father is properly invisible, unapproachable, and ineffable, but that the Son can reveal himself and is the principle of all revelation, as in Justin Martyr, Theophilus, Irenaeus, and Tertullian.
But this separation and contrast between the Father and the Son was rightly contested by the later church fathers, Athanasius, the three Cappadocians, and others.
The Son was true God and therefore just as invisible as the Father.
Thus the conception of Augustine was prepared, who thought that God’s theophanies in the Old Testament were always mediated through created angels, _De trin._ III 11; _De civ._ XVI, c. 29.
The scholastic and Roman Catholic theologians generally adopted this exegesis of Augustine, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 51, artt. 2 and 3, ad 5; I 2, qu. 98, art. 3; _Sent._ II, dist. 8, qu. 1, art. 6; Petavius, _De trin._ VIII, c. 2; Scheeben, _Kath. Dogm._ I 784 f.; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 245; etc.
Luther and Calvin thought sometimes of a created angel and at other times of the uncreated Angel, Trip, 49–58; but later Protestant interpreters understood these passages mostly of the Logos, especially also in opposition to the Socinians, Remonstrants, and rationalists, who saw nothing but angelophanies in them.
While Hofmann, Baumgarten, and Delitzsch align themselves with the latter interpretation, the ancient interpretation was defended again by Stier, Hengstenberg, Keil, Kurtz, Ebrard, and others, and also Philippi, _Kirchl. Glaub._ II³ 191–196.
The difference between these two interpretations is not as great as it appears.
For the advocates of the ancient ecclesiastical conception must acknowledge that the Logos assumed a human form; and Augustine and his followers must concede that in that created angel the Logos revealed himself in an entirely special manner, Rivetus in Trip, 65.
In addition, the passages that speak of the Angel of the Lord cannot all be understood in the same sense.
This much, then, stands established: in the _Malak YHWH_ who bears that name preeminently, God, and specifically the Logos, was present in an entirely unique manner.
This is clearly evident from the fact that, although distinguished from YHWH, he is nevertheless also one with him in name, power, redemption and blessing, worship and honor, volume I, p. 250; Trip, op. cit., 100 f.
This exegesis is moreover commended by the whole of Old and New Testament Scripture, Job 33:23; Pss. 34:8; 35:5; Prov. 8:22 f.; 30:4; Isa. 9:5; Hos. 12:5, 6; Mic. 5:6; Zech. 1:8–14; 3:1 f.; 12:8; Mal. 3:1; John 8:56, 58; cf. John 1:1–5; 1 Cor. 10:4, 9; and Acts 7:30, 35, 38; Gal. 3:19; Heb. 2:2 are not in conflict with it.
And just as YHWH reveals himself objectively in re-creation through his word, in the _Malak YHWH_, so he does so subjectively in and through his Spirit.
The Spirit of God is the principle of all life and salvation, of all gifts and powers within the domain of revelation: of courage, Judg. 3:10; 6:34; 11:29; 13:25; 1 Sam. 11:6; of physical strength, Judg. 14:6; 15:14; of artistic skill, Ex. 28:3; 31:3–5; 35:31–35; 1 Chron. 28:12; of the ability to govern, Num. 11:17, 25; 1 Sam. 16:13; of understanding and wisdom, Job 32:8; Isa. 11:2; of holiness and renewal, Ps. 51:13; Isa. 63:10; cf. Gen. 6:3; Neh. 9:20; 1 Sam. 10:6, 9; of prophecy and prediction, Num. 11:25, 29; 24:2, 3; Mic. 3:8; etc.
In a special measure he will rest upon the Messiah, Isa. 11:2; 42:1; 61:1, but afterward will also be poured out upon all flesh, Joel 3:1, 2; Isa. 32:15; 44:3; Ezek. 36:26, 27; 39:29; Zech. 12:10, and give all a new heart and a new spirit, Ezek. 36:26, 27.
On the Holy Spirit in the Old Testament, see Kleinert, _Zur altt. Lehre vom Geiste Gottes_, _Jahrb. f. d. Th._, 1867, S. 3–60; Oehler, _Theol. des A. T._² 178 f., 214 f.; Smend, _Altt. Religionsgesch._ 459 f.; Warfield, “The Spirit of God in the Old Test.,” _Presb. and Ref. Rev._, Oct. 1895, pp. 665–687.
This threefold divine principle, which underlies creation and re-creation and sustains the entire economy of Old Testament revelation, is now also mentioned together a few times.
The threefold repetitions in Dan. 9:19; Zech. 1:3; Isa. 6:3; 33:22 do not qualify for this.
Only the high-priestly benediction, Num. 6:24–26, in the threefold character of its blessing points back to a threefold revelation of God and is thus the Old Testament prototype of the apostolic benediction, 2 Cor. 13:13.
The plural forms in Gen. 1:26, 27; 3:22; Isa. 6:8; etc., lack sufficient force because they can be explained in the same way as the _pluralis_ Elohim; cf. above, p. 104.
Passages such as Gen. 19:24; Ps. 45:8; 110:1; Hos. 1:7 are of greater significance because they point to a self-distinction within the divine essence.
And a threefold self-distinction within the divine essence is indicated most clearly in Ps. 33:6; Isa. 61:1; 63:9–12; Hag. 2:5, 6.
Many formerly also saw a revelation of the Trinity in the three men who appeared to Abraham, Gen. 18, for example, Witsius, _Oec. foed._ IV 3, §§3–8.
Others thought that one of the three was the Logos and the other two ordinary angels, for example, Calvin, _Kantt. Statenvert._; Moor I 807.
Much more plausible, however, is the exegesis of Augustine, _De civ._ XVI 29, according to whom the three men were three created angels in whom YHWH nevertheless revealed himself and was present in a special manner.
Literature on the Trinity in the Old Testament may be found in M. Vitringa I 213 sq., 218 sq.
