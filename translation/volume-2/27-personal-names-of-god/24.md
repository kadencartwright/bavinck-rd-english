## 24.

The doctrine of the Trinity so far transcends human understanding that from the beginning people devoted themselves to clarifying it by images or proving it by reasoning.
First of all, it was already remarkable that the number three had such a rich and profound significance in Scripture.
There we read of three parts of creation—heaven, earth, and what is under the earth; three groups of peoples according to the three sons of Noah; three dispensations of the covenant of grace—before, under, and after the law; three patriarchs; three parts of the tabernacle; three principal feasts; three parts of the Old Testament; three years of Jesus’ public ministry; three offices; three days from Jesus’ burial to the resurrection; three crosses on Golgotha; three languages in the inscription above Jesus’ cross; three beloved disciples; three witnesses, 1 John 5:8; three chief Christian virtues; three kinds of desire, 1 John 2:16; three woes, Rev. 8:13, and so forth; a threefold blessing; a threefold action in bowing and blessing; a three-day fast; three times of prayer each day, and so forth; Zöckler, _Theol. Nat._, 682 f., 696 f.; Bähr, _Symb. des mos. Cultus_, 2d ed., 1874; Herzog² 17, 410 f.
Yet the number three occupies such a large place not only in Scripture; outside it as well, this number is of great significance.
Analogies for the Christian doctrine of the Trinity were sought not only in the intermediary beings that gradually arose in Jewish theology, Weber, _Syst. der altsyn. pal. Theol._, 172 f., and in the three Sephiroth, כתר, חכמה, and בינה, of which the Kabbalah spoke, Ad. Franck, _La Kabbale_, 1843; Agrippa of Nettesheim in Stöckl, _Gesch. d. Philos. des M. A._ III 413, cf. II 236.
But traces and indications of the Trinity were also found in the Trimurti of the Indians—Brahma, Vishnu, and Ciwa; in the three forms of the Chinese Tao; in the three principal Germanic gods—Odin, Thor, and Loki; and in other Chaldean, Egyptian, and Greek representations of the gods.
With particular fondness, appeal was made especially to a statement of Hermes Trismegistus and to the three _principia_ that Plato adopted to explain the world: the supreme νους (being, the good), the world of ideas, and ὑλη; Pfanner, _Syst. theol. gentilis purioris_, Basel, 1679, cap. 3; Moor, _Comm. in Marck_ I 885–890; Tholuck, _Die specul. Trinitätslehre des späteren Orients_, 1826; Lange, _Chr. Dogmatik_ II 143 f.; Kahnis, _Luth. Dogm._ I² 352; Baur, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Dreieinigkeit_ I 10 f., 18 f., 33 f.; Delitzsch, _Apol._, 286; Zöckler, _Theol. Nat._, 689; cf. also Schelling, _Werke_ II 2, p. 78; II 3, p. 312 f.
But all these analogies are polytheistic and can therefore hardly be compared with the Christian doctrine of the Trinity.
Of greater value, then, are the physical analogies that have been sought in nature.
Following Philo, Justin Martyr employed the image of fire, which, in kindling another fire, nevertheless remains the same.
Tertullian said that God brought forth the Logos _sicut radix fruticem et fons fluvium et sol radium_, and he spoke of spring, flood, and stream, of root, trunk, and crown, and so forth.
These images constantly recurred among later church fathers and theologians and were expanded and multiplied.
The more deeply everything was considered, the more it appeared that every thing consisted in threes.
Space with its three dimensions; time with its three moments; nature with its three kingdoms; the world of matter, of spirit, and of the union of both in the human being; bodies in their solid, liquid, and gaseous states; the forces of attraction, repulsion, and the equilibrium of both; the three functions of the human soul, το λογικον, το θυμικον, and το ἐπιθυμητικον, or the three capacities, head, heart, and hand; the three constituents of the family, man, woman, and child—cf., however, Augustine, _De trin._ XII 5 sq.; the three estates in society, _Lehr-, Wehr-_ and _Nährstand_; the three ideal goods of the true, the good, and the beautiful; the triad in music, consisting of the root tone with its third and fifth; the rainbow and its many colors; the sun with its _vigor, splendor, calor_, its life-giving, illuminating, and warming power; the three primary colors, yellow, red, and blue, and so forth—all these have in earlier and later times been used as analogies of the Christian Trinity; Delitzsch, _Syst. d. christl. Apol._, 1870, p. 282 f.; Zöckler, _Theol. Naturalis_, 1860, p. 672 f.
The logical analogies that have been traced stand higher.
Augustine repeatedly points out that every thing must possess, first, a being, a unity, a measure—_esse, unitas, mensura, modus_; second, a certain form, _species_, in order to be something determinate and distinguished from other things; and finally, a certain relation and correspondence, _ordo_, between that universal and this particular.
Matter, form or beauty, and the harmony between the two or love are the fundamental forms and moments of all being; _De trin._ VI 10; _De vera relig._ c. 7; _De vita beata_ 34; cf. Gangauf, _Des h. Augustinus specul. Lehre v. Gott d. Dreieinigen_, 1883, p. 209 f.
_Omne quod est, aliud est quo constat, aliud quo discernitur, aliud quo congruit_, _83 quaest._ qu. 18.
Medieval theology elaborated this in all sorts of ways and sought everywhere to trace a _trias_.
It found an analogy of the Trinity in the _trivium_ of _grammatica, dialectica, rhetorica_; in the three philosophical disciplines, _logica, physica, ethica_; in the three grammatical persons; in the _activum, passivum_, and _medium_; in the _singularis, dualis_, and _pluralis_; in the three basic vowels and in the _radices trilitterales_ of the Hebrew language; in the _dispositio, elocutio_, and _actio_ of rhetoric; in the _definitio, divisio_, and _argumentatio_ of dialectic; in the three forms of poetry—epic, lyric, and drama; in the three stages of mysticism—_cogitatio, meditatio, comtemplatio_, or _fides, ratio, contemplatio_, or _via purgativa, illuminativa, unitiva_, and so forth.
Dionysius the Areopagite arranged his celestial hierarchy, and Dante his _Divina Comoedia_, according to the triad.
In modern philosophy, threeness attained formal dominion.
According to Hegel, _Werke_ II 37, Kant rediscovered triplicity as though by instinct and used it to schematize the organs of knowledge, the capacities of the soul, the categories, the ideas of reason, and so forth.
But only in the idealistic philosophy of Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel, derived from Kant, did this triplicity become a dialectical method.
Idealism seeks to comprehend things as a product of consciousness, as an unfolding of the idea.
That idea must therefore be conceived as living, moving, and productive.
This is possible only if it always exists in a contradiction between what it is and what it does, and then resolves and reconciles this contradiction in a third.
The law of contradiction is the essence of spirit.
Thus, in all its unfoldings and developments, the idea advances through the moments of position, negation, limitation; thesis, antithesis, synthesis; _an-sich, für-sich, an-und-für-sich_.
The whole world develops in the “_Schema der Dreieinigkeiten_.”
Logic, with its doctrine of being, essence, and the concept, considers the _Geist an sich_; the philosophy of nature, in the three forms of mechanics, physics, and organics, deals with the _Geist für sich_, spirit in its otherness, in its self-alienation; the philosophy of spirit, with its subjective, objective, and absolute spirit, treats the _Geist an und für sich_, spirit in its return to itself, in its becoming conscious of itself; Windelband, _Gesch. der Philos._, 1892, p. 464 f., 481 f.; Rümelin, _Reden und Aufsätze_, 1888, p. 47 f.
Through the influence of this philosophy, triplicity was made the foundation of numerous philosophical and theological systems.
Yet it was not enough that analogies for the Trinity were traced in all sorts of ways; attempts were also made to prove it positively and to deduce it as necessary either from the nature of thought or from that of love.
The doctrine of the Logos, both in Scripture and in Greek philosophy, naturally and involuntarily led people to see in human thinking and speaking an image of the trinitarian process in the divine being.
Justin Martyr, _C. Tr._ c. 61; Tatian, _Or. c. Gr._ c. 5; Tertullian, _C. Prax._ c. 5 sq.; Lactantius, _Inst._ IV 29, and so forth, already employ this comparison.
Athanasius and the Cappadocians repeatedly presented generation as God’s knowledge of himself in his image, as an eternal speaking of a word; Father and Son were related as νους and λογος, e.g., Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 35 sq.; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or. theol._ IV 20; and especially Gregory of Nyssa, _Or. catech._ c. 1–3.
But Augustine in particular found clear traces of the Trinity in the inner, rational human being.
And he found them in different ways and in different directions.
First, he found them in the threeness of _esse, nosse_, and _velle_; _essentia, scientia, amor_; _mens, notitia, amor_; _Conf._ XIII 11; _De civ._ XI 26–28; _De trin._ IX 4, 5.
Next, he discovered traces of it in the capacities of the soul, specifically in sensory perception, which comes about through an object, _ipsa res_, an image of it in the eye, _visio_, and the _intentio animi_, which directs the sense toward the object, _De trin._ XI c. 1, 2; and this _trinitas_ remains even when the object disappears, for then an image of it is preserved in the _memoria_, external vision gives way to the _interna visio_, and the will remains that which unites the two, ib. c. 4 sq.
Augustine, however, finds the greatest likeness of the Trinity in the human spirit’s self-knowledge, _De trin._ IX 4 sq.; X 9 sq.; XIV 6 sq.; XV 6 sq.
He usually calls the _trinitas_ he finds here _mens, notitia, amor_ (_dilectio_), or _memoria, intelligentia, voluntas_.
First the spirit is _memoria_, that is, consciousness both of other things and of itself; there is, namely, a consciousness, a _scientia_, that is not yet knowledge properly so called; in the spirit as _memoria_ many _notitiae_ lie hidden, including knowledge of itself; someone can know something even if at the moment he is not thinking about it, _De trin._ XIV c. 6, 7.
But from that spirit as _memoria_, from the _scientia_ preserved in the _memoria_, knowledge, _intelligentia_, is now brought forth by _cogitatio_, by thinking.
In that _intelligentia_ the spirit forms an adequate image of itself, comprehends and knows and beholds itself.
_Mens igitur quando cogitatione se conspicit, intelligit se et recognoscit._
And this self-knowledge and self-contemplation is a bringing forth: _gignit ergo intellectum hunc et cognitionem suam_.
And these two are now united by will or love.
_Haec autem duo gignens et genitum dilectione tertia copulantur, quae nihil est aliud quam voluntas fruendum aliquid appetens vel tenens_, _De trin._ XIV 6.
Thus Augustine is deeply convinced that all creatures, as works of the triune God, also display _vestigia_ or _indicia trinitatis_ to a greater or lesser degree, _De trin._ VI 10; XV 2.
Above all, he seeks an _imago trinitatis_ in the human being, who was created in the image of the triune God, ib.; _De civ._ XI 26.
For Augustine, the whole creation was a mirror of God.
Accordingly, in all sorts of ways he also attempts to show the correspondence between the _trinitas_ he discovers in creatures, especially in the human being, and the _trinitas_ in the divine being.
In both, the _trias_ consists in this: all three are one and equal; each of the three is in the other two, and these in turn are in the one, and thus they are _omnia in omnibus_, _De trin._ IX 5; X 11.
But in doing so he does not conceal from himself that all these comparisons are only analogies and images and that, alongside the correspondence, a very great difference also exists.
Thus the _trinitas_ in the human being is not the human being himself but something in or belonging to the human being, whereas in God the Trinity is God himself and the three persons are the one God.
The _memoria, intelligentia_, and _amor_ are only powers in the human being, but the three persons are three subjects in the divine being.
In the human being these three powers are often unequal and serve to supplement one another, but in the divine being there is perfect unity and equality of the persons, _De trin._ XV 7, 17, 20 sq.
Augustine therefore did not intend to prove the Trinity _a priori_ with these analogies and images; he proceeded from faith and accepted it on the ground of God’s Word; he only attempted to point it out _a posteriori_ throughout nature and to elucidate it by thought, _De vera relig._ 7; _De trin._ I 1; V 1; IX 1.
The first seven books of his work _De trinitate_ are therefore devoted chiefly to proofs from Scripture; only in the final eight books does he seek to confirm it from nature and the human being.
And finally he added that, although this _trinitas_ in the human spirit could be observed by everyone, it could nevertheless be recognized as an image of God’s triune being only by the believer.
It is indeed essentially proper to the human spirit and was not eradicated by sin; yet it has been obscured and is renewed by faith, when the human being again learns to _meminisse, intelligere et amare_ God.
And we become his image perfectly only when we shall see him face to face, _De trin._ XIV 12 sq.
_Ibi esse nostrum non habebit mortem, ibi nosse nostrum non habebit errorem, ibi amare nostrum non habebit offensionem_, _De civ._ XI 28.
This argument for the Trinity from thought was adopted by many, e.g., Erigena, _De div. nat._ II 32; Anselm, _Monol._ c. 29–67; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 3, 6–23; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 45 art. 7; _S. c. Gent._ IV 26; _Sent._ I dist. 3 qu. 2 art. 3; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ II c. 12; _Itiner. mentis_ c. 2–4; _Sent._ I dist. 3 art. 1 and 2; Duns Scotus, _Sent._ I dist. 3 qu. 9; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacr._ I pars 3 c. 21 sq.; Luther, in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ I² 99 f.; Melanchthon, _Loci Comm._ loc. 3; _Enarratio symb. Nic._, _Corpus Ref._ XXIII col. 235; _Explicatio Symb. Nic._, ib. col. 359 sq.; _Examen Ordinandorum_, ib. col. 3; Jak. Schegk, _Contra Antitrinitarios_, in Trechsel, _Die protest. Antitrin._ II 380 f.; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, p. 202; Zanchi, _Op._ I col. 356 sq.; Keckermann, _Syst. Theol._, 1603, p. 20 sq.; Poiret, _Cogitationum rationalium de Deo, anima et malo libri quatuor_, 1677, in Baur, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Dreieinigkeit_ III 315 f.; Mornaeus, _De verit. relig. christ._ c. 5; Leibniz, _Syst. Theol._, Mainz, 1825, p. 30; Lessing, _Erziehung des Mensch._ § 73; Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, p. 315; Twesten, _Dogm._ II 194–216; Lange, _Dogm._ II 141; Bilderdijk, _Opst. v. godg. en zedek. inhoud_ I 24v.; Kuyper, _De Schrift, het woord Gods_, 1870; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 183, and so forth.
But in connection with this argument for the Trinity from thought, Augustine employs yet another, namely, from love.
He proceeds from the statement of Scripture, “God is love,” and shows that there is always a threeness in love, _amans et quod amatur et amor_.
In love there is always a subject, an object, and a bond between the two.
_Immo vero vides trinitatem, si caritatem vides_, _De trin._ VIII 8; IX 1, 2.
This speculation too was followed by many, especially by Richard of St. Victor, _De trinitate_ III c. 2 sq.
The fullness of divine love, like that of divine goodness, blessedness, and glory, demands a plurality of persons in the divine being, for love desires an object, indeed one that is equal to the one who loves.
But this love is perfect only when both—the one who loves and the one who is loved—include a third in the love of them both and are loved by him in return.
And likewise we find this reasoning in Bonaventure, _Sent._ I dist. 2 art. 1 qu. 2, and in more recent times in J. Müller, _Die christl. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ II⁵ 181 f.; Sartorius, _Lehre v. d. heiligen Liebe_ I³, p. 11 f.; Liebner, _Christ. Dogm._ I 69 f.; Schöberlein, _Grundlehren des Heils_, 1848, p. 22 f.; Peip, art. “Trinität,” Herzog² 16, 465 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Glaub._ I 415 f.; _Ges. Schriften_, 345 f., and so forth.
Alongside these more general speculations from God’s understanding and will, still other constructions of the Trinity have been attempted.
The most remarkable is that of theosophy, which, under the influence of Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, and the Kabbalah, appeared again shortly before the Reformation in Pico della Mirandola, Reuchlin, Nettesheim, and Paracelsus; then found its true philosopher in Jakob Böhme; and was again advocated in this century by Schelling and Baader.
Schelling proceeds from the fact that actual being cannot be explained from pure thought.
The what of things may be deduced from reason; the that of things cannot possibly be explained from it.
The essence and the existence of things point back to different _principia_, _Philos. der Offenb._, _Werke_ II 3, p. 57 f.
Both deism and pantheism must therefore be rejected, II 2, p. 68 f.
God must not be conceived in Eleatic fashion as an abstract unity.
He is an _All-einheit_, a _Mehrheit_, which joins the unity of deism and the allness of pantheism to one another, ib. 73.
Already in the concept of God three moments must be distinguished: first, that of being-able, the subject, the will; next, that of pure-being, the object, the idea; and finally, the identity of the two, the subject-object, II 2, p. 24 f.; II 3, p. 205 f.
In these three the concept of the absolute is complete; thereby it is spirit, perfect spirit, II 3, p. 238 f.; personal and self-conscious, II 2, p. 73 f.; an _Einzelwesen_, II 3, p. 174.
But although in our thinking we arrive at perfect Spirit only by passing through these three, it has not therefore arisen from them.
On the contrary, Spirit is first and bears these three moments within itself as _immanente Bestimmungen_, II 3, p. 240 f.
The perfect, absolute Spirit is thus the _an sich, für sich_ and _bei sich seiende Geist_, ib. 251 f.
But within that Spirit there also lies hidden what shall be.
It is free Spirit and can reveal itself outside itself, ib. 261.
The three _Bestimmungen_ that Spirit has in itself become the _Potenzen_ of an extra-divine being, ib. 272.
Schelling derives the whole of creation, mythology, and revelation from the separation and union of the three _Potenzen_; they are the forms of all possibilities, the _principia_, the ἀρχαι, of all being, II 2, pp. 61, 112; II 3, p. 267 f.
This cosmogony, however, is at the same time a theogony in Schelling.
In the development of the world, the _Potenzen_ in God rise to an ever higher stage; in God’s revelation to his creatures, he at the same time becomes manifest to himself.
The three _Bestimmungen_ in the absolute spirit, the three _Potenzen_ in the creation of the world and in mythology, prepare the way for conceiving the one and same Godhead as three persons, II 3, p. 316.
The absolute Spirit becomes Father, Son, and Spirit in the historical course of revelation.
God, namely, not a particular form but the whole God, the absolute personality, can be called Father, not only as _Urheber_ of all things, but also insofar as through the first _Potenz_ of his being he compels the second to realize itself, II 3, pp. 311, 322; but he is not actually Father at the beginning and during the progress of the process, but only at the end, ib. 335 f., 339.
The second form is the Son, whose generation is not eternal but refers to the Son’s being outside the Father and therefore begins at the moment of creation; accordingly, the Son is fully Son only at the end of the process, ib. 312, 318, 321 f., 330 f.
And the same applies to the third _Potenz_, 333 f.
At the end, the whole Godhead has been realized in three persons distinct from one another, who are neither three distinct Gods nor merely three distinct names, 335, 337.
Through this speculation modern philosophy restored the trinitarian dogma to honor.
There was no lack of attempts to construct the Trinity by thought, especially from the essence of spirit, self-consciousness, and personality: Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I 444 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ I 405 f.; J. H. Fichte, Deutinger, Rosenkrantz, and others; cf. Drews, _Die deutsche Spekul. seit Kant_ I 285–531.
Günther even went so far that, like Raymundus de Sabunde, Raymundus Lullus, and some other rationalists, he rejected the distinction between _articuli puri_ and _mixti_, counted the Trinity also among the truths of reason, and considered it demonstrable from the nature of self-consciousness; Drews I 447 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I² 399–451.
