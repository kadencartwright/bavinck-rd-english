## 23.

These immanent relations of the three persons in the divine being also appear outwardly in their revelations and works. All _opera ad extra_ are indeed commonly proper to the three persons. _Opera Deitatis ad extra sunt indivisa, servato ordine et discrimine personarum._ It is always the one and same God who acts in creation and re-creation. But within that unity the order of the three persons is preserved. The ontological Trinity is also reflected in the economic. Therefore particular attributes and works are ascribed to each of the three persons, not to the exclusion of the other two persons, as Abelard thought, _Introd. ad theol._ I, c. 7–14, but in such a way that in them the order existing between the persons in the ontological Trinity comes to expression. These attributes are therefore not _propriae_ but _appropriatae_ to them, and Holy Scripture leads the way in this, above, pp. 238, 240. Appealing to Matt. 28:19 and 1 Cor. 8:6, Hilary says that the Father is the _auctor_ from whom all things are; the Son, the Only-Begotten, through whom all things are; and the Holy Spirit a gift in all. There is therefore one power, one son, one gift. Nothing is lacking in this perfection. In the Father is the _aeternitas_, in the Son the _species in imagine_, in the Spirit the _usus in munere_, _De trin._ II 1. Hilary assigns these attributes to the three persons because the Father is the ἀρχη and without _principium_; the Son the image of the Father, who reveals him in his glory; and the Holy Spirit a gift of Father and Son who makes us partakers of fellowship with God. Augustine raised some objection to this, _De trin._ VI 10, and indicated the distinction differently: _in Patre unitas, in Filio aequalitas, in Spiritu Sancto unitatis aequalitatisque concordia; et tria haec unum omnia propter Patrem, aequalia omnia propter Filium, connexa omnia propter Spiritum Sanctum_, _De doctr. christ._ I 5. In his work _De Trinitate_ he developed this distinction more broadly. Here he ascribes power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, and goodness or love to the Holy Spirit. This may not, however, be understood as though the Father first became wise and good through the wisdom of the Son and the goodness of the Holy Spirit. For Father, Son, and Spirit all partake of the same divine nature and the same divine attributes. Yet it is permissible to assign the named attributes to the three persons in an economic sense, _De trin._ VII 1 sq.; XV 7. Later theologians adopted this distinction, Erigena, _De div. nat._ I 13; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 39, arts. 7, 8; Bonaventure, _Brev._ I, c. 6; _Sent._ I, dist. 31, art. 1, qu. 3, and art. 2, qu. 3; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacr._ I, pars 3, c. 27.

An economic distinction in the _opera ad extra_ also corresponds to this. They are indeed all works of the being, but each of the three persons nevertheless occupies in them the place corresponding to the order of his existence in the divine being. The Father works from himself through the Son in the Spirit. Holy Scripture clearly indicates this distinction in the so-called _praepositiones distinctionales_ ἐκ, δια, and ἐν, 1 Cor. 8:6; John 1:3, 14. The text Rom. 11:36, frequently cited for this, cannot be divided in a Trinitarian manner, and Col. 1:16 conflicts only apparently with this use of the prepositions. The distinction that Scripture makes between the three persons was perceived early and emphatically pointed out, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ V 18. Athanasius appeals repeatedly to Eph. 4:6 and says that God as Father is above all, as Son through all, and as Spirit in all, and that the Father creates and re-creates all things through the Son in the Spirit, _Ep. ad Serap._ I 14, 28; II 6, 7. Basil was accused because in his prayer he sometimes thanked the Father μετα του υἱου συν τῳ πνευματι τῳ ἁγιῳ, and at other times δια του υἱου ἐν τῳ ἁγιῳ πνευματι. In his writing _De Spiritu Sancto_ he defends the first expression because Son and Spirit are of one being with the Father and must therefore receive the same honor, and he speaks extensively about the distinction of the prepositions, _De Sp. S._ 3 sq. Against the Arians he argues that the unequal prepositions do not prove inequality of the persons but designate a particular order in their existence and activity. The Father is the αἰτια προκαταρκτικη, the Son the αἰτια δημιουργικη, the Spirit the αἰτια τελειωτικη, _De Spir. S._, nos. 21, 22, 38. This same distinction returns in all later theologians; cf. also Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, p. 341 f. All God’s works outwardly have one _principium_, namely God, but they come about through the cooperation of the three persons, who occupy a place of their own and fulfill a task of their own both in the works of creation and in those of redemption and sanctification. Everything proceeds from the Father, is accomplished through the Son, and is completed in the Holy Spirit. Indeed, to a certain extent the outward works are also divided among the three persons. Gregory of Nazianzus, as was already said above, explained the great difference of opinion concerning the Holy Spirit in his time from the fact that the Spirit first clearly made himself known through his indwelling in the church, _Orat. theol._ V 26. Pantheism in its various forms has greatly abused the truth expressed by Gregory. Repeatedly, from Montanus to Hegel, the opinion arose that the three persons represented three successive periods in the history of the church. Thereby the economic Trinity was detached from its metaphysical foundation, the being of God drawn down into the stream of becoming, and cosmogony transformed into theogony. It was precisely the struggle of the fathers to banish this pagan, pantheistic element from Christian theology, to detach God as the One Who Is from the evolution of becoming, and thus to understand the Trinity as an eternal movement of life in the divine being itself. Nor does Gregory of Nazianzus teach in the words named above that the deity of the Son and the Holy Spirit first came into being later; he attempts to prove both as decisively as possible from Scripture. But God did indeed take account in his self-revelation of the capacity of human beings. It was dangerous, Gregory says at the place cited, to teach the deity of the Son so long as the deity of the Father was not yet acknowledged, and, as it were, to impose upon us the deity of the Holy Spirit so long as that of the Son was not yet confessed. He did not wish to overload us with food or at once blind our eyes with the full light of the sun. All outward works—creation, preservation, government, incarnation, satisfaction, renewal, sanctification, and so forth—are works of the entire Trinity. Nevertheless, in an economic sense creation is ascribed more particularly to the Father, redemption to the Son, sanctification to the Holy Spirit. Just as in the ontological Trinity the Father is first in order, the Son second, and the Holy Spirit third, so also in the history of revelation the Father preceded the Son, and the Son in turn preceded the Holy Spirit. The economy of the Father was especially that of the Old Testament, Heb. 1:1; the economy of the Son began with the incarnation; and the economy of the Holy Spirit commenced with the day of Pentecost, John 7:39; 14:15. The Father comes from himself, but the Son does not come except as sent by the Father, Matt. 10:40; Mark 9:37; Luke 9:48; John 3:16; 5:23, 30, 37; 6:38 f.; etc., and in turn the Holy Spirit does not come except as sent by both, John 14:26; 16:7; Augustine, _Tract._ VI _in Joann._; _De trin._ II 5; IV 20; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 14–17; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 43; Petavius, _De trin._ VIII, c. 1. But this mission in time is a reflection of the immanent relation of the three persons in the being of God and has its foundation in generation and spiration. The incarnation of the Word has its eternal archetype in the generation of the Son, and the outpouring of the Holy Spirit is a weak analogy of the procession from Father and Son. Therefore the church fathers inferred from the relations that appeared between the three persons in time before human eyes their eternal immanent relations. And this was entirely correct. For, says Augustine, the Son cannot be called sent because he became flesh, but he is and is called sent in order that he would become flesh. No word was spoken by the Father in time that the eternal Son would be sent. _Sed utique in ipso Dei verbo quod erat in principio apud Deum et Deus erat, in ipsa scilicet sapientia Dei sine tempore erat, quo tempore illam in carne apparere oporteret. Itaque cum sine ullo initio temporis in principio esset verbum, et verbum esset apud Deum et Deus esset verbum; sine ullo tempore in ipso verbo erat, quo tempore verbum caro fieret et habitaret in nobis. Quae plenitudo temporis cum venisset, misit Deus filium suum, factum ex muliere, id est, factum in tempore, ut incarnatum verbum hominibus appareret, quod in ipso verbo sine tempore erat, in quo tempore fieret. Ordo quippe temporum in aeterna Dei sapientia sine tempore est. Cum itaque hoc a patre et filio factum esset, ut in carne filius appareret, congruenter dictus est missus ille qui in ea carne apparuit, misisse autem ille qui in ea non apparuit. Quoniam illa quae coram corporeis oculis foris geruntur, ab interiore apparatu naturae spiritalis existunt; propterea convenienter missa dicuntur._ And the same applies to the mission of the Holy Spirit. _Facta est enim quaedam creaturae species ex tempore in qua visibiliter ostenderetur spiritus sanctus. . . . Haec operatio visibiliter expressa et oculis oblata mortalibus missio Spiritus Sancti dicta est, non ut ita appareret ejus ipsa substantia, qua et ipse invisibilis et incommutabilis est sicut pater et filius, sed ut exterioribus visis hominum corda commota a temporali manifestatione venientis ad occultam aeternitatem semper praesentis converterentur_, _De trin._ II, c. 5; cf. Petavius, _De trin._ VIII, c. 1, §§7–12. The Holy Spirit was already gift before he was granted to anyone. _Quia sic procedebat ut esset donabile, jam donum erat et antequam esset cui daretur. Aliter enim intelligitur cum dicitur donum, aliter cum dicitur donatum. Nam donum potest esse et antequam detur, donatum autem nisi datum fuerit, nullo modo dici potest_, ibid., V, c. 15. The mission in time therefore stands in the closest connection with the eternal procession in the divine being. And while Son and Spirit appeared under visible form in incarnation and outpouring, their mission is completed in their invisible coming into the hearts of all believers, into the church of the Son, into the temple of the Holy Spirit. The Son and the Spirit eternally proceeded from the Father so that he himself through and in them would come to his people and God would finally be all in all.
