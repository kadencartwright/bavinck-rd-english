## 9.

The church, however, did not follow Origen.
It rejected his subordinationism and at Nicaea confessed the true, full deity of the Son.
This confession bore an entirely religious character.
It maintained the soteriological principle of Christianity.
But from that point onward the significance of the doctrine of the Trinity changed.
Nicaea expressed the distinction in God and taught that Father, Son (and Spirit) were God; what mattered from then on was to maintain the unity within this distinction.
Before Nicaea there was difficulty in moving from the unity of God to a triad; now it is the reverse.
From this point on the Trinitarian dogma acquires a value of its own, an independent value, a theological significance.
It is Athanasius, with the three Cappadocians and Augustine, who elaborate and complete the dogma in this way.
Athanasius understood better than anyone in his time that Christianity stood or fell with the deity of Christ and the Trinity.
He devoted his whole life and all his strength to the defense of this truth.
He fought not for a philosophical problem but for the Christian religion itself, for the revelation of God, the teaching of the apostles, the faith of the church.
The Trinity is the heart of Christianity.
Through it Christianity is distinguished in principle from Judaism, which denies the distinction in God, and from paganism, which denies the unity of God, Athanasius, _Ad Serap._ I 28.
In Athanasius the philosophical mingling of ontology and cosmology is therefore completely banished.
He rejects the Gnostic dualism between God and the world that was adopted by Arius, and every kind of intermediate being, _C. Ar._ II 26.
The Trinity, he says, has nothing foreign mingled with it; it does not consist of the Creator and something that has come into being, but it is wholly and perfectly divine, ibid.
Therefore the Trinity is also eternal.
There is nothing accidental in God; he does not become anything; he is everything eternally.
As the Trinity always was, so it is and remains, and in it the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, _Ad Serap._ III 7; _C. Ar._ I 18.
The Father was always Father; unlike with us, it belongs to his nature to be Father, _De decr. Nic. syn._ 12.
Just as the sun cannot be conceived without light and the fountain without water, so the Father cannot be conceived without the Son; God is not ἀγονος; he always speaks, _C. Ar._ II 2; _Ad Serap._ II 2.
Whoever denies the Trinity makes God into a lifeless principle or arrives at the doctrine of the eternity of the world, _C. Ar._ I 14.
And therefore the generation and existence of the Son are also eternal.
There was no time, either for the Father or for the Son, when he was not, _C. Ar._ I throughout.
This Son cannot be a creature and was not produced by God’s will but was generated from his essence, _C. Ar._ I 25.
And the same is said of the Holy Spirit, though less frequently and extensively, _Ad Serap._ I 20, 21, and so forth.
These three persons are truly distinct; they are not three parts of one whole, nor three names for one thing.
The Father alone is Father, the Son alone is Son, and the Spirit alone is Spirit, _C. Ar._ III 4; IV 1; _Ad Serap._ IV 4, 6, 7.
But at the same time he maintains the unity in such a way that all three are ὁμοουσιοι and have one ὑποστασις (in Athanasius still equivalent to οὐσια) and the same attributes, _C. Ar._ III 3, 4; _De decr. Nic. syn._ 19-25; that the Father is the ἀρχη and the πηγη θεοτητος, _C. Ar._ IV 1; that the three persons exist in one another, _Ad Serap._ I 14; III 6; _C. Ar._ III 6, and are one in their operation, _Ad Serap._ I 28.
Cf. Fr. Lauchert, _Die Lehre des h. Athan. d. Gr._, Leipzig, 1895, S. 10-65; Schwane, _Dogmengesch._ II² 83-108.
This doctrine of the Trinity in Athanasius is also found in its essentials, only further clarified by names, images, and comparisons, in Basil’s _Libri V_ against Eunomius, in his treatise _De Spiritu Sancto_, and in many of his letters and homilies; in Gregory of Nyssa’s _Libri XII c. Eunomium_ and his _Oratio catechetica_; and in Gregory of Nazianzus’s _5 Orationes theologicae_.
John of Damascus summarizes the result and follows Gregory of Nazianzus especially, _De fide orthod._ I c. 8.
The entire Greek church accepted this doctrine at the councils, of which it recognizes the first seven, and differs from the West only on the _filioque_.
