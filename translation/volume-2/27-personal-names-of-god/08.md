## 8.

In all these moments of revelation Holy Scripture naturally does not yet offer us a developed dogma of the Trinity.
But it nevertheless teaches that the one name of God first unfolds fully in that of Father, Son, and Spirit.
It declares clearly and plainly that all God’s works toward the outside, both in creation and re-creation, have a threefold divine _causa_.
It leaves no doubt that this threefold _causa_ consists of three distinct subjects who stand in personal relation to one another.
And it thereby contains all the data from which theology constructed the dogma of the Trinity.
Philosophy had nothing essential to add to it; even the doctrine of the Logos is New Testament teaching.
Everything only awaited the time when the _ratio christiana_ would be sufficiently developed to think through the holy mystery that lay here before it.
There is not yet any question of this among the apostolic fathers.
They repeat Holy Scripture without yet understanding the profound meaning and mutual connection of the truths, and they use expressions that could no longer be defended in later times.
Nevertheless, they too are of the highest importance for the dogma of the Trinity insofar as they contest both the Ebionite and the Docetic tendencies and express in stronger or weaker terms the high nature of Christ, exalted above the angels.
The dogma of the Trinity, as becomes evident already at the beginning, was born not from a philosophical reasoning about the essence of God but from reflection upon the facts of revelation, upon the person and work of Christ.
In the dogma of the Trinity the concern from the beginning was the deity of Christ, the absolute character of Christianity, the truth of God’s revelation, the true atonement for sins, and the absolute certainty of salvation.
Among the apostolic fathers Christ now occupies an entirely unique place; predicates are attributed to him that belong to no creature.
He is called Son, God’s own, only, only-begotten Son, Clement, _1 Cor._ 36; Ignatius, _Rom._ 1; _Eph._ 20; _Smyrn._ 1; _Diogn._ 9, 10; _Barn._ 7, 12; radiance of God’s majesty, scepter of his majesty, Clement, _1 Cor._ 16, 36; Lord of the earth to whom all is subjected, Creator of all things, Judge of the living and the dead, _Barn._ 7, 12; _Diogn._ 7; _Did. Apost._ 16; Polycarp, _Phil._ 1, 2, 6, 12; holy, incomprehensible Logos, who was sent to earth as a God, ὡς θεος, _Diogn._ 7, and may be called θεος, Clement, _2 Cor._ 1; Ignatius, _Rom._ 3; _Smyrn._ 1, 10; _Eph._ 1, 18, 19.
And Father (God), Son (Christ), and Spirit are named together, Clement, _1 Cor._ 46; Ignatius, _Eph._ 9; _Magn._ 13.
There is little mention of the Holy Spirit among the apostolic fathers, but he is nevertheless distinguished from the Father and the Son and placed alongside them; only with respect to the _Pastor_ of Hermas, _Sim._ V 5, 6, is there disagreement whether he identifies the Holy Spirit with the Son or distinguishes him from the Son, Baur, _Dreiein. u. Menschw._ I 134, and against him Dorner, _Entwicklungsgesch._ I 191–205.
When Gnosis appears in the second century, Christian thought too awakens.
The deity of Christ acquires dogmatic significance and is therefore also expressed much more clearly.
Justin Martyr repeatedly gives Christ the name God, even ὁ θεος, _C. Tryph._ 34, 56, 58, 113, 126; etc., and attributes to him all kinds of exalted predicates.
He is the firstborn of creation, the beginning of another race, equipped not with a single _charisma_ but with all the powers of the Spirit, possessing not a σπερμα of the Logos but the whole Logos, λογικου το ὁλον, powerful to deify the human race and therefore himself God, _C. Tr._ 87, 138; _Apol._ II 10, 12.
Further, he clearly teaches Christ’s preexistence, not merely as power but as person, _C. Tr._ 128.
Because the Father is hidden, ineffable, and exalted above time and space, _C. Tr._ 127; _Apol._ II 6, all revelations under the Old Testament and also in the Gentile world are revelations of the Logos, _C. Tr._ 127; _Apol._ I 46, 61, 63; _Apol._ II 10, 13.
He even existed at creation; the word in Gen. 1:26 was spoken to him, _C. Tr._ 62.
But the immanent relation between Father and Son is not yet clear in Justin.
It appears that the Logos, who is indeed another than the Father ἀριθμῳ ἀλλ’ οὐ γνωμῃ, was first generated by the Father before and for the sake of creation, and that he did not arise through division but was nevertheless brought forth δοναμει και βουλῃ of the Father, as one light is kindled from another and as the word proceeds from our mouth, _Apol._ II 6; _C. Tr._ 61, 100, 128.
Therefore he is called πρωτογονος, πρωτοτοκος του θεου, _Apol._ I 46, 58.
Generation is called προβαλλειν but usually γενναν, _C. Tr._ 62, 76, 129; _Apol._ I 23, 2, 6, and the Logos is to that extent called a γεννημα or ἐργασια, _C. Tr._ 62, 114, 129.
Justin seeks to maintain God’s unity by saying that the Son is indeed another than the Father ἀριθμῳ ἀλλ’ ού γνωμη, _C. Tr._ 56, and is subordinate to the Father.
The Son is ἡ πρωτη δυναμις μετα τον πατερα, _Apol._ I 32; he occupies the second place, δευτερα χωρα, _Apol._ I 13; he received everything from the Father, _C. Tr._ 86; is God and Lord because the Father willed it, _C. Tr._ 127; and is ὑπο τῳ πατρι και κυριῳ τεταγμενος, _C. Tr._ 126.
Thus Justin still has an inadequate conception on various points.
The hidden essence of the Father in contrast to the Son, the generation of the Son by the will of the Father and for the sake of creation, and the subordination of the Son to the Father were later rejected by the church.
Some have therefore called Justin an Arian, but wrongly.
For, first, this question did not yet exist in Justin’s time; and further, there are various elements in him that run directly counter to the opinion of Arius: he decisively and clearly teaches the deity of the Son; he says that the Son was not created but generated and elucidates this with the images of word and light that later became very common; and he clearly sees the significance of Christ’s deity for the whole work of salvation and for the truth of Christianity.
Therefore he also repeatedly names the Father, the Son, and the Spirit together as the object of our worship, _Apol._ I 6, 13, 60, 61, 65, 67; and although he assigns the δευτερα χωρα to the Son and the τριτη ταξις to the Spirit, Justin clearly expresses in these passages the personality of the Holy Spirit and his distinctness from the Son.
An appeal has indeed been made against this to _Apol._ I 33, but this passage teaches nothing other than that by το πνευμα in Luke 1:35 Justin understood not the person of the Holy Spirit but the Logos, an exegesis that also occurs in others.
Further, it also stands established that Justin does not number the Spirit among the angels or among creatures in general.
But concerning the divine nature of the Holy Spirit and his ontological relation to the Father and the Son, we find virtually nothing in Justin.
The religious significance of the doctrine of the Holy Spirit was not yet felt.
The Spirit is still conceived only as the Spirit of prophecy, who inspired the prophets and apostles and equipped Christ.
But Justin has no awareness of a continual, necessary operation of the Holy Spirit in the church.
God’s objective revelation in the Logos appears sufficient; the necessity of subjective illumination is not perceived, cf. Semisch II 305–333; Schwane, _Dogmengesch._ I² 79 f.
Finally, Justin Martyr makes very clear the nature of the influence Greek philosophy exercised in Christian theology.
That such an influence was exercised, and not least in Justin, is denied by no one.
But that influence is most noticeable precisely in those elements of Justin’s teaching that were later banished by the church—namely, in his distinction between the λογος ἐνδιαθετος and προφορικος, in his conception of the Son as δευτερος θεος, in his doctrine of the hidden God, and in his placing the Son outside the divine essence.
All the other elements—the Logos-nature of Christ, preexistence, generation, the creation of all things through the Logos, sonship, and the deity of Christ—were consciously derived by Justin from Scripture and argued from Scripture; cf. Semisch, _Justin der Martyrer_ II 295 f.
The defects that adhere to Justin’s doctrine of the Trinity are not avoided by the following apologists, Theophilus, Tatian, and Athenagoras.
Tatian, _Or. c. Gr._ 5, does indeed say that insofar as all things have their ground in God, they all exist in him ideally, as Logos, but this Logos is brought forth by the will of God and is ἐργον πρωτοτοκον του πατρος; κατα μερισμον, οὐ κατα ἀποκοπην.
In Theophilus the Logos indeed exists before creation as λογος ἐνδιαθετος, since he is θεου νους και φρονησις, but he is nevertheless generated by the Father as λογος προφορικος for the sake of creation, _Ad Autol._ II 10, 22.
Athenagoras, _Leg. pro Chr._, c. 10, likewise teaches that the Logos indeed existed eternally because God is the eternal νους, but he is nevertheless the first γεννημα of the Father because he proceeded from God as the idea and energy of things.
Just as Theophilus was the first to speak of a τριας in God, _Ad Autol._ II 26, so Athenagoras too joins God the Father, God the Son, and the Holy Spirit—who was active in the prophets and is an effluence of God, proceeds from God, and returns to him like the ray from the sun—and calls them the object of Christian worship, ibid., c. 10, 12.
But in distinguishing the three persons, their unity is not sufficiently maintained.
The Father is the one, unbegotten, eternal, invisible God, and Son and Spirit are one with him not in essence but in spirit and power, ibid., c. 24.

The next development of the doctrine of the Trinity, consisting especially in the elimination of the philosophical elements, is due to three men, each of whom contributed his part to the construction of Christian dogma.
Irenaeus is the vigorous opponent of the Gnostic concept of God and of the conception of the Logos as the world-idea.
Now and then he too shows that he has not entirely overcome the old conception; he still calls the Father the invisible, hidden God in contrast to the Son, _Adv. haer._ IV 20, 10.
But he nevertheless contests as forcefully as possible the conception of God as βυθος and the emanation of the aeons, and he maintains the scriptural distinction between Creator and creature.
The Logos is as it were divested of his ambiguous nature and transferred entirely to the side of God.
The Logos is no creature but a hypostatic word, III 8; preexistent, II 6; IV 12; true God, IV 10, 14; etc.
The distinction between λογος ἐνδιαθετος and προφορικος must also be rejected, II 17, 18.
For besides the fact that this distinction detracts from the personality of the Logos and connects his generation with creation, the Logos may not be represented as God’s understanding and reason.
For God is simple, wholly spirit, wholly understanding, wholly thought, wholly Logos, II 16, 48, so that both the Son and the Father are truly God.
The unity of Father, Son, and Spirit is expressed very clearly by Irenaeus; their divine nature is emphatically maintained, and they are repeatedly named together, IV 6, 20, 33.
The generation of the Son did not occur in time; the Son had no beginning but existed eternally with God, II 18; III 22; IV 37.
But Irenaeus falls short in indicating how the Trinity exists in the unity and how Father, Son, and Spirit, although partaking of one divine nature, are nevertheless distinct.
Here he is supplemented and corrected by Tertullian.
Tertullian indeed stands behind Irenaeus in overcoming Gnostic dualism.
He distinguishes between Father and Son as between a _Deus invisibilis et invisus_ and a _Deus visibilis et visus_, _Adv. Prax._ 14, 15.
In all manner of ways and with all manner of arguments he demonstrates that distinction—with the name Logos, with the incarnation, with the theophanies, and so forth.
Indeed, in Tertullian the Logos first arrives at full sonship and independent personality through the three moments of God’s speaking, generation, and incarnation, _Adv. Pr._ 6, 7, so that there was a time in which the Son did not exist, _Adv. Hermog._ 3.
But although in opposition to Patripassianism he goes too far in distinguishing the persons, for that very reason he seeks all the more, on the other side, to maintain unity in the Trinity and Trinity in the unity.
The three persons are _unius substantiae, unius status, unius potestatis, unus Deus_.
They are distinct with respect to the _ordo_ and the _oeconomia_: _Oeconomiae sacramentum unitatem in trinitatem disponit._
They are three _non statu, sed gradu_, and yet the one God, _ex quo et gradus isti et formae et species in nomine Patris, Filii et Spiritus Sancti deputantur_.
Just as a ray of the sun is also sun, so there are different _species, formae, effigies, moduli_ in the one and undivided substance.
The three persons are therefore _unum_, not _unus_.
The Son is another than the Father and the Spirit again another, but the name God and Lord is common to them.
They are _unus Deus_; they cannot be separated.
Just as trunk and branch, spring and stream, sun and ray cannot be separated, neither can the Father and the Son.
The _trinitas_ therefore does not nullify the _monarchia_.
The Son is indeed another than the Father but not _divisus, separatus_.
There is _distinctio, distributio_, not _diversitas_ and _divisio_.
It is a _unitas ex semetipsa derivans trinitatem_, _Adv. Prax._ 2 sq.
Thus Tertullian molds the stiff Latin language in order to maintain equally both the _unitas_ and the _trinitas_ in God; both formally and materially he has been of the greatest significance for the dogma of the Trinity.
Although he does not always rise above subordinationism and distinguishes too little among the ontological, cosmological, and soteriological in the doctrine of the Trinity, he furnished the concepts and words that the dogma of the Trinity needed in order to express its true meaning.
He replaced Logos speculation with _filiatio_ and thereby definitively detached the ontological Trinity from cosmological speculation.
And he was the first to attempt to derive the Trinity of the persons not from the person of the Father but from the essence of God.
While Tertullian, however, does not yet free the ontological Trinity from the cosmological and soteriological process, it is Origen who conceives it wholly as an eternal process within God’s own essence.
Generation is an αἰωνιος γεννησις, _De princ._ I 2, 4.
Light cannot exist without shining; so the Father cannot be without the Son, ibid., I 2, 2, 4, 7, 10.
There was no time when the Son did not exist, ibid., I 2, 2, 4; _C. Cels._ VIII 12.
The Father is not Father before the Son but _per filium_, _De princ._ I 2, 10.
There is no separation: ἀχωριστος ἐστι του υἱου ὁ πατηρ, _C. Cels._ IV 14, 16.
All the divine attributes are common to Father and Son; the Son is one with the Father; we worship the Son not alongside God but in God, _C. Cels._ VIII 12, 13.
The Son has the same wisdom, truth, and reason as the Father; he is αὐτοσοφια, αὐτοαληθεια, αὐτολογος, _C. Cels._ V 41.
But in order now to maintain the distinction within this unity and equality, Origen calls subordinationism to his aid and retreats behind Tertullian in deriving the Trinity not from God’s essence but from the person of the Father.
And thus Origen came to represent the Father as ὀ θεος, αὐτοθεος, πηγη or ριζα θεοτητος, μεγιστος ἐπι πασι θεος, as κρειττων than the Son, as the one entire Deity, exalted above all being, invisible and incomprehensible; and the Son as θεος without the article, as ἑτερος του πατρος κατ’ οὐσιαν, as much less than the Father as the world is less than the Son; cf. Dorner, _Entw._ I 652 f.
