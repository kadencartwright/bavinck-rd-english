## 2.

These thoughts of the Old Testament proved fruitful in many directions. First, they were adopted and further developed in the apocryphal literature. In the Wisdom of Jesus Sirach, wisdom occupies a broad place. It is from God and was created by him before all things, and it remains with him into eternity. It is spread over all God’s works, but has its resting place above all in Zion and is found in the law, ch. 1:1–40 and ch. 24; cf. also Baruch, ch. 3:9–4:4. But the Wisdom of Solomon goes further, 6:22–9. Here wisdom is so hypostatized that divine attributes and works are ascribed to it. It is clearly distinguished from God, for it is the breath of his power, an effluence of his glory, a reflection of his light, 7:25, 26. But it is also most intimately connected with God, lives together with him, is initiated into his knowledge, and selects the ideas that will be carried out, 8:3, 4. It is the παρεδρος on his throne, knows all his works, and was present at the creation of the world, 9:4, 9. Indeed, it is wisdom itself that creates, governs, and renews everything, 7:27; 8:1, 5. It is identical with God’s Word, 9:1, 2; cf. 16:12; 18:15, 16, and with his Spirit, 1:4–7; 9:17; 12:1. Already in this book of Wisdom the influence of Greek philosophy is perceptible, especially in 7:22 f., but this is much more strongly the case in Philo. The relation of God to the world had already been investigated by Plato. From the distinction and opposition between δοξα and ἐπιστημη, Plato inferred that, just as the former had a sensory object in actuality, so the latter had to have as its object an eternal and immutable being, that is, an idea. These ideas, although properly nothing other than universal concepts, were elevated by Plato into metaphysical principles, into substances in their own right, into a kind of intermediate beings according to which the δημιουργος formed the cosmos and which are thus the παραδειγματα and αἰτιαι of things. Although Aristotle subjected this doctrine of ideas to sharp criticism, the thought that an intelligent, spiritual principle underlay all things never again disappeared from Greek philosophy. The Stoa in particular emphasized that a divine reason was the ground of all phenomena. It used for this the name λογος σπερματικος, because all being and life germinates from this Logos as from a seed, and it also sometimes spoke in the plural of λογοι σπερματικοι, in order thereby to designate alongside the unity also the diversity of nature’s all-creating power, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ IV³ 159, 160; cf. 140 n. Even the later highly significant distinction between λογος ενδιαθετος and προφορικος is derived from the Stoa, ibid., 67 n. Even before Philo, this Greek doctrine of the idea, the νους, and the λογος had been connected with the Old Testament doctrine of the word and wisdom, Zeller, ibid., V 266. Yet it was above all Philo who fused all these different elements—the Platonic doctrine of ideas, the Stoic doctrine of the Logos, the Old Testament doctrine of wisdom, and so forth—into one system. In doing so he proceeds from the dualism of God and world. God is αποιος, indescribable; we can say only that he is, not what he is. Therefore he also cannot stand in immediate contact with matter. Before God created the sensory world, he made a plan and formed in his thought the κοσμος νοητος, the ideas, as παραδειγματα and δυναμεις of all things. These ideas are now presented by Philo as the powers by which God can work in the world, and are described—now more and now less figuratively, now more and now less personally—as servants, governors, envoys, mediators; as λογοι and powers; as bonds and pillars, which were called angels by Moses and demons by the Greeks; and as thoughts that exist in the divine mind and are uncreated and infinite as God himself. These ideas are also many in number, but they find their unity in the Logos—the idea that contains all ideas, the power that contains all powers, the book that contains all thoughts, the κοσμος νοητος itself. In the same way as the divine ideas, this Logos too is described now more as an attribute of God and one with his wisdom, and then more as a being distinguished from God. He stands, as it were, between God and the world and partakes of both. He is not uncreated as God is, and yet he is also not created as finite things are. He is God’s representative, envoy, interpreter, governor, angel, instrument, image, and shadow—indeed, his firstborn son, his eldest son in distinction from the world, which is his youngest son. Philo even calls him God, but δευτερος θεος. Zeller clearly brings to light that the Logos in Philo, like the divine ideas, bears and must bear this double character. He is an intermediate and therefore a double being: an attribute of God and yet a person, neither identical with God nor a creature like the world, an idea in God’s thought and a power in the world, hovering back and forth between an impersonal attribute and a personal substance, but for that very reason considered suitable as mediator between God and the world; cf. Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ V³ 370–386, and the literature on Philo there, 338; also Max Heinze, _Die Lehre v. Logos in der gr. Philos._, 1872; Schürer, _Neut. Zeitgesch._ 653 f.; Meyer-Weiss, _Ev. Joh._, 6th ed., p. 52 f.; Jean Réville, _La doctrine du Logos_, Paris, 1881; Kuenen, _Volksgodsd. en wereldgodsd._ 163 f.; Weizsäcker, _Das apost. Zeitalter der chr. Kirche_, 1890, p. 549 f.; Hatch, _Griechentum und Christentum_, 1892, p. 129 f.; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴, p. 552 f.; Kaftan, “Das Verhältnis des evang. Glaubens zur Logoslehre” (Gottschick, _Zeits. f. Th. u. K._ VII, 1897, issue 1, pp. 1–27); Anathon Aall, _Geschichte der Logosidee in der griech. Philosophie_, Leipzig: Reisland, 1896; _Theol. Stud._ by Dr. Danbanton, XI, 1893, part 2. In Jewish theology this doctrine of intermediate beings was developed still further. As completely transcendent, God cannot enter into immediate connection with creatures. For this he requires all kinds of intermediate beings. When he wills to do nothing other than direct the forces present in nature and humanity, he makes use of angels. But when he wills to work creatively or re-creatively in the world, hypostases appear that, though creatures, nevertheless bear divine attributes because they are God’s representatives. Such hypostases are Metatron, God’s μεταθρονος; Memra, the Word of God; Shekinah, the presence of God’s glory; Bat Qol, the voice of God that gives revelations and proclaims oracles; Ruach ha-Qodesh, the spirit that proceeds from God and communicates higher knowledge, Eisenmenger, _Entdecktes Judenthum_ I 265 f.; II 393 f.; etc.; Weber, _System der altsyn. paläst. Theol._ 172–189; Herzog² 2, 230 f.; 13, 458 f.
