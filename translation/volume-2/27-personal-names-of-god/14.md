## 14.

The doctrine of the Trinity is in the Christian religion the dogma, and therefore also the mystery, _par excellence_. As soon as the data that Holy Scripture provides for it became the object of theological thought, a need arose for all kinds of names and expressions that did not occur in Holy Scripture and yet proved indispensable for rendering the truth to some extent, however imperfectly, and for maintaining it against misunderstanding and opposition. The Arians and many movements in later times, such as the Socinians, the Anabaptists, the Remonstrants, the biblical theologians, and others, disapprove of the use of such unscriptural names. But Christian theology has always defended their right and value, Augustine, _De trin._ VI 10; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 29, art. 3; Calvin, _Inst._ I 13, 5; Moor I 710–712; M. Vitringa I 191–195; Gerhard, _Loc._ III, c. 2; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II³ 149 f. After all, Scripture was not given to us merely to be repeated but to be thought through and rendered in our own language; Jesus and the apostles used it in this way and by reasoning derived further conclusions from it. Scripture is not a law code and not a dogmatics either, but the _principium_ of theology; as the Word of God it binds us not only in its literal words but also in what is legitimately inferred from it, cf. vol. I 528. Further, no reflection on the truth of Scripture, and therefore no theological activity whatsoever, is possible without using such words that do not occur in Scripture. Not only in the doctrine of the Trinity but also with every other dogma and throughout theology, expressions and terms are used that are not found in Scripture. Thus the independence of the Christian and the right of theology are at stake in these names. And finally, these terms serve not to introduce new _dogmata_ apart from or in conflict with Scripture but precisely to maintain the truth of Scripture against heresy. They have a much more negative than positive significance. They indicate the lines within which Christian thought must move if it is not to lose the truth of revelation in the process. Under the appearance of being scriptural, biblical theology has always strayed farther from Scripture; and with its nonbiblical terms, ecclesial orthodoxy has repeatedly been vindicated in its scriptural character. Thus in the doctrine of the Trinity too all kinds of unusual names gradually arose, such as ὁμοουσιος, οὐσια, ὑπαρξις, ὑποστασις, προσωπον, γενναν, τριας, _unitas, trinitas, substantia, personae, nomina, gradus, species, formae, proprietates_, and so forth. At first, however, the meaning of these terms was far from fixed. The word οὐσια was indeed usually used of the one being of God, but in Origen, Athanasius, and Gregory of Nyssa it nevertheless still frequently served to designate the three persons in that one being. Athanasius expressly defended himself against Sabellianism by calling the Son not μονοουσιος but ὁμοουσιος with the Father, _Expos. fid._, c. 2. Likewise the word ὑποστασις was used at one time of the being and at another of the three persons; and thus it was said at one time that there was only one hypostasis in God and at another that there were three ὑποστασεις. But Sabellianism saw in the persons only forms of revelation of the one being. Over against this, the church had to emphasize that those persons were genuinely existing subsistences in the divine being. And for this the name ὑποστασις was used. Basil, in his letter περι οὐσιας και ὑποσταεως, brought greater uniformity into this by designating the being of God by οὐσια and the three persons by ὑποστασις or προσωπον; every ὑποστασις thus has an existence of its own, ἰδια ὑπαρξις, is ὑφεστωσα, and is distinguished from the others by ἰδιοτητες, ἰδιωματα, ἰδιαζοντα, σημεια, ἰδια γνωρισματα, χαρακτηρες, μορφαι. The usage of both Gregories and further of John of Damascus and of Greek theology and the Greek church follows this. Petavius, _De trin._ V, c. 1 sq.; Schwane, _Dogmengesch._ II² 99, 151 f.; Dorner, _Entw._ I² 914 f.; Harnack II² 252; Hatch, _Griech. u. Christ._, trans. Preuschen, 1892, p. 200 f. In the West the confusion was not so great. Tertullian had fixed the term _essentia_ or _substantia_ for the being and the name _persona_ or _subsistentia_ for the persons. Later church teachers and creeds adopted this usage. In his work _De trinitate_, Hilary speaks consistently of _una essentia, substantia, natura, genus_ and of _tres personae_, who are distinguished from one another by _proprietates_. Augustine disapproved of rendering the Greek ὑποστασις by _substantia_. In Latin, _substantia_ and _essentia_ could not be distinguished in the same way as ὑποστασις and οὐσια in Greek. One could not speak in Latin of _una essentia_ and _tres substantiae_. Rather, _substantia_ was equivalent to the Greek ουσια, while _essentia_ still sounded strange and unusual to Latin ears. Latin therefore retained the expression _una substantia_ and _tres personae_. Yet Augustine preferred to avoid the word _substantia_ altogether, both for the being and for the persons. For in Latin, in distinction from _accidens_, _substantia_ designated the bearer of the attributes as existing in itself. Because that contrast cannot occur in God, since being and attributes are one in him, Augustine considered it better to designate the divine being as _essentia_, _De trin._ V 8; VII 4. And just as in the East it was necessary over against Sabellianism to emphasize the subsistence, the ὑποστασις, of the three persons, so over against Arianism the Latins maintained that the three persons were not three _substantiae_ but three _personae_. Scholasticism extended this terminology still further and gave it a fixed schema that was later generally adopted, also in the theology of the Reformation. In God there is one being, _una essentia, unitas naturae_, and three persons, _tres personae, trinitas personarum_. These three persons are one in that being, ὁμοουσιοι, _coessentiales_, and mutually exist in one another, ἐμπεριχωρησις, _circumincessio personarum_. But they are distinct. For in God there are _duae emanationes_: _per modum naturae_ and _per modum voluntatis_; _tres hypostases_: _Pater, Filius, Spiritus S._; _quatuor relationes_: _paternitas, filiatio, spiratio activa_ and _passiva_; _quinque notiones_: _innascibilitas, paternitas, filiatio, spiratio activa_ and _passiva_; _tres proprietates personales_: _Pater, qui est ingenitus, Filius, qui est genitus, Spiritus Sanctus, qui est spiratus_, Bonaventure, _Brevil._ I, c. 3, 4; cf. Anselm, _Monol._, c. 29 sq.; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 22 sq.; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 27–32, etc.
