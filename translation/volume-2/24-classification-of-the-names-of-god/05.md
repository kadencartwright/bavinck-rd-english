## 5.

By defining God’s essence in this way and making one attribute the principal concept, however, theology ran the danger of making a distinction between God’s essence and the attributes subsequently added to it. Naturally it could not follow the path of polytheism, which made the various attributes, as they were revealed in creatures, into so many divine persons. Plato too gave the ideas, as archetypes of real things, their own independent existence alongside God. Gnosticism defined God as the unknowable and unnameable but made the Platonic ideas into aeons that emanated from God and in descending order moved away from God. They were called ἐννοια, νους, λογος, ζωη, σοφια, and so forth, and were the personified attributes of God, emanating from God and represented as divine beings, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I 11, 24. Under Plato’s influence Philo repeatedly represented the divine powers, especially goodness, power, and the Logos, as hypostases, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ V³, p. 358 f. Jewish theology accepted various hypostases, such as Metatron, Memra, Shekinah, Ruach, Bath Kol, Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._, p. 172; and in Kabbalah the ten Sefirot or attributes of God were repeatedly described as emanations from the divine being, Franck, _La Kabbale ou la philos. religieuse des Hébreux_, Paris, Hachette, 1873; Ueberweg, _Gesch. der Philos._ II, §27; Herzog² 7, 375 f.; Dr. Th. Bloch, _Gesch. der Entw. der Kabbala u. der jüd. Rel. Philos._, Trier, Mayer, 1894. This Gnostic and Kabbalistic philosophy frequently exercised great influence later as well. Something of this Gnosticism still worked on in Arianism insofar as it accepted a gradation among the divine persons. Monotheism, no longer supported and maintained by the doctrine of the Trinity, easily passes into polytheism. We hear daily that state, science, art, fortune, chance, fate, and so forth are regarded and honored by many as independent powers alongside and in place of God.

Although Christian theology did not go so far, it nevertheless soon made a distinction between God’s essence and attributes. The church fathers had long spoken of God’s essence in distinction from the three persons, but gradually the word _essence_ was also set over against his attributes. This distinction arose especially through the struggle against Eunomius. In his _Liber apologeticus_, Eunomius repeatedly declared that names were an indication of the essence of the thing, τας προσηγοριας εἰναι των οὐσιων σημαντικας, ibid., c. 18; _Bibl. patr. gr. dogm._, ed. Thilo, II, p. 598. Over against this Basil and Gregory of Nyssa maintained that there was no single name that expressed God’s essence but that there were nevertheless many ὀνοματα, ἰδιωματα, ἐννοιαι, νοηματα, ἀξιωματα, through which χαρακτηρ τις ἡμιν ἐγγινεται του θεου, _Bibl. patr. gr. dogm._ II, p. 42. And Gregory of Nyssa spoke of God’s οὐσια as το ἱποκειμενον and of ποιοτητες or ἰδιωματων διαφοραι in relation to that essence, Diekamp, _Die Gotteslehre des h. Gregor. von Nyssa_, Münster, 1896, I 190 f. As soon as this distinction arose, the question inevitably had to be asked what belonged to the essence and what to the attributes. With that the right road was abandoned. When the church fathers proceeded in their description of God from the name YHWH and defined him as being, they did not thereby have in view God’s essence in distinction from his attributes but the whole essence of God as it reveals itself in his attributes. The being attributed to God was therefore no _abstractum_ but a living, infinitely full, concrete being, a _summum esse_ that immediately and all at once was identical with _summa vita, summa veritas, summa sapientia, summus amor_, and so forth, as Augustine repeatedly said; a πελαγος οὐσιας ἀπειρον και ἀοριστον, _mare substantiae infinitum et interminatum_, Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or._ 38; Damascene, _De fide orth._ I 9. The definition of God as _essentia_ signified that he was the sum of all reality, by no means that he was abstract being in the philosophical sense of that word. Baur, _Gesch. der Lehre von der Dreiein._ II 644 f., and Ritschl in his studies on God, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1865, pp. 275–318; 1868, pp. 67–133, 251–302, did not sufficiently keep this distinction in view. In Basil and Gregory of Nyssa too, the distinction between essence and attributes still served principally to maintain the incomprehensibility of God’s essence over against Eunomius.
