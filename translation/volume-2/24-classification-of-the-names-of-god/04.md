## 4.

This pantheism too did not go unopposed. A whole series of theistic philosophers arose to demonstrate the untenability of pantheism and maintain the legitimacy of theism. They sought their strength in the idea of absolute personality, connected this with the idea of becoming, and thus introduced into God a theogonic process, whether in a unitarian sense (Jacobi, Herbart, Drobisch, Rothe, Lotze, Ulrici, Carrière, and others) or in a trinitarian sense (Baader, Schelling, J. H. Fichte, Weisse, Dorner, and others). But this theistic speculation too has had its day. Separation of theology and philosophy, of religion and metaphysics, has become the watchword. On the one hand, science increasingly withdraws from the domain of religion and theology; it becomes exact, positive. On the other hand, religion too increasingly seeks to free itself from science and to banish all metaphysics and philosophy. For the _locus de Deo_ this has the result that the essence of the Deity is sought exclusively in the ethically good. Groningen theology placed fatherhood and love in God in the foreground, Hofstede de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._, 1855, p. 177 f. Scholten did indeed proceed from God’s absolute sovereignty, _L. H. K._ II⁴, but the ethical Modernists rose in opposition to his speculative intellectualism and monistic determinism and conceived God as Father, Hoekstra, _Des Christens godsvrucht_, 1866, p. 103 f.; _Bronnen en grondslagen_, 1864, p. 265 f.; as the moral ideal, the power of the good, holy power, Hooykaas, _God in de geschiedenis_, 1870; Hooykaas, H. Herderschee, Oort, and Van Hamel, _Godsd. volg. de beginselen der eth. rigting onder de modernen_, 1876. In Germany, Ritschl’s Neo-Kantianism arose in the same way against the speculative _Vermittelungstheologie_. Ritschl denies that religion is a legal relationship. According to Duns Scotus, Socinianism, and Arminianism, God is the unrestricted ruler who nevertheless treats human beings equitably, even though they have no rights over against him. Arbitrariness, _dominium absolutum_, is the essence of God and the law of the world; God can do one thing just as readily as another. The relation between God and the human being belongs to private law and finds its image in the slaveholder who treats his slave equitably, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 223–229. According to the orthodox Protestants, God’s relation to the world is determined by justice. The human being has a right to eternal life if he keeps God’s law and otherwise deserves punishment. This theory is public-law in nature; it is derived from the Old Testament, especially from Pharisaism, and was adopted by Paul and thus entered the Christian church, ibid., III² 229–245. Cf. also his studies on God, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1865 and 1868, now republished in _Gesammelte Aufsätze, Neue Folge_, 1896. But both conceptions are wrong. Law and religion stand opposed to one another. No dualism may be introduced into God between justice and grace. The whole abstract, Areopagitic concept of God, which places God negatively above the world and assumes no communion between God and the human being, must be surrendered, ibid., S. 253. Religion is a moral relation, and Christianity is the _vollendet geistige_ and _absolut sittliche Religion_, ibid., III², S. 8 f. The relation of God and the human being finds its image not in the relation of a master to his servant or of a government to its subjects but in that of a father to his child. The family is its model, III² 90. God must therefore be conceived as love; alongside it no other concept of equal value comes into consideration, ibid., S. 255. This must be the point of departure at once. The Christian dogmatician should not begin by a synthetic method with all manner of metaphysical abstractions concerning God as the Absolute, _Theol. u. Methaph._ 12 f.; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 2–8, 32; nor with a so-called _theologia naturalis_, which does not exist, III², S. 200 f.; _Theol. u. Met._, S. 8 f.; nor with the concept of personality or the attribute of holiness, III² 255, but immediately and entirely with love. From it everything—creation, providence, atonement, justification, and so forth—must be derived, ibid., 255; _Theol. u. Met._ 20. Cf. Kaftan, _Das Wesen der chr. Relig._, S. 383; Herrmann, _Die Religion im Verhältniss zum Welterkennen und zur Sittlichkeit_, S. 121 f.; Gottschick, _Die Kirchlichkeit der s. g. kirchl. Theol._, S. 152 f. And so every school is recognizable by its concept of God. In turn God is described as the Being, absolute being, substance; or as the Sovereign, the Lord, the Supreme Being; or as infinite Spirit, absolute causality, absolute personality; or as the Father, love, goodness, and so forth. It proves so difficult to proceed from the harmony of all God’s excellencies and to maintain it to the end. Every human being is limited and emphasizes one attribute at the expense of another: Jansenius saw in God especially truth, Francis de Sales love, Vincent de Paul goodness, Saint-Cyran omnipotence, Quack, _Port-Royal_, offprint from _Gids_ of 1872, p. 49. Yet it is the calling of theology, following the example of Holy Scripture, to honor all God’s excellencies equally.
