# § 24. Classification of the Names of God

## 1.

Holy Scripture calls God by many names but never proceeds from an abstract concept of God.
It never elevates one attribute of God at the expense of another.
Now one attribute and then another may come more to the foreground, but there is perfect harmony among all the attributes.
It is Scripture’s intention to honor all God’s excellencies equally.
Just as the person of Christ does not represent a particular one-sided character or temperament and yet is a person full of life and reality, so God too in his revelation is always unfolding all his excellencies in harmonious coherence.
Nowhere is the essence of God spoken of _in abstracto_.
The Hebrew word תּוּשִׁיָּה, from the root יָשָׁה, to exist, to be—Arabic form III, to help, establish—denotes that which has stability, is enduring, and provides advantage; _in concreto_, it especially denotes true wisdom and happiness, Job 5:12; 6:13; 12:16; 26:3 (30:22); Prov. 2:7; 3:21; 8:14; and further only Isa. 28:29; Mic. 6:9, but in none of these passages does it stand for the essence of God; cf. Delitzsch on Job 5:12.
Neither can the New Testament words θεοτης, Col. 2:9; θειοτης, Rom. 1:29; μορφη θεου, Phil. 2:6; and θεια φυσις, 2 Pet. 1:4; cf. Gal. 4:8, prove that these passages speak of the essence or nature of God in distinction from his attributes, as Polanus maintains, _Synt. theol._, p. 135.
God’s essence becomes knowable to us in his revelation—that is, in his names.
The names of God are designations of his ἀρεται, 1 Pet. 2:9.
This usage accords with Isa. 42:8, 12; 43:21; 63:5, where the Hebrew תְּהִּלָה, praise, honor, is rendered in the Septuagint by ἀρετη; cf. Hab. 3:3; Zech. 6:13.
The church has the calling to proclaim God’s excellencies—that is, to give him honor for the δοξα displayed in all his works.
Holy Scripture never speaks of a description of God’s essence apart from the attributes.
Nor was anything of this sort attempted in Christian theology in the earliest period.
Everything that one had to say about God was comprehended under the _nomina Dei_.
The names of God designated not only the _nomina propria_ but also what were later called the attributes and even the persons in the divine being: Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ V 12; Augustine, _De doctr. chr._ I 5; Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 1 sq.; Damascene, _De fide orth._ I, c. 12; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13; _Catech. Rom._ I, c. 2, qu. 11, 13; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I², S. 211 f.; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, loc. 2; Hyperius, _Meth. Theol._, p. 89 sq., c. 3; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, lib. 2, c. 6; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 9, 10; Walaeus, _Op._ I, p. 152 sq.; Alsted, _Theol. did. schol._, p. 61.
The attributes were therefore also immediately incorporated into the idea of God, as, for example, in Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I, c. 14; II, c. 13, 35; etc.
Augustine does indeed speak of God’s _essentia_, but by this he understands the fullness of God’s being and immediately includes in it all the attributes—simplicity, eternity, goodness, wisdom, and so forth.
The confessions too repeatedly speak of God in this way, without distinguishing between essence and attributes and without classifying the attributes: _Conc. Lateran._ in Denzinger, _Enchir. symb._, n. 355; _Conc. Vatic._, _De fide_, c. 1; _Conf. August._, art. 1; _Conf. Gall._, art. 1; _Scot._, art. 1; _Belg._, art. 1; _Conf. Westm._, cap. 2.
And even later various theologians do not enter into these distinctions and treat the attributes without first speaking about the nature of God, for example, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 3 sq.; Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, pp. 46–70.
