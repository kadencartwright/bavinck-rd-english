## 2.

But a distinction nevertheless soon arose. People were led to it by the question what attribute immediately distinguished God from all creatures, what the principal concept was, and therefore from what one had to proceed in the doctrine of God. Platonic philosophy had already sought that principal concept in being, and Philo had connected this with the name YHWH, the only name that indicated not an operation or power but God’s essence itself, and therefore frequently called God ὁ ὠν or το ὀν, Zeller, _Philos._ V³ 356. This definition of God’s essence was adopted in Christian theology. Irenaeus repeatedly describes God as absolutely simple, _Adv. haer._ II, capp. 13, 28; IV 11, and even calls him _inexcogitabilis et insubstantivus_, I, c. 14, but over against Gnosticism he nevertheless emphasizes above all that God is the Creator of all things and that he revealed himself in his works, Harnack, _D. G._ I 485 f. By contrast, in Origen, _De princ._ I 1 sq.; _C. Cels._ VI 64; VII 42–51; Athanasius, _De decr. nic. syn._, c. 11; Damascene, _De fide orth._ I, capp. 2, 4, 9; etc.; cf. Harnack, _D. G._ II 119 f.; Schwane, _Dogm._ I 132, II 35; Kattenbusch, _Vergl. Confessionskunde_ I 310 f., God is the one, being, indeed the one exalted above all being, ὁ ὠν, ὁ ὠν καθ’ ἑαυτον, το ὀν, who is being itself, who has being from and through himself. And in the footsteps of Philo all this was derived from or connected with the name YHWH in the Old Testament. In the West these definitions were adopted. Augustine repeatedly defines God as _summum esse, summum bonum, verum, pulchrum_, and so forth. God is a _substantia_, for what is not substance does not exist, _Enarr. in Ps._ 67. But because the word _substance_ is frequently used in contrast with _accidentia_, which inhere in substance and have it as their bearer, Augustine prefers to use of God the word _essentia, quam Graeci οὐσιαν vocant_, for which the word _natura_ is also used, _De trin._ V 2; VII 5 sq.; _De civ._ XII 2; _De doctr. chr._ I 6. For in God there is no distinction between substance and accidents; his being is no bearer of the attributes, but these are identical with being itself. God is the highest, best, most beautiful, most perfect being, _quo esse aut cogitari melius nihil possit_, _De lib. arb._ II 6; _De doctr. chr._ I 7. He is _Deus, supra quem nihil, extra quem nihil, sine quo nihil est; summa vita, summa veritas, summa beatitudo, summa sapientia, summa essentia_, _De civ._ XII 2; _Solil._ I 1. Augustine too appeals for this definition of God to the name YHWH, _De trin._ V 2; VII 5; _De doctr. chr._ I 32. We then repeatedly find this same definition later, in Hilary, _De trin._ I, c. 1 sq.; Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._, cap. 1, §6, who, however, in cap. 4, §1, proceeds from the idea of the good and in c. 5, §1, says that this extends still further than the idea of being; Anselm, _Monol._ c. 28; _Prosl._ c. 17 sq.; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 2, art. 3 and qu. 3; _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 16 sq.; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ I, cap. 2; _Sent._ I, dist. 8; etc.; Petavius, _Theol._, lib. 1, c. 6; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II 81–90; C. Pesch, _Prael._ II 46–70; Jansen, II, pp. 26–46. Although this became the common opinion, there were nevertheless some who chose another starting point. They sought the principal concept in the idea of God not in absolute being, in _esse a se_ (_aseitas_), but in infinity, and therefore preferred to define God’s essence as an _ens infinitum_. Duns Scotus did so, maintaining that being could be attributed _univoce_, in the same sense, to God and creature, but that the distinguishing mark between God and the creature lay in the fact that God was an _ens infinitum_ and the creatures were finite, _Sent._ I, dist. 3, qu. 1 and dist. 8, qu. 3. Further, there were a few Thomists who thought that the proper essence of God lay in intellectual nature and therefore defined God as _ens intelligens_, such as Gotti, for example; cf. Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II, p. 82; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I² 229.
