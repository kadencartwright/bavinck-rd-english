## 6.

But when the doctrine of God’s incomprehensibility later receded into the background, the distinction between essence and attributes acquired another meaning. Just as in creatures a distinction was made between their substance and their attributes, so in God people distinguished between his essence and his perfections. This led, however, to all manner of difficulties. First, people came into conflict with their own point of departure. It was the unanimous doctrine of the whole Christian church and theology that God’s essence _an sich_ was unknowable, that no name and no definition could be given of it, and that God could be known only from his revelation, by his own light. With this theology from the beginning adopted a positive standpoint and cut off all philosophical speculation at the root. Now, however, it imperceptibly brought philosophy back in. God’s essence was distinguished from his attributes. Something could and had to be said of that essence apart from the attributes. God’s incomprehensibility was in fact denied. But there was more. This distinction between God’s essence and attributes entailed that this essence could be defined only in very abstract terms, as _ens spirituale, ens infinitum, ens absolutum_, without further predicates. That might be a philosophical definition, but it was no longer the Christian, biblical concept of God. God never meets us in Scripture in this way. The theological and philosophical concepts of God looked remarkably alike. The first chapters of Christian dogmatics gave a schema of concepts derived far too much from Plato and Aristotle and afterward supplemented by Christian ideas. Especially when theology in this century adopted the idea of the Absolute from philosophy, the mixing of the two became evident. It is Ritschl’s merit that he saw this. He was entirely right when he required dogmatics to proceed immediately from the Christian concept of God. Only he too made himself guilty of one-sidedness when on that ground he demanded that God be conceived solely as love. In Scripture, which in its entirety and not merely in one portion—for example, the Gospels, the words of Jesus, the Sermon on the Mount—is the _principium_ of dogmatics, God meets us in a fullness of attributes. And it is a requirement of dogmatics to proceed equally not from one but from all God’s perfections. Only then does harmony come into our knowledge of God and thus also into our entire doctrine and our entire life. Further, the incorrectness of that distinction between essence and attributes emerged in the fact that, once accepted, it was nevertheless immediately revoked. Through the doctrine of the _simplicitas Dei_, Christian theology was preserved from the error of accepting an essential difference between the two. It generally said that there was no distinction. Every attribute is God’s essence. God is what he has. In creatures there is a distinction between what they are and what they have; a human being is still human even if he has lost the image of God and become a sinner. But in God all the attributes are his essence itself. God is wholly light, wholly understanding, wholly wisdom, wholly Logos, wholly Spirit, and so forth, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 28; IV 11. In God _esse quod sapere, quod bonum esse, quod potentem esse_. _Una eademque res dicitur sive dicatur aeternus Deus sive immortalis sive bonus sive justus_, and so forth, Augustine, _De trin._ VI 7; Damascene, I 9; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 2, art. 3; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, 42, 51–53; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, p. 81. God is everything together, simultaneously and wholly. _Deus habet nullas proprietates sed mera est essentia. Proprietates Dei sunt realiter ipsamet Dei essentia nec ab essentia Dei nec inter se re ipsa differunt._ All this was well and beautifully said. Only, why make a distinction that was immediately nullified again and whose incorrectness was demonstrated once more afterward in the doctrine of the _simplicitas Dei_? Worse was that others, proceeding from the distinction between essence and attributes acknowledged by all, now also sought an objective and real distinction and thus to a greater or lesser degree made the attributes independent over against the essence. Gilbert of Poitiers thus distinguished between the essence, the nature of God, _divinitas_, and God, _Deus_; _divinitas_ was the _forma_ by which God is God but was not itself God: _Divinitate Deus est sed divinitas non est Deus_, Petavius, _De Deo_ I, cap. 8; art. in Herzog² 5, 168. Duns Scotus indeed denied that there was a _distinctio realis_ between essence and attributes but nevertheless said, with an appeal to Damascene and Augustine, that the attributes were _formaliter_ distinguished from God’s essence and from one another, _Sent._ I, dist. 8, qu. 4, n. 17 sq. Socinianism spoke of _accidentia_ in God and considered a different conception and description of the attributes necessary for the subject, Fock, _Der Socin._, p. 427. Many went further still and even accepted an objective distinction in God, at the expense of God’s simplicity and immutability, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ §38; Martensen, _Chr. Dogm._ §85; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 190 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Glaub._ I 181 f.; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 34 f.; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 371–374; Oosterzee, _Chr. Dogm._ §47. Continuing along this line, Doedes treated first those determinations given of themselves with the idea of God, such as unity, communicability, incomparability, incomprehensibility, independence, and so forth, and then the attributes that additionally belong to that divine being and are only five in number: omnipotence, wisdom, goodness, love, holiness. The Roman Catholic theologian C. Pesch likewise speaks of _attributa substantiae divinae superaddita_, _Praelect. theol._ II 72. The disadvantage of the separation emerges most strongly in F. A. B. Nitzsch, who first treats God’s essence with aseity, holiness, unity, and so forth, _Ev. Dogm._, pp. 351–367; then the works of creation, pp. 367–396; and only after that the attributes according to Schleiermacher’s division, pp. 396–423.

For these reasons the distinction between essence and attributes cannot be approved. Naturally, this does not deny that God is a being, a substance, nor does it disapprove the use of the word _essence_; in the doctrine of the Trinity it is even indispensable. But the rejection of the distinction means that God’s essence is in no respect knowable apart from the attributes and that no division may be given which makes some attributes appear as given with the essence and others as added to it. Christian theology has therefore rightly taught at all times that the attributes are identical with God’s essence. Every attribute is the divine essence itself. It is therefore impossible to describe the whole essence of God with one attribute or one name, as has frequently been attempted, for example with the idea of absolute personality or of love. Love is no more or less an attribute of God and a description of his essence than every other perfection. It stands on the same level with all the attributes, as Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, p. 351 f., also rightly observes. Whether God has still more perfections than those he reveals to us in creation and dominion can remain an open question. Spinoza said that the more reality a substance had, the more attributes it also possessed, _Eth._ I, prop. 9, and that God as infinite substance therefore also had infinitely many attributes, ibid., prop. 11, although only two—thought and extension—are known to us. And Reinhard, _Dogm._, p. 100, found it very probable that _Gott eine Menge von Eigenschaften besitzen könne, wovon wir gar keinen Begriff haben, weil die unendliche Vollkommenheit in so eingeschränkten Geschöpfen, wie wir sind, unmöglich alle Aehnlichkeiten mit sich hat vereinigen können_; cf. also L. Meijer, _Verhandelingen over de goddelijke eigenschappen_, Groningen, 1783, I 186 f. But however this may be, the calling of dogmatics is to display the harmonious unity of all the perfections God has exhibited in his works.
