## 3.

The Reformation brought no change in this conception.
Theologians aligned themselves with one or another definition.
The Reformed, at least in the earliest period, mostly adopted the definition of Augustine and Thomas.
They proceeded from the concept of _aseitas_ or _independentia_ and frequently defined God as an _ens independens_, Zwingli, _Op._ III 155 sq., IV 81 sq.; Hyperius, _Meth. Theol._, p. 87; Martyr, _Loci Comm._, p. 39b; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, lib. II c. 5; Zanchi, _Op._ II col. 49 sq.; Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, 1584, p. 46 sq.
Alongside this, there also occur definitions of God as _spiritus increatus, spiritus simplicissimus, spiritus a se ipso existens_, sometimes further supplemented by the trinitarian formula _unus essentia, trinus personis_, Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, S. 35 f.; Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. ref. K._ I 244 f.
The Lutherans used the definition _essentia spiritualis infinita, ens spirituale a se subsistens, spiritus independens_, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._⁶, S. 73-77; Hase, _Hutt. red._ § 54.
By contrast, Socinianism adopted a different standpoint.
Disregarding all metaphysical questions, it emphasized only the will of God.
The knowledge of God consists solely in knowing his will.
Religion loses all mysticism and is absorbed entirely in _cultus_.
God comes to stand farther and farther removed from the world and humanity.
This is continued in Remonstrantism, rationalism, and English deism; cf. above, pp. 15, 16.
Against this cold, moralistic concept of God, a reaction arose once again from the side of philosophy.
Spinoza returned to the idea of being and conceived God as _substantia unica, infinita, necessario existens, ens absolute infinitum, absolute causa prima et immanens_, _Eth._ I prop. 7 sq.; and he again spoke of a love of God, _amor Dei intellectualis_, which bestowed the highest blessedness, _Eth._ V prop. 15 sq.
Rationalism and deism did indeed remain dominant after Spinoza, but around the middle of the 18th century people grew increasingly weary of them.
The great minds Goethe, Lessing, and Herder felt attracted to Spinoza, and soon his pantheism made its entrance into philosophy.
Kant had already undermined the foundations of rationalism, although he still continued to call himself a deist, Drews, _Die deutsche Spekul._ I 104.
Fichte undertook the struggle against the concepts of God and immortality as products of eudaemonism.
In his exaggerated moralism, God was for him identical with the pure ego, with the moral world order; God was no being, no substance, but _absolutes Thun, Thätigkeit_; so in his _Wissenschaftslehre_, 1794; _Ueber den Grund unseres Glaubens an eine göttliche Weltregierung_, 1798; _Appellation an das Publikum_, 1799.
Later he partly retreated from this and aligned himself more closely with Spinoza, _Anweisung zum seligen Leben oder Religionslehre_, 1806.
Schleiermacher displayed an even stronger agreement with Spinoza.
God and world are _correlata_, _Dialektik_, S. 162.
God is the _Woher unseres empfänglichen und selbsthätigen Daseins_, _Glaub._ I⁵, S. 20.
Schelling distinguished himself from both by taking account not only of the spiritual—religion and morality—but also of objective nature.
His goal was to raise natural science out of the deplorable condition in which it found itself.
And so he combines both, nature and spirit; nature is visible spirit, spirit invisible nature; in both he sees a continuous, organic revelation of the Absolute, which is exalted above all oppositions, one, simple, eternal, without predicates, the unity of the infinite and the finite, of God and world, _ἑν και παν_, Drews, _Die deutsche Spekul._ I, S. 201 f.
Finally, Hegel transformed this system into a logical idealism.
Nature and history are a logically necessary self-unfolding of the idea.
Everything is rational; everything is embodied thought.
Reason is therefore the absolute substance; it is God himself.
For God is nothing other than the one living world-idea developing toward self-consciousness.
For Hegel, then, God is indeed reason, thought, spirit, subject, but not in the sense that he would have a life of his own prior to and outside the world.
_Ohne Welt ist Gott nicht Gott_; the world is an essential moment in the life of God, Drews, _Die deutsche Spekul._ I 229 f.
