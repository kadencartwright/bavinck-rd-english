## 7.

If, then, no distinction can be made between essence and attributes because every attribute is the very essence of God, it still does not follow that the distinction among the attributes themselves is only nominal and subjective, without any ground in reality.
Many, however, have judged this to be so.
Aëtius and Eunomius reasoned as follows: God is simple and without any composition; what is in him or is predicated of him cannot differ from his essence; goodness, wisdom, power, and so forth, and especially _ἀγεννησια_, are distinguished from the essence neither _realiter_ nor in our thought; therefore, whoever names one attribute, for example, unbegottenness, thereby expresses the whole divine essence; hence the Son cannot be God as the Father is; see Eunomius, _Liber apologeticus_, _Bibl. patr. gr. dogm._, ed. Thilo II 581-615.
In the Middle Ages many arrived at the same thought.
The perfections of God are distinguished neither _virtualiter_ nor _formaliter_ (Duns Scotus) from the essence and from one another, but only in name.
The concept of one is contained in that of the other, _involvitur unum in altero_.
Whoever calls God good thereby at the same time calls him just, powerful, and so forth, Stöckl, _Phil. des M. A._ II 968.
This subjective conception of God’s attributes also occurs in Arabic and Jewish philosophy, Stöckl, ib. 18, 27, 60, 88, 268 f.; Kaufmann, _Gesch. der Attributenlehre in der jüd. Rel. Philos. des M. A. von Saadja bis Maimuni_, 1877.
Spinoza understood by attribute _id quod intellectus de substantia percipit tanquam ejusdem essentiam constituens_, _Eth._ I def. 4.
Depending on whether the emphasis is placed upon the first or the second part of this definition, judgments differ as to whether Spinoza regarded the attributes as subjective conceptions of the intellect or as objective, real properties of substance, Falckenberg, _Gesch. d. neuern Philos._, S. 90.
The pantheism that gained entry into philosophy through him no longer has any place for the attributes of God.
God no longer has a being distinct from the world, no life of his own.
His attributes are identical with the laws of the world, Strauss, _Gl._ I 613.
Schleiermacher therefore also defined them wholly subjectively, as _etwas Besonderes in der Art, das schlechthinige Abhängigkeitsgefühl auf Gott zu beziehen_; their origin lies in religious _Dichtung_; they are without speculative content; they express neither God’s essence, which is unknowable, nor his relations to the world, since God would then have to stand in manifold relations to the world; they are simply subjective conceptions without an objective ground.
He therefore did not treat the doctrine of the attributes separately either, but throughout the whole of dogmatics, _Chr. Gl._ § 50.

Against this conception of the names of God, it must be maintained on the ground of God’s revelation that every attribute is certainly identical with the divine essence, but that the attributes are nevertheless still distinct.
Basil and Gregory of Nyssa taught this in their writings against Eunomius.
On the one hand, they said that the attributes did not differ really, _τω πραγματι_, because God was simple and exalted above all composition, but on the other hand that they were not distinguished merely in name, _λογῳ_.
Avoiding both these extremes, they judged that the names of God differed _κατ’ ἐπινοιαν_, that in our intellect we had different _ἐννοιαι, ἐπινοιαι, ἐπενθυμησεις_ of the one and the same divine essence, and thus that with the different attributes, such as goodness, wisdom, and so forth, we not only used different names but also actually thought something different.
The concepts we place in the names of God are therefore mutually distinct.
The names of God were therefore not to be mixed or confused with one another; each could be considered by itself.
Thus God was certainly identical with the attributes _divinitas, bonitas, sapientia, paternitas, filietas_, and so forth, but these are not for that reason identical in conception with one another.
One attribute is thus not so involved in the other that we cannot think the one without the other; rather, every attribute expresses something distinctive, Petavius, I c. 7-10; Schwane, _Dogmengesch._ II², S. 19-31.
Augustine states even more strongly that every attribute is God’s essence and to that extent identical with every other attribute.
_Quidquid enim secundum qualitates_—namely, of God—_dici videtur, secundum substantiam vel essentiam est intelligendum_, _de trin._ XV 5.
_Deo autem hoc est esse, quod est fortem esse aut justum esse aut sapientem esse etc._, _de trin._ VI 4; cf. above, p. 89.
The attributes are not mutually different either: _quae justitia ipsa bonitas et quae bonitas ipsa beatitudo_, _de trin._ XV 8.
_Eadem magnitudo ejus est, quae sapientia, non enim mole magnus est sed virtute; et eadem bonitas quae sapientia et magnitudo et eadem veritas quae illa omnia; et non est ibi aliud beatum esse et aliud magnum aut sapientem aut verum, aut bonum esse aut omnino ipsum esse_, _de trin._ VI 6.
Indeed, he says explicitly that these _praedicamenta_ or _qualitates_, when used of God, are properly _affectiones_ of our mind: _quidquid horum de Deo dicis, neque aliud et aliud intelligitur et nihil digne dicitur, quia haec animarum sunt, quas illa lux perfundit quodam modo et pro suis qualitatibus afficit, quomodo cum oritur corporibus lux ista visibilis. Si auferatur, unus est corporibus omnibus color, qui potius dicendus est nullus color. Cum autem illata illustraverit corpora, quamvis ipsa unius modi sit, pro diversis tamen corporum qualitatibus diverso eos nitore adspergit. Ergo animarum sunt istae affectiones, quae bene sunt affectae ab illa luce quae non afficitur et formatae ab illa quae non formatur_, _Serm._ 341 n. 8.
But however strongly Augustine speaks here, he nevertheless fully maintains that all these _praedicamenta_ are rightly and truly used of God.
God is all that he has and all that is ascribed to him in the names.
In this _simplicitas Dei_, Augustine is concerned not to take anything away from God but, on the contrary, always to conceive him in the fullness of his being.
Therefore he also speaks of the _simplex multiplicitas_ or _multiplex simplicitas_ in God, _de trin._ VI 4, and calls God’s wisdom _simpliciter multiplex et uniformiter multiformis_, _de civ._ XII 18.
In later times the distinction between _ratio ratiocinans_ and _ratio ratiocinata_ was used to bring some resolution to the difficulty that arose in the doctrine of the attributes.
On the one hand, the distinction among the attributes was not permitted to detract from the unity, simplicity, and immutability of God’s essence; on the other hand, it could not be understood as a subjective, arbitrary, and false invention of the human being.
It was therefore rightly said that this distinction was grounded in God’s revelation itself.
After all, we are not the ones who name God in this way.
We do not invent those names.
On the contrary, if it depended upon us, we would remain silent about him, try to forget him, and deny all his names.
We take no delight in the knowledge of his ways.
Again and again we resist all his names—his independence, his sovereignty, his righteousness, his love—and we rebel against all his perfections.
But it is God himself who reveals all his perfections and places his names upon our lips.
He gives himself his names and maintains them despite our resistance.
It is of little use for us to deny his righteousness; he shows it to us every day in history.
And so it is with all the perfections.
Despite ourselves, he brings them to revelation.
The final goal of all his ways is that his name may shine in all his works and stand written upon every forehead, Rev. 22:4.
Therefore we cannot name him otherwise than with the many names that his revelation itself places at our disposal.
This distinction among the attributes is also not in conflict with the simplicity of God.
For _simplicitas_ does not designate God as an abstract concept, as the Absolute, being, the idea, and so forth, but makes known precisely that he is an absolute fullness of being.
And therefore God can reveal himself to finite creatures only under many names.
The divine essence is so infinitely rich that no creature can survey it all at once.
Just as a child cannot conceive the value of a large coin but first gains some awareness of it when its value is laid out in a number of smaller coins, so we too can form no conception of the infinite fullness of God’s essence unless it reveals itself to us each time in a different relation and under a different point of view, Augustine, _tract._ 13 _in Ev. Joan._; Martyr, _Loci_, p. 39; Moor, _Comm._ I 582, and so forth.
God remains eternally and immutably one and the same, but the relation in which he stands to his creatures and they stand to him differs.
The light remains the same even though it breaks into different colors (Augustine).
Fire does not change whether it warms, illumines, or burns (Moses Maimonides).
And grain remains grain, though according to its different relations we call it at one time fruit, then seed, then food (Basil).
God is named differently on account of the _varii effectus_ that he produces in creatures through his always one and the same essence.
Here it must be remembered that God can appear in such different attributes and be named by such different names because there is a kinship between him and his creatures.
If this kinship did not exist, all names would be false.
But as it is, there is in creatures an analogy of what is present in God himself.
The names do not merely designate God as the cause of things; however weakly and imperfectly, they nevertheless give some conception of the divine essence.
Thus when we designate God by all these names, we indeed speak in an imperfect, finite, limited, and human manner, but not falsely and untruthfully.
For although knowing and willing, righteousness and grace, are in God one and always the same full and entire essence, God nevertheless unfolds that one rich essence in these many perfections, as it were, successively and alongside one another before our eyes.
Although it is always the same essence that meets us in those names, in every name we are given a conception of what that essence truly is in infinite fullness.
Even though holiness and mercy may be the same in God with respect to the essence, our conceptions of the two, formed from God’s own revelation, are nevertheless different.
There is no name that can express God’s essence adequately; many names therefore serve to give us an impression of his all-transcending greatness.
