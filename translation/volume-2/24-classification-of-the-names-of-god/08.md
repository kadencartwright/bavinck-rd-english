## 8.

As was already said above, in the earliest period everything that could be thought and said of God was comprehended under his names.
But the wealth of material made ordering necessary.
Some delimitation arose immediately as the term names gradually came to be used for the _nomina propria_—God, Lord, and so forth.
Then the doctrine of the Trinity was soon treated separately, either before or after the so-called attributes, and in a terminology entirely its own.
Further, the distinction between essence and attributes entailed that the former was separated from the latter, and the doctrine of the attributes thus came to stand by itself.
The division that now lay most readily at hand for these attributes, and that is also the oldest and first, was that into negative and positive names.
For as soon as people began to reflect on the way in which they had arrived at these names, they saw that the names had been obtained from creatures either by the _via negationis_ or by the _via eminentiae_ and _causalitatis_.
We already find this division, or at least this twofold or respectively threefold way of learning to know God, in Philo and Plotinus, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._, S. 355 f., 483; cf. also Herzog² 10, 521.
Among the church fathers God was both unknowable and knowable, unknowable according to his essence and knowable according to his revelation; on the one hand only what he is not could be said of God, but on the other hand something positive could nevertheless be predicated of him in a defective and inadequate manner.
Pseudo-Dionysius, _De myst. theol._ I 2; Damascene, _De fide orth._ I 2, 4; and Erigena, _De div. nat._ I 78, explicitly worked this thought out into a division, into a twofold theology, the apophatic and the cataphatic.
The first-named indicates the three ways in so many words when he says that we come to knowledge of God ἐν τῃ παντων ἀφαιρεσει και ὑπεροχῃ και εν τῃ παντων αἰτιᾳ, _De div. nom._, c. 7, §3.
And scholasticism began to speak of three _viae_ by which one came to knowledge of God, the _via negationis_, _eminentiae_, and _causalitatis_, especially since Durandus de S. Porciano.
These three _viae_ were acknowledged in dogmatics down to the most recent times.
Roman Catholics, Lutherans, and Reformed theologians all adopted them and sometimes treated them at length.
But they have also repeatedly been sharply criticized.
Spinoza rejected the _via eminentiae_ with the observation that a triangle, if it could speak, would say _Deum eminenter triangularem esse_, _Epist._ 60; cf. Xenophanes in Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ I 490.
Kant thought that the path of causality could be followed only within the sphere of the _phaenomena_.
Schleiermacher rejected the _via negationis_ and _eminentiae_ and retained only the _via causalitatis_, _Chr. Gl._ §§50, 51; cf. further Twesten II 30 f.; Strauss, _Gl._ I 536 f.; Bruch, _Lehre v. d. göttl. Eigensch._ 83 f.; Dorner I 188 f.; Vilmar I 190; Doedes, _L. v. God_ 208; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I² 228; Lange, _Dogm._ II 42 f.; Hodge I 339; Wichelhaus, _Die Lehre der H. Schrift vom Worte Gottes u. s. w._, 1892, S. 332 f.; etc.
Now it is certainly true that the names of God existed long before these three _viae_ were devised, that the latter arose only later from reflection upon the names, and that the _via eminentiae_ and _via causalitatis_ are really one and can thus together be set as the _via positionis_ over against the _via negationis_; but it is just as certain that there is no knowledge of God except from the creatures, that all the names of God are derived from the creatures, and that all these names either deny something of God or posit in God, in a perfect manner, something found in creatures.
Only it must never be forgotten in this connection that the _ratio cognoscendi_ is not identical with the _ratio essendi_.
In reality it is not the creature but God who is first.
He is archetype; the creature is ectype.
In him everything is original and absolute; in creatures everything is derived and relative.
God is therefore, properly speaking, not named after what is in creatures, but creatures are named after what exists in God; cf. above, p. 74 f.
Keeping this in view, one can nevertheless say that Holy Scripture too follows both these ways.
And everyone who wishes to think and speak about God does so according to the negative or positive relation in which God stands to the world, F. A. B. Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, S. 400.
This certainly also explains why the division of the names of God into negative and positive arose so quickly, found such general acceptance, and in fact underlies all the other divisions that arose later.
This division is already found in Philo, Plotinus, and the church fathers, and is then used by Damascene, _De fide orthod._ I, c. 4, 9, 12; Anselm, _Monol._, c. 15; _Prosl._, c. 5, 6; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, art. 2; Petavius, _Theol. dogm._ I, c. 5; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II, p. 91; etc.
Among Roman Catholics this division is the most common, although some Reformed and Lutheran theologians have also adopted it.
Within this division further distinctions are then also made.
Augustine, _Serm._ 38, _De tempore_, already observed that some names of God were used _proprie_, others _metaphorice_, and still others _relative_.
Accordingly, the _nomina negativa_ were again divided into purely negative and relative names, and the _nomina positiva_ into _propria_ and _metaphorica_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, artt. 2 and 3.
Alongside this division into negative and positive attributes another also arose.
Plato had already taught that God is good through himself, but creatures only through μετοχη.
In Christian theology this thought bore mature fruit, especially in Augustine.
Over against pantheism it was maintained that God’s essence was incommunicable and that the soul was no _pars Dei_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 90, art. 1; Zanchius, _Op._ II, col. 53 sq.
But at the same time it was taught that all creatures were related to God and that the human being in particular was his image and likeness.
There was analogy between _Creator_ and _creatura_.
This led to the division into _attributa communicabilia_ and _incommunicabilia_.
Both these terms had first come into use in the doctrine of the Trinity, for the essence of God, the _deitas_, was _communicabilis_, since it was communicated by the Father to the Son in generation, but the persons and the personal properties, for example, the _paternitas_, were _incommunicabiles_, Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 2, §5; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ I, c. 4; idem, _Sent._ I, dist. 27, p. 1, art. 1, qu. 3; etc.
From there these two terms were also adopted in the doctrine of the attributes in order to maintain both God’s transcendence and God’s immanence, Anselm, _Monol._, c. 15; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, artt. 9, 10.
This division found acceptance especially among Reformed theologians, no doubt partly because it offered a suitable opportunity to contest the Lutheran doctrine of ubiquity, Sohnius, _Op._ I 97; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 50–51; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, lib. II, c. 7 and 14; Mastricht II 5, 12; etc.; and it is still found today in Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 337, and Gravemeyer, _Geref. Geloofsleer_, part 3, p. 62; etc.
Yet all concede that the communicable attributes, in the absolute sense in which they are in God, are just as incommunicable as the others; cf. Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, S. 52 f.
The Lutherans generally preferred another division—namely, that into _attributa quiescentia_ and _operativa_, or _immanentia_ and _extra se exserentia_—which was also employed in defense of the doctrine of the _communicatio idiomatum_, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, S. 78.
Under the first group (negative, incommunicable, metaphysical, quiescent) were then usually treated the attributes of unity, simplicity, independence, immutability, eternity, and omnipresence.
The second group (positive, communicable, operative, personal) was generally subdivided into attributes of understanding, will, and power.
The division into attributes of being, understanding, and will in Bretschneider I 480; Thomasius I 14 f.; Vilmar I 195; Oosterzee §47; cf. Frank I 232 f.; Philippi II 23—to which Hase, _Lehrb._, S. 271, also adds those of feeling—differs only slightly from the divisions named above.
By contrast, Schleiermacher’s division proceeds from a different standpoint.
For him the attributes are merely subjective conceptions and are grounded only in the _ratio ratiocinans_.
And so, according to God’s relation to the world, sin, and redemption, he divides them into those that are present in the feeling of dependence when the antithesis of sin has not yet arisen within it (eternity, omnipresence, omnipotence, omniscience); those present when that antithesis is indeed felt (holiness, righteousness); and those present when it has been overcome (love and wisdom).
In agreement with this, many divide the attributes according to God’s relation to the world, and then specifically to the world _überhaupt_ (infinity, eternity, omnipresence), to the ethical world (holiness, righteousness; grace, mercy), and to the ethical-physical world (wisdom, blessedness), Wegscheider §60; Böhme, _Lehre v. d. göttl. Eig._, 1826; Bruch, _Lehre v. d. göttl. Eig._, S. 110 f.; Lange, _Dogm._ II 61 f.; Martensen §47; Luthardt, _Komp. der Dogm._, §29; Lipsius, _Dogm._, §298; Grétillat, _Exposé de theol. syst._ III 220; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. godsd._ II 92–99; etc.
