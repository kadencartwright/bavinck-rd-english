## 3.

But this doctrine of God’s incomprehensibility and of the unknowability of his essence has also been the point of departure and fundamental thought of Christian theology. God is not exhausted in his revelation, either in creation or in re-creation. He cannot communicate himself fully to creatures, since they would then themselves have to be God. There is therefore no adequate knowledge of God. There is no name that makes his essence known to us. There is no concept that fully comprehends him. There is no description that completely defines him. What lies behind revelation is utterly unknowable. We can approach it neither with our thought or imagination nor with our language. Already the Epistle of Barnabas, c. 5, asks: if the Son of God had not come in the flesh, how could human beings have seen him and remained alive? Justin Martyr calls God ineffable, immovable, nameless, _Apol._ I 61; even words such as Father, God, Lord are not proper names, ἀλλ’ ἐκ των εὐποιϊων και των ἐργων προσρησεις, _Apol._ II 6. God cannot appear, walk, be seen, and so forth; if the Old Testament speaks of this, it refers to the Son, who is his messenger, _Dial._ 127. In Irenaeus too, _Adv. haer._ IV 20, there occurs the contrast—very common in his time but nevertheless incorrect and partly Gnostic—between the Father, who is hidden, invisible, and unknowable, and the Son, who revealed him. In Clement of Alexandria God is pure μονας, _Strom._ V 11. If in thought we remove everything creaturely, we attain not to what God is but to what he is not. Form, motion, place, number, quality, name, and so forth cannot be attributed to him. If we nevertheless call him one, good, Father, Creator, Lord, and so forth, we do not thereby express his proper essence but only indicate his power, _Strom._ V 12. He is exalted even above the one, ὑπερ αὐτην την μοναδα, _Paedag._ I 8. In a word, God is, as Athanasius says, _C. gent._ 2, ἐπεκεινα πασης οὐσιας και ἀνθρωπινης ἐννοιας. Origen speaks similarly, _De princ._ I 1, 5 sq.; _C. Cels._ VI 65; Eusebius, _Praep. Evang._ V 1; Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ I 3; Tatian, _C. Gr._ 5; Minucius Felix, _Octav._ c. 18; Novatian, _De trin._ 2; Cyprian, _De idol. vanit._ 5; Lactantius, _Instit. div._ I 6. Cf. Münscher–von Coelln, _Dogmengesch._ I 132 f.; Hagenbach, _Dogm._ §37; Schwane, _Dogmengesch._ I² 72 f. We encounter the same conception in Augustine and John of Damascus. In defining God, Augustine proceeds from the concept of being. He is the one who is, as his name YHWH also indicates; this is his proper name, his _nomen in se_; all other names are _ad nos_, _Serm._ 6, n. 4; _Serm._ 7, n. 7. Therefore, when we wish to say what he is, we say only what, in distinction from all finite substances, he is not. He is _ineffabilis_. _Facilius dicemus quid non sit quam quid sit._ He is not earth, sea, heaven, angel, and so forth, nothing of all that the creature is; _hoc solum potui dicere quid non sit_, _Enarr. in Ps._ 85, n. 12; _De doctr. chr._ I 6; _De ord._ II 47. He is thought in such a way _ut aliquid quo nihil melius sit atque sublimius illa cogitatio conetur attingere_, _De doctr. chr._ I 7. But he cannot be thought as he is. For he is exalted above everything corporeal, mutable, and originated, _Tract._ 23 _in Ev. Joh._, n. 9. _Quis est autem, qui sic cogitat Deum, quemadmodum ille est?_ _Quaest. in Jos._ VI 29. He is incomprehensible and must be so: _si enim comprehendis, non est Deus_, _Serm._ 117, n. 5. And when finally we wish to say what we think of him, we struggle with language. _Verius enim cogitatur Deus quam dicitur, et verius est quam cogitatur_, _De trin._ VII 4; _De doctr. chr._ I 6. If we nevertheless say something concerning him, it is not adequate but serves only to enable us to say something and to cause us to think of a being who surpasses everything, _De doctr. chr._ I 6. _Sicut Deus a nullo intellectu valet proprie cogitari, ita nulla definitione potest proprie definiri aut determinari_, _De cogn. verae vitae_ 7. _Deus scitur melius nesciendo_, _De ord._ II 44. Likewise, Damascene says that God, το θειον, is ἀρρητον και ἀκαταληπτον, _De fide orth._ I, c. 1. We speak of God in our manner and know what God has revealed of himself, but what God’s essence is and how he is in all things, we do not know, ibid., c. 2. That God is, is clear; but τι ἐστιν κατ’ οὐσιαν και φυσιν, ἀκαταληπτον τουτο παντελως και ἀγνωστον. That God is unbegotten, immutable, without beginning, and so forth says only what he is not. To say what he is is impossible. He is none of all beings, not because he is not, but because he is ὑπερ παντα τα ὀντα και ὑπερ αὐτο το εἰναι. What we say positively of God indicates not his nature but the relations surrounding his nature, τα περι την φυσιν, ibid., c. 4; cf. c. 9.
