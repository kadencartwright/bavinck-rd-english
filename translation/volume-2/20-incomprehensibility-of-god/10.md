## 10.

To a considerable degree this doctrine of the unknowability of God can be conceded and wholeheartedly affirmed. Scripture and church both emphatically maintain the unsearchable majesty and sovereign highness of God. There is no knowledge of God as he is in himself. We are human beings, and he is the Lord our God. There is no name that fully expresses his essence, no definition that circumscribes him. He is infinitely exalted above our representation, above our thought, above our language. He can be compared with no creature; all nations are accounted before him as less than nothing and vanity. _Deo nomen non est. Deus definiri nequit._ He can be _apprehendi_, not _comprehendi_. There is some γνωσις but no καταληψις of God. So it is said throughout the whole of Scripture and the whole of Christian theology. And when a superficial rationalism considered an adequate knowledge of God possible, Christian theology always opposed it most strongly. According to Socrates, _Hist. Eccl._ IV 7, Eunomius, a follower of Arius, is said to have taught that he knew God as well as he knew himself. According to him, God’s essence consisted solely in ἀγεννησια, and in this he had a clear, distinct, adequate concept of the divine essence; God was no more known to himself and no less known to man than was expressed in this predicate. Later Spinoza said that he had as clear a concept of God as of a triangle, although by this he did not wish to claim _Deum omnino cognoscere_, _Epist._ 60. And in this century Hegel taught that in the philosophy of the concept the absolute came to complete self-consciousness and was therefore known completely and adequately by the philosopher; the consciousness of God within us is nothing other than God’s consciousness of himself. God is insofar as he is known by us, that is, insofar as he knows himself in us. This rationalism was opposed and rejected with all force in the Christian church, Basil, _Hexaem._, hom. 1; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat._ 28; etc.; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ II 27 f. Indeed, a profound religious interest is involved here. Augustine once expressed it in this manner: _de Deo loquimur, quid mirum si non comprehendis? Si enim comprehendis, non est Deus. Sit pia confessio ignorantiae magis, quam temeraria professio scientiae. Attingere aliquantum mente Deum, magna beatitudo est; comprehendere autem, omnino impossibile_, _Serm._ 38 _in Ev. Joan._ God is therefore precisely the only object of all our love because he is the Infinite and Incomprehensible One. Yet although Scripture and church thus accept, as it were, the premises of agnosticism and are penetrated even more deeply than Kant or Spencer by man’s limitation and God’s incomparable greatness, they nevertheless derive from them another conclusion, a conclusion expressed by Hilary, _De trin._ II 7, as follows: _perfecta scientia est, sic Deum scire, ut licet non ignorabilem, tamen inenarrabilem scias_. The knowledge we have of God is entirely distinctive. It can be called positive insofar as through it we acknowledge a being who is infinite and essentially distinct from all finite creatures. On the other hand it is negative because we can attribute no predicate to God as we think it in creatures. And it is therefore analogical, since it is knowledge of a being who, unknowable in himself, can nevertheless make something of himself known in creatures.

There is indeed something of an antinomy here—or rather, agnosticism suffers from a confusion of concepts and therefore sees an irreconcilable contradiction here, while Christian theology beholds an adorable mystery. It is entirely incomprehensible to us that and how God can reveal and to some extent make himself known in the creature, eternity in time, immensity in space, infinity in the finite, immutability in change, being in becoming, the all, as it were, in nothing. This mystery cannot be comprehended; it can only be gratefully acknowledged. But mystery is something other than contradiction. Pantheistic philosophy has made it one. When it identifies the absolute with the indeterminate and calls every determination a limitation and negation, it makes itself guilty of a confusion of concepts. There is a world of difference between infinite and endless, between almighty and the sum of all power, between absolute being and the sum of all being, between eternity and the totality of all moments of time, and so forth, and likewise between the absolute and the indeterminate, unlimited, boundless. Pantheism first places its own concept of God into these words and can then easily accuse theism of conflicting with it. That God is the Infinite and nevertheless can reveal himself in finite creatures and has also actually revealed himself is indeed the acknowledgment of an incomprehensible mystery, namely the wonder of creation, but by no means the admission of a palpable absurdity. Finite being cannot detract from God’s infinity, provided that it has its existence absolutely in God’s being. And likewise our knowledge is no limitation of God because it is grounded in him himself, can exist only through him, and has him himself precisely as Infinite for its object and content. Moreover, if the absolute excludes every limitation and every determination is a negation, then it is not only impermissible to speak of God as personality, but it is equally wrong still to call him the absolute, the one, the good, being, substance, and so forth. Pantheism labors under the delusion that it has finished with the concept of God when it has removed personality and self-consciousness from it as contradictory elements. And in reaction the theistic philosophers from the first half of this century, such as J. H. Fichte, Carus, Steffens, Weisse, Ulrici, and others, attached themselves too strongly to this concept of personality and thought that in the concept of absolute personality they possessed an adequate description of God’s essence. Certainly many good observations were made over against pantheism to prove that personality does not conflict with God’s absolute being. If _absolute_ is not conceived as boundless, as endlessly extended in all directions, then it cannot be seen how personality would conflict with it. It then implies only that God’s self-consciousness is as rich and deep, as infinite, as his being. Personality may indeed awaken in us human beings over against a non-ego, but in God it is the eternal gathering of himself with himself, infinite self-knowledge and self-determination, and is therefore not dependent on a non-ego. J. H. Fichte, _Die Idee der Persönlichkeit u. der indiv. Fortdauer_, 1855, p. 52 f.; J. W. Hanne, _Die Idee der absol. Persönl._, 2e Aufl., 1865; Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I 552 f.; Lotze, _Mikrok._ III³ 549–580; Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ §39; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 213–223; Kaftan, _Das Wesen der Chr. Religion_, 378 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ §229; Martensen, _Dogm._ 75; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 11 f.; Lange, _Dogm._ I 167, II 53; Bruch, _Lehre v. d. göttl. Eigensch._, 122 f.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 53 f.; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 391; Bovon, _Dogm. Chrét._ I 229 s.; Doedes, _Inl. tot de Leer van God_, 74 f. Yet what the older Fichte said is true: personality is a concept derived from ourselves and therefore, when transferred to God, always contains something imperfect. The concept of personality, used of God, is inadequate and in principle no better than all the other anthropomorphisms in which we speak of God. The Christian church and theology also never used this word for the essence of God and spoke of persons in connection with the three _modi subsistendi_ in that essence only hesitantly and for lack of something better. But at the same time it is clear from this that pantheism has gained nothing when it points to the incongruity of this concept. The opposition between absoluteness and personality is essentially the same as that between negative and positive theology. Even if the word _personality_ is wrong, in fact nothing has yet been decided by that. The same question therefore continually returns. If every determination is a negation, then God may not be called the one, being, or the absolute either. Then all thinking and speaking about God is forbidden. If we, who are human beings, may not speak of God in an analogical, human manner, then nothing remains to us but silence. For we cannot think and speak of God in a divine manner. But then all religion also falls. If God cannot be known, neither can he be felt and enjoyed in feeling. Feeling is as finite as the understanding and finitizes and humanizes God in the same manner. There is then no possibility that God objectively reveals himself in his creatures or that we subjectively perceive him through any organ. All religion is then sacrilege, and all theology is blasphemy against God.
