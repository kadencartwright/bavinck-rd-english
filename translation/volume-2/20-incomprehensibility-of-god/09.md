## 9.

The grounds to which this agnosticism can appeal are of very great weight. First, it can make use of the argument advanced from ancient times by Sophists and skeptics against the possibility of all knowledge, namely that all human knowledge is subjective and relative. Nothing in the world stands isolated in itself; object and subject are dependent upon each other. Things and their qualities exist only then and in that way when they stand in relation to someone’s perception; something first becomes something through its relation to the subject’s senses. We can therefore never say what a thing is in its essence independently of our perception; we can state only that at a given moment something appears thus to us: ἀνθρωπος μετρον παντων. This argument is indeed of great significance, but it proves too much. If it were valid, it would make impossible not only the knowledge of God but all knowledge of man and world. Moreover, this idealism was already discussed in Volume I, pp. 145–151, and can therefore be left out of consideration here. But agnosticism has various grounds that are advanced specifically against the knowability of God. Philosophy and theology, as became evident above, were indeed convinced at all times of the inadequacy of our knowledge of God. Negative predicates say only what God is not, and positive predicates belong to him in an entirely different manner than they are found in creatures. The restricted, finite, anthropomorphic character of our knowledge of God was acknowledged by all. But in modern times the unattainability of the knowledge of God has been argued in another and more emphatic manner. Subjectively, attention was drawn to the limited nature of the human faculty of knowledge; objectively, to the internal contradiction from which every concept of God suffers. Kant did the former, Fichte the latter. Kant investigated the human faculty of knowledge and reached the conclusion that the forms of intuition and the categories of the understanding were supplied by the subject itself, possessed validity in the world of _phaenomena_, but also made all true knowledge of _noumena_ impossible for us. The transcendent ideas of God, world, and soul are indeed rules for our action, and a moral person cannot act otherwise than as if an objective reality corresponded to these three ideas; but this cannot be proved. Fichte added the objection that the concepts of absoluteness and personality can never be united with each other. Spinoza had already said that every determination was a negation, _Epist._ 50, and that the more determined and concrete a thing became, the more finite and restricted it also was. It thereby ceases to be what other things are: if something is white, it can no longer be red or black. God therefore cannot be something determinate alongside and in distinction from creatures, but he is the substance of all creatures, the one infinite being, so that all that is is in him, _Eth._ I, props. 14, 15. This philosophical premise was applied by Fichte to the concept of personality; personality and self-consciousness were things that we found within ourselves and that cannot be thought without restriction and finitude. If we transfer them to God, we make him a finite, restricted, human being. The moral world order is therefore the one thing needful in religion; we need nothing more. A particular personal existence of God is unnecessary for religion, and it is also indemonstrable, indeed impossible and self-contradictory. Whoever desires such a God is still captive to eudaimonism, _Ueber den Grund unseres Glaubens an eine göttliche Weltregierung_, 1798, _Werke_ V 184 f.; cf. Kuno Fischer, _Gesch. der neuern Philos._ V² 628 f. This reasoning then spread in wide circles and recurs continually; cf. Schelling in his first period, _Werke_ I 1, p. 200; Hegel, _Werke_ V 18; Schleiermacher, _Reden über die Religion_³, p. 167 f.; Schopenhauer, _Parerga und Paral._ I⁵ 123 f.; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._ II⁹ 175 f.; idem, _Religionsphil._ II 145–163; idem, _Lotzes Philos._, 1888, p. 168 f.; Strauss, _Christl. Gl._ I 502 f.; Biedermann, _Dogm._ II² 457–472, 537–547; etc. In essence this opposition between absolute and personal is no other than the one always felt in Christian theology and expressed in negative and positive, apophatic and kataphatic theology. However much the proofs against the knowability of God are repeatedly presented in another form, in fact they are always the same and continually amount to this. Man is bound to sensory perception and always derives the material of his thought from the visible world. He does not intuit the spiritual and cannot raise himself to the world of invisible things, since he always remains bound to space and time. His thought too is material, finite, and restricted. Just as the eagle remains enclosed within the atmosphere (Hamilton) and the fish within the pond (Lange), so our thought always moves within the sphere of the finite. More than this, thought is of such a nature that it of itself presupposes a separation between subject and object, which, standing over against and restricting each other, can neither of them be absolute. Hamilton therefore said, “To think is to condition,” and Mansel expressed the same as follows: “Distinction is necessarily limitation.” Knowledge of the absolute is thus a contradiction, for it indicates that one has knowledge concerning something that is absolute and therefore stands in no relation, yet precisely as known by a subject stands in relation after all. If the nature of thought is constituted in this way, and if we nevertheless wish to think and know God, then we always do one of two things. Either we draw the absolute down within the sphere of the finite and make God into a personal, restricted, human being; or in our thought we try to raise ourselves above all limits of space and time, strip our thought concerning God of all conformity to the finite creature, but then retain in our thought nothing but an empty, abstract, contentless concept that has not the slightest value for religion. Indeed, even that concept eludes us; under our thinking the absolute becomes nothing. Absoluteness and personality, infinity and causality, immutability and communicability, absolute exaltation above and conformity to the creature appear irreconcilable in the concept of God. We find ourselves in an insoluble antinomy. It is as if only the choice were left us between a crude realism and a thin idealism, between a God who is nothing but a magnified man and a cold abstraction that causes the religion of the heart to freeze and die.
