## 11.

The question of God’s knowability has thereby been reduced to this other question: whether God has willed and been able to reveal himself in the world of creatures. For what Kant says is entirely true, that our knowledge extends no further than our experience, _Kr. der r. Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, 508. If God has not revealed himself, then there is also no knowledge of him. But if he has revealed himself, then there is something, however little, that falls under our perception and can thus lead to knowledge. It then speaks for itself, however, that the denial of God’s knowability coincides entirely with the denial that God has revealed himself in the works of his hands. Agnosticism falls back into the error of ancient Gnosticism. God is ὁ Βυθος ἀρρητος, eternal σιγη; there is no communion and no kinship between him and his creatures. The entire world is in the absolute sense ἀθεος; no trace of God can be perceived in the world. The world is the product of a lower god, a demiurge, or chance; man was not created in God’s image but is simply a product of nature, without God in the world. No _religio_, no _theologia_, is possible, either from creation or from re-creation. God and world are absolutely separated. Worse still, agnosticism cannot escape the consequence that God cannot reveal himself. It locks him up within himself; it makes him into an unknown, invisible power that has no consciousness and no will, can communicate itself in no way, and is eternal σιγη. And the world, as in Manichaeism, is elevated into a power alongside and over against God, which can receive him within itself in no way and is entirely unfit to radiate anything of his glory. Agnosticism thus in fact ends in atheism, of which it is only the nineteenth-century form and name. Yet agnosticism usually does not go so far and still maintains the existence of the Unknowable. But then it immediately suffers from an internal inconsistency that is untenable. Augustine already observed that the proposition that we know nothing of God presupposes a large measure of knowledge concerning God, and that there was thus a _pugna verborum_. For when we call God _ineffabilis_, at that very moment we say something, and much, of him, so that once again he is not _ineffabilis_, _De doctr. chr._ I 6. _Non enim parvae notitiae pars est . . . si antequam scire possimus quid sit Deus, possumus jam scire quid non sit_, _De trin._ VIII 2. Not-knowing is itself a positive, comprehensive knowing; it is _non parva inchoatio cognitionis Dei_, _Ep. ad Consent._ It says this much already, namely that he is none of all that is creature. So it is also among the agnostics. Spencer, for example, says that through our thought we are compelled to accept the existence of an absolute being as the ground of the universe, although because of our finitude and limitation we can form no concept of it. But if we are so finite and limited, how do we come to form the thought of such an absolute being and accept its existence? And if we actually must accept the existence of such an absolute being, why would any knowledge of that being be entirely impossible? No agnostic therefore confines himself to the _non liquet_. Spencer, for example, says continually that we do not know the absolute, and yet he has an idea of it, proves its existence, and also attributes all manner of qualities to it. He says that it is not a negative but a positive concept, that it is the cause of everything, that it is a power most analogous to our will, that it is infinite, eternal, omnipresent, and so forth. Surely this is no longer agnosticism but a very definite knowledge and a fairly well-defined concept of God. Agnosticism, untenable in itself and fearful of atheism, finally serves to justify a pantheistic concept of God.
