## 8.

Since then the doctrine of God’s unknowability has penetrated ever more deeply into the consciousness of the modern age. Hegel admittedly occupied a different standpoint. While acknowledging that religious representation was inadequate and suited only to the people, he nevertheless considered philosophy capable of stripping this representation of its sensible form and thus elevating it into an adequate concept. In his opinion, reason gradually ascended through various stages to absolute being, then saw the truth face to face, and knew its essence to be reason, thought, the concept itself. Philosophy, pure science, or more specifically logic, is the description of God in his essence, _an sich_. It comprehends the Absolute in its fitting, adequate form, as thought, in the form of the concept, _Werke_ III, S. 33 f. In his spirit many attempted, by purifying and deepening concepts, to come ever closer to transcendent reality, such as Strauss, _Gl._ I 523 f., 547 f., 609 f.; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ II², S. 516 f.; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._ II⁹ 155 f., 175 f.; _Religionsphilos._ 608 f.; Scholten, _Initia_, p. 109 sq.; _L. H. K._ II 199, 200. But in others Hegel’s philosophy led to an entirely different result: they maintained that in the idea of God representation could never be overcome and therefore arrived at atheism. Feuerbach said that the personal God was nothing other than the essence of the human being himself, and theology nothing but anthropology. Everything predicated of God is derived from the human being—not only personality but all attributes and names. Religion is a deification of the human being himself. The human being cannot rise above his own essence, and therefore God always is and remains a sensible, human being, not only in Christian dogmatics but also in philosophy, _Wesen des Christ._, 1841. Many spoke similarly and, along with the anthropomorphic representation, cast aside the entire idea of God, for example, Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_¹⁶, S. 392 f.; idem, _Der Gottesbegriff und dessen Bedeutung in der Gegenwart_², 1874; Haeckel, _Nat. Schöpfungsgesch._⁵, S. 30 f.; Strauss, _Der alte u. d. neue Glaube_², S. 122, 144 f.; Friedrich Nietzsche, _Zur Genealogie der Moral_, S. 179. But this atheism went too far for others. Attending to the limitation of the human faculty of knowledge, which always continues to move within the finite, they thought that the human being must confine himself to what is given in perception (positivism), refrain from every assertion concerning the existence and nature of the supersensible (abstentionism), and therefore confess only his absolute ignorance in this respect (agnosticism). In France, Auguste Comte limited the task of science to ascertaining and explaining phenomena and therefore also excluded theology from the circle of the sciences, cf. vol. I, p. 151 f. In England it was especially Herbert Spencer who contested the knowability of God with all manner of arguments, _First Principles_, 5th ed., 1887, pp. 68–97. And in Germany, after becoming satiated with Hegel’s panlogism, people returned to Kant’s critical philosophy. Thus in the latter half of this century agnosticism became almost universally dominant. All metaphysics is mistrusted; speculation is regarded with aversion; only what is positive and belongs to the domain of the _sciences exactes_ stands firm. Theology has fallen so much under the fear of this agnosticism that it scarcely dares any longer to speak of the knowledge of God; it tries as much as possible to exclude all metaphysics and to restrict itself to the religious; it is ashamed of its own name and has allowed itself to be rechristened a science of religion. For although agnosticism is really the death of theology, theology has nevertheless still been maintained by many in another form. Kant regained through practical reason what he had lost through theoretical reason. Spencer leaves room for a religious veneration of _the Unknowable_. Already before him Sir William Hamilton, in his essay _On the philosophy of the unconditioned_, _Edinb. Rev._, Oct. 1829, and Dr. Henry Longueville Mansel, in his work _The limits of religious thought examined in eight lectures, preached before the university of Oxford_, 1858, had maintained that because our thought is always bound to space, time, distinction, opposition, and so forth, it can never penetrate to the Absolute, even though on religious grounds the representation of God as a personal being must be maintained; cf. Dorner, _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ VI, 1861, S. 320 f., 347 f. The Neo-Kantians in Germany arrived at a similar dualism. Thought may at best bring us to the concept of the Absolute, but in religion that is nevertheless not enough for us. Here we need a God who is like us, whom we represent as a person and who cares for his children as a Father. Such a religious representation is indeed always subject to criticism from the side of science; it is not the highest and the true, but nothing more can be attained, Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 229 f., 243 f.; Ritschl, _Metaph. u. Rel._ 8 f.; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 211 f.; Rauwenhoff, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._ 611 f.; Hoekstra, _Wijsg. godsd._ II 11 f. Likewise, others have sought in humanity, ethical idealism, the formation of ideals, spiritism, theosophy, Buddhism, and so forth, a compensation for what science had taken from them in Christian theology, cf. vol. I, pp. 156, 202.
