## 5.

Although scholasticism expressed itself more cautiously on various points and in particular assigned a higher value to positive theology than did Pseudo-Dionysius and Erigena, it nevertheless agreed entirely with the doctrine that God’s essence _an sich_ was unknowable to man. Anselm says that the names of God indicate his essence only _per similitudinem_, _Monol._ c. 63; that relative attributes cannot be predicated of his essence, and absolute attributes only in a quidditative, not a qualitative, sense, _Monol._ capp. 15–17. According to Albert the Great, God is exalted above all being and thought. He cannot be reached by human thought, _attingitur sed non capitur per comprehensionem_. No name expresses his essence. He is incomprehensible and unnameable; cf. Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II 370. Thomas distinguishes a threefold knowledge of God: _visio Dei per essentiam, cognitio per fidem_, and _cognitio per rationem naturalem_. The first far surpasses man’s natural knowledge and can be obtained only through supernatural grace. It is reserved for heaven, is granted only a single time here on earth, and nevertheless never makes comprehension of God possible, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 12, artt. 1–11. Here on earth the knowledge of God is mediated. We cannot know God as he is in himself, but only _secundum quod omnium prima et eminentissima causa_ is. From the effects we can ascend to the cause, ibid., art. 12. The same is true of the knowledge we receive of God from his special revelation through faith. Through this we know him better, _in quantum plures et excellentiores effectus ejus nobis demonstrantur_, ibid., art. 13. But it gives no knowledge of God _per essentiam_. There is no knowledge of God’s essence, of his _quidditas_, _per speciem propriam_; we know only _habitudinem ipsius ad creaturas_, ibid., art. 12. There is no name that adequately expresses his essence. His _essentia_ stands high _supra id, quod de Deo intelligimus et voce significamus_, ibid., qu. 13, art. 1. Positive names may indicate God’s essence, but they do so in a very imperfect manner, just as the creatures from which they are derived represent him imperfectly. God is knowable only _secundum quod repraesentatur in perfectionibus creaturarum_, ibid., art. 2. But in the further development of scholasticism this truth of God’s incomprehensibility was pushed into the background. The doctrine of God was unfolded ever more broadly. Existence, names, essence, persons, and attributes were developed so subtly and precisely that no place remained for God’s incomprehensibility. It became an ordinary attribute alongside the others, treated just as extensively and dialectically. Against Thomas, Duns Scotus maintained that there was indeed a quidditative, though imperfect, knowledge of God, _Sent._ I, dist. 3, qu. 2. Nominalism already rose in opposition to this and became more or less skeptical. Durandus said that there was no _cognitio abstractiva_ of the divine essence. And Occam declared: _nec divina essentia, nec divina quidditas nec aliquid intrinsecum Deus, nec aliquid, quod est realiter Deus, potest hic cognosci a nobis, ita quod nihil aliud a Deo occurrat in ratione objecti_, Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II 1009. Mysticism sought to obtain another knowledge of God than by the way of dialectic. And toward the end of the Middle Ages Nicholas of Cusa argued in his writing _De docta ignorantia_ that no truth could be obtained through reason, but only through faith, which was mystically conceived as a new organ in man. After the Reformation Roman Catholic theology returned to scholasticism and also adopted the doctrine of the unknowability of God’s essence in the sense of Thomas, e.g., Sylvius, _Comm. in Thomam_, ed. 4, Antwerp, 1693, I, p. 96 sq.; Billuart, _Cursus theol._ I, 1747, p. 228 sq.; Petavius, _Theol. dogm._ I, c. 5; VIII, c. 6; _Theol. Wirceb._, ed. Paris, 1880, III 73 sq.; Jansen, _Praelect. Theol._ II 78 sq.; C. Pesch, _Praelect. dogm._, 1895, II, p. 46 sq.; etc. At the Lateran Council under Innocent III this doctrine was even ecclesiastically established and declared by Rome: _Deus ineffabilis est_.
