## 7.

But in Reformation theology too, the significance of this incomprehensibility of God was understood less and less.
It was still taught, but it stood entirely by itself and exercised no influence.
The schema in which the doctrine of God was treated soon became almost unalterably fixed.
The situation was still much worse in other movements.
Socinianism did not address the question of the knowability of God at all.
It took not the least interest in the knowledge of God’s essence.
Knowing God was approximately equivalent to knowing that he is absolute Lord, Fock, _Der Socin._, S. 414–416.
Crell wrote a work _de Deo ejusque attributis_ and in it did indeed prove God’s existence with all manner of arguments, but abstained from all questions concerning God’s essence, knowability, and so forth.
Conrad Vorstius wrote a _Tractatus de Deo s. de natura et attributis Dei_, 1610, that fell into the same Socinian errors; cf. _Acta Syn. Dord._, sess. 149 sq.
The Remonstrants too felt no need to enter into metaphysical questions; rather, they warned against vain speculation and urged simplicity.
The only thing strictly necessary to know was the will of God.
The _cultus Dei_ is far more necessary than the _cognitio Dei_, Episcopius, _Instit. theol._, Lib. 4, sect. 2, cap. 1; Limborch, _Theol. Chr._, Lib. II, c. 1.
Rationalism believed itself certain of God’s existence and attached little value to the knowledge of his essence.
It is as though God’s majesty and greatness were no longer sensed at all.
Setting aside all so-called metaphysical questions, people hastened to the will of God in order to know and do it.
Eternal life lies not in knowing God but in doing his will.
Bretschneider calls the question whether God can be defined entirely superfluous, _Dogm._ I⁴ 443; _Syst. Entw._ 341.

But when this truth of the unknowability of the divine essence had been almost entirely forgotten by theology, philosophy recalled it once more.
Rationalism believed that with its proofs for God’s existence and its doctrine of God’s attributes it stood on firm scientific ground.
But Kant, although in his doctrine of God, virtue, and immortality still entirely caught in Rationalism and Moralism, nevertheless brought about a great change in the foundation on which this doctrine rested.
Just as the _Sinnlichkeit_ brings with it the forms of space and time and the understanding the categories _a priori_, so reason also contains _a priori_, synthetic _Grundsätze und Regeln_, especially this _Grundsatz_: that it ascends from the _Bedingte_ to the _Unbedingte_, _Kr. d. r. Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, S. 300.
It thereby forms three transcendent ideas: soul, world, and God, S. 308–318.
But these three ideas cannot be demonstrated objectively and can only be derived subjectively from the nature of reason, S. 319.
We cannot perceive the object of these ideas and therefore cannot obtain scientific knowledge of them, S. 321, 322.
Judged scientifically, they are paralogisms, antinomies, and ideals, S. 322.
Our knowledge extends no further than the domain of experience, S. 469, 475, 508.
These ideas therefore do not increase our knowledge but only regulate it; they bring unity into our concepts and cause us to regard everything _as if_ God, the soul, and the world existed, S. 512, 530, 537, 541.
Science can neither prove nor deny the reality of these ideas, S. 532.
Psychology, cosmology (teleology), and theology are therefore not sciences, S. 547.
The critique of pure reason ends with a negative result.
Practical reason does indeed cause us to accept the reality of these ideas, _Kr. der prakt. Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, S. 149 f.
And Kant does ascribe understanding and will and other attributes to God, S. 150, 157, but God’s essence nevertheless remains hidden.
Practical reason does know that those three ideas have objective reality, but nothing more.
It does not increase the content of our scientific knowledge.
Speculative reason can do nothing with those ideas but regulate and purify our knowledge, and it employs the idea of God to counter and ward off anthropomorphism as the source of superstition and fanaticism, S. 162 f.
When understanding and will are ascribed to God, this is only a knowledge of God _in praktischer Beziehung_, but in no respect a speculative knowledge, S. 165.
Whoever abstracts from what is anthropomorphic in it retains nothing but the word, S. 166.
The idea of God belongs not in metaphysics, which does not exist, but in morality, S. 166 f.
Fichte at first adopted entirely the same standpoint as Kant.
In his _Versuch einer Kritik aller Offenbarung_, Königsberg, 1792, he grounds belief in God on the postulates of reason and also ascribes definite attributes to God, such as holiness, blessedness, omnipotence, righteousness, omniscience, and eternity, S. 3 f., 104.
But with this we still have no definite conception of God and do not know his essence as it is _an sich_; this would not promote pure morality either but would harm it, S. 105.
Religion always represents God in a human manner, in time and space and even corporeally, and does no harm thereby, provided that it does not come into conflict with morality and that this sensuous representation is not held to be objectively valid, S. 124 f.
It must all be understood as _Herablassung zu unserm subjectiven Bedürfniss_, S. 127.
_Nur eine solche Offenbarung also kann göttlichen Ursprungs seyn, die einen anthropomorphisirten Gott, nicht als objectiv sondern blos für subjectiv gültig giebt_, S. 129.
And Schleiermacher too, although differing from Kant and Fichte in many respects and aligning himself more closely with Spinoza, nevertheless agreed with them in the doctrine of the unknowability of God.
The idea of the unity of being and thought, of the real and the ideal—that is, the idea of God—is the presupposition of all our knowing, _Dialektik_, ed. Jonas, 1839, S. 397, and the ground of our thought, S. 60, 163; but it cannot be grasped in thought and remains _hinter dem Vorhang_, S. 78, 79.
As soon as we wish to bring the Absolute closer to us, it is rendered finite in our thought, S. 69, and we speak in images, S. 78.
In a word, it is inaccessible to knowing, S. 87.
In his _Glaubenslehre_ Schleiermacher presented the same thoughts, though in a more religious and less elaborated form.
God is the _Woher_ of our _Daseyn_; and as such an absolute causality, he is not an object of our knowing but only the content of the feeling of absolute dependence, § 4, 4.
