# Part II. The Dogma

## Chapter IV. On God

# § 20. The Incomprehensibility of God

## 1.

Mystery is the vital element of dogmatics. Scripture indeed does not understand by μυστηριον an abstract-supernatural truth in the Roman Catholic sense; but it is equally far removed from the thought that the believer could understand and comprehend the revealed mysteries in a scientific sense, cf. vol. I, p. 532. Rather, the knowledge that God has revealed concerning himself in nature and Scripture far transcends human representation and conception. To that extent, everything with which dogmatics deals is mystery. After all, it does not deal with finite creatures, but from beginning to end it rises above every creature to the Eternal and Infinite One himself. As soon as it begins, it already stands before the Incomprehensible One. From him it takes its beginning, for from him are all things. But also in the subsequent _loci_, when it descends to creatures, it considers them only in their relation to God, as they are from him and through him and to him. Thus the knowledge of God is the one dogma, the exclusive content of the whole of dogmatics. All the doctrines treated in it—concerning the world, humanity, Christ, and so forth—are only the explication of the one central dogma of the _cognitio Dei_. From him all things are considered. Under him all things are subsumed. To him all things are led back. It is always God and God alone whose glory in creation and re-creation, in nature and grace, in world and church, dogmatics must ponder and describe. It is the knowledge of him alone that it must display and exhibit. Dogmatics thereby does not become a dry, scholastic science without fruit for life. The more it contemplates him, the knowledge of whom is its sole content, the more it will pass over into admiration and worship. If only it never forgets to think and speak about the _things_ rather than the _words_; if only it remains a _Theologie der Thatsachen_ and does not degenerate into a _Theologie der Rhetorik_, then as a scientific description of the knowledge of God it is also supremely fruitful for life. For the knowledge of God in Christ is life itself, Ps. 89:16; Isa. 11:9; Jer. 31:34; John 17:3. Augustine therefore desired to know nothing else and nothing more than God and himself: _Deum et animam scire cupio. Nihilne plus? Nihil omnino._ Calvin therefore began his _Institutes_ with the knowledge of God and of ourselves. And for this reason the Geneva Catechism answered its first question, _quis humanae vitae praecipuus est finis?_, with: _ut Deum, a quo conditi sunt homines, ipsi noverint_; cf. also the Westminster Catechism.

But as soon as we undertake to speak about God, the question arises how we shall be able to do so. We are human beings, and he is the Lord our God. There seems to be no kinship and no fellowship between him and us by which we could name him truthfully. Between him and us there is a distance like that between the infinite and the finite, between eternity and time, between being and becoming, between the all and nothing. However little we know of God, even the faintest notion entails that he is a being infinitely exalted above every creature. Scripture not only confirms this; it first places the majesty and exaltedness of the Lord of lords before us in the full light. The very first chapter of Genesis portrays his incomprehensible exaltedness: he speaks, and it is there. And although Scripture then goes on to describe God in his condescending love and says that he dwells with the one who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, he is nevertheless the High and Exalted One who inhabits eternity, Isa. 57:15. God is great, and we do not comprehend him, Job 26:14; 36:26; 37:5. He is incomparable, Isa. 40:18, 25; 46:5, dwelling in darkness, 1 Kings 8:12; Isa. 45:15, in inaccessible light, 1 Tim. 6:16. No one has seen him or can see him, Exod. 33:20; John 1:18; 6:46; 1 Tim. 6:16. The hidden ground, the depth of God, ‎‏חֵקֶר‏‎, and the boundary, the uttermost reach, the essence of the Almighty, ‎‏תַּכְלִית‏‎, cannot be attained, Job 11:7; Eccl. Sir. 43:34, 35. He is spirit, without form, Deut. 4:12, 15; John 4:24; 5:37, infinitely exalted above all that is creaturely: above need, Job 22:2, 3; Acts 17:25; change, James 1:17; time, Isa. 41:4; 44:6; 48:12; Rev. 1:8; 22:13; space, Acts 17:27, 28; etc. He cannot be described or called by any name. He is nameless; his name is wonderful, Gen. 32:29; Judg. 13:18; Prov. 30:4.
