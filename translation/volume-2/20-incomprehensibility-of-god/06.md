## 6.

Reformation theology brought no change in this regard.
In his work _de servo arbitrio_, Luther distinguished between _Deus absconditus_ and _revelatus_, between _Deus ipse_ and _verbum Dei_.
And later he came more and more to hold only to the latter, that is, to God as he has revealed himself in Christ: _quod supra nos, nihil ad nos_.
Yet for him the full being of God was not exhausted in this revelation.
There remained in God a dark, hidden background, namely, _Deus ut est in sua natura et majestate, Deus absolutus_.
And according to Luther, this God was _plane incognoscibilis, incomprehensibilis, inaccessibilis_, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ I 47, 76 f., 314 f.
Later Lutheran theologians did not speak of such a sharp distinction between God’s being and his revelation, but they all nevertheless taught that no adequate name and no proper definition of God can be given, Gerhard, _Loci theol._ II c. 5; cf. Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ § 17; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I⁴ 443.
The Reformed were of one mind on this point.
Their profound aversion to all creature-worship caused them everywhere to distinguish sharply between what belongs to God and what belongs to the creature.
More than any other theological tradition, they took seriously the proposition that _finitum non est capax infiniti_.
Zwingli said: _quid Deus sit, tam ex nobis ipsis_ (these words may not be omitted, as Hoekstra does, _Wijsg. Godsd._ II 14) _ignoramus, quam ignorat scarabeus, quid sit homo_, _Opera_ III 157.
Calvin considered it vain speculation to investigate _quid sit Deus_; for us it is enough to know _qualis sit et quid ejus naturae conveniat_, _Inst._ I 2, 2; I 5,-9; _Comm. in Rom._ 1:19.
Later theologians expressed the unknowability of the divine being even more strongly.
Because the finite cannot comprehend the infinite, all the names of God serve not to make his being known to us but to indicate, to some extent and according to our capacity, that concerning God which we need to know.
The propositions _Deus definiri nequit, Deo nomen non est, finitum non est capax infiniti_ recur in the work of all these theologians.
They all teach unanimously that God infinitely transcends our conception, our representation, and our language.
Thus Polanus says, for example, that the attributes ascribed to God in Holy Scripture do not explain his nature and being.
They teach us much more _quis et quomodo non sit, quam quis et quomodo sit. Quidquid de Deo dicitur, non est Deus, quia est ineffabilis. Nulla attributa divina essentiam naturamque Dei sufficienter explicant, quia illa est infinita. Infinitum autem per id quod finitum est digne satis pleneque explicari non potest_, _Synt. Theol._ II c. 6, p. 137; cf. also Zanchi, _de natura Dei seu de attributis_, _Op._ II col. 9-10; Hyperius, _Methodus theol._, 1574, p. 83-84; Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, 1584, p. 35, 36, 49; _Synopis par. theol._ VI 2; Maresius, _Syst. theol._, p. 48; Alsted, _Theol. schol. did._, p. 56 sq.; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 505 sq.
