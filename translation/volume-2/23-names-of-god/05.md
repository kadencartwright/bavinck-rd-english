## 5.

The right of these names may thus stand established, but what is their value? What and what kind of knowledge do they furnish us concerning the divine being? There can be no question that this knowledge is adequate. It is finite and restricted in every respect, yet it is not therefore impure and untrue. We possess very little adequate knowledge; everywhere and in every domain we finally come up against a mystery; the inner essence of things, the _Ding an sich_, escapes our perception. We perceive _phaenomena_ and from them conclude to the essence; we learn to know properties and thereby ascend to substance, but this itself lies behind the phenomenon and is unknown to us. Natural science accepts atoms as the ultimate constituents of reality but does not possess the slightest empirical knowledge of them. A definition can be given only of the very simplest things; as soon as they are of a somewhat higher order, they cannot be completely circumscribed in any concept. This applies already to the visible world, but it applies still more in the world of invisible things. For man is a corporeal, sensible being. All his knowledge begins with and arises from sensory perception. Our thought is bound to the senses as our soul is to the body. We never perceive spiritual things directly but only through the medium of material things. We see everything ἐν αἰνιγματι. Not only God but also the soul and the whole spiritual world comes to our knowledge only through the sensible world. Hence we designate everything spiritual with names that apply first of all in the visible world. We name the human soul after bodily phenomena. Its activities, such as knowing, thinking, understanding, comprehending, judging, concluding, feeling, and so forth, we bring to our own and others’ consciousness by means of words that originally signify a bodily action. All our speaking of invisible things is therefore metaphor, image, likeness, poetry. Τα γαρ νοητα . . . προσηγοριας δε πασης ἐκτος, ἐπειδη κυριον ὀνομα των νοητων τε και ἀσωματων οὐδεν, Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat._ 45. But for that reason we nevertheless do not speak falsely and incorrectly. On the contrary, true poetry is truth, for it rests on the likeness, correspondence, and kinship of the different groups of phenomena. The whole of language is built upon this; all figures of speech proceed from it. If speaking in images were untrue, all our thought and all our knowing would be appearance, and speech itself would be impossible. The same applies in religion and theology. There is no adequate knowledge of God. We cannot name him as he exists in himself. All his names are derived from creatures. But they are not therefore untrue, a fruit of human imagination. Just as there is likeness, and comparison is therefore possible, between the various parts of the world, so there is also kinship between God and his creatures that gives us the right to speak of him in a creaturely manner. More than this: temporally, in reality, the natural indeed precedes the spiritual, but logically and ideally the spiritual is first and the natural afterward. The natural could not lead us to the spiritual if it had not itself proceeded from it. Plato saw in the cosmos a realization of the ideas. And Scripture teaches that all things were made through the Logos and did not come into being from things that appear before the eyes, John 1:3; Heb. 11:3. It is God himself who made the whole creation, including the material creation, serviceable to the revelation of his perfections. He could do this because he is the almighty Creator and has absolute dominion also over matter. Even though we name God with names derived from creatures, they were first placed in those creatures by God himself. We indeed first use the names by which we designate God of creatures, because we know these earlier than God. But as to the matter itself they apply first to God and afterward to creatures. All perfections are first in him, then in the creatures. He possesses them _per essentiam_; they have them only _per participationem_. Just as the temple was made according to the τυπος shown to Moses on the mountain, Heb. 8:5, so every creature was first eternally thought and afterward created in time. Every πατρια—that is, not every race but every fatherhood in heaven and on earth—is named from the Πατηρ who created all things, Eph. 3:15; cf. Matt. 23:9; Damascene, _De fide orthodox._ I 9; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 3, art. 2; qu. 13, artt. 3 and 6; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 22, art. 1, qu. 3; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 11–14; etc. All heavenly things are painted for us in Scripture with earthly paints and colors. God himself comes to us through the whole of creation and dwelt among us in the human nature of Christ. This human nature was certainly no adequate organ of his deity; it was even a concealment of his glory. And yet the fullness of deity dwelt in him bodily; whoever saw him beheld the Father. The two can therefore be reconciled very well: knowledge that is inadequate, finite, and restricted, yet at the same time true, pure, sufficient. God reveals himself in his works, and according to that revelation he is named by us. He permits us to name him in our weak, human language because he himself displayed his perfections before us in the creatures. Properly speaking, therefore, it is not we who give God a name; how could and may we do that? It is God himself who through nature and Scripture places his glorious names upon our lips. According to an old distinction, the names of God are not a product of the _ratio ratiocinans_, man’s subjective reason, but of the _ratio ratiocinata_, objective reason in revelation. God’s self-consciousness is the archetype, and our knowledge of God, drawn from his Word, is its ectype.
