## 6.

This indicates the character that the knowledge of God always bears in creatures. Two extremes are thereby avoided. On the one side stand those who consider an essential, quidditative, adequate knowledge of God possible, whether through mystical intuition (Plotinus, Malebranche, the ontologists, and the Roman Catholics insofar as they teach the _visio Dei per essentiam_ in the state of glory) or through logical thought (Eunomius, Scotus, Spinoza, Hegel). Over against all these the word remains true: no one has ever seen God; only the Son, who was in the bosom of the Father, has declared him to us. Moses saw only God’s glory after it had passed by. The prophets beheld God only in vision. Both in creation outside us and in our own soul-life, what is created always remains standing between God and our consciousness; there are no _ideae innatae_. We see in a mirror and walk by faith. An absolute, adequate knowledge of God is therefore impossible. The conversion of representation into concept, of the language of imagination into that of thought, of kataphatic into apophatic theology, of the Semitic-concrete into the Japhetic-abstract, always ends in the loss of all knowledge of God. But on the other side, the designation _ectypal theology_ also rejects the opinion of those who indeed consider creaturely names unavoidable for designating the essence of God but see in them nothing but symbols, products of the poetic imagination. In a certain sense Damascene, _De fide orthod._ I 12, and Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 1, belong here; cf. also Moses Maimonides, _More Neb._ I, c. 58; Cusanus, _De docta ignor._ I, c. 24. For they maintained that all those names made God known to us only as the cause of all things, so that, for example, the name wisdom attributed to God entailed nothing more than that he was the cause of all wisdom. But Thomas already rightly observed against this that God could then equally well be called gold, silver, sun, moon, body, and so forth, since he is indeed the cause of all those creatures; and further, we all mean something different and more when we call God good than when we call him the cause of the good. The perfections of God are not all to be seen in all creatures and in all in the same way, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, art. 2; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 11–13. Yet later this opinion was renewed especially by Schleiermacher. God is only absolute causality; his attributes are subjective designations; the character of theology is to be called not ectypal or analogical but symbolic, Schleiermacher, _Reden über die Relig._, p. 46; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I⁴ 477; Paulsen, _Einl. in die Philos._, 263. Religious representations are then products of the poetic imagination, ideals to be appreciated aesthetically (Rauwenhoff, Pierson, F. A. Lange, and others). In some modern circles there has lately been a perceptible inclination to continue using biblical and ecclesiastical names as symbols of higher spiritual truths. This conception of the character of theology, however, is untenable. That theological science which occupies itself with explaining the sacred symbols occurring in Scripture and church can indeed be called symbolic, as, for example, Vitringa gave it the name _theologia symbolica_, 1726; cf. d’Outrein, _Proefstukken van heilige Sinnebeelden_, 1700. A symbol is always a sensible object or sensible action designating a spiritual truth. But theology as such deals not with such symbols but with spiritual realities. When consciousness, will, holiness, and so forth are attributed to God, no one understands this in a symbolic sense. No religious person sees in such representations products of his imagination, as everyone readily acknowledges with works of art. On the contrary, the religious person regards those religious representations as objectively true, and his religion languishes and dies as soon as he begins to doubt them. If they are products of the imagination, their objective truth cannot be maintained. They may then still be appreciated aesthetically, but religiously and ethically they have lost their value; religion can no more be converted into art than into philosophy. Attempts nevertheless to maintain them as symbols always end in disappointment. Whoever, like Hegel, distinguishes between representation and concept is no longer satisfied with the representation; he always tries to arrive at the pure concept and afterward reaches back in vain to the religious representations as symbols. The symbolic character of theology makes the names of God a reflection of one’s own emotional life, deprives them of all truth, and seeks their ground only in the ever-changing _ratio ratiocinans_. Man is then the measure of religion; as the man is, so is his God. For these reasons theology is to be called not symbolic but ectypal or analogical. This implies: 1º, that all our knowledge of God is from and through God, grounded in his revelation, that is, in the _ratio ratiocinata_; 2º, that in order to communicate his knowledge to creatures, God must descend to those creatures and accommodate himself to their capacity; 3º, that the possibility of this συγκαταβασις cannot be denied, since it is given of itself with creation, that is, with the existence of a finite being; 4º, that our knowledge of God is therefore nevertheless always only analogical, that is, formed according to the analogy of what can be perceived of God in his creatures; that it has for its object not God himself in his unknowable essence but God in his revelation, in his relation to us, in τα περι την φυσιν, Damascene, _De fide orthod._ I 4, in his _habitudo ad creaturas_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 12, art. 12; and thus is only a finite image, weak likeness, and creaturely imprint of that perfect knowledge which God possesses of himself; and 5º, that our knowledge of God is nevertheless true, pure, and reliable, since it has God’s self-consciousness as its archetype and his self-revelation in the cosmos as its foundation, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, art. 5; _C. Gent._ I, capp. 32–34; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 23, 24; Junius, _De theologia_, _Op._, 1607, I 1378 sq., ed. Kuyper, p. 51 sq.; Gomarus, _Disp. theol._ I; Owen, _Θεολογουμενα_, 1661, p. 10 sq.; Calovius, _Isag. ad theol._, 1652, p. 25 sq.; Kuyper, _Enc._ II 201 f.
