## 4.

The names by which God calls himself in his revelation, however, present a peculiar difficulty for thought. For earlier it became evident to us that God is incomprehensible and exalted far above all that is finite; here, however, in his names, he descends to all that is finite and becomes like the creatures. Here an antinomy presents itself that seems insoluble. Ἀνωνυμος on the one hand, God is nevertheless πολυωνυμος on the other. After all anthropomorphism has first been banished, it is nevertheless brought back in here. By what right, then, can these names be used of God? On what ground are they ascribed to him who stands infinitely high above every creature and cannot be comprehended by the finite? That ground can lie only in the fact that the whole creation, though as creature infinitely far removed from God, is nevertheless a creature, a work of God, and related to him. The world is no antithesis alongside and over against God, no independent power, no second God, but wholly and entirely God’s work in both its being and its mode of being: from the beginning it is designed to reveal God. The entire people of Israel, in all its laws and institutions, offices and ministries, character and customs, and so forth, is ordered to proclaim God’s excellencies; and the human nature of Christ was enabled by the Holy Spirit to declare the Father and make his name known to human beings. The band of apostles, with its diversity of upbringing, preparation, gift, and calling, is destined to recount the μεγαλεια του θεου. God can and may therefore be named by us in a creaturely manner. The right of anthropomorphism rests upon the fact that God himself has descended in and to his creatures and has revealed his name in and through creatures. Anthropomorphism is therefore by no means confined, as was already observed, to a single word such as personality. God cannot be spoken of in any way other than anthropomorphically. For we do not see him himself. We can see him only in his works and name him according to the revelation in those works. More than that, at least here on earth we cannot see God face to face. If God nevertheless wills that we should know him, he must descend to us, accommodate himself to our limited, finite, human consciousness, and speak to us in human language. Whoever therefore contests the right of anthropomorphisms thereby denies in principle the possibility that God reveals himself in his creatures, must proceed from there to the denial of creation, and finally retains nothing but an eternal dualism between God and world, between the infinite and the finite. For if our naming God with anthropomorphic names entails a finitizing of God, this applies still more to revelation in creation. God as the Infinite One is then powerless to bring forth another being outside his own essence; the world is in no respect a revelation but solely and entirely a concealment of God; the human being is solely and entirely opposed to God and not related to him; and God is an eternal βυθος, a nameless σιγη, both for himself and for the human being. Naturally, no knowledge of God is then possible either. If anthropomorphic, creaturely names detract from God’s essence, then we cannot and may not call him by any name; then we must be absolutely silent. Every name by which we might wish to designate him is then a dishonoring of God, a violation of his majesty, a blasphemy; cf. above, p. 22. People have indeed sought to evade this consequence, though it is obvious, by distinguishing between representation and concept. Plato already began this. Neoplatonism and Gnosticism continued it. And Hegel brought it forward anew. But this does not take us a single step farther. Even the loftiest speculation and most profound philosophy must nevertheless think and speak about God. Even if they cast away all representations and retain only pure, abstract concepts, they do not thereby rise above human, creaturely thought and speech or draw near to the Infinite One himself. Even the most abstract names—such as being, substance, the Absolute, the One, spirit, reason—are and remain anthropomorphisms. For the human being there is only a choice between these two: absolute silence or human thought and speech about God; between agnosticism—that is, theoretical atheism—or anthropomorphism. Philosophy has therefore always returned again to anthropomorphism; otherwise, naturally, it would also have ended with a negative critique. Plato, Philo, Plotinus, Pseudo-Dionysius, Damascene, and Erigena nevertheless ultimately ascribed all manner of names to God again. Cataphatic theology built up what apophatic theology had torn down. The substance of Spinoza received numerous attributes and modes. In Hegel God again became life, spirit, thought, reason, subject. Rauwenhoff allows imagination to enter where the understanding must come to an end, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._ 611 f. Unbew._ II⁹ 414; etc. And naturally Christian theology has always judged in the same way.
