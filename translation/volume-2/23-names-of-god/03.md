## 3.

The whole creation, all of nature with all its kingdoms, and above all the human world are pressed into service in Holy Scripture for the description of the knowledge of God.
No limit is set to anthropomorphism.
All creatures—living and lifeless, animate and inanimate, organic and inorganic—furnish names by which to bring the greatness of God to our consciousness to some degree.
Although _ἀνωνυμος_ in himself, God is nevertheless _πολυωνυμος_ in his revelation.
_Omnia possunt dici de Deo, et nihil digne dicitur de Deo. Nihil latius hac inopia. Quaeris congruum nomen, non invenis; quaeris quoquo modo dicere, omnia invenis_, Augustine, _tract._ 13 _in Ev. Joh._ n. 5.
And to clarify why God can be named in so many ways, Augustine employed a striking image.
Our body has many needs—for light and air, food and drink, shelter and clothing, and so forth—and all these things are different and lie alongside one another among the creatures.
Our spirit too has many and manifold needs, but that which supplies them is not manifold; it is always the one and the same divine being.
_In terra aliud est fons, aliud lumen. Sitiens quaeris fontem, et ut pervenias ad fontem, quaeris lucem, et si dies non est, accendis lucernam, ut ad fontem pervenias. Fons ille ipse est lux: sitienti fons est, caeco lux est; aperiantur oculi ut videant lucem, aperiantur fauces cordis ut bibant fontem; quod bibis hoc vides, hoc audis. Totum sit tibi Deus, quia horum quae diligis totum tibi est. Si visibilia attendis, nec panis est Deus, nec aqua est Deus, nec lux ista est Deus, nec vestis est Deus, nec domus est Deus. Omnia enim haec visibilia sunt et singula sunt; quod est panis non hoc est aqua, et quod est vestis, non hoc est domus, et quod sunt ista, non hoc est Deus, visibilia enim sunt. Deus tibi totum est; si esuris, panis tibi est; si sitis, aqua tibi est; si in tenebris es, lumen tibi est, quia incorruptibilis manet; si nudus es, immortalitatis vestis tibi est, cum corruptibile hoc induerit incorruptionem et mortale hoc induerit immortalitatem_, _tract._ 13 _in Ev. Joh._
Likewise Pseudo-Dionysius says that God is _ἀνωνυμος_ and at the same time _πολυωνυμος_; he is simultaneously _παντα τα ὀντα και οὐδεν των ὀντων_, _de div. nom._ c. I, §§ 6, 7.
In Thomas we read, _quod Deus in se praehabet omnes perfectiones creaturarum, quasi simpliciter et universaliter perfectus_, _S. Theol._ I qu. 13 art. 2.
And still more beautifully in Bonaventure: _ut laudibus Deum possimus extollere, atque ad Dei cognitionem promoveamur, necessaria nobis sunt translationes ad divina. Ratio vel finis translationis duplex est. Una in qua est laus Dei, alia manuductio intellectus nostri. Propter laudem Dei necessaria est translatio. Et quoniam Deus multum est laudabilis, ne propter inopiam vocabulorum contingeret cessari a laude, sacra Scriptura docuit nomina creaturarum ad Deum transferri; et hoc in numero indefinito, ut sicut omnis creatura laudat Deum, sic Deus laudatur ex omni nomine creaturae, et qui non poterit uno nomine laudari, tamquam superexcellens omne nomen, laudaretur ex omni nomine. Alia ratio est manuductio intellectus nostri. Quia cum per creaturas ad cognoscendum creatorem venimus, ut plurimum, fere omnes creaturae habent proprietates nobiles, quae sunt ratio intelligendi Deum, ut leo fortitudinem, agnus mansuetudinem, petra soliditatem, serpens prudentiam et consimilia; ideo oportuit plura nomina transferri ad Deum_, _Sent._ I dist. 34 art. 1 qu. 4.
And Calvin agreed with this when he said: _nulla est mundi particula, in qua non scintillae saltem aliquae gloriae ipsius emicare cernantur_.
God is immanent in all that has been created.
The one who is pure in heart sees God everywhere.
Everything is full of God.
_Fateor pie hoc posse dici, modo a pio animo proficiscatur, naturam esse Deum_, _Inst._ I 5, 1, 5.
But there is an ascending order among creatures.
The place and rank that creatures occupy are determined by their kinship with God.
Every creature names a name of God.
But among all creatures the human being stands at the top.
He alone bears the exalted name of image, son, and child of God.
He alone is called God’s offspring.
And therefore the greatest number and the most exalted names of God are drawn from the human being.
The human being, however, may never be detached from nature.
And no creature or part of the world may ever be placed alongside or over against God.
There is nothing outside and apart from God.
This principle has repeatedly been violated.
The dualism of Plato, Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, and Manichaeism restricted God’s revelation and set matter over against him as hostile.
And in all sorts of forms this exerted influence and continued to work in theology.
When in modern times, under the influence of Kant and Jacobi, God’s revelation is restricted to the religious and ethical; when Scripture is acknowledged only in its religious-moral content; when the seat of religion is sought only in heart or conscience, in feeling or will—the same dualistic principle is at work in all this.
Nature, with its elements and forces; the human being in his social and political life; art; and science come to stand outside God’s revelation and become neutral, that is, godless domains.
It is natural, then, that the Old Testament and a very large part of the New Testament can no longer be appreciated; that nature and the world no longer have anything to say to the believer; that revelation, the Word of God, loses all influence upon public life; that religion, driven back into a hidden corner of the heart and the private chamber, forfeits every claim to respect; that dogmatics, particularly also the _locus de Deo_, shrinks from day to day; and that theology no longer knows how to maintain its place.
Theology no longer knows how to speak of God because it does not speak from and through him.
It no longer has names by which it can name God.
God becomes the great Unknown, and the world becomes first _ἀθεος_, then _ἀντιθεος_.
