## 8.

Finally, the moral proof attends to the moral phenomena that appear in the human being and in humanity—conscience, responsibility, remorse, reward and punishment, virtue and happiness, fear of death and judgment, the triumph of the good, and so forth—and infers from them a moral being who created and maintains that entire moral world order.
Some focus more attention upon the conscience that speaks in every human being, upon the moral law to which everyone feels bound, and infer from it a supreme, sovereign lawgiver.
Others attend especially to the disharmony of virtue and happiness, of sin and punishment, and on that basis postulate a being who will bring about a just harmony in the hereafter.
Or one considers the entire moral world order in its internal coherence and in its supremacy above the order of nature and infers from it the existence of a being who created that moral order and placed it above the order of nature.
This argument, however, is always based upon the ethical phenomena in the human world and thereby arrives at God as the supreme moral being.
Objections have also been raised against this proof.
First, materialism argues against it that moral life, like conscious life, is not an independent order but arose gradually through evolution, manifests itself one way here and another way elsewhere, and rests ultimately upon accidental transformations of matter, Darwin, _Afst. v. d. mensch_, ch. 4; Hellwald, _Kulturgesch._ I³ 26 f.; Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_, 462 f., 478 f.
Second, Fichte taught that the moral world order rests and subsists in itself and no longer needs a God above and outside it; and the advocates of autonomous and independent morality have followed him in this, C. Coignet, _La morale indép._, Paris, 1869.
Once again, all kinds of weighty objections can be brought against this materialistic and autonomous conception.
It is even acknowledged more and more generally that materialism has not explained the moral phenomena of freedom, responsibility, remorse, and so forth, Du Bois Reymond, _Die sieben Welträtsel_, 1882, S. 84; Carneri, _Sittl. u. Darw._, 1877; Weygoldt, _Darwin. Relig. Sittl._, 1878, and so forth.
And the _morale indépendante_ has also been opposed with strong arguments, Caro, _La morale indép._, Paris, 1876; Cramer, _Christ. en Hum._, p. 159 f.
But this much is certain: the premises of this proof themselves require so much substantial support that the proof scarcely reaches its conclusion.
Nevertheless, through the influence of Kant this proof has found so much sympathy among many that the other proofs have been almost entirely neglected alongside it; cf. above, p. 52, and further the literature on the moral world order: Zeller, _Vortr. u. Abh._ III, 1884, S. 189 f.; Carrière, _De zedelijke wereldorde_, 1880; Hartmann, _Das sittl. Bewustsein_, 1886, S. 570 f.; Christ, _Die sittl. Weltordnung_, 1894; Traub, _Die sittl. Weltordnung_, 1892; Pierson, _Gids_, Nov. 1895, p. 248.
Yet this proof cannot be called stronger than the others.
It even bears a much more personal character than the preceding arguments.
Although there is often little evidence of a moral world order in the world around us, in his conscience everyone feels himself, despite himself, bound to a moral order just as strongly as in his intellect he is bound to the laws of thought.
This moral order, which everyone discovers in the life of his own soul, does not immediately coincide with the idea of God, nor does it compel acknowledgment of God’s existence; it even allows all kinds of evasions for anyone who wishes to escape it, but it nevertheless remains a powerful testimony that leads again and again to the acknowledgment of a holy and righteous being.
