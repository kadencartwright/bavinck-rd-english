## 9.

It is regrettable that in theology these arguments for the existence of God acquired the name of proofs.
Yet this is not for the reason given by Jacobi.
Although proving consists in deriving one proposition from another, proving the existence of God is not therefore a _contradictio in adjecto_.
Dependence within a proof is something entirely different from dependence in reality.
The _causa cognoscendi_ is by no means the same as the _causa essendi_.
Although God’s existence can be the conclusion in a syllogism, just as in general the maker can be inferred from the work, that existence nevertheless remains in reality the beginning and ground of the being of all things; indeed, it is posited in the conclusion precisely as such.
But the name proofs is less well chosen for the arguments mentioned above because it has transferred them to a category in which they do not belong—namely, that of logical, mathematical, exact, compelling demonstrations—and has thus deprived them of their ethical and religious character.
It now gives the impression that faith in the existence of God rests on these proofs and stands or falls with them.
And yet that would be _ein elender Glaube, der sich Gott erst beweisen müsste, ehe er zu ihm betete_, Kahnis, _Luth. Dogm._ I 128.
Rather, the opposite is the case.
The existence of no single thing is first established for us on the basis of proofs.
Before all reasoning and demonstration, we are firmly and certainly convinced of our own existence, of that of the external world, of the laws of thought and morality, and so forth, through the impressions all these things irresistibly make upon our consciousness.
We accept that existence of itself, spontaneously, without coercion or force.
And so it is also with the existence of God.
The so-called proofs may bring greater clarity and lucidity, but they do not strengthen certainty concerning the existence of God in the least.
This certainty is determined solely by faith—that is, by the spontaneity with which our consciousness bears witness to the existence of God, which presses itself upon us from every side.
The proofs are therefore not grounds but rather products of faith.
Faith, after all, seeks to give an account of the religious impressions and affections that every human being receives and carries within the soul.
Faith also acts upon the understanding, which gradually seeks to bring order into that chaos of impressions and awarenesses; it categorizes and classifies them and reduces them to a few kinds.
Impressions come to us from the world of ideas (the ontological proof), from the world of finite, contingent, mutable things (the cosmological proof), from that of beauty and harmony (the teleological proof), and from that of the moral order (the moral proof).
But although they can thus be classified, it must never be thought that these four proofs are the only and isolated testimonies that God sends out to us.
No, all things speak to the devout of God; the whole world is a mirror of his excellencies; there is _nulla mundi particula_ in which his eternal power and divinity are not beheld.
God’s testimony comes to us from within and from outside us.
He does not leave himself without witness, neither in nature nor history, neither in heart nor conscience, neither in life nor fate.
And therefore this testimony of God is so powerful that almost no one denies it.
All human beings and peoples have heard something of the voice of the Lord.
The _consensus gentium_, considered by many to be a fifth proof, is a confirmation that God does not leave himself without witness; it is humanity’s answer to the call of God.
These testimonies that proceed from God and address the human being throughout the whole world are ordered and arranged in the proofs.
They gain no greater power from the syllogistic form in which they are clothed.
Weak as proofs, they are strong as testimonies.
They are not arguments that compel the understanding of the unbeliever, but _signa_ and _testimonia_ that never fail to make an impression upon every person’s heart.
Analyzed, isolated, detached from one another, and placed side by side, they can be contested at every point in the reasoning, and they impede the act of believing, which occurs spontaneously and of itself.
But understood as testimony and proclaimed as revelation of that God of whose existence every human being is assured by nature, before reasoning and study, in the depths of the soul, they are of no small value.
For even though, when proclaimed in this way, they encounter objections and reservations, they are nevertheless not nullified thereby.
Just as no one believes in God’s love because everything reveals his love, but rather despite everything that awakens doubt, so too everyone is convinced of God’s existence beforehand; through the proofs he does not arrive at his faith, nor do objections make shipwreck of his faith.
In every domain it is the case that difficulties first arise upon reflection, but no one therefore casts aside the moral law, the law of thought, religion, art, or science as folly because his thinking is unable to explain the phenomena it acknowledges or to defend them against every objection.
Nevertheless, reflection, although it does not furnish certainty concerning existence, does furnish clarity and lucidity.
The so-called proofs for the existence of God give the believer an account of his own religious and ethical consciousness; they put weapons into his hands with which the opponent, who in any case is no better armed, can be repulsed; for the Christian they are a sign that it is the same God who reveals himself in nature and grace, and that therefore creation and re-creation, _physis_ and _ethos_, do not stand side by side in a Manichaean and dualistic fashion; the excellencies of God that shine there in the world are the same as those that also glitter in the kingdom of heaven.
And together the testimonies that proceed from God to us in the world and are summarized in the so-called proofs are nothing other than the revelation of the Name of the Lord, by which he designates himself for the ear of his creatures and by which he gives us the right to address him.
Taken together, they make him known to us as the divine being whom we must necessarily think and must think as necessarily existing, who is the sole, first, absolute cause of all creatures, who consciously and purposefully governs all things, and who above all reveals himself in the conscience as the Holy One to everyone who believes.
