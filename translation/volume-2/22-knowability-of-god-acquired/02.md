## 2.

The division into _cognitio Dei insita_ and _acquisita_ is usually applied only to _theologia naturalis_. This is then distinguished from and often opposed to _theologia revelata_. This conception was already opposed at length earlier, I 219–244. There is no separate _theologia naturalis_ that man could obtain through thoughtful contemplation of the world apart from all revelation. The knowledge of God summarized in _theologia naturalis_ is no product of human reason. It presupposes first of all that God himself reveals himself in the works of his hands; it is not man who seeks God, but God who seeks man, also through his works in nature. Further, it is not man who understands and knows this revelation of God with the natural light of reason—all the religions of the pagans are positive—but on man’s part a sanctified understanding and an opened eye are needed to discern God, the true and living God, in his creatures. And even this is not enough. The believer too, the Christian too, would not understand God’s revelation in nature or be able to reproduce it purely if God himself had not described in his Word how and what he reveals of himself in the entire world. _Cognitio Dei naturalis_ is incorporated into Scripture itself and extensively unfolded there. It is therefore also an entirely mistaken method for the Christian, when treating _theologia naturalis_, to divest himself as it were of special revelation in Scripture and of the illumination of the Holy Spirit, to discuss it without any Christian _Voraussetzung_, and afterward to pass over to _theologia revelata_. As dogmatician too, the Christian stands from the beginning with both feet upon the foundation of special revelation; not first in the _locus de Christo_ but also in the _locus de Deo_ he is a believer in Christ. Standing in this way, however, he looks around him and, armed with the spectacles of Holy Scripture, sees in the whole world a revelation of that same God whom in Christ he knows and confesses as his Father in heaven. But then by _cognitio Dei insita_ and _acquisita_ is not to be understood that knowledge we obtain from creation apart from special revelation. From our earliest youth God’s special revelation in Christ has worked upon us all; we were born in the covenant of grace and therefore as Christians; all manner of Christian influences formed the knowledge of God that is now our possession to a far greater degree than the operation that proceeded upon our heart from God’s revelation in nature; and in the light of that special revelation we all also learned to view nature and the world around us. We all owe the knowledge of God from nature to God’s special revelation in his Word. Nature would cause us to hear voices as confused as those heard by the pagans if we had not listened to God’s speech in the works of grace and thereby now also understood his voice in the works of nature. But now that revelation of God in nature is also of great value. It is the same God who speaks to us in nature and grace, in creation and re-creation, in the Logos and in Christ, in the Spirit of God and in the Spirit of Christ. Nature and grace are not opposites; we have one God, from whom and through whom and unto whom they both are.
