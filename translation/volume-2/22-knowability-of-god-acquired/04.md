## 4.

Already among the ancient philosophers—Anaxagoras, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Plutarch, Seneca, and Cicero—we encounter proofs advanced for the existence of God. From the beauty, harmony, motion, and purposiveness of the world; from reason and innate awareness; from the importance of religion for state and society; and from the agreement of the nations, they concluded to the existence of a self-conscious, rational, divine being, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ I⁴ 885 f., II⁴ 771 f., III³ 357 f., IV³ 133 f. Christian theology adopted all these proofs and treated them with favor; Augustine added another from universal concepts, _De lib. arb._ II, cc. 3–15; Münscher–von Coelln, _D. G._ I 125–129. It did not disdain the support offered it by pagan scholarship and, rejecting dualistic Gnosticism and Manichaeism, saw in nature a work of God. This standpoint of Christian theology is all the more remarkable because Christianity stood in part hostile over against the cosmos and placed all emphasis on the salvation of the human being. Yet despite all the asceticism that entered the church, nature was and remained for Christian theology a creation of that same God who had revealed himself in Christ as Re-creator and Savior. Indeed, the value and force of the proofs were very soon overestimated. Some church fathers did affirm that the pagans never arrived at a pure _religio naturalis_ and that the way of these proofs is very difficult for the sinner, Athanasius, _C. Gent._ c. 35 sq. But gradually the proofs lost their religious character; they were detached from all moral conditions; the _cognitio Dei acquisita_ came to stand independently alongside the _insita_; and Christian theology became increasingly convinced that the truths of _religio naturalis_ were demonstrable in the same sense as those of mathematics or logic. Properly speaking, they were not _articuli fidei_ but _praeambula ad articulos_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 2, art. 2; _C. Gent._ I 10–13. Anselm’s ontological argument in his _Proslogium_, though adopted by some such as Hales and Bonaventure, _Itiner._ c. 5, was rejected by most because we can ascend to God only through creatures, Thomas, _C. Gent._ I 10–12. Apart from this, however, great value was attached and much care devoted to the proofs; in _theologia naturalis_ one stood on rational, scientific ground prior to and outside faith, Schwane, _D. G._ III 107 f. Rome and Roman Catholic theology still occupy that same rationalistic standpoint to the present day, Petavius, _Theol._ I, cap. 2; Sylvius, _Comm. in totam pr. p. S. Thomae_, ed. 4a, 1693, I, pp. 22–26; _Theol. Wirceb._, 1880, III 1–20; Perrone, _Prael. Theol._ II 3–41; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorz._ I² 673 f., II² 668 f.; Schwetz, _Theol. fund._ I, §§ 4–8; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 1–20; Hettinger, _Apol. des Christ._ I⁷ 103 f.; Jansen, _Prael._ I 39–51; etc. The Vatican Council declared that God can be known with certainty from creation by the natural light of reason; cf. vol. I, p. 221.

The Reformation did indeed adopt this _theologia naturalis_ with its proofs, but it did not place it before the doctrine of faith; rather, it incorporated it into that doctrine. Calvin proceeded from the _semen religionis_ and indeed saw in every _particula mundi_—in the starry heavens, in the human body, in the soul, in the preservation of all things, and so forth—incontrovertible _signa_ and _testimonia_ of God’s majesty, _Inst._ I 5, 1–10, but then immediately acknowledged again that although the _semen religionis_ was ineradicably implanted in all, it could nevertheless be choked and fail to produce good fruit. The human being no longer has an eye with which to behold God; for this he needs faith, I 4, 1; 5, 11–15. _Theologia naturalis_ occupies this same place in Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, 1584, pp. 37–44; Martyr, _Loci_, p. 2 sq.; Musculus, _Loci_, pp. 2–5; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 4; cf. Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. ref. K._ I 156 f. But Protestant theology soon entered upon the rationalistic path. Whereas _theologia naturalis_ was at first a description, guided by Scripture, of what the Christian could know about God from creation, it soon became an exposition of what the unbelieving, rational human being could come to know from nature by his own thought. _Theologia naturalis_ became _theologia rationalis_. Descartes found in the innate idea an incontrovertible proof for the existence of God. Voetius still discerned the difference between this rationalistic argumentation and the Reformed doctrine of _theologia naturalis_, _Disp._ V 455–525. But this awareness was increasingly lost in Alsted, _Theol. nat._, 1623; Mastricht, _Theol._ II, c. 2; Marck, _Merch_ I 1, 14; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III, qu. 1; and others. Rationalism made its entrance everywhere. _Theologia naturalis_ became theology proper—the scientific, demonstrable theology—by comparison with which _theologia revelata_ increasingly receded into the shadows and finally disappeared altogether. The proofs were divided into classes and elaborated at length: metaphysical proofs from the motion, causality, and contingency of the world; physical proofs from the harmony, order, and purpose of the universe and of particular creatures, such as sun, moon, stars, fire, light, earth, water, animal, plant, human being, body, soul, ear, eye, hand, instinct, and so forth; historical proofs from the _consensus gentium_, the _societas_, arts, sciences, revelation, prophecy, and miracle; moral proofs from conscience, freedom, morality, judgment, reward and punishment, and so forth; and finally mathematical proofs as well, Buddeus, _De atheismo et superst._, ed. Lulofs, 1767, pp. 224–318; and further literature in M. Vitringa I, p. 11 sq.; Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ I 676–744; Doedes, _Inl. tot de Leer v. God_, pp. 200–217.

This self-sufficiency of _theologia naturalis_ lasted until Kant. He subjected the proofs to severe criticism and argued that theoretical reason had to end here with a _non liquet_, _Kr. d. r. V._, ed. Kirchmann, S. 470 f., but based the existence of God on the postulate of practical reason; cf. vol. I, p. 447 f. Since then the proofs have fallen into disrepute among many philosophers and theologians. Jacobi even considered it impossible to prove God’s existence because God would then depend as a conclusion upon the grounds of proof, _Von den göttl. Dingen_, _Werke_ III 368, 567 f. The proofs were rejected or despised as unnecessary for the believer and useless for the unbeliever by Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 30; Schleiermacher II, § 33; Twesten II 19 f.; Hofmann, _Schriftbew._ I² 62; Beck, _Vorles. über die Glaubenslehre_ I 63 f.; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I² 102–109; Philippi, _K. Gl._ II 1–17; Bovon, _Dogm. Chrét._ I 216; Böhl, _Dogm._ 16 f.; Rauwenhoff, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._ 499 f.; etc. Others did not go so far but at least assigned an important place to the moral proof in Kant’s sense, such as Köstlin, _Die Beweise für das Dasein Gottes_, _Stud. u. Kr._, 1875, S. 601–655; 1876, S. 7–80; cf. also Herzog² 5, 308; Ritschl, _Theol. u. Met._ 8 f.; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 200 f.; Kaftan, _Wahrh. d. chr. Rel._ 490 f. The proofs received a much greater significance in Hegel’s philosophy: he regarded them as God’s self-demonstration in the human spirit and therefore attached great value especially to the ontological proof, _Ueber die Beweise vom Dasein Gottes_, _Werke_ XII 291 f. Biedermann regards them as proofs not for the existence of a personal God but indeed of an _einheitlichen, absoluten Grund der Welt_, _Dogm._ § 645 f.; cf. also Hartmann, _Religionsphil._ II 114 f.; Paulsen, _Einl. in die Philos._ 239 f.; etc. Herbert Spencer considers the essence of the Absolute unknowable but its existence nevertheless demonstrable, _First Principles_, 5th ed., pp. 1–123, especially pp. 87–97; cf. Spruyt, _Gids_, April 1886; Molenaar, _Bijbl. v. d. Herv._, May 21, 1885. Furthermore, attempts have been made from various sides to present the old proofs in a new form and to maintain them either as proofs or as grounds for faith in a personal God: Dorner, _Gl._ I 199 f.; Kahnis, _Luth. Dogm._ I² 118 f.; Lange, _Dogm._ I 207 f.; Ulrici, _Gott. u. die Natur_, 2e Aufl., 431 f.; Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I 301 f.; Lotze, _Mikrok._ III³ 553 f.; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_, 1886, S. 65; Barry, _Die natürl. Theol._, 1882; Kennedy, _Gottesglaube u. mod. Weltanschauung_, 1893; Zöckler, _Theol. naturalis_, 1860; Fricke, _Ist Gott persönlich_, Leipzig, Wigand, 1896; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 202; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 222; Bruce, _Apologetics_, 1892, pp. 149–163; Caird, _Introd. to the philos. of religion_, 1880; Flint, _Theism_, Edinburgh, 1877; James Orr, _Chr. view of God and the world_, 1893, pp. 104–137; Land, _Godg. Bijdr._, 1856, p. 353 f.; Doedes, _Inl. tot de Leer v. God_, p. 236 f.; _Leer v. God_, p. 50 f.; Bruining, _Het bestaan van God_, 1892; Maronier, _Wat wij van God weten_, 1889; Graswinckel, _Geloof en Vrijheid_, 1890, pp. 144–185; etc.
