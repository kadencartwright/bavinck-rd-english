## 3.

This applies to both _cognitio Dei insita_ and _acquisita_; we owe both to Holy Scripture.
It is true that Scripture makes no effort to prove the existence of God.
It proceeds from that existence and presupposes that the human being knows and acknowledges God.
It does not consider the human being to have fallen so deeply that he first needs a proof in order to believe.
For he is still the image-bearer of God, God’s offspring, and endowed with a _νους_ in order to discern God’s eternal power and divinity in creation.
In the eyes of Scripture, denial of God’s existence is a sign of folly, of profound moral corruption, Ps. 14:2.
Such human beings are the exception, not the rule.
As a rule, Scripture reckons with those who freely and by virtue of the prompting of their nature acknowledge God’s existence.
It appeals not to the reasoning intellect but to rational and moral consciousness.
It does not analyze and demonstrate but shows God in all the works of his hands.
Yet it does do this.
Heaven and earth together with all creatures, foliage and grass, rain and drought, fruitful and unfruitful years, food and drink, health and sickness, wealth and poverty, and all things speak to the devout of God.
There is no part, no little piece of the world, in which his power and divinity are not beheld.
The human being is directed to heaven and earth, birds and ants, flowers and lilies, in order to behold and acknowledge God.
Scripture does not reason in the abstract and make God the conclusion of a syllogism, then leaving it to us whether we find that proof compelling.
Rather, it speaks with authority; it proceeds theologically and religiously from God, displays his perfections in his works, and demands that we acknowledge God.
An ox knows its owner, and a donkey its master’s manger, but Israel has no knowledge; my people do not understand, Isa. 1:3.
Scripture takes it as certain that God reveals himself in the creatures and leaves no one without witness, Acts 14:17; Rom. 1:19.
And thus, in appealing to the whole creation as a testimony and revelation of God, Scripture contains in germ everything that was later elaborated at length in the proofs and unfolded dialectically.
There is truth in C. J. Nitzsch’s observation, _Syst. der Chr. Lehre_, 6te Aufl., S. 142, that Holy Scripture gives us a beginning and analogy of the aetiological proof in Rom. 1:20; of the teleological proof in Ps. 8; 19; Acts 14:17; of the moral proof in Rom. 2:14; and of the ontological proof in Acts 17:24; Rom. 1:19, 32.
But in speaking this way—not in dialectical demonstration but in the language of testimony, not appealing to the reasoning intellect but addressing the heart and conscience, the whole rational and moral consciousness of the human being—Scripture has never been without power and influence.
In this regard too it is a powerful Word, sharp as a two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and spirit, a discerner of the thoughts and deliberations of the heart.
Even without logical demonstration and philosophical proof, Scripture is powerful in this testimony of its own because it is the Word of God and finds support in the rational and moral nature of every human being.
It is God himself who leaves no one without witness.
And it is the human being himself who, because he was created in God’s image, must despite himself give ear and assent to this testimony.
In this light the so-called proofs for the existence of God must also be considered.
This will preserve us both from overestimating and from despising the proofs.
