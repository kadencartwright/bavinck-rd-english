## 5.

The ontological proof occurs in three forms. From the universal ideas and forms in the human spirit, that is, from _the_ reason that stands above our individual reason and above the whole world, it concludes to absolute reason, the sum of all those ideas and forms, the absolutely true and good and beautiful, that is, to God (Plato, Augustine, Boethius, Anselm in his _Monologium_). Or it proceeds from the necessary thought of the highest, absolute idea, that is, of God, and from it concludes to the existence of that idea, since otherwise it would not be the absolute, the highest idea, because an idea that also actually exists is greater and higher than one that does not actually exist (Anselm in his _Proslogium_). Or it sees in the innateness of the idea of God a fact that cannot be explained otherwise than by the existence of God, who implanted this idea in man (Descartes). The forms differ, but the proof is one insofar as it concludes from thought to being. Now, with respect to creatures, this is impermissible, as everyone acknowledges. For they exist contingently; they can be thought as not existing without any logical contradiction. Thought and being belong to entirely different categories and are wholly different things. From the thought of something—for example, a winged horse—it by no means follows that this something actually exists. Being is no product of thought. No one intended this with the ontological proof either. But the question is whether precisely with the idea of God one may not conclude from thought to being. To this the answer is, first: yes, but on one condition, namely if God exists. _If_ God exists, then he exists necessarily, and in him _essentia_ and _existentia_ are one. But precisely this condition had to be proved; it had to be demonstrated that God exists, Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, p. 156 f. Second, it must be answered that from _our_ thought, even from our _necessary_ thought of the idea of God, one cannot nevertheless conclude to God’s existence, since God’s being is not, and cannot be, a product of our thinking of the idea of God. The world of ideas and forms, including also the idea of God, is not to be identified with God; it is something within us. God’s existence is thus not yet given of itself with those ideas. At most, from the presence of those ideas within us one can conclude to the existence of God, but then this is properly not an ontological but a cosmological proof. The very being of the idea of God within us, however necessary it may be, never includes and cannot as such already include God’s existence. The ontological proof is therefore no proof in the strict sense. It entails only: 1º, that the world of ideas and forms, and thus also the highest idea, is thought by us not arbitrarily but necessarily; and 2º, that the world of ideas and forms, and specifically also the idea of God, as soon as we think it, is and must also be thought by us as really existing. The necessary thought of the idea of God also entails the necessary thought of that idea as really existing. But it brings us no further. We remain moving within the sphere of thought. It does not carry us across the chasm from thought to being. It would do that only if one could conclude from those ideas, not as ideas but from the being of those ideas within us, to a being who created and maintained those ideas within us and must himself be the highest idea; but then, as has been said, it would not be an ontological but a cosmological proof. Cf. Runze, _Der ontol. Gottesbeweis, krit. Darst. seiner Gesch. seit Ans. bis auf die Gegenwart_, Halle, 1882; Körber, _Das ontol. Argument_, Bamberg, 1884.
