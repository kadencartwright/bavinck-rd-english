## 7.

The teleological proof proceeds from the order and beauty, the harmony and purpose, that can be observed in the world, whether in its entirety or in particular creatures—the starry heavens, elements, earth, man, animal, plant, hand, eye, and so forth—and from these concludes to an intelligent cause. Although the teleological proof never fails to make an impression and was also mentioned with respect by Kant, many objections have nevertheless been brought against it, especially in the present day. First, from the materialistic side it is maintained that there is no purpose in things and that the teleological explanation of nature must make way for the mechanical; e.g., Haeckel, _Nat. Schöpf._, 1874, p. 16 f.; Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._, 1882, p. 570 f.; Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_, 16te Aufl., p. 214 f. Second, pantheism objects that the presence of order and purpose in the world by no means gives the right to accept a conscious, intelligent cause, because the unconscious, both in every human being and in the world-whole, works much more wisely and certainly than conscious reflection and deliberate calculation, Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._, 9te Aufl., 1882, passim. Finally, Kant raised the objection that this proof led at most to a world-former but not to a world-creator, _Kr. d. reinen Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, p. 500. Now many other considerations outweigh these objections. Scripture everywhere acknowledges purpose in creation, Gen. 1; Prov. 8; Ps. 8; 1 Cor. 3:21–23; Rom. 8:28; etc. The teleological worldview was honored by almost all philosophers—Anaxagoras, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, and others—and repeatedly presses itself upon every human being despite the abuse repeatedly made of it, especially in the previous century. Purposes can be observed both in particular phenomena—climate, seasons, the temperature of water, the fertilization of plants, blood circulation, organisms, hand, eye, and so forth—and in the world-whole. All atoms obey one law; everything rests on thought that can be comprehended by man; the universe can no more be explained from an accident than the _Iliad_ from a throw of letters. And even the fact that we repeatedly cannot indicate a purpose proves that, where we do observe it, we also do so rightly. Moreover, the teleological worldview does not exclude the mechanical in physical nature; the latter only goes beyond its power and competence when it wishes to explain all phenomena in the world, including conscious and spiritual ones, from matter and metabolism. Ulrici, _Gott u. die Natur_, 1866; idem, _Gott u. d. Mensch_, 1874; P. Janet, _Les causes finales_, 1882; Ebrard, _Apolog._ §74 f.; Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_ I² 241 f., 310 f., 788 f.; Kennedy, _Gottesgl. und mod. Weltanschauung_, 1893; Doedes, _De Leer van God_, p. 118 f.; etc. If the presence of order and purpose stood established for all and did not itself need to be argued so forcefully and extensively in this age, the teleological proof would indeed give us the right to conclude from the beauty and harmony of the world to a conscious being, for there is no unconscious purpose. Hartmann has indeed tried to argue the contrary, but his proofs for the purposeful operation of the unconscious fail because it is one thing whether a person does something consciously and with deliberate purpose, and another whether a purpose can be observed in an unconscious action. Instinct works unconsciously, but nevertheless in the unconscious action of instinct a purpose can be discerned that points back to a being who consciously produced such an instinct. If there is purpose in the world, it must also have been thought beforehand. Even if it is true that with the teleological proof we advance no further than a world-former, that proof would nevertheless be of no small significance. Indeed, even if this proof did not specifically lead to one intelligent being but left open the possibility of many divine beings who together produced the world, the teleological proof would still not be devoid of all value. The arrangement of the world would nevertheless require intelligence in the first cause or causes, and this proof would have furnished what it could furnish. Only then, having arrived along this path at one or more world-formers, would one confront the difficulty how a being that did not produce matter could nevertheless give that matter form and shape, or also how more than one being could be the _prima causa_ of the world. Logically these objections may still have some value; practically they no longer have significance for our consciousness. Everything here depends on the presence of purpose in the world; if that stands established, the consciousness of the highest being is thereby given, Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_ II² 305 f.
