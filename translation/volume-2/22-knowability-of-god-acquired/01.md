# § 22. The Knowability of God (_Cognitio Dei acquisita_)

## 1.

Although there may be no opposition between the _cognitio Dei insita_ and the _cognitio Dei acquisita_, there is undoubtedly a distinction between them.
This distinction is often understood to mean that the former denotes the knowledge of God that is innate in the human being and belongs to him by virtue of his own being, while the latter denotes the knowledge that comes to him partly from outside, from the contemplation of the world, in order to augment and expand the former.
But that does not state the difference between them clearly and precisely.
For, in the proper sense, no knowledge is innate, either of God or of the world.
All knowledge enters human consciousness from outside.
What is innate is only the capacity for knowledge; but this capacity first passes into operation and action through the influence of the world within and around us.
The seed of religion does indeed lie within the human being, but it germinates only in the soil of the whole of human life.
Just as the eye is brought along by a person at birth but first sees objects in the light of the sun, so the believer first beholds God in all the works of his hands.
Furthermore, in the preceding section it became clear to us that by the innate and implanted character of the knowledge of God Christian theology never understood that a person brought some conscious knowledge of God along at birth; by this it meant only that the knowledge of God does not need to be instilled in a person by coercion or force, nor by logical reasoning or compelling proofs, but that it is proper to him by nature, spontaneously, of itself.
A person developing normally arrives at some knowledge of God spontaneously, without coercion or effort.
Therefore the _cognitio Dei insita_ does not stand in opposition to the _acquisita_, for in a broader sense the former too can be called _acquisita_.
After all, God’s revelation precedes the _cognitio Dei_, both _insita_ and _acquisita_.
He does not leave himself without witness.
With his eternal power and divinity he acts upon the human being from every side, not only from without but also from within; in nature and the human world, in heart and conscience, in prosperity and adversity, God, as it were, comes to meet the human being.
And having been created in God’s image, the human being received the capacity to take in the impressions of this and thereby to arrive at some awareness and some knowledge of the Eternal Being.
As soon as the _cognitio insita_ is _cognitio_, and thus not only _potentia_ but _actus_, it never arises except under the influence of God’s revelation within and outside the human being and is therefore always _acquisita_ to that extent.
For these reasons, the distinction between the _cognitio Dei insita_ and the _cognitio Dei acquisita_ was generally stated differently in former times.
It consisted in the following two points.
First, the _cognitio Dei innata_ was obtained of itself, spontaneously, without effort or coercion, whereas the _cognitio Dei acquisita_ was acquired by reasoning and demonstration, by reflection and proof, along the _viae causalitatis, eminentiae_, and _negationis_; the former was _noetica_, the latter _dianoetica_.
And second, from this there also followed the distinction that the former consisted only in principles and was universal and necessary, while the latter was more elaborated and developed, yielded more concrete propositions, and was therefore also subject to all manner of doubt and dispute.
That there is a God was and remains firmly established for virtually everyone.
But the proofs for the existence of God were discovered by thought and have therefore been alternately despised and highly esteemed, Moor, _Comm._ I 41, 44.
And with this the distinction is stated correctly.
It does not lie in the idea that the innate knowledge of God would arise from the human being and the acquired knowledge from the world.
The moral proof too is derived from the moral consciousness found within the human being.
In both cases it is the same, entire revelation of God from which the _cognitio Dei_ penetrates into our consciousness.
But in the _cognitio Dei insita_ that revelation acts upon human consciousness and produces impressions and awarenesses there.
In the _cognitio Dei acquisita_, that revelation of God is thought through by the human being; his understanding sets to work, his reflection awakens, and with clear, lucid consciousness he seeks by reasoning and proof to ascend from the creatures to God.
For in no domain of knowledge is the human being content with impressions and awarenesses.
He is not satisfied with consciousness alone.
It is not enough for him _that_ he knows.
He also wants to _know_ that he knows; he seeks to explain the _how_ and _why_ of his knowing.
Hence ordinary, everyday, empirical knowing always seeks to pass over into proper, scientific knowing; faith rises to theology, and the _cognitio Dei insita_ completes itself in the _cognitio Dei acquisita_.
