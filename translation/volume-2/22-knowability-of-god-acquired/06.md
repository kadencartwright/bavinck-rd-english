## 6.

The cosmological proof too appears in various forms. From motion it concludes to a _primum movens, quod a nullo movetur_ (Aristotle), from the mutable to the immutable (Damascene), from the relative to the absolutely perfect (Boethius, Anselm), from the _series causarum_, which cannot be infinite, to a _prima causa efficiens_ (Thomas), from the contingent existence of the world to an _ens necessarium_ (idem), from that which does not exist of itself to that which exists through itself (Richard of St. Victor), from the existence of the relative to that of the absolute (Spinoza, Hegel, Hartmann, Scholten), and so forth. But in all these forms the cosmological proof nevertheless always amounts to the same thing, namely a conclusion from an effect to a cause. In itself this is also an entirely legitimate conclusion, despite the criticism of Hume and Kant; if we may no longer apply the law of causality, all science is impossible. But the cosmological proof proceeds from various suppositions that are not established in themselves and for all. It presupposes not only that the particular things in the world but also that the entire universe is contingent, finite, relative, imperfect; that a _series causarum in infinitum_ is unthinkable; and that the law of causality also applies to the world as a whole. Only if all these suppositions were correct would the cosmological proof have force. It rightly concludes that, just as all things, so also the world as a whole, as composed of those things, must have a cause. But it carries us no further; it says nothing concerning the character and nature of that cause. Whoever concludes from the world to a cause which itself again needs a cause has satisfied the logical force of this proof. That this cause is infinite, absolute, perfect does not follow directly from the cosmological proof but flows from other considerations. Thus a _series causarum infinita_ is indeed unthinkable and impossible; there is no one who accepts it. All acknowledge an absolute ground, a first being, whether this bears the name of God or the absolute, of substance or power, of matter or will, and so forth. If this supposition is correct, as it is in fact acknowledged by all, then the cosmological proof brings us one step further, namely to a cause of the world that exists of itself and is therefore infinite, eternal, absolute. But whether that cause is also transcendent or only immanent, whether it is personal or impersonal, conscious or unconscious, is by no means decided by this. Many, however, have tried to derive something more from the cosmological proof, whether directly or indirectly. They tried to do so directly by concluding, because an effect cannot contain more than lies in the cause, from the personal, the conscious, the free, the ideas present in the world, to a personal, conscious, free being who was at the same time the highest idea, absolute reason. This reasoning, however, founders on the fact that the relation of cause and effect is then arbitrarily conceived as an emanation and, applied to God, would also require that he be material, corporeal, indeed even impure and unholy. Others therefore took a side road and reasoned that an infinite, absolute cause, a being who exists from, in, and through itself, can exist only as spirit, person; e.g., Dorner, _Gl._ I 243 f.; Kahnis, _Luth. Dogm._ I² 122; Doederlein, _Zeits. f. Philos. u. philos. Kritik_, 1886, p. 52 f. But against this stands the objection that through the cosmological proof we know nothing of the inner nature of such a first cause, that we have no right to apply the law of causality in turn to such a first cause, and therefore can determine nothing concerning it. And so it remains the case that the cosmological proof, at most—namely, with the acknowledgment that an infinite series of causes is impossible—leads us to a first, self-existent, absolute world-cause, Strauss, _Der alte u. d. neue Gl._, 1872, p. 214 f.; Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_ II² 288 f.
