## 2.

In the Christian church there was initially considerable difference of opinion concerning the content of the image of God.
It was located sometimes in the human body, then in the rational nature or in freedom of the will, then again in dominion or also in other moral virtues such as love, righteousness, and so forth; cf. passages in Suicerus, s.v. εἰκων; Petavius, _De sex dier. opif._ II, c. 2; Münscher-v. Coelln I 339 f.; Hagenbach § 56.
Gradually, however, two views came to stand clearly alongside and over against one another, both of which appealed to the distinction between צלם and דמות.
Some, namely, such as Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ II, c. 22; Origen, _De princ._ III 6; and others, observed that Gen. 1:26 does say that God intends to create the human being according to his image and likeness, but that according to verse 27 he actually creates him only according to his image, that is, with a rational nature, so that the human being might now acquire likeness to God himself in the way of obedience and at the end receive it from God’s hand as a reward.
Others, by contrast, were of the opinion that with the image, that is, the rational nature, the human being also immediately received the likeness as a gift and that, having lost it through sin, he regains it through Christ, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ V 16, 2; Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 59; _C. Gent._ 2; _De incarn._ 3.
The first view, which might be called the naturalistic view, found support in the doctrine of free will, as a result of which holiness could not be conceived as a gift of God bestowed upon the human being immediately but only as a good to be acquired by the human being himself in the way of moral exertion, Harnack II 134 f.
Many therefore also taught that the first human being was created in a state not of positive holiness but of childlike innocence, Tertullian, _De an._ 38; Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ II 24, 27; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV 38.
Pelagius later appealed to such statements when he located the essence and original state of the human being in moral indifference, in nothing but formal freedom of choice.
The image of God consists only in the God-given _naturalis possibilitas perfectionis_, which cannot be lost and is therefore still the portion of every human being.
God grants the _posse_, but the _velle_ is ours, Augustine, _De gratia Christi_ I 3 sq.
Later this view gained acceptance among the Socinians, who held that the image of God consisted solely in dominion, Fock, _Der Socin._ 484; among the Anabaptists, who said that the first human being, as a finite, earthly creature, was not yet the image of God but could first become it through regeneration, Menno Simons, _Werken_ 125, 126, 180; cf. Erbkam, _Gesch. der prot. Sekten_ 461; Cloppenburg, _Op._ II 144 sq.; among the Remonstrants, _Conf._ V 5; _Apol. conf._, ibid.; Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV 3, 7; Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ II 24, 5; among the Rationalists and Supernaturalists, Wegscheider, _Inst. theol._ § 99; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ §§ 115, 116; Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 70; and among many more recent theologians, Dorner, _Gl._ I 515; Lange, _Dogm._ II 298 f.; Müller, _Sünde_ II 457 f.; Beck, _Lehrw._ I 186 f.; _Chr. Gl._ II 328 f.; Martensen, _Dogm._ 139; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 432; Zöckler, _Lehre v. Urstand des Menschen_, 1879, S. 40 f., 333; Grétillat, _Theol. syst._ III 464; S. Hofstede de Groot, _Gron. Godg._ 89 f.; Doedes, _Leer der Zaligheid_ § 24; _Ned. Geloofsbel._ 145; _Heid. Catech._ 69; and so forth—all of whom make the _status integritatis_ consist in a state of childlike innocence.
Ordinarily, all these theologians still hold to the historical truth of such an original state.
But substantially, in their understanding of the image of God in the first human being, they agree completely with those who, detaching the idea from the fact, deny the reality of the _status integritatis_ and locate the image of God solely in free personality, in the rational or moral nature, in the religious-ethical disposition, in the destiny of the human being for communion with God, Kant, _Relig._, ed. Rosenkranz, 27 f.; Fichte, _Die Bestimmung des Menschen_, 1800; Hegel, _Werke_ XI 183 f.; Schleiermacher, _Gl._ §§ 59-61; Strauss, _Gl._ II 72; Biedermann, _Dogm._ II 562 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ §§ 420, 440; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III³ 314; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 306 f.; Bovon, _Dogm. Chrét._ I 132, 139; Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 304 f., II 67 f.
Imperceptibly, this view then leads to the doctrine of evolution, according to which the essence of the human being lies not in what he was or is but in what he can become in endless development through the exertion of his own strength.
Paradise lies not behind us but before us.
An evolved ape is to be preferred to a fallen human being.
Originally bearing the image of the orangutan and the chimpanzee, the human being gradually raised himself from a state of crude brutality to one of noble humanity; cf. literature, vol. I, 236.
