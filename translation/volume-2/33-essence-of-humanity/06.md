## 6.

The Reformers unanimously rejected this doctrine, but chiefly because it led to a weakening of original sin. Their opposition was directed especially against the scholastic proposition _supernaturalia amissa, naturalia adhuc esse integra_. From this they reasoned back to the image of God. If through sin, through the loss of the image of God, the human being was wholly corrupted, then that image must also have belonged to human nature. Thus Luther said, _justitiam non esse quoddam donum, quod ab extra accederet, separatumque a natura hominis sed fuisse vere naturalem, ut naturae Adae esset diligere Deum, credere Deo etc._, _In Genes._ III. Yet the Reformers too had to continue distinguishing between what remained of the image of God and what had been lost from it. For this they used the names _substantia, essentia_ and _dotes, dona_, even _supernaturalia dona_. The _Apol. Conf._ calls the knowledge and fear of God in Adam _dona_, and the _Form. Conc._ speaks of _dotes in paradiso naturae concreatae_, Müller, _Die symb. Bücher der ev. luth._ K⁵, pp. 80, 81, 580. The Lutheran dogmaticians indeed called the image of God natural insofar as human nature could not be pure without that image and was created with it from the outset. But they denied that the image of God was natural in the sense that it flowed automatically from human nature as such and would thus constitute an inalienable, essential component of it. Some, such as Gerhard, Quenstedt, and others, specifically designated the _supernaturalis Dei favor_, the _gratiosa s. trinitatis inhabitatio_, and the _suavitas et delectatio_ flowing from it as _dona supernaturalia_, Gerhard, _Loci theol._ VIII, c. 1–3; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ II 1–48; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 461–488. Likewise Calvin distinguishes between the _substantia animae_ and its _dotes_, _Inst._ I 15, 2, and says with Augustine, _naturalia dona fuisse corrupta in homine per peccatum, supernaturalibus autem exinanitum fuisse_; he even calls the latter _adventitia, praeter naturam_, II 2, 12. And many Reformed theologians distinguish in the same sense between _qualitates naturales_ and _dona supernaturalia_, Maccovius, _Loci Comm._ 405 sq. The _immortalitas_ is derived by many not from Adam’s nature but from the _gratia Dei_, Zanchius, _Op._ III 497; Polanus V 29; Junius, _Op._ I 211; Bucanus, _Theol._ XI 12; Maccovius, p. 409. The old distinction between image and likeness was even adopted by many and applied in this sense, Zanchius, _Op._ III 486; Martyr on Gen. 1:26; Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 281. Yet it soon became clear that Protestants, even where they still retained the expression _dona supernaturalia_, understood it in a different sense from the Roman Catholics. The latter mean that a human being is perfectly conceivable and possible without these _dona supernaturalia_. Such a human being, as a rational moral being, indeed then possesses some knowledge of God, the moral law, and righteousness. But there is an essential difference between knowledge, love, and righteousness in the natural and supernatural senses; between the _homo naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_; between the human being and the Christian; between the world and the church; between nature and grace. Grace is not merely a restoration but an elevation and supplementation of nature, an _elevatio, sublimatio naturae_. This was opposed in principle by the Reformation. It therefore had to arrive, and did in fact arrive, at the doctrine that the image of God belonged to the human being by nature and that without this image the human being could exist only _in natura impura_, as a sinner. Yet among the Reformers there was still a difference in the understanding of the image of God. In the earliest period some Lutherans still placed the image of God in the human being’s essence and in the substance of the soul, Luther in Köstlin, I 121; Melanchthon, Hemming, Selnecker in Heppe, _Dogm. d. d. Prot._ I 338 f. Lutheran theology nevertheless proceeded from a different thought. Its subjective, soteriological character had to lead it to seek the image of God only in the moral qualities received by the first human being, the loss of which made the human being a stick and a block in religious and moral respects. Luther repeatedly placed all the emphasis on the gifts and made the image of God consist in them, Köstlin II 358 f.; Heppe I 345. The confessional writings speak in the same spirit, Müller, pp. 80, 576, and likewise the theologians, Heerbrand, Hunnius, Gerhard VIII, c. 1; Quenstedt II 3–10, 17–23; Hollaz, p. 464 sq.; etc. The Lutherans indeed do not deny that the essence of the human being also expresses _quaedam θεια sive divina_, but the image of God proper lies only in the _justitia originalis_ together with the _immortalitas, impassibilitas, dominium_, and _conditio felicissima_ connected with it. For the Son alone is essentially and substantially the image of God, Heb. 1:3; in the human being it is a _perfectio accidentalis_, capable of being lost and in fact lost, Rom. 3:23, and renewed and restored only in the believer, Rom. 8:29; 2 Cor. 3:18; 5:17; Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10. The Reformed, however, included the human being’s essence in the image of God from the beginning. Heppe, _Dogm._ 169, 170, incorrectly claims that Calvin and Zanchius did not teach this. Calvin indeed distinguishes between the _substantia animae_ and its _dotes_, but expressly says that the image of God consisted in that _tota praestantia, qua eminet hominis natura inter omnes animantium species_, and that it further, _proinde_, also consists in _integritas_, _Inst._ I 15, 2; II 12, 6; cf. on Gen. 1:26; 9:6; James 3:9. All the Reformed agree with this, Zanchius, _Op._ III 486, 677 sq.; Ursinus, _Catech._, qu. 7; Martyr, _Loci C._ 46 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ V 34; _Synopsis_ XIII 36; Leydecker, _Fax Verit._, p. 395; etc. Cocceius was the first to present another opinion, _S. Theol._ XVII § 12–24, and taught that the soul and its properties were indeed the presupposition but not the content of the image of God. They were, as it were, the canvas upon which the image was painted by God, but the image itself consisted only in the gifts, as is evident from 2 Cor. 3:18; Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10; cf. also Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ VI 119; Braun, _Doctr. foed._ I 2, 15, 5 f. Others expressed it by saying that the image of God existed _antecedenter in natura spirituali, formaliter in sanctitate, consequenter in dominio_, Turretin, _Theol. El._ V, qu. 10, § 6; Ryssenius, _Theol._ 178; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ I 2, 11; Brakel, _Red. Godsd._ X 25. Most often, however, they continued to speak of the image of God in a broader and a narrower sense. In this way Reformed theology maintained the bond between the physical and ethical nature of the human being and thus further between nature and grace. To this another distinction was soon added in Reformed theology, one worked out especially in the doctrine of the _foedus operum_. This answered the question not of what Adam was but of what Adam was to become. Only under these three points—the image of God in the broader sense, the image of God in the narrower sense, and the unfolding or destiny of the image of God, that is, in the doctrine of the covenant of works—can the _locus de imagine Dei_ be treated fully.
