## 7.

Between Rome’s doctrine and that of the Reformation concerning the image of God there is a profound difference that extends throughout the whole of theology.
This difference does not lie in the expression _justitia originalis_.
For although this term is used by Roman Catholic theologians in different senses, later theologians also sometimes designate by it the _justitia supernaturalis_; above, p. 519.
Justice in the first human being can be called _originalis_ because his positive conformity to God’s law was proper to him from his origin, because as such it is to be distinguished from the _justitia habitualis_ and _actualis_, and because in Adam it was the principle and root of his actual righteousness.
Since Thomas, there was not even any disagreement about this: if Adam had remained standing, the _justitia originalis_ would also have been the source of the actual righteousness of all humanity, for Adam received it not as _persona privata_ but as _persona publica_.
The entire dispute concerned whether that _justitia originalis_ was _naturalis_ or, at least in part, _supernaturalis_.
The Reformers asserted the former.
By this they did not mean that this original righteousness flowed of itself from human nature understood as the union of spirit and matter, nor that it might not be called a gift, even a gift of God’s grace in the broad sense; by this expression they maintained only that the image of God, original righteousness, was inseparable from the idea of the human being; that it was the human being’s normal state, harmony, and health; that without it a human being cannot be a true, complete, normal human being.
If the human being loses that image of God, he loses no substance and does indeed remain human, but he becomes an abnormal, sick, spiritually dead human being, a sinner; he then lacks something that belonged to his kind and nature, as the blind person lacks sight, the deaf person hearing, and the sick person health.
According to Rome, the human being can lack the _justitia supernaturalis_ and still be a good, true, complete, sinless human being, with a _justitia naturalis_ that is without any defect in its kind.
But according to the Protestants, this is impossible.
There is no _status medius_ between the human being as image of God and the human being as sinner.
He is a son of God, his offspring, his image, or he is a child of wrath, dead in sins and trespasses.
When the human being receives again through faith in Christ that image of God, this perfect righteousness, it is indeed a _donum supernaturale_ but _supernaturale per accidens_; he receives back what belongs to his being, like the blind person who receives sight again.
This doctrine is grounded in Holy Scripture, which nowhere speaks of _dona supernaturalia_ at the creation of the human being; Rome therefore appeals not to Gen. 1:26, 31; Eccles. 7:29, and so forth, but to the New Testament representation of the _status gratiae_ and _gloriae_, which, however, can by no means serve as proof.
Scripture everywhere proceeds from the fact that the human being is related to God and is his offspring; service of God, love for God, and fellowship with God are not a _donum superadditum_ but are originally, by nature, proper to the human being.
God claims the whole human being—with understanding, heart, soul, body, and all powers—for his service and his love.
The moral law is one for all human beings in all times and holds the moral ideal equally high for all.
There is no lower and higher righteousness, no twofold morality, no double kind of duties.
Original righteousness is so natural that, according to most Roman Catholic theologians as well, it would have been inherited by Adam’s descendants in the event of his obedience, and the pagans even now do by nature the things of the law, Rom. 2:15.
When it is therefore objected against this Reformation doctrine that it exists in an antinomy because it calls the _justitia originalis_ on the one hand _naturalis_ and yet on the other _amissibilis_ and _accidentalis_, Benschop in _De Katholiek_ CX, p. 43; also Strauss, _Glaub._ I 708; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 434, this objection arises solely from misunderstanding; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ III 408.
Original righteousness is naturally so called not because it consists in a certain substance or essence but because it is a natural property or quality.
Just as health belongs to human nature but is nevertheless capable of being lost, so it is also with the image of God.
Both Rome and the Reformation agree that original righteousness is not matter, not a spiritual substance as the Manichaeans teach, but an _accidens_, a _qualitas_.
And the difference concerns only whether it is naturally accidental or, at least in part, supernaturally accidental; _De Katholiek_, ib., pp. 56–60.
Rome teaches only of the _justitia naturalis_ that it is naturally accidental; the Reformation teaches this of the whole _justitia originalis_.
But therefore, as was already said above, Rome’s objection to the doctrine that the image of God is natural also falls away completely; for Rome itself acknowledges that the _justitia naturalis_ is natural and yet capable of being lost and can therefore no longer raise this objection against the doctrine of the Protestants.
Rome’s doctrine did not arise from the objection that the naturalness of original righteousness cannot be reconciled with its capacity to be lost, but owes its origin to an entirely different series of thoughts, namely, to the Neoplatonic conception of the Christian ideal of life.
It is this Neoplatonism that the Reformation rejected on the ground of Scripture.
And in doing so it guarded itself against every Manichaeism.
Through sin the human being lost no substance.
In this sense the human being remains human even after the fall.
But through the loss of original righteousness he lost the harmony and health of his nature and became entirely a sinner; his nature in the sense of substance, essence, remained, but the moral qualities proper to him by nature were lost; cf. Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 309f.
This beautiful conception of the image of God and of original righteousness came into its own even better in the Reformed church and theology than in the Lutheran.
In Lutheran theology the image of God is restricted to original righteousness and was therefore entirely lost with it.
The separation between the spiritual and the worldly, between the heavenly and the earthly, is drawn so sharply here and divided into two _hemisphaeria_ that the connection between nature and grace, creation and re-creation, is entirely misunderstood; cf. I 222f.
The supernaturalistic representation of the image of God has not yet been entirely overcome here; it stands detached alongside and above nature, and its loss does indeed result in the human being being completely deaf and blind in spiritual things, but in earthly things he is still capable of much good and to a certain extent independent of God’s grace in Christ.
The Reformed, however, through their distinction between the image of God in a broader and a narrower sense, maintained most purely the connection between substance and quality, nature and grace, creation and re-creation.
This distinction too has indeed often been conceived far too mechanically and ought to be further developed organically.
But here it is nevertheless expressed most clearly that the image of God in the narrower sense was most closely connected with that in the broader sense; that both components constituted the full image of God; that not something in the human being but the human being himself, the whole human being, was the image of God; that sin, which caused the loss of the image of God in the narrower sense and corrupted and devastated it in the broader sense, affected the whole human being; and that consequently grace in Christ also restores the whole human being and is of the greatest significance for his whole life and labor, including in family, society, state, art, science, and so forth.
