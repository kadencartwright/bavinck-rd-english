## 5.

First, this doctrine is built upon an incorrect conception of humanity’s final destiny.
The _status gratiae_ and _status gloriae_ in which the church of Christ shares here and hereafter are described in Holy Scripture in the most beautiful manner: as being children of God, as participation in the divine nature, as the vision of God, as eternal life, as heavenly blessedness, and so forth.
On this there is no difference between Rome and us: no eye has seen, no ear has heard, nor has it entered the human heart, what God in the New Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace has prepared for those who love him, 1 Cor. 2:9.
But Rome understands this final destiny of the human being, which Christ has realized, as a Neoplatonic vision of God and a mystical fusion of the soul with God; cf. above, 152 f. and 518.
And this is not the doctrine of Holy Scripture.
For all those benefits that Christ acquired for his own are not first granted to them in the state of glory but in principle are already their portion here on earth, 1 Cor. 2:9, and yet, according to Rome, they do not include the vision of God _per essentiam_.
Being a child of God is a fruit of faith, John 1:12; Rom. 8:14 f.; Gal. 4:6; 1 John 3:1, 2.
Eternal life is already enjoyed here and consists in knowing God in the face of Christ, John 3:16, 36; 17:3.
Christ is and remains the way to the Father, to the knowledge and vision of God, Matt. 11:27; John 1:18; 14:6; 1 John 3:2b.
The _visio Dei_ can be attained only in an ethical sense, Matt. 5:8; 1 John 3:6.
Even participation in the θεια φυσις is not something future only but is a goal at which the granting of God’s promises already aims here on earth, 2 Pet. 1:4, and is again ethically _vermittelt_, Heb. 12:10.
Second, nowhere in Holy Scripture is it taught that this _status gloriae_, which already begins in the _status gratiae_ on earth, is _supranaturalis_ and _superadditus_ in the Roman Catholic sense.
Certainly, this state of grace and glory far surpasses every human thought and imagination, 1 Cor. 2:9; 13:12; 1 John 3:2.
But it should be remembered that according to 1 Cor. 2 it is for the wisdom of the world, the spirit of the world, and the psychical human being, vv. 6, 8, 12, 14, that these benefits of God are a mystery; and this psychical human being is the person who allows himself to be led solely by animal life, is deprived of the πνευμα ἐκ θεου, and is therefore darkened in his understanding.
In addition, what God grants and will grant believers remains for them too an unmerited gift of grace, which continually moves them to wonder and worship.
And neither should it be forgotten that Christ acquired not only what Adam lost but also what Adam would have acquired in the way of obedience.
The blessing of salvation in the covenant of grace therefore far surpasses all our thoughts; but not a word is said of its being a _superadditum_ that did not originally belong to our human nature.
Third, the conclusion that Rome draws from these benefits of grace in Christ for the state of Adam before the fall is incorrect.
Rome reasons as follows: if the _status gloriae_ for the restored human being consists in conformity to God, then it must also have consisted in this for the first human being.
And if believers now attain that _status gloriae_ only through the _status gratiae_, then this also applies to Adam before the fall.
According to Rome, the connection between the _status gratiae_ and the _status gloriae_ consists in this: in justification the human being receives the _gratia infusa_ and by its power does good works that merit eternal life _ex condigno_.
Rome transfers all this to the first human being.
He too had the _status gloriae_ as his final destiny; he could attain it only through the _status gratiae_; Adam was therefore granted a _gratia_ by which he could merit eternal life _ex condigno_.
Like that _status gloriae_, this _gratia_ too is supernatural by its very nature, elevated above the psychical human being, and thus a _donum superadditum_.
This reasoning of Rome is correct insofar as it reasons from the image of God in the restored human being to that image in the first human being.
There is indeed no more than one image of God.
It is also correct insofar as it assumes that the final destiny for Adam was no different from what the believer now receives through Christ, namely, eternal life.
There really is only one ideal for the human being.
But it is incorrect in that it posits a relationship of merit between _gratia_ and _gloria_ and then applies this to Adam as well.
The meritorious value of the _bona opera_ can be treated only later.
Here it should merely be pointed out that even if the Roman Catholic conception of humanity’s final destiny were correct, it would still provide no right to understand the _gratia_ in an adequate, supernatural sense.
For a connection is also possible in which God attaches particular promises and gifts to particular works without these thereby having been merited in the strict sense, _ex condigno_.
Of this kind was the promise of eternal life to Adam in the event of obedience, as the Reformed taught in their doctrine of the _foedus operum_; cf. § 32.
There was a merit _ex pacto_, not _ex condigno_.
The good works of the human being are never worth heavenly glory; they never measure up to it.
Rome, however, introduces the meritoriousness of good works both in the believer and in Adam and thereby detracts from grace.
In Rome’s theology, grace changes character completely.
It stands in a physical, not an ethical, opposition to nature.
It presupposes not sin and guilt but only a lower nature.
On the one hand, it makes everything grace and thereby ensures precisely that there is no grace at all.
For the _gratia_ in Adam and in the believer is indeed grace, but in no other sense than that in which life, understanding, wisdom, strength, and so forth are also grace.
There is no reason to call only the _donum superadditum_ grace; Adam’s being created and receiving understanding and will were then just as much grace, even though there is a quantitative difference among the gifts of grace.
And thus everything that God grants the human being at the first moment in creation or re-creation becomes grace.
But it is grace only at that first moment.
For as soon as he has received those first gifts, according to Rome the human being himself goes to work through that _gratia_; and everything he receives thereafter he receives as a reward for his merits.
Even eternal life is no longer a gracious gift of God but a worthy, fitting, adequate reward for the work of the human being.
It can still be called a gracious gift only because the power that enabled the human being to merit was grace.
It is just as the Pelagians said of old: the _posse_ is from God; the _velle_ is from the human being.
Fourth, it was shown above, 520-521, that the Roman Catholic doctrine of the _donum superadditum_ has led and must lead to a threefold conception of the human being.
For Rome there is not one idea, one moral law, and one destiny for the human being; rather, just as among the other creatures, hierarchy and gradation continue throughout the human world as well.
In the abstract, it is possible to conceive of one _homo_ with _justitia naturalis_, another with _justitia praeternaturalis_, and a third with _justitia supernaturalis_.
The first of these three human beings is really elevated only a little above the animal; he is subject to _concupiscentia_, to the natural opposition between flesh and spirit, and to susceptibility to physical suffering and temporal death.
Yet in his will he still has the power to do good, to keep the _concupiscentia_ from passing over into sinful deeds, and thus to lead a life that is natural but nevertheless sinless.
The human being after the fall rather closely resembles this picture.
Although original sin is still frequently understood not only negatively but also positively, its conception has nevertheless been weakened more and more as the physical contrast between nature and grace has replaced the ethical antithesis between sin and grace.
According to many, it consists only negatively in the loss of that _donum superadditum_ which was granted to the first human being by God’s good pleasure, for example, Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch._ 59, 137 f.
The human being without that _donum superadditum_ therefore remains complete and perfect in his kind and, however difficult it may be—especially in the long run—can even live sinlessly if he wills.
Hence Rome’s mild judgment concerning children who die unbaptized and concerning the pagans who have made good use of the light and power of nature.
They have no guilt and thus no punishment; they receive a _poena damni_, not a _poena sensus_.
God did not, however, create the first human being as such a _homo naturalis_.
He immediately gave him a _justitia praeternaturalis_ in addition, and therefore Adam, even apart from the actual _donum superadditum_, that is, the _gratia gratum faciens_, was elevated above _concupiscentia_, suffering, and death, also with respect to the body.
This is a second idea of the human being, one that is possible and viable; such a human being could then have kept God’s commandments without conflict from the side of the flesh (_donum integritatis_), but his righteousness would nevertheless have remained natural and would not have merited the _visio Dei per essentiam_.
This second conception of the human being is now raised one level higher still by the addition of the _donum superadditum_, through which a human being arises with _justitia supernaturalis_.
As throughout the whole Roman Catholic system, so here too in the doctrine of the image of God we encounter the opposition between the natural and the supernatural, between the human and the divine, between the earthly and the heavenly, and then within both domains all kinds of gradations once again.
There are all kinds of rank, class, and station in the ethical and religious realm.
Not all stand or rise equally high.
If God is the center, the creatures group themselves around him in ever wider circles.
The _homo naturalis_ is furthest removed from him, then the _homo praeternaturalis_, and after that the _homo supernaturalis_.
And within the last class there is again every kind of distinction and degree.
There are _clerici_ and laypeople, monks and ordinary people, _praecepta_ and _consilia_, a lower and a higher morality.
Highest stands the mystic, who already here on earth attains contemplation through meditation, asceticism, and prayer.
And above human beings stand the angels in turn, arrayed in all kinds of ranks.
Everything ascends upward, toward God; it draws ever nearer to him.
The fusion of the soul with God is the highest blessedness.
Therefore in Rome there is a place for everyone; it takes account of each person’s capacity and aptitude; it has a different ideal for different people; it does not make the same moral and religious demands of everyone.
Pierson rightly emphasized this; only he regarded as principle and essence what was merely consequence and phenomenon, _Gesch. van het R. Kath._ I 24 f.
It was against this fundamentally Neoplatonic and Areopagitic philosophy that the Reformation, resting upon Scripture, arose.
Scripture knows no such physical opposition between the natural and the supernatural.
It knows only one idea of the human being, one moral law, one final destiny, one priesthood, which is the portion of all believers.
Fifth, it is clear from this that the reason Rome teaches a _justitia praeternaturalis_ and _supernaturalis_ is not that otherwise the possibility of losing the _justitia originalis_ cannot be explained.
Roman Catholic theologians do continually raise this objection against the doctrine of the Reformation: the _justitia originalis_ either belongs to the essence of the human being or it does not; if the former, then it cannot be lost, or, if it is nevertheless lost, the human being thereby loses a part of his essence and ceases to be a complete human being.
But however often this objection is repeated, it was not in order to escape it that Rome arrived at the doctrine of the _donum superadditum_.
Rome retains precisely the same difficulty.
For Adam also had a _justitia naturalis_.
And this _justitia naturalis_ is indeed natural, flowing from the _principia_ and powers of the natural human being without supernatural assistance—though not without the general assistance of God’s preservation—and yet it can be lost and is in fact lost by many.
Nevertheless, even the most deeply fallen sinner, deprived of all _justitia naturalis_, still remains a human being.
The possibility of losing and the actual loss of the _justitia originalis_ can therefore be of no service whatever as an argument against its natural character.
If that were the case, then the _justitia naturalis_ taught by Rome would also have to bear the name _supernaturalis_.
The question between Rome and us is an entirely different one.
The _justitia originalis_ can be lost because it is an _accidens_, not a substance.
But among the _accidentia_, all of which as such can be lost, Rome once again distinguishes between what is _naturaliter accidens_ and what is _supernaturaliter accidens_; for example, Benschop in _De Katholiek_ CX, p. 56.
According to us, the whole _justitia originalis_ is an _accidens naturale_; according to Rome, the _gratia gratum faciens_ is an _accidens supernaturale_.
Why?
Not in order to make its loss possible and intelligible, but because without it the human being remained a defective creature.
Rome expressly teaches that, granted that God wished to create a being consisting of soul and body, of spirit and matter, it was impossible for him to create these two, soul and body, in perfect harmony without supernatural assistance, just as it is impossible for him to make a square circle.
Flesh and spirit contend with each other _per se_, and God cannot prevent conflict between these two elements without supernatural grace.
Bellarmine says clearly that the human being consists of flesh and spirit and thus has, in part, a _propensio ad bonum corporale_ and, in part, one _ad bonum spirituale_, and that _ex his diversis vel contrariis propensionibus existere in uno eodemque homine pugnam quandam_.
He further teaches that _divinam providentiam initio creationis ut remedium adhiberet huic morbo seu languori naturae humanae, qui ex conditione materiae oriebatur, addidisse homini quoddam donum insigne, justitiam videlicet originalem, qua veluti aureo quodam freno pars inferior superiori et pars superior Deo facile subjecta contineretur_, _De gr. pr. hom._, c. 5; cf. c. 7.
Here it is clearly stated that the flesh by its nature stands opposed to the spirit.
Matter is a power over against God which, though not sinful _per se_ as in Manichaeism, is nevertheless of a very low order, moves in a direction entirely its own, and of itself entices the human being into conflict and sin.
Indeed, this power is so great that the _ratio_ alone cannot control the _motus animi_, or at least can do so only with great difficulty.
A special, supernatural grace is needed for this.
As in the philosophy of Plotinus, matter is a creature that stands very far from God, is by nature hostile to everything spiritual, and must therefore be powerfully restrained.
Sixth and finally, Rome’s doctrine of the _donum superadditum_ entails a peculiar conception of Christianity.
For Rome, the Christian religion does indeed also serve to deliver from sin; but in the first and principal place it serves to restore to the human being that _gratia_ which was granted to Adam as a _donum superadditum_ but was lost by him.
This _gratia_ was just as necessary for the human being before the fall as it is for us now, and it was just as supernatural then as it is now after the fall.
According to Rome, grace is therefore a _donum supernaturale per se_ and not _per accidens_, not supernatural only on account of sin, Bellarmine, _De gr. pr. hom._, c. 5.
Sin brought no change in the nature of the _gratia_.
Perhaps it was increased through sin.
But before and after the fall it was completely the same, namely, an _elevatio supra naturam_.
That is its character and essence.
Christianity may therefore also be an _Erlösungsreligion_; in the very first place it is not a _reparatio_ but an _elevatio naturae_; it serves to raise nature above itself, to deify the human being.
For this purpose the _gratia gratum faciens_ served in Adam, and for this purpose Christianity now serves.
This grace is therefore the same then and now; that is, what is proper and essential in Christianity was by no means made necessary by the fall; it was already necessary before the fall.
Christianity as _elevatio naturae_ was already present in Adam before the fall.
The reception of the _gratia infusa_ is now bound, in addition to _praeparationes_, specifically to faith in two _dogmata_, the Trinity and the incarnation; cf. I 518 f.
Well then, so it was with Adam too.
Before the fall he already knew both, Becanus, _Theol. schol._ I, tr. 5, c. 2, qu. 2; Simar, _Dogm._ 332; Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 89; and others; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 2, art. 7; qu. 5, art. 1.
The incarnation was therefore also _per consequentiam_ necessary before the fall and apart from sin.
In other words, in order that the human being may become like God, God must become human.
This law applied both before and after the fall.
Now the incarnation brings reconciliation with it only as something subordinate.
But for Rome the center of gravity lies not in satisfaction and the forgiveness of guilt but in the incarnation of God and the deification of the human being; cf. I 220 f., 276 f.
