# § 33. The Essence of the Human Being

## 1.

The essence of the human being lies in being the image of God. The whole world is a revelation of God, a mirror of his virtues and perfections; every creature is, in its own way and measure, the embodiment of a divine thought. But among all creatures the human being alone is the image of God, the highest and richest revelation of God and thereby at the same time the head and crown of the whole creation, _imago Dei_ and _compendium naturae_, μικροθεος and μικροκοσμος together. Even pagans have recognized this truth and called the human being God’s image. Pythagoras in Cicero, _De senect._, c. 21; Plato, _De Leg._ IV 713 C; _De Rep._ III 415 A; X 517; Ovid, _Met._ I; Cicero, _De Leg._ I 8; Seneca, _De cons. ad Helv._ 6; and others state clearly and plainly that the human being, or at least the human soul, was created in God’s image, that the human being is akin to God and is his offspring; cf. Pfanner, _Syst. theol. gent._, 1679, p. 189 sq. And not only this, but nearly all peoples have traditions of a golden age, an _aurea aetas_. Among the Chinese, Indians, Iranians, Egyptians, Babylonians, Greeks, Romans, and others, one encounters accounts of an earlier time in which the human being lived in innocence and blessedness and stood in communion with the gods. They were celebrated by the poets, Hesiod, _Op. et dies_ 109 sq.; Ovid, _Met._ I 89 sq.; Virgil, _Georg._ I 125 sq.; _Aen._ VIII 315 sq.; and acknowledged as true by the philosophers, Plato, _Pol._ 272 A; cf. J. G. Friderici, diss. _De aurea aetate quam poetae finxerunt_, Leipzig, 1736; Lüken, _Traditionen des Menschengeschl._, 1869; Zöckler, _Lehre v. Urstand des Menschen_, 1879, p. 84 f.; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch. der Menschheit_, 1887, p. 37 f.; E. L. Fischer, _Heidenthum und Offenbarung_, Mainz, 1878; Zöckler, _Bibl. u. Kirchenhist. Studien_, 1893, V 1 f. Yet Holy Scripture first sets this doctrine of the human being’s likeness to God in its true, full light. The first creation narrative reports that after express deliberation God created the human being בְּצַלְמֵנוּ כִּדְמוּתֵנוּ, κατ’ εἰκονα ἡμετεραν και καθ’ ὁμοιωσιν, _ad imaginem et similitudinem nostram_, Gen. 1:26, 27. Further, in Gen. 5:1 and 9:6 it is repeated that God created the human being בִּדְמוּת אֱלֹהִים and בְּצֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים; Psalm 8 celebrates the human being as lord of creation; and Eccl. 7:29 recalls that God made the human being יָשָׁר. Otherwise the Old Testament says little about the _status integritatis_. More than any other people, Israel was a people of hope; its eye was directed not toward the past but toward the future. Even the New Testament speaks comparatively seldom about the image of God in which the human being was originally created. We find direct mention of it only in 1 Cor. 11:7, where the man is called εἰκων και δοξα θεου, and James 3:9, where human beings are said to be καθ’ ὁμοιωσιν θεου γεγονοτας, while Luke 3:38 calls Adam the son of God and Paul says with a pagan poet, του γαρ και γενος ἐσμεν, Acts 17:28. Indirectly, however, Eph. 4:24 and Col. 3:10 are also of great importance here. They speak of the καινος or νεος ἀνθρωπος whom believers must put on; and of this new human being it is said that in conformity with God, κατα θεον, he was created, κτισθεντα, in righteousness and holiness of the truth, and is renewed in knowledge κατ’ εἰκονα του κτισαντος αὐτον. This implies 1º that the new human being whom believers put on was created by God; 2º that this new human being was made in conformity with God and according to his image; and 3º that this conformity emerges particularly in righteousness and holiness as the fruit of the truth that is known. This refers back to the original creation insofar as the words Paul uses are clearly derived from it; insofar as the second creation, according to the whole teaching of Scripture, is not a _creatio ex nihilo_ but a renewal of what existed; and insofar as the believer’s ἀνακαινουσθαι clearly describes this creation as a renewal. The thought underlying Eph. 4:24 and Col. 3:10 is therefore that the human being was originally created in God’s image and is now renewed according to it in re-creation.

But Scripture not only relates the fact of the creation of the human being in God’s image; it also explains its meaning to us. The two words צלם and דמות, εἰκων and ὁμοιωσις, are certainly not identical, but no essential, substantive distinction can be identified between them. They are used interchangeably and alternate without any definite reason. In Gen. 1:26, cf. 5:3, both occur; in Gen. 1:27 and 9:6, cf. Col. 3:10, only _image_; in Gen. 5:1 and James 3:9, only _likeness_. The distinction between them amounts to this: צלם means image, both _Urbild_ and _Abbild_; דמות means likeness, both _Vorbild_ and _Nachbild_. _Der Begriff von צלם ist starrer, der von דמות flüssiger und so zu sagen geistiger; in jenem überwiegt die Vorstellung des Urbilds, in diesem die Vorstellung des Ideals_, Delitzsch on Gen. 1:26. Likeness is a closer specification, a strengthening and supplementation of image. Likeness is in itself weaker and broader than image; an animal indeed has some features of likeness and correspondence with the human being but is nevertheless not an image of the human being. _Image_ expresses that God is archetype and the human being ectype; _likeness_ adds that this image corresponds in every respect with the original, Augustine, _Quaest._ 83, qu. 74; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 93, art. 9; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ VIII § 18; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ V 10; etc. Just as little as between these two concepts is there an essential distinction between the prepositions ב and כ used with them. These too alternate with one another; in Gen. 5:1 ב occurs with דְמוּת; in verse 3 likewise, and at the same time כ with צֶלֶם; in the New Testament κατα occurs with εἰκων, Col. 3:10, but also with ὁμοιωσις, James 3:9. Nothing can therefore be built upon this. With Delitzsch one can only say: _bei ב denkt man sich die Urform gleichsam als Gussform, bei כ als vorgehaltenes Muster_. There is therefore no ground for deriving from the preposition ב, with Böhl, _Dogm._ 154 f., that the image of God is a sphere and element in which the human being was created; cf. in opposition Kuyper, _De Vleeschwording des Woords_, 1887, p. VIII f.; Daubanton, _Theol. Stud._, 1887, pp. 429–444.

Besides these words, Scripture supplies the following data for the content of the image of God. First, it is clear that the words _image_ and _likeness_ express something not in God but in the human being; they designate not the _imago increata_ but the _imago creata_. The meaning is not that the human being was created according to something in God bearing the name image and likeness, so that, for example, the Son would be indicated by it, but that the human being was so created according to God that he is his image and likeness. Next, this creation in God’s image is not restricted in any respect, either on the side of the archetype or on that of the ectype. It is not said that the human being was created only according to certain virtues or also according to one person in the divine being, nor that only in one part—according to the soul or the understanding or holiness alone—the human being bears God’s image and likeness. Rather, the whole human being is the image of the whole Godhead. Third, the meaning of the image of God is further explained to us by the Son, who in an entirely unique sense is called λογος, υἱος, εἰκων, χαρακτηρ του θεου, John 1:1, 14; 2 Cor. 4:4; Col. 1:15; Heb. 1:3, and to whom we must again be conformed, Rom. 8:29; 1 Cor. 15:49; Phil. 3:21; Eph. 4:23 f.; 1 John 3:2. The Son bears all these names because he is God from God and light from light, sharing the same being and the same virtues with the Father. He is therefore so called not on account of a part or portion of his being but because he corresponds absolutely with the Father. This in turn also applies to the human being. Like the Son, the human being as such, wholly and entirely, is the image of God. He does not merely bear the image of God; he is it. Yet naturally there is this difference: what the Son is in an absolute sense, the human being is in a relative sense. The former is the eternal, only-begotten Son; the latter is the created son of God. The former is the image of God within the divine being; the latter outside it. The former is the image of God in a divine manner; the latter in a creaturely manner. But in this way the human being too is, in his measure, the image and likeness of God. Finally, fourth, here and there it is also indicated in what that image reveals and externally manifests itself. Nowhere is the full content of that image of God unfolded. But Gen. 1:26 clearly indicates that this image of God emerges in the human being’s dominion over all that has been created; cf. Ps. 8; 1 Cor. 11:7. The portrayal of the paradisal state in Gen. 1 and 2 shows that the image of God also includes conformity with the will of God; cf. Eccl. 7:29. And re-creation according to the image of God or Christ is placed primarily in putting on the new human being, which consists, among other things, in righteousness and holiness of the truth.
