## 8.

In treating the doctrine of the image of God, this thought must therefore stand in the foreground, in agreement with Scripture and the Reformed confession: the human being does not _bear_ or _have_ the image of God but _is_ the image of God. As a human being he is God’s son, likeness, and offspring, Gen. 1:26; 9:6; Luke 3:38; Acts 17:28; 1 Cor. 11:7; James 3:9. This includes two things. First, the archetype of the human being is not something in God, some virtue and perfection to the exclusion of others; nor is it one person, for example the Son to the exclusion of the Father and the Spirit; rather, it is God himself, the whole Godhead. It has indeed often been taught that the human being was made specifically according to the Son or the _Christus incarnandus_, Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ V 14; Tertullian, _De resurr. carnis_, c. 6; Osiander, in Calvin, _Inst._ I 15, 2; II 12, 6; Hofmann, _Schriftbew._ I 290; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 126; Beck, _Gl._ II 329; Schoeberlein in Herzog² 4, 7; Martensen, _Dogm._ § 72; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._ 70; etc. But Scripture nowhere says this. It repeatedly states that the human being was made in God’s image, that we were not created according to Christ but that he became like us, Rom. 8:3; Phil. 2:7, 8; Heb. 2:14, and that we, now re-created according to the image of Christ, again become like God, Rom. 8:29; 1 Cor. 15:49; 2 Cor. 3:18; Phil. 3:21; Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10; 1 John 3:2; cf. above, p. 511. It is therefore much better to say that God’s triune being is the archetype of the human being, Augustine, _De trin._ XII 6; Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 16; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 13, art. 5; cf. above, p. 311, although every caution must be observed in the psychological search for trinitarian moments in the human essence, Calvin, _Inst._ I 15, 4; _Comm. in Gen._ 1:26; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XIII 7; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. pol._ II 4; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 466. On the other hand, from the doctrine that the human being was created in God’s image it follows that this image extends over the whole human being. Nothing in the human being is excluded from the image of God. All creatures display _vestigia Dei_; the human being is _imago Dei_. And he is this wholly and entirely—in soul and body, in all faculties and powers, in all states and relations. The human being is the image of God because and insofar as he is truly human, and he is human, truly and essentially human, because and in the same measure as he is the image of God. Naturally, just as the cosmos is an organism and therefore reveals God’s virtues more clearly in some creatures and less clearly in others, so in the human being as an organism the image of God also emerges more clearly here than elsewhere, more in the soul than in the body, more in the ethical virtues than in the physical powers. But this does not detract from the truth that the whole human being is God’s image. Scripture could not and might not speak of God in human fashion and transfer all human attributes to God, cf. above, p. 66, if God had not first made the human being wholly and entirely in his image. And it is the task of Christian theology to identify this image of God in the whole essence of the human being. Compare here my _Beginselen der Psychologie_, 1897, which treats some subjects from the doctrine of the human being more broadly than space permits here.

First of all, the image of God can be identified in the human soul. According to Gen. 2:7, the human being was formed from the dust of the earth through the inbreathing of the נִשְׁמַת חַיִּים, and thus became a נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה, ψυχη ζωσα. The breath of life is the principle of life; the living soul is the essence of the human being. Through both, Scripture gives the human being a distinctive, independent place and avoids both pantheism and materialism. The names רוּחַ and נֶפֶשׁ, πνευμα and ψυχη, which in Scripture designate the invisible component of the human being, set this clearly in the light. Trichotomism, which is rooted in principle in Plato’s dualism and repeatedly gained entrance into the Christian church among Gnostic and theosophical movements, sees in them two distinct substances. But wrongly so. Heb. 4:12 and 1 Thess. 5:23 no more give an enumeration of the essentially different components of the human being than, for example, Luke 10:27, and therefore prove nothing. Soul and spirit repeatedly stand in parallelism in Scripture and alternate with one another. At one time body and soul, at another body and spirit, constitute the essence of the human being, Matt. 10:28; 1 Cor. 7:34; James 2:26. Psychical activities are attributed by turns to the spirit and to the soul, Ps. 139:14; Prov. 19:2 and Prov. 17:27; Ps. 77:7; 1 Cor. 2:11; Num. 21:4 and Job 21:4; 1 Sam. 1:10 and Isa. 54:6; Luke 1:46 and 47; etc. Dying is called the surrender both of the soul, Gen. 35:18; 1 Kings 17:21; Matt. 20:28; Acts 15:26; 20:10, and of the spirit, Ps. 31:6; Matt. 27:50; Luke 8:55; 23:46; Acts 7:59. At times the spirit and at times the soul is immortal, Eccl. 12:7; Matt. 10:28; and the dead are called souls, Rev. 6:9; 20:4, and spirits, Heb. 12:13; 1 Pet. 3:19. But although they are not essentially distinct, neither are they identical. The human being is spirit because he did not arise from the earth as the animals did but had the breath of life breathed into him by God, Gen. 2:7; because he has his principle of life from above, from God, Eccl. 12:7; because he has a spirit of his own, distinct from the Spirit of God, Gen. 41:8; 45:27; Ex. 35:21; Deut. 2:30; Judg. 15:19; Ezek. 3:14; Zech. 12:1; Matt. 26:41; Mark 2:8; Luke 1:47; 23:46; John 11:33; Acts 7:59; 17:16; Rom. 8:16; 1 Cor. 2:11; 5:3–5; 1 Thess. 5:23; Heb. 4:12; 12:23; etc.; and because as such he is akin to the angels, can also contemplate spiritual, heavenly things, and if necessary can exist without a body. But the human being is soul because from the first moment, in distinction from the angels, the spiritual component in him is directed toward a body and organized for a body; because through that body he is bound to the earth and, also for his higher life, to the sensory and external; because he can ascend to the higher only from the lower; because he is thus a sensory, material being and as such akin to the animals. The human being is an _animal rationale_, _un roseau pensant_; standing between angels and animals, akin to both and distinguished from both, within himself joining and reconciling heaven and earth, the invisible and visible things. And precisely as such he is the image and likeness of God. God is most certainly spirit, and insofar as they are spirits the angels too are akin to him. But sometimes his soul is also spoken of, and throughout all Scripture there are also attributed to God all those distinctly psychical affections and activities that essentially belong to the human being. In Christ, God assumed not the nature of angels but that of human beings. And therefore precisely the human being, in distinction from the angels, is the image, son, and offspring of God. The _spiritualitas, invisibilitas, unitas, simplicitas, immortalitas_ of the human soul are features of the image of God. This image itself emerges in the fact that the human being has a πνευμα that from the outset is organized as ψυχη.

Second, the human faculties belong to the image of God. While spirit is the principle and soul the subject of life in the human being, according to Holy Scripture the heart is the organ of his life. It is first the center of bodily life, but then further, in a figurative sense, the foundation and source of all psychical life—of affections and passions, desire and will, even thought and knowledge. From the heart are all תּוֹצְאוֹת חַיִּים, Prov. 4:23. This life, which originates in the heart, then divides into two streams. On the one hand, there is to be distinguished that life which includes all impressions, awarenesses, sensations, perceptions, deliberations, thoughts, knowledge, and wisdom, which particularly in its higher form has the νους as its organ and embodies itself in the word, in language. On the other hand, from the heart originate all the affections, impulses, drives, inclinations, dispositions, desires, and decisions of the will, which must be led by the νους and express themselves in action. In all these psychical faculties and activities of the human being too, features of the image of God are visible. The very diversity and richness of those powers points back to God. The lower a creature stands, the less richly it is organized and therefore also the less akin and receptive it is to the highest good, which is God. Even the angel stands below the human being in this respect. Precisely because the human being is so wondrously rich in organization, he can in the richest way, as it were from every side, become conformed to and enjoy God as the highest good in all his virtues and perfections. Augustine even saw in heart, understanding, and will (_memoria, intellectus, voluntas_) an analogy of God’s trinitarian being. Just as the Father gives life to the Son and the Spirit and the Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son, so in the human being it is the heart, the _memoria_, the deep, hidden life of the soul, that gives existence to _intellectus_ and _voluntas_ and particularly also causes the will to follow the understanding in proper order. Rationalism and Pelagianism detach understanding and will from the heart and seek in both together the whole, full essence of the human being. Mysticism, despising conscious, volitional life, withdraws into the depths of the inner life. The Greek church and theology place both, head and heart, _unvermittelt_ alongside each other. But the theology of the West, under Augustine’s guidance, avoided all these errors. It saw that the doctrine of God and that of the human being stood in the closest connection. In the Trinity it therefore maintained the unity of being, the distinction of the three persons, and the _filioque_; and correspondingly in psychology it also taught that the deep, hidden life of the soul came to revelation only through the cognitive and appetitive faculties, and that among these two the latter was led and governed by the former.
