## 4.

Alongside and over against this naturalistic conception of the image of God there arose another view that we may call the supernaturalistic, above, p. 512.
It did not arise from the distinction between צלם and דמות, although it was later connected with this distinction.
Nor is it built upon the interpretation of Gen. 1 and 2, for many acknowledge that it does not occur there, or at least not literally.
Rather, it was derived from the idea of the _status gloriae_ that gradually arose in the Christian church, to which believers are elevated through Christ and his Spirit, John 1:12; Rom. 8:14–17; 1 Cor. 2:7 f.; Eph. 1:15 f.; 2 Pet. 1:2 f.; 1 John 3:1, 2; etc.; cf. Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 272–281.
Under Neoplatonic influence this _status gloriae_ gradually came to be conceived as a condition that far surpassed the state of nature not only in an ethical but also in a physical sense.
From the fourth century onward, this thought came so much to the fore in the Christological controversies that the deity of the Son and the Spirit was demonstrated especially from the fact that they were the authors of humanity’s deification.
The essence of the _status gloriae_ came increasingly to lie in the _visio Dei per essentiam_, in _deiformitas_ and _deificatio_, in a _physica participatio divinae naturae_ that was not merely moral, in the _Verschmelzung mit Gott_; cf. above, p. 152 f.
To this doctrine of the _status gloriae_ was now added that of the meritoriousness of good works.
The _gratia infusa_ granted in baptism was absolutely necessary, but it also enabled the human being to perform such good works as could merit _ex condigno_ eternal blessedness, the _visio Dei per essentiam_.
From these two thoughts—the mystical conception of humanity’s final destiny and the meritoriousness of good works—was born the Roman Catholic doctrine of the _donum superadditum_.
Alexander of Hales was the first to cast it into a definite formula.
Heavenly blessedness and the vision of God, which is the human being’s final destiny and was so also for Adam, cannot be merited _ex condigno_ except by such good works as correspond to that final destiny, that is, like that destiny bear a supernatural character and thus flow from a supernatural principle, the _gratia infusa_.
The _justitia_ that Adam naturally possessed through creation as a human being, as an earthly being, was by the nature of the case insufficient for this.
And so Adam too, in order to attain his final destiny, had to be granted a supernatural grace, namely, the _gratia gratum faciens_, the image of God.
_Haec autem sublimatio creaturae rationalis est supranaturale complementum; et ideo nec consecratio nec adoptio nec assumptio hujusmodi fit per aliquam proprietatem naturae sed per donum superadditum naturae, consecrans naturam, ut sit templum, assimilans Deo, ut sit filius sive filia, confoederans Deo sive uniens per conformitatem voluntatis, ut sit sponsa; hoc autem fit a Deo mediante gratia gratum faciente_, Halesius, _S. Theol._ II, qu. 91, membr. 1, art. 3.
This doctrine found general acceptance among the scholastics, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 95; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ II, cc. 11, 12; V, c. 1; and the commentaries of Thomas, Bonaventure, and Duns Scotus on _Sent._ II, dist. 29.
It was included in the _Catechismus Romanus_ I 2, qu. 18, 3; was later defended and maintained against the Reformers, Baius, Jansen, and Quesnel—Bellarmine, _De gratia primi hominis_; Denzinger, _Enchir._ n. 881 sq.—and forms one of the most important and characteristic _loci_ in Roman Catholic theology; cf., for example, Becanus, _Theol. Schol._ I, tract. 5; Casini, _Controv. de statu purae naturae_, printed as an _appendix ad librum II de opif. sex dierum_ of Petavius, _Theol. dogm._, ed. 1868, IV, pp. 587–653; _Theol. Wirceb._ VII, p. 145 sq.; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ III 166–182; Kleutgen, _Theol._ II² 6–151, 507–615; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 239–514; idem, _Natur u. Gnade_, 1861; Schäzler, _Natur u. Gnade_; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch. der Menschheit_, 1887, S. 1–61; Simar, _Dogm._³, S. 326 f.; C. Pesch, _Prael._ III 76–111; Möhler, _Symb._ §1; etc.
Although there is agreement on the main point, there are nevertheless all kinds of differences on subordinate points.
Some, such as Halesius, _S. Theol._ II, qu. 96, m. 1; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II, dist. 29, art. 2, qu. 2; Albert the Great, Duns Scotus, Biel, and others, claimed that the supernatural gift of the _gratia gratum faciens_ was distinct from the _justitia originalis_ that the human being immediately possessed by nature through creation and was also granted later in time.
According to them, the human being was first created with the _justitia originalis_; through this he was enabled to merit the _gratia gratum faciens ex congruo_; and once he had received the latter, he could thereby acquire heavenly blessedness _ex condigno_.
Thomas, however, objected that in that case the _gratia gratum faciens_ would itself rest upon merits, that as a personal gift to Adam it could not have been lost or acquired by him for all his descendants, and that it could then not be granted in baptism to little children without merit either.
Thomas therefore taught that at the moment of creation Adam received grace together with the _justitia originalis_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 100, art. 1; _Sent._ II, dist. 20, qu. 2, art. 3; dist. 29, qu. 1, art. 1.
Trent avoided a decision on this point of dispute between Franciscans and Dominicans and declared only that Adam had lost the _justitia_ and _sanctitas_, _in qua **constitutus** fuerat_, sess. V 1.
Although later theologians mostly followed Thomas and allowed the _justitia originalis_ to coincide _actually_ and _temporally_ with the _gratia gratum faciens_, ideally and logically the conception nevertheless remained the same.
Roman Catholic theology has a twofold conception of the human being: the human being _in puris naturalibus_, without supernatural grace, is indeed sinless but has only a natural religion and virtue and has his destiny on earth; the human being endowed with the _donum superadditum_ of the image of God has a supernatural religion and virtue and has his destiny in heaven.
But these two ideas of the human being were not enough.
As soon as one considered what belonged to the one and what to the other idea of the human being, one became perplexed about various gifts granted to the first human being.
The _immortalitas_ and _impassibilitas_ could not in the strict sense be called natural, for they were not properties of Adam’s earthly body as such and could be lost.
On the other hand, they could not be a consequence of the _gratia gratum faciens_ either, for then the human being _in puris naturalibus_, without the _donum superadditum_, would have been susceptible to death and suffering, and death would then not have been a punishment for sin.
The situation is the same with _concupiscentia_.
According to Rome, the struggle between flesh and spirit is natural; the subjection of the flesh to the spirit is therefore something supernatural, not already given with creation itself.
Yet neither can it be ascribed solely to the _gratia gratum faciens_, for then a sinless human being without the _donum superadditum_ would not be possible.
Therefore a third had to be inserted between the two ideas that Rome forms of the human being.
And thus, according to Rome, a human being is conceivable and possible with _dona naturalia, praeternaturalia_, and _supernaturalia_.
There is a threefold righteousness, a _justitia naturalis, praeternaturalis_, and _supernaturalis_.
No wonder that some, such as Berti, Norisius, and others, could not conceive such a human being endowed only with _dona naturalia_ and _praeternaturalia_; according to God’s _potentia absoluta_ such a human being would indeed be possible, but not according to his _potentia ordinata_; the _visio beatifica_ actually belongs to the human being by nature, _saltem quoad inclinationem et appetitum_, Perrone III 167; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch._ 52 f.; Pesch, _Prael._ III 109.
But even when the conceivability and possibility of a human being _in puris naturalibus_ is maintained, there are still all kinds of differences in the doctrine of the image of God.
Some distinguish image and likeness in such a way that the former comprises the _dona naturalia_ and the latter the _supernaturalia_; others, such as the _Catech. Rom._, make both refer to the _dona supernaturalia_.
Halesius, Bonaventure, Thomas, and others understood the _justitia originalis_ as the _justitia naturalis_ and designated the _justitia supernaturalis_ as _gratia gratum faciens_; but the _Catech. Rom._ and most later theologians speak precisely of the _dona superaddita_ as _justitia originalis_.
The _immortalitas, impassibilitas, liberum arbitrium_, and _temperatio concupiscentiae_ are derived at one time from a _divinum beneficium_, at another from the _imago_ or the _similitudo_, and are also sometimes reckoned to the _justitia originalis_, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 95, art. 1; _Sent._ II, dist. 19, qu. 1, art. 4; Pesch, _Prael._ III 89 sq.
The _Catech. Rom._ places all these things alongside one another and brings them to no unity: Adam was incapable of death and suffering _divino beneficio_; his soul was created according to God’s image and likeness; in addition, concupiscence was restrained and subjected to reason; and finally God added to these the _justitia originalis_ and dominion, I 2, qu. 18, 3.
And to all this must be added the difference concerning the nature of the _gratia gratum faciens_, concerning its relation to the Spirit of God, to the soul and its powers, to the theological virtues, to good works, and so forth; cf. later the locus _De gratia_.
This is enough to show that the Roman Catholic doctrine of the image of God is not yet complete even in itself and for that reason too cannot satisfy theological thought.
