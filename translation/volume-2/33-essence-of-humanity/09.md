## 9.

Third, the image of God reveals itself in the virtues of knowledge, righteousness, and holiness with which the human being was immediately created.
For an orderly treatment it was necessary above first to speak separately about the nature and faculties of the soul, but this was intended as nothing other than a logical distinction.
The human being was not created as a neutral being with merely indifferent _potentiae_, but was immediately created physically and ethically mature, with knowledge in the understanding, righteousness in the will, and holiness in the heart.
For the human being, goodness consists in moral perfection, in complete conformity to the law of God, in a holy and perfect being like God himself, Lev. 19:2; Deut. 6:5; Matt. 5:48; 22:37; Eph. 5:1; 1 Pet. 1:15, 16.
That law is one and the same for all human beings; Scripture knows no two kinds of human beings, no twofold moral law, no twofold moral perfection and destination.
If the human being was created good, then he must have been created with the _justitia originalis_.
On the one hand, this is not to be conceived as childish simplicity, but neither may it be exaggerated as though the _status integritatis_ were already equal to the _status gloriae_.
Adam’s knowledge was pure yet limited and capable of increase; he walked not by sight but by faith; he knew not only intuitively but also discursively; he did not know the future except through special revelation; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 94 arts. 1–3.
And it was the same with his righteousness and holiness; they were his possession from the beginning, for otherwise he could never have done any good work.
Good fruits presuppose a good tree; _operari sequitur esse_.
Yet that inborn righteousness and holiness had to be preserved and unfolded by the human being and converted into deeds.
This does not mean that Adam, equipped with the necessary gifts, now had to set to work himself in his own strength, without and outside God.
The _justitia originalis_ was a free gift of God, and from moment to moment it was also maintained in him by God’s providence.
It cannot be conceived for a moment without fellowship with God.
Just as the Son was already before the fall the _Mediator unionis_, so the Holy Spirit was already then the author of all knowledge, righteousness, and holiness in the human being.
Some church fathers argued this from Gen. 2:7 and then said that the human being was first formed by the Logos and afterward the breath of life, that is, the Holy Spirit, was breathed into him; cf. Kleutgen, _Theol._ II 541 f.; Thomasius, _Christi Person und Werk_ I³ 155.
This exegesis was incorrect, but it is completely true that the human being _in statu integritatis_ possessed the virtues of knowledge and righteousness only through and in the Holy Spirit.
There is indeed a great difference between the indwelling of the Holy Spirit in the human being before sin and in the state of sin.
Now that indwelling is _supra naturam_, for the Holy Spirit must, as it were, come to the human being from outside and stands directly opposed to his entire sinful nature.
In Adam, however, that entire opposition did not exist; his nature was holy and therefore did not need, as with believers, to be made holy; from the outset it was suited for the indwelling of the Holy Spirit.
In Adam, therefore, this indwelling was also natural.
No truly good, perfect human being is conceivable without the fellowship of the Holy Spirit.
There is no _homo naturalis_ in the Roman Catholic sense between the fallen, sinful human being and the perfect human being created in God’s image.
Whoever is human, human in the full, true sense, is and must be the image of God, a son of God, the offspring of God, living in fellowship with him through the Holy Spirit.
And thus even before the fall the human being was _totius sacrosanctae trinitatis domicilium, pulcherrimum Spiritus Sancti templum_.

Fourth, the human body also belongs to the image of God.
Philosophy, which does not know or rejects revelation, repeatedly falls into empiricism or rationalism, into materialism or spiritualism.
But Scripture reconciles the two.
The human being has a πνευμα, but that πνευμα is psychically organized and by virtue of its nature must dwell in a body.
It belongs to the essence of the human being to be bodily and sensory.
Therefore, if not temporally then nevertheless logically, first his body is formed from the dust of the earth and afterward the breath of life is breathed into him.
He is called Adam after the earth from which he was formed.
He is and is called dust, Gen. 2:7; Ps. 103:14; Job 10:9; 33:6; Isa. 2:22; 29:16; 45:9; 64:8; ἐκ γης χοικος, 1 Cor. 15:47.
The body is not a prison but a wondrous work of art of God Almighty and, just as much as the soul, constitutes the essence of the human being, Job 10:8–12; Ps. 8; 139:13–17; Eccles. 12:2–7; Isa. 64:8; it is our ἐπιγειος οἰκια, 2 Cor. 5:1; our σκευος, that is, our serving organ, our instrument, 1 Cor. 12:18–26; 2 Cor. 4:7; 1 Thess. 4:4; and the members of the body are the ὁπλα with which we fight in the service of righteousness or unrighteousness, Rom. 6:13.
The body belongs so essentially to the human being that, although violently torn loose from the soul by sin, it is nevertheless reunited with the soul in the resurrection.
The nature of the union of soul and body is incomprehensible, but it is much closer than occasionalism, the _harmonia praestabilita_, or the _systema influxus_ conceives it to be; it is not ethical but physical; it is so intimate that one nature, one person, one I is the subject of both and of all their activities.
It is always the same soul that sees through the eye, thinks through the brain, grasps with the hand, and walks with the foot.
Although it is not present in every part of the body _secundum totalitatem virtutis_, it is nevertheless present in all members _secundum totalitatem essentiae_.
It is one and the same life that flows through the entire body but works and reveals itself in each member in a distinct, corresponding manner.
Now this body too, which is so intimately united with the soul, belongs to the image of God.
This must not, however, be conceived as though God himself has a material body, as the Audians thought; nor as though God had assumed a body when creating the human being, as Eugubinus taught; nor as though God had created the human being after the _Christus incarnandus_, as Osiander judged.
For God is πνευμα, John 4:24, and has no body.
The human body forms part of the image of God not in its material substance, as σαρξ, but in its organization as an instrument of the soul, in its _perfectio formalis_; Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ VI 12; Gregory of Nyssa, _De hom. opif._ c. 8; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 93 art. 6; _S. c. Gent._ IV 26; Petavius, _De sex dier. opif._ II 4, 7 sq.; Gerhard, _Loci_ VIII 3; Calvin, _Inst._ I 15, 3; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, p. 328; Zanchius, _Op._ III 677 sq.; Bucanus, _Inst. theol._ VIII 13; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XIII 13; Mastricht III 9, 30.
Just as God, although πνευμα, is nevertheless Creator of a material world that may be called his revelation and appearance, and this revelation attains its culmination in the incarnation, so the human spirit as soul is also designed for the body as its appearance.
The incarnation of God is the proof that not the angel but the human being was created in God’s image and that his body forms an essential component of it.
Creation was arranged from the beginning, and human nature was immediately created, in such a way that it was receptive and suited for the highest conformity to God and the most intimate indwelling of God.
God could not have become a human being if he had not first made the human being in his image.
And precisely because the body as organ of the soul also belongs to the essence of the human being and to the image of God, it therefore originally shared in immortality as well.
God is not a God of the dead but of the living, Matt. 22:32.
Death is a consequence of sin, Gen. 2:7; 3:19; Rom. 5:12; 6:23.
This immortality in Adam, however, did not yet consist in the _non posse mori_, in eternal, incorruptible life, but only in the _posse non mori_, in not dying in the event of obedience.
It was not absolute but conditional; it depended on an ethical condition.
It is therefore incorrect to say with the Pelagians, Socinians, Remonstrants, and others that the human being was created mortal and that death is given of itself with the material organism and is therefore the normal, natural state.
But on the other hand, there is nevertheless an essential difference between Adam’s not dying so long as he remained obedient and the inability to die that he would have received as a reward for his obedience.
Just as Adam’s knowledge, righteousness, and holiness were still without the _donum perseverantiae_, so immortality was not yet one with eternal, indefectible life.
Adam’s human nature was so created that, in the event of transgression of God’s command, it could and must die.
Adam was still earthly, from the earth; only Christ is the Lord from heaven; the natural is first, afterward the spiritual.
Through this body the human being was bound to the earth but could also exercise dominion over the earth.
Dominion over the earth, like immortality, is a part of the image of God.
The Socinians indeed went much too far when they located the whole essence of the human being and the entire content of the image of God in dominion; but Gen. 1:26 nevertheless clearly teaches that this dominion is most closely connected with creation in God’s image and is given directly.
It is not an external addition; it rests not upon an additional, special ordinance; rather, the human being, who is the image of God, is thereby at the same time exalted above all other creatures and appointed lord and king over them all.
And finally, dwelling in paradise also belongs to the image of God, Gen. 2:8–15.
Holiness and blessedness belong together; the conscience of us all testifies that a connection ought to exist between virtue and happiness; the ethical and the physical, the moral and the natural order in the world, being and appearance, spirit and matter may not form oppositions.
To the fallen human being therefore corresponds an earth that lies under the curse; a place of darkness awaits the wicked hereafter; the righteous will one day walk in the light of God’s countenance; the human being who has not yet fallen and is nevertheless still earthly dwells in a paradise.
