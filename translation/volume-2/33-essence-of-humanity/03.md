## 3.

It requires no demonstration that Holy Scripture stands directly opposed to this doctrine of evolution.
The Christian churches have therefore almost unanimously rejected the naturalistic, Pelagian conception of the image of God and of humanity’s original state.
Apart from the arguments for the Darwinian hypothesis already refuted earlier, there are in fact no direct historical proofs for the animal state of the human being as evolution conceives it.
Upon closer investigation, the human bones and skulls that have been found have all proved to come from beings completely like us in nature.
As far back as we can go in history, we discover a state of fairly high civilization in China, India, Babylonia, and Egypt; and every proof is lacking that the peoples there developed from an animal state.
The appeal to the so-called primitive peoples, who moreover are not devoid of all civilization, has no force.
For it cannot be proved that they stand closer to humanity’s original state than civilized peoples do.
Rather, there is reason to believe that, isolated from humanity, they gradually declined into a state of barbarism.
For none of those peoples has been able to raise itself from that state by its own resources alone.
They all bear the character of degenerates who, like branches torn from the tree, die and disappear without new life-force from outside; Waitz, _Ueber die Einheit des Menschengeschlechts und den Naturzustand des Menschen_, 1859, p. 334 f.; Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, 5th ed., 135 f.; Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._ II 744 f.; Vigouroux, _Les livres saints_ IV 171 s.
The question of humanity’s primitive state is therefore in fact not a historical but a philosophical question, for that state precedes all historical testimonies.
The answer one gives to that question is determined by the conception one forms of the human being.
The more deeply this being is considered, the more impossible it becomes to allow human history to begin with an animal, barbaric state.
Life, consciousness, language, religion, morality, and so forth cannot be explained by evolution but presuppose an origin of their own, a creation from nothing.
This comes to expression even in modern theology.
It does indeed deny creation in God’s image and the _status integritatis_; but when it comes to the critical point, the idea of creation suddenly reappears.
Consciousness is something specifically human; or at least religion has a proper, original principle in the human being; or, if evolution is accepted here as well, then theologians nevertheless halt at the ethical and maintain its independence; the moral life or moral disposition is _sui generis_ and _sui originis_; Hoekstra, _Wijsg. Godsd._ I 1f., 14, 213f., and so forth.
But this position between creation and evolution is untenable.
It has repeatedly been adopted by all kinds of Pelagian movements in the Christian church because they objected both to creation and to evolution and therefore sought a mediation.
The first human being was not an animal, but neither was he a perfect, holy human being; he was an innocent child.
He was neither positively good nor positively evil, but stood between the two, was morally indifferent, and could do either.
_Actu_ he was nothing; potentially he was everything—pious and godless, holy and unholy, good and evil.
The disposition, the _posse_, arose through creation, but all that is now erected upon that potential foundation is the human being’s own will and work.
Now, as we shall see in the following section, there is indeed something true in this representation, insofar as the first human being did not yet possess the highest and therefore did indeed have to develop.
But apart from this, it is nevertheless completely unacceptable for many reasons.
First, Scripture clearly teaches that the human being was created as an adult, both psychically and physically, _in virili aetate_; Augustine, _De Gen. ad lit._ VI c. 13, 14; Lombard, _Sent._ II dist. 17.
The narrative in Genesis concerning the first human beings is very simple, but the state of those first human beings is not that of simple, naive children but of adult, fully conscious, freely acting beings.
Creation in the image of God, Gen. 1:27; Eccles. 7:29; Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10; the blessing of multiplication, Gen. 1:28; the divine approval, Gen. 1:31; the probationary command, Gen. 2:17; the naming of the animals, Gen. 2:19; the statement concerning Eve, Gen. 2:23, 24; the manner of the temptation, Gen. 3:1f.; and the posture of Adam and Eve after the fall, Gen. 3:7 f., prove abundantly that the first human beings were created not indifferent but positively good.
The only counterproof could be derived from the fact that shame was absent among the first human beings.
And this has therefore always been advanced by the opposing party as a very powerful argument.
It cannot stand, however, because sexual life was very well known to the first human being before the fall, Gen. 1:27, 28; 2:23, 24, and shame is derived not from the awakening of sexual life but specifically from the fall.
Second, this representation suffers from half-heartedness and makes the problem before us still more complicated.
It is half-hearted insofar as, on the one hand, it espouses evolution and yet, upon reaching a certain point, again espouses creation.
It wishes to know nothing of a creation of _actus_ but nevertheless accepts a creation of _potentiae_.
It speaks of _Fähigkeit ohne Fertigkeit_ and considers the creation of a child, both in the physical and in the psychical sense, simpler and more rational than that of an adult.
This in itself is already absurd, for whoever allows the _potentiae_ to arise through creation and not through evolution can no longer have any principial objection to the doctrine of the _justitia originalis_ and the _status integritatis_.
But in addition it makes the matter still more difficult to conceive.
A potency does not develop of itself, without more, into actuality.
Max Müller rightly said: _wenn wir es versuchen wollten, uns den ersten Menschen als Kind geschaffen und allmählich seine physischen und geistigen Kräfte entfaltend zu denken, so könnten wir nicht begreifen, wie er nur einen Tag ohne übernatürliche Hülfe zu leben vermochte_, _Vorles. über die Wiss. der Sprache_ I³, Leipzig, 1875, p. 410.
Schelling expressed himself in the same spirit: _Ich halte den Zustand der Cultur durchaus für den ersten des Menschengeschlechts u. s. w._, _Werke_ I 5, p. 286 f.; 6, p. 57 f.; II 1, p. 238 f.
And J. G. Fichte too said: _Es drängt sich die Frage auf, wenn es nothwendig sein sollte, einen Ursprung des ganzen Menschengeschlechts anzunehmen, wer erzog denn das erste Menschenpaar? Erzogen mussten sie werden--ein Mensch konnte sie nicht erziehen. Also ist es nothwendig, dass sie ein anderes vernünftiges Wesen erzogen, das kein Mensch war, es versteht sich bestimmt nur so weit, bis sie sich selbst untereinander erziehen konnten. Ein Geist nahm sich ihrer an, ganz so wie es eine alte ehrwürdige Urkunde vorstellt, welche überhaupt die tiefsinnigste, erhabenste Weisheit enthällt und Resultate aufstellt, zu denen alle Philosophie am Ende doch wieder zurück kommen muss_, _Grundlage des Naturrechts_, 1796.
To escape one miracle, many miracles must be assumed.
Third, underlying this representation is the error that no innate holiness is possible.
Holiness, it is said, is always the fruit of struggle and exertion.
If Adam was created positively holy, he was necessarily good and his freedom was lost, for example Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 480 f.
Thus one comes to imagine a state between good and evil, holy and unholy, an indifferent state that precedes the moral _in bonam_ or _malam partem_ and from which the human being must then develop in one direction or the other by a free decision of the will.
The human being is then stripped of all intellectual and ethical content, and the image of God is located in pure, naked, merely formal personality; Nitzsch, _Syst. der chr. Lehre_, p. 211; Müller, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 154 f., 493 f.; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 432; Thomasius, _Dogm._ I 110 f.; Beck, _Glaub._ II 333; Doedes, _Leer der Zaligheid_, p. 55f.
Such a concept of personality, however, is a sheer abstraction to which nothing in reality corresponds.
No human being is conceivable without definite qualities of understanding and will.
A complete indifference of the will, without character, without inclination to one side or the other, is simply an impossibility.
Just as in nature only a good tree can bear good fruit, so also in the ethical life the good nature precedes good works.
_Operari sequitur esse._
Scripture therefore also teaches that holiness, both in creation and re-creation, is a gift of God.
Whoever possesses it can unfold it further in word and deed; but whoever lacks it can never acquire it.
And finally, this representation detracts from God’s justice, which would have allowed his creature to be tempted beyond his ability; from the seriousness of the temptation, which then becomes a cunning deception; and from the character of the fall, which ceases to be a dreadful guilt and changes into an unaccountable misfortune and an almost unavoidable fate.
It erases the boundaries that exist between the _status integritatis_ and the _status corruptionis_ and allows the human being to retain undisturbed even after the fall the image of God, which consists in something purely formal.
It conceives the relation between the formal (personality, freedom of the will) and the material (religious and ethical life) as just as loose and dualistic as Rome presents it between _pura naturalia_ and _donum superadditum_, with only this difference: for Rome holiness is a fruit of _gratia_, while for Pelagius and his followers it is a product of arbitrary will.
