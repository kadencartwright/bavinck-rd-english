## 10.

Thus the whole human being is the image and likeness of God. The human being is this according to soul and body, according to all capacities, powers, and gifts. Nothing in the human being is excluded from the image of God; it extends as far as the human does; it is the human in the human being. The human is not the divine itself, but it is nevertheless its finite, creaturely imprint. Everything that is in God—his spiritual being, his virtues and perfections, his immanent self-distinctions, his communication and revelation in creation—finds its analogy and likeness in the human being, even if only a finite and limited one. There is a profound truth in the Kabbalah’s representation that God, who is the Infinite in himself, reveals himself in the ten _sefirot_ or attributes and that these together constitute Adam Kadmon, Franck, _La Kabbale_, 1843, p. 179. Among the creatures, human nature is the highest and most perfect revelation of God. And it is by no means this according to its pneumatic side alone but equally according to its somatic side; it is this precisely as human nature, that is, as psychical nature. According to the teaching of Scripture, God and world, spirit and matter, are not opposites. There is nothing contemptible and nothing sinful in matter. The visible world is just as much a beautiful, rich revelation of God as the spiritual world. In the former he displays his virtues just as surely as in the latter. All creatures are embodiments of his divine thoughts, and all display _vestigia Dei_. But all these _vestigia_, scattered side by side in the spiritual and material worlds, are comprehended in the human being and are so joined to one another and elevated that they clearly form the image and likeness of God. The whole world rises upward, closes itself, completes itself, receives its unity, its goal, its crown in the human being. To be the image of God, the human being therefore had to be a recapitulation of the whole of nature. The Jews said that the material for the human body had been gathered by God from the various lands of the earth, Weber, _System_ 202 f.; cf. a Frisian myth in Dr. J. te Winkel, _Gesch. der Ned. Lett._ I 28. In this strange form a true, beautiful thought was expressed. As spirit the human being is akin to the angels and ascends to invisible things; but he is also a citizen of the visible world and connected with all bodily creatures. There is not a single material in the human body that does not occur in nature around him. Thus the human being is the unity of the material and spiritual worlds, the mirror of the universe, _vinculum, compendium, epitome totius naturae_, μικροκοσμος, and precisely thereby also the image and likeness of God, his son and heir, μικροθεος. He is the prophet who interprets God and proclaims his virtues; he is the priest who, together with all creation, consecrates himself to God as a holy sacrifice; he is the king who in righteousness leads and governs all things. And in all this he points toward One who in a still higher and richer sense is the revelation and image of God, toward him who is the only-begotten of the Father and the firstborn of all creatures. Adam, the son of God, was a type of Christ.
