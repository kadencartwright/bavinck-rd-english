## 4.

Infinity as not being limited by space is God’s omnipresence.
This too is described in Holy Scripture in a very vivid manner.
God is the Creator, and all that exists is and remains his in the absolute sense.
He is the Lord, the Possessor of heaven and earth, Gen. 14:19, 22; Deut. 10:14, and is exalted above every creature, even above place and space.
Heaven and earth do not contain him, much less an earthly temple, 1 Kings 8:27; 2 Chron. 2:6; Isa. 66:1; Acts 7:48.
Yet neither is he excluded by space.
He fills heaven and earth; no one can hide from his presence; he is a God both far off and near, Jer. 23:23, 24; Ps. 139:7–10; Acts 17:27; ἐν αὐτῳ γαρ ζωμεν και κιυουμεθα και ἐσμεν, Acts 17:28.
Indeed, he is present at different places in creation in different measures and ways.
The whole of Scripture proceeds from the thought that heaven, which after all is also created, has ever since it came into existence been God’s dwelling and throne in a special sense, Deut. 26:15; 2 Sam. 22:7; 1 Kings 8:32; Pss. 11:4; 33:13; 115:3, 16; Isa. 63:15; Matt. 5:34; 6:9; John 14:2; Eph. 1:20; Heb. 1:3; Rev. 4:1 f., etc.
But from there he descends, Gen. 11:5, 7; 18:21; Ex. 3:8; walks in the garden, Gen. 3:8; appears repeatedly and at particular places, Gen. 12; 15; 18; 19, etc.; and comes down especially to his people on Mount Sinai, Ex. 19:9, 11, 18, 20; Deut. 33:2; Judg. 5:4.
While he lets the Gentiles walk in their own ways, Acts 14:16, he dwells in a special manner among his people Israel, Ex. 19:6; 25:8; Deut. 7:6; 14:2; 26:19; Jer. 11:4; Ezek. 11:20; 37:27; in the land of Canaan, Judg. 11:24; 1 Sam. 26:19; 2 Sam. 14:16; 2 Kings 1:3, 16; 5:17; in Jerusalem, Ex. 20:24; Deut. 12:11; 14:23, etc.; 2 Kings 21:7; 1 Chron. 23:25; 2 Chron. 6:6; Ezra 1:3; 5:16; 7:15; Ps. 135:21; Isa. 24:23; Jer. 3:17; Joel 3:16, etc.; Matt. 5:34; Rev. 21:10; in the tabernacle and the temple on Zion, which is called his house, Ex. 40:34, 35; 1 Kings 8:10; 11:2; 2 Chron. 5:14; Ps. 9:12; Isa. 8:18; Matt. 23:21; and above the ark between the cherubim, 1 Sam. 4:4; 2 Sam. 6:2; 2 Kings 19:15; 1 Chron. 13:6; Pss. 80:2; 99:1; Isa. 37:16.
But the prophets repeatedly warn against a fleshly confidence in this dwelling of God in the midst of Israel, Isa. 48:1, 2; Jer. 3:16; 7:4, 14; 27:16.
For the Lord is far from the wicked, Pss. 11:5; 37:10 f.; 50:16 f.; 145:20, but his countenance beholds the upright, Ps. 11:7.
He dwells with the one who is of a contrite and lowly spirit, Isa. 57:15; Ps. 51:19.
When Israel forsakes him, he comes to them again in Christ, in whom the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily, Col. 2:9.
Through him and through the Spirit whom he sends, he dwells in the church as in his temple, John 14:23; Rom. 8:9, 11; 1 Cor. 3:16; 6:19; Eph. 2:21; 3:17, until one day he will dwell with his people and be all in all, 1 Cor. 15:28; Rev. 21:3.
In polytheism, Gnosticism, and Manichaeism, this omnipresence of God could not be acknowledged.
But even in the Christian church there were many who were willing to acknowledge an _omnipraesentia_ of God’s power but not of his essence.
The Anthropomorphites could not conceive of God without a determinate form and place.
To preserve God from mingling with material substance and from the impurity of the world, some church fathers, over against the Stoa, went so far as to assert that God was πορρω κατ’ οἰσιαν, ἐγγυτατω δε δυναμει, Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ II, c. 2, and that he dwelt in heaven just as the human spirit dwells in the head, Lactantius, _De opif. Dei_, c. 16; _Inst. div._ VIII 3.
Yet by this they did not deny the essential presence of God at every place.
Only later was it decisively denied and opposed by Augustinus Steuchus, bishop of Eugubium, † 1550, in his commentary on Ps. 138, and then by Crell, _De Deo ejusque attributis_, c. 27; they accepted an _omnipraesentia operativa_ but not _essentialis_ and confined the latter to heaven.
Remonstrantism expressed itself cautiously, called the question of lesser importance, and preferred to refrain from a decision, just as it did concerning God’s eternity, Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV, 2, 13; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II, 6.
Cocceius too was accused of understanding by God’s omnipresence only the _efficacissima Dei voluntas omnia sustentans ac gubernans_, but he defended himself against this in a few letters to Anslar and Alting, _Opera_ VIII, p. 137 sq., epist. 169, 170, 176.
The Cartesians said that God was omnipresent not through an extension of his essence but through a simple act of his thought or a powerful act of his will, each of which was one with his essence, and denied that an _ubi_ could be attributed to God, Burmannus, _Synopsis_ I, 26, 6; Wittichius, _Theol. pacifica_, c. 14.
Rationalism went still further, restricting God’s essential presence to heaven and deistically separating him from the world, Reinhard, _Vorles._, pp. 106, 120; Wegscheider, _Instit._ §63.
Deism arrived at this restriction of God’s omnipresence out of fear of the pantheistic mingling of God and the world and of the defilement of the divine essence by the moral and material impurity of created things.
Indeed, that fear is not imaginary.
The Stoa already taught that the Deity, as fire, ether, air, and breath, permeates all things, including the bad and ugly, Zeller IV 138.
Spinoza spoke of the _substantia_ as _corporea_, _Eth._ I, prop. 15, called God a _res extensa_, ibid. II, prop. 2, and taught a presence of God that coincides with the being of the world, _Cog. metaph._ II, c. 3.
In Hegel, _Werke_ XI 268, God’s omnipresence is identical with his absolute substantiality.
Along this same line lies Schleiermacher’s description of God’s omnipresence as _die mit allem Räumlichen auch den Raum bedingende schlechthin raumlose Ursächlichkeit Gottes_, _Gl._ §53.
Likewise Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ §702, says that the _reine In-sich-sein Gottes_ is the pure antithesis of all space and place and to that extent is transcendent, but that God as the ground of the world is immanent in it and that this _Grundsein_ is his very being; cf. also Scholten, _Initia_, p. 124.

Here again Christian theology has avoided both deism and pantheism.
For Scripture clearly taught that God is exalted above space and place and cannot be limited or enclosed by them, 1 Kings 8:27; 2 Chron. 2:6; Jer. 23:24.
Even where Scripture speaks in a human manner and, in order to give us a conception of God’s being, enlarges space, as it were, infinitely, Isa. 66:1; Ps. 139:7; Amos 9:2; Acts 17:24, the underlying thought is nevertheless that God stands beyond all the bounds of space.
There is therefore, just as between eternity and time, an essential distinction between the _immensitas Dei_ and space.
Space or place was defined by Aristotle as the immovable boundary of something surrounding or enclosing, το του περιεχονυος περας ἀκινητον, _Phys._ IV, c. 5.
This definition, however, proceeds from too external a concept of space.
For space is certainly the distance of a particular object from other fixed points.
But even if we were to conceive of only one single, simple object, space and place would nevertheless belong to it because of its relation to imaginary points that we could posit in thought.
Space and place therefore constitute a property of all finite being and are given with the finite as such.
A finite being necessarily exists in space.
Its limitedness brings the concept of a _ubi_ with it.
It is always somewhere and not simultaneously at another place.
Apart from every measurable distance from other points (_locus extrinsecus_), a _locus intrinsecus_ is proper to all creatures.
Nor are spiritual beings excepted from this.
In another dispensation the distances can be entirely different from those we know here on earth, just as steam and electricity have already brought about a great change in our perception of them.
But limited, localized being remains proper to all creatures.
Space is therefore not a form of perception, as Kant maintained, but a mode of existence of all that is created.
Still less correct is the claim that space is a form of external perception and time a form of internal perception, and thus that the representation of space applies only to the corporeal world and that of time only to the spiritual world.
Both time and space are intrinsic modes of existence of all finite being.
But from this it follows that, just like time, space cannot apply to God, the Infinite One.
He is transcendent, exalted above all space.
Philo and Plotinus already spoke in this sense, Zeller V 354, 483, and Christian theology likewise said that God was παντα χωρων, μονος δε ἀχωρητος, Hermas, _Pastor_, mand. 1; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 1; IV 19; Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ VII, c. 7; Origen, _C. Cels._ VII 34; Athanasius, _De decr. Nic. Syn._, c. 11; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat._ 34; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I 9.
In his earlier Manichaean period Augustine thought that God, like a subtle ether, was diffused through the whole world and through endless space, _Conf._ VII 1.
But later he came to see it differently.
God is exalted above all space and place.
He is not somewhere and yet he fills heaven and earth; he is not diffused through space like light and air, but he is at all places with his entire essence, _ubique totus_ and _nusquam locorum_, _Conf._ VI 3; _De civ._ VII 30.
He is enclosed by no space or place; and therefore it would be better to say that all things are in him than that he is in things.
But neither is this to be understood as if he were the space in which things were situated, for he is not a _locus_.
Just as the soul is _tota in toto corpore et in qualibet parte_, and just as one and the same truth is known everywhere, so, by way of comparison, God is in all things and all things are in God, _Enarr. in Ps._ 74.
And these thoughts of Augustine recur later in all theologians: Anselm, _Monol._, c. 20–23; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 36 and 37; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 8; _S. c. Gent._ III, 68; _Sent._ I, dist. 37; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 37.
And Roman Catholic and Protestant theologians have added nothing essentially new to them: Petavius, _De Deo_ III, c. 7–10; Gerhard, _Loci_ I, c. 8, sect. 8; Polanus, _Synt._ II, c. 12; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 90–138, etc.
Indeed, space and place cannot be predicated of God either.
Space is a form of existence of finite being; _immensitas_ is a property of God alone and belongs to no creature, not even to the human nature of Christ.
It implies first of all that God is outside all space and place and infinitely exalted above them.
_Deus sibi ipsi locus_, αὐτος ἑαυτου τοπος, Tertullian, _Adv. Prax._, c. 5; Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ II, 10; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I 13.
He is in himself, _in se ipso ubique totus_.
In this sense God can be said to be nowhere just as well as somewhere (Philo, Plotinus).
For an _ubi_, a _locus_, does not apply to him.
God’s omnipresence expresses not only, and not even in the first place, this being of God in himself, but designates above all a particular relation of God to space, which was created along with the world.
Here too we can speak of God only on the ground of creation.
Scripture even speaks of God’s going, coming, walking, and descending.
It speaks in a human manner, and we too cannot get beyond this.
_Difficile invenitur ubi sit, difficilius ubi non sit_, Augustine, _De quant. an._, c. 34.
It is therefore nevertheless not useless, cf., e.g., Lipsius, _Dogm._ §306; Hoekstra, _Wijsg. Godsd._ II 121–128, to remind ourselves with every attribute that we speak of God in this human manner.
Precisely the awareness that God is measured by neither time nor space, even though this is purely negative, keeps us from robbing God of his exaltation above every creature.
In the negation there is nevertheless again a strong affirmation.
God’s relation to space cannot consist in his being in space and enclosed by it, as Uranos and Kronos were powers above Zeus.
For God is not a creature.
_Si in aliquo loco esset, non esset Deus_, Augustine, _Enarr. in Ps._ 74.
Not only is he not a body that extends through space and is _circumscriptive_ in space, but neither is he a finite, created spirit that is always bound to a _ubi_ and thus is _definitive_ in space.
Nor can the relation be of such a nature that space is within him and is enclosed by him as the greater, infinite space, as some formerly called God τοπος των ὁλων and as Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I §492 f., speaks of infinite space as immanent in God.
For space is by nature a form of existence of the finite creature and does not apply to God, the Infinite One.
But God is in space in such a way that, as the Infinite One existing in himself, he nevertheless _repletive_ fills and sustains every point of space through his _immensitas_.
Certainly pantheism must be avoided here, for it turns God’s essence into the substance of things and thereby also makes the divine essence spatial.
But deism must equally be resisted, for it allows God to be omnipresent _per potentiam_ but not _per essentiam et naturam_.
There is an essential distinction, but no separation, between God and the creature.
For every part of being and every point of space requires nothing less than his _immensitas_ in order to exist.
The deistic conception that God dwells in a particular place and from there now rules all things by his omnipotence conflicts with God’s essence.
It actually denies all his perfections—his simplicity, his immutability, his independence; it makes God into a human being and makes creation independent.
God is not present in his creation as a king is in his kingdom or a captain on his ship.
His activity is no _actio in distans_.
Rather, as Gregory the Great expressed it, he is present in all things _per essentiam, praesentiam et potentiam_; _enter, praesenter Deus hic et ubique potenter_; his omnipresence is ἀδιαστασια, _adessentia_.
He is in hell just as much as in heaven; in the wicked as well as in the pious; in places of impurity and darkness just as much as in the halls of light.
For he is not defiled by that impurity, because his _essentia_, though omnipresent, is nevertheless different from that of creatures.
Therefore Anselm said that it was actually better to say that God was _cum tempore et loco_ than _in tempore et loco_, _Monol._, c. 22.
Yet this does not take away the fact that, in another sense, God is again present in creatures in different ways.
There is a distinction between his physical and his ethical immanence.
Human beings too can be immediately beside one another in the natural sense and yet differ worlds apart in sympathy and spiritual orientation, Matt. 24:40, 41.
The soul is present in the whole body and in all its parts, but in each of them in its own way, differently in the head than in the heart and differently in the hand than in the foot.
_Haec autem facit atque agit unus verus Deus; sed sicut idem Deus, ubique totus, nullis inclusus locis, nullis vinculis alligatus, in nullas partes sectilis, ex nulla parte mutabilis, implens coelum et terram praesente potentia, non indigente natura. Sic itaque administrat omnia quae creavit, ut etiam ipsa proprios exercere et agere motus sinat. Quamvis enim nihil esse possit sine ipso, non tamen sunt ulla quod ipse_, Augustine, _De civ._ VII, 30.
God’s immanence is no unconscious emanation but a conscious presence of his essence in all creatures.
And therefore that presence of God differs according to the nature of those creatures.
Certainly every being, even the smallest and least, comes into being and exists only by divine power.
Nothing less than the essence of God is needed to grant the least creature its origin and continued existence.
God dwells in all creatures but not in all _aequaliter_, Augustine, _Epist._ 187, c. 5, n. 16; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 37, pars 1, art. 3, qu. 1–2.
All things are indeed _in eo_ but not _cum eo_, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 37.
God dwells in heaven differently than on earth, in animals differently than in human beings, in inanimate creatures differently than in animate ones, in the wicked differently than in the pious, in Christ differently than in the church.
Creatures differ according as God indwells them in different ways.
The nature and essence of creatures is determined by their relation to God.
They therefore all reveal God, but in different measures and in different respects.
With the pure you show yourself pure, but with the crooked you show yourself a wrestler, Ps. 18:27.
In all creatures God is present _per essentiam_, but in no one except Christ does the fullness of the Godhead dwell bodily.
In Christ he dwells in an entirely unique manner, _per unionem_.
In other creatures he dwells according to the measure of their being, in some _per naturam_, in others _per justitiam_, in others _per gratiam_, in others _per gloriam_.
There is endless diversity so that all together might reveal the glory of God.
It is of little avail for us to deny this omnipresence of God.
He makes us feel it in heart and conscience.
He is not far from each one of us.
What separates us from him is sin alone.
It removes us from God not spatially but spiritually, Isa. 59:2.
To forsake God, to flee from him as Cain did, consists not in local separation but in spiritual dissimilarity.
_Non loco quisque longe est a Deo, sed dissimilitudine_, Augustine, _Enarr. in Ps._ 94.
And conversely, going to him and seeking his face consists not in a pilgrimage but in humbling oneself and repentance.
And whoever seeks him finds him, not far away but in his immediate nearness.
For in him we live and move and have our being.
_Propinquare illi, est similem illi fieri; recedere ab illo, dissimilem illi fieri_, Augustine, _Enarr. in Ps._ 34.
_Noli ergo cogitare Deum in locis; ille tecum est talis, qualis fueris. Quid est talis, qualis fueris? Bonus, si bonus fueris; et malus tibi videtur, si malus fueris; sed opitulator, si bonus fueris; ultor, si malus fueris. Ibi habes judicem in secreto tuo. Volens facere aliquid mali, de publico recipis te in domum tuam, ubi nemo inimicorum videat; de locis domus tuae promtis et in faciem constitutis, removis te in cubiculum; times et in cubiculo aliunde conscium, secedis in cor tuum, ibi meditaris: ille in corde tuo interior est. Quocumque ergo fugeris, ibi est. Te ipsum quo fugies? Nonne quocumque fugeris, te sequeris? Quando autem et te ipso interior est, non est quo fugias a Deo irato nisi ad Deum placatum. Prorsus non est quo fugias. Vis fugere ab ipso? Fuge ad ipsum_, Augustine, _Enarr. in Ps._ 74.
