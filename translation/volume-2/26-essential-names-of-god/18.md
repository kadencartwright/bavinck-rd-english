## 18.

Finally, God’s sovereignty reveals itself in his omnipotence, which, however, after what has already been said, requires less extensive treatment. Nowhere in Scripture is any limit ever set to God’s power. Already in the names El, Elohim, El Shaddai, and Adonai the idea of power comes to the fore. Further, he is called אֵל גָּדוֹל וְנוֹרָא, before whose face no one can stand, Deut. 7:21 f.; אָבִיר יִשׂרָאֵל, Isa. 1:24; הָאֵל הַגָּדוּל הַגִּבּוֹר, whose name is YHWH Tsebaoth, Jer. 32:18; אַמִּיץ כֹּחַ, Job 9:4; כַּבִּיר, Job 36:5; עִזּוּז וְגִבּוֹר, Ps. 24:8; אָדוֹן, κυριος, Matt. 11:25; Rev. 1:8; 22:5, that is, the Lord, the Owner, the Ruler, who possesses authority and supremacy; the King who reigns eternally over all things, Ex. 15:18; Pss. 29:10; 93–99; 2 Chron. 29:15; Jer. 10:7, 10; etc., but above all is King over Israel and as such governs, protects, and leads it to salvation, Num. 23:21; Deut. 33:5; Judg. 8:23; 1 Sam. 8:7; Pss. 10:16; 24:7; 48:3; 74:12; Isa. 33:22; 41:21; 43:15; etc.; and likewise in the New Testament the μεγας βασιλευς, Matt. 5:33; 1 Tim. 1:17; the βασιλευς των βασιλευοντων και κυριος των κυριευοντων, 1 Tim. 6:15; cf. Rev. 19:16; παντοκρατωρ, 2 Cor. 6:18; Rev. 1:8; 4:8; 11:17; μονος δυναστης, 1 Tim. 6:15; who possesses both ἐξουσια, ἀρχη, _potestas_, the right, authority, and competence, Matt. 28:18; Rom. 9:21, and δυναμις, κρατος, _potentia_, the capacity and power to act, Matt. 6:13; Rom. 1:20. But God’s omnipotence is further evident from all his works. Creation, preservation, the deliverance of Israel from Egypt, nature with its ordinances, and the history of Israel with its miracles loudly and clearly proclaim God’s omnipotence. The psalmists and prophets repeatedly return to these great deeds and apply them to humble the proud and comfort the believer. He is mighty in power, Isa. 40:26; creates earth and heaven, Gen. 1; Isa. 42:5; 44:24; 45:12, 18; 48:13; 51:13; Zech. 12:1; maintains their ordinances, Jer. 5:22; 10:10; 14:22; 27:5; 31:35; and forms rain and wind, light and darkness, good and evil, Amos 3:6; 4:13; 5:8; Isa. 45:5–7; 54:16. He makes mute and speaking, dead and alive; he saves and destroys, Ex. 4:11; Deut. 32:39; 1 Sam. 2:6; 2 Kings 5:7; Ex. 15; Deut. 26:8; 29:2; 32:12; 1 Sam. 14:6; Hos. 13:14; Matt. 10:28; Luke 12:20. He has absolute power over all things, so that nothing can resist him, Pss. 8; 18; 19; 24; 29; 33; 104; etc.; Job 5:9–27; 9:4 f.; 12:14–21; 34:12–15; 36–37. Nothing is too wonderful for him; all things are possible for him, Gen. 18:14; Zech. 8:6; Jer. 32:27; Matt. 19:26; Luke 1:37; 18:27; he can raise up children for Abraham from stones, Matt. 3:9. He does all his good pleasure, Ps. 115:3; Isa. 14:24, 27; 46:10; 55:10, and no one can summon him to account, Jer. 49:19; 50:44. Above all, his δυναμις comes to expression in the works of redemption: in the resurrection of Christ, Rom. 1:4; Eph. 1:20; in the working and strengthening of faith, Rom. 16:15; Eph. 1:19; in the distribution of grace beyond all asking and thinking, Eph. 2:20; 2 Cor. 9:8; 2 Pet. 1:3; in the resurrection on the last day, John 5:25 f.; etc. Finally, this power of God is also the source of all power and authority, of all force and strength in creatures. From him come the dominion of humanity, Gen. 1:26; Ps. 8; the authority of government, Prov. 8:15; Rom. 13:1–16; the strength of his people, Deut. 8:17, 18; Ps. 68:36; Isa. 40:26 f.; the strength of the horse, Job 39:22; the force of thunder, Pss. 29:4; 68:34; etc. In a word, strength is his, Ps. 62:12, and to him belong power and might, Ps. 96:7; Rev. 4:11; 5:12; 7:12; 19:1.

Entirely in accord with their doctrine concerning God’s will and freedom, cf. above, p. 210 f., the nominalists defined God’s omnipotence in such a way that by it God not only can do everything he wills but can also will everything. Distinguishing between the _potentia absoluta_ and _ordinata_, they judged that according to the former God could also sin, err, suffer, die, become a stone or an animal, change bread into the body of Christ, do contradictory things, undo what had been done, make false what was true and true what was false, and so on. According to his _potentia absoluta_, therefore, God is sheer arbitrariness, pure potency without any content, which is nothing and can become everything; cf. Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._ II, l. 2, c. 3, §5; Voetius, _Disp._ I 411, 427. In principle this is the position of all who uphold the primacy of the will, and for that reason this opinion repeatedly returned later and occurs not only in Christianity but also among other religions, especially in Islam. On the other side stand those who say that God can do only what he wills and that what he does not will he also cannot do. The possible coincides with the actual. What does not become actual is not possible either. God has completely exhausted his power in the world. This was already the opinion of Plato and Plotinus, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II 928; V 496 f., and also of some church fathers, Petavius, _De Deo_ V, c. 6, but in the Middle Ages it was taught especially by Abelard: _Deus non potest facere aliquid praeter ea quae facit_, _Introd. theol._ III, c. 5. The Cartesian theologians later judged similarly, Burmannus, _Synopsis_ I, c. 21, §24; 26, c. 25, §§10, 11; Braun, _Doctr. foed._ I, 2, c. 3, §7 sq.; Wittichius, _Theol. pacif._ §199 sq.; and further Spinoza, _Cogit. metaph._ II, c. 9; _Eth._ I, prop. 16, 17; Schleiermacher, _Gl._ §54; Strauss, _Gl._ I 582 f.; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ I 246 f.; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 403–406; Hoekstra, _Wijsgeerige Godgeleerdheid_ II 112 f.; etc. Scripture condemns both positions. On the one hand, it expressly says that God cannot do many things: he cannot lie, repent, change, or be tempted, Num. 23:19; 1 Sam. 15:29; Heb. 6:18; James 1:13, 17; ἀρνησασθαι γαρ ἑαυτον οὐ δυναται, 2 Tim. 2:13. His will, after all, is one with his being, and the _potentia absoluta_ that detaches God’s power from his other virtues is nothing but an empty and impermissible abstraction. On the other hand, Scripture declares just as decidedly that the possible extends far beyond the actual, Gen. 18:14; Jer. 32:27; Zech. 8:6; Matt. 3:9; 19:26; Luke 1:37; 18:27. Christian theology adhered to this. Augustine says, on the one hand, that God’s will and power are not distinguished from his being. _Homo aliud est quod est, aliud quod potest. . . . Deus autem cui non est aliud substantia ut sit, et alia potestas ut possit, sed consubstantiale illi est quidquid ejus est et quidquid est, quia Deus est, non alio modo est et alio modo potest; sed esse et posse simul habet, quia velle et facere simul habet_, _Tract._ 20 _in Ev. Joan._, n. 4; _Conf._ XI 10; XII 15. God’s omnipotence indeed consists in his ability to do what he wills: _certe non ob aliud vocatur omnipotens, nisi quoniam quidquid vult potest_, _De civ._ XXI 7. But God cannot will everything; he cannot deny himself. _Quia non vult non potest, quia et velle non potest. Non enim potest justitia velle facere quod injustum est, aut sapientia velle quod stultum est, aut veritas velle quod falsum est. Unde admonemur Deum omnipotentem non hoc solum, quod ait apostolus: negare se ipsum non potest, sed multa non posse. . . . Deus omnipotens non potest mori, non potest mutari, non potest falli, non potest fieri, non potest vinci_, _Serm._ 214. But Augustine then goes on to argue that this is no lack of power but precisely true, absolute omnipotence. It would be impotence if he could err, sin, and so forth, _Serm._ 213, 214; _De civ._ V 10. Augustine clarifies this especially with respect to the statement frequently brought against God’s omnipotence, namely, that God cannot undo what has been done. This statement can have two meanings. First, one can mean by it that God annihilates the fact that has occurred; but this makes no sense, for a fact that has occurred no longer exists and cannot and need not be annihilated. Second, one can mean by it that God undoes the fact that occurred in human consciousness, so that it now begins to think that it did not occur. But this too makes no sense, for God, who is truth, would then have to make what is true untrue. Other theologians spoke in the same sense about God’s omnipotence and merely repeated what Augustine had said: Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 42–44; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 25; _S. c. Gent._ II, c. 6–10; Bonaventure, _Breviloquium_ I, c. 7; Petavius, _De Deo_ V, c. 7; Gerhard, II, c. 8, sect. 9; Buddeus, _Instit. theol. dogm._ II, c. 1, §30; Musculus, _Loci Comm._, p. 952 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 29; Zanchius, _Op._ II 159 sq.; Voetius, _Disp._ I 403 sq.; V 113 sq.; Alsted, _Theol. schol. did._, pp. 93–96; Mastricht, _Theor. pract. theol._ II, c. 20; Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._ II, l. 2, c. 1–3; Leydecker, _Fax Veritatis_, p. 163 sq., 233 sq.; etc. In particular, the Reformed theologians recognized the distinction between the _potentia Dei absoluta_ and _ordinata_ only up to a certain point. The nominalists had abused this distinction to claim that according to the former God could do everything, even what was contrary to his nature, and thereby especially argued for the doctrine of transubstantiation. Calvin opposed this and rejected such a _commentum potentiae absolutae_ as profane, _Inst._ III, c. 23, §§1, 5; cf. I 16, 3; II 7, 5; IV 17, 24; _Comm. in Isa._ 23:9; Luke 1:18. The Roman Catholics therefore accused Calvin of limiting and thus denying God’s omnipotence, Bellarmine, _De gratia et lib. arb._ III, c. 15. But Calvin did not therefore deny that God could do more than he actually did; he opposed only such a _potentia absoluta_ as would not be bound to his being and virtues and thus could also do all kinds of contradictory things. Understood in this way, in the sense of Augustine and Thomas, the said distinction was also generally accepted by the Reformed theologians, Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 29; Alsted, _Theol. schol. did._, p. 96; Heidegger, _Corpus theol._ III 109; _Synopsis pur. theol._ VI 36; Mastricht, II 20, 13. And understood in this way, this distinction may also be approved. Pantheism does say that God and the world are correlates, and that God has no being and life of his own, no consciousness and will of his own, in distinction from the world. But it thus confuses everything hopelessly and introduces boundless confusion into thought as well. God and the world, eternity and time, infinity and finitude, being and becoming, the possible and the actual, the necessary and the contingent, and so forth, are not words with the same content and meaning. The world is of such a nature that our thought cannot deprive it of the character of contingency. The thought of its nonexistence contains not the slightest logical contradiction. There may be motives why God called the world into existence; the cosmos as a whole and in each of its parts may be an embodiment of divine thoughts; but it is impossible to explain the origin of the world logically without the will of an almighty God. Therefore, alongside the actual there remains a realm of the possible. God is not exhausted in the world; eternity does not empty itself into time; infinity is not identical with the sum of all that is finite; omniscience does not coincide with the thought-content of rational creatures. And so God’s omnipotence is still infinitely exalted above the boundless power that comes to manifestation in the world. Cf. Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ §499 f.; Dorner I 441 f.; Ebrard §179 f.; Philippi II³ 59 f.; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, pp. 503–506; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrheit_ I² 249 f.; Grétillat III 256 s.; Hodge I 406 f.; Shedd I 358 f.
