## 9.

Considered from another point of view, God’s knowledge bears the name wisdom. The distinction between the two is generally known. Nearly all languages also have different words for these two concepts. Everyone knows that learning, science, knowledge, and wisdom are far from the same and by no means always go together. A simple person often excels a scholar in wisdom. The two rest on different faculties in humanity. We obtain knowledge through study, wisdom through insight. The former comes about discursively, the latter intuitively. Knowledge is theoretical; wisdom is practical, teleological—it places knowledge in the service of an end. Knowledge stands apart from the will; wisdom is indeed a matter of the understanding but is made serviceable to the will. Knowledge therefore often stands entirely outside life, but wisdom is most closely connected with life; it is ethical in nature, an “art of living well,” the attribute and act of one who makes right use of greater skill and chooses the best means to the best ends. Etymology already points to this distinction. _Wijs_ comes from _wijzen_, to show, and is the attribute of the one who points out the direction; _sapiens_, from _sapere_, cf. σαφης, to taste, indicates that through personal experience someone has acquired a sound, independent judgment; σοφος is probably also related to σαφης and then has the same basic meaning. Among the Eastern peoples too we encounter this distinction, but with an important modification. Just as knowledge stands in the foreground in the West, so wisdom does in the East. The Eastern peoples live much more from intuition than from abstract thought; they work not with concepts but with images of the things themselves; subjectively, they live closer to their heart, and objectively they stand nearer to life’s immediate reality. Not in the head but in the heart, at the root of personality, subject and object meet and either repel one another in the harshest way or attract one another in the tenderest way. It is as though the substances of things flow into one another through a sympathy of life. We encounter that Eastern character intensely and profoundly in Israel too. Here too, in due time, _chokmah_ had to arise, חכמה from חכם, to be firm, close, solid; substantively, soundness, _Tüchtigkeit_. But here it entered the service of revelation. Genuine, true wisdom is not a fruit of the human understanding but is rooted in the fear of the Lord and consists in that moral _Tüchtigkeit_ which directs itself according to the Lord’s law and reveals itself in a moral life, Deut. 4:6–8; Pss. 19:8; 111:10; Job 28:28; Prov. 1:7; 9:10. And when insight later penetrated more deeply into God’s revelation in nature and in the law, wisdom received an independent place, developed a literature of its own, and rendered its thoughts in a form of its own, the form of proverbs, מְשָׁלוֹת. Alongside understanding and knowledge, wisdom is now also ascribed to God in Scripture. By speaking through the word he created the world, Gen. 1:3; Pss. 33:6; 107:20; 119:105; 147:15; 148:5; Isa. 40:8; 48:13. In the earliest period, it is especially God’s power and exaltedness that are observed in creation. But gradually the Israelite spirit, nourished and guided by the fear of the Lord, penetrates more deeply into nature and law and admires in them his adorable wisdom. Both the world and God’s revelation among Israel are derived from that wisdom, Job 9:4; 12:13, 17; 37–38; Isa. 40:28; Ps. 104:24; Deut. 4:6–8; Jer. 10:12; Ps. 19:8, and in Prov. 8:22 f.; Job 28:23 f., that wisdom by and with which God produced all things is presented personally. The New Testament continues this line and not only ascribes wisdom to God in various places, Rom. 16:27; 1 Tim. 1:17; Jude 25; Rev. 5:12; 7:12, but also says that the world was created through the Word of God, John 1:2; Heb. 11:3, and sees God’s wisdom revealed above all in the foolishness of the cross, 1 Cor. 1:18; in Christ, 1 Cor. 1:24; in the church, Eph. 3:10; and in God’s entire direction of Israel and the Gentiles, Rom. 11:33. Weisheit_, 1854; idem, _Theol. des A. T._ §235 f.; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴ 548 f.; Delitzsch, _Comm. über das Salom. Spruchbuch_, introduction; Cremer, _Wörterbuch_, s.v. σοφος; König, _Der Offenbarungsbegriff des A. T._, 1882, I 194 f.; Smend, _Lehrb. der altt. Rel._, p. 508 f.; Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ §§168, 169; Frank, _Syst. der chr. Wahrh._ I 254 f.

This teaching of Scripture concerning the divine Word and divine wisdom was connected especially by Philo with the ἰδεαι. In this way the foundation was laid for the doctrine of the Logos, which will be discussed more fully in the locus _De Trinitate_. But Plato’s doctrine of ideas later once again exercised a powerful influence upon Christian theology, now by way of Neoplatonism. Thus it came to Augustine, who assigned it a place in theology. Naturally, however, he had to modify it at important points for this purpose. First, the ideas are not a world of their own, κοσμος νοητος, alongside and outside God, but are in God and constitute the content of his thought. _Has autem rationes ubi arbitrandum est esse nisi in mente creatoris_, _83 qu._, qu. 46; _Gen. ad litt._ IV 6, 24, 29; V 13. They are God’s thoughts concerning all created things, both before and after creation, and are therefore eternal and immutable, for nothing can be in God except what is eternal and immutable, ibid.; _De trin._ IV 1; _De civ._ XI 5. _In Deo enim novum exstitisse consilium, ne dicam impium, ineptissimum est dicere_, _De ord._ II 17. Yet in God’s understanding those ideas are not identical with his self-knowledge, nor with the Logos or the Son, as earlier fathers had sometimes said. Augustine maintains the essential distinction between God and the world and avoids pantheism. _Aliter ergo in illo sunt, quae per illum facta sunt; aliter autem in illo sunt ea, quae ipse est_, _De Gen. ad litt._ II 6. On the other hand, Augustine can think only that God created all things according to ideas. His wisdom comes to expression in this. _Sic ergo sapientia Dei, per quam facta sunt omnia, secundum artem continet omnia, antequam fabricet omnia_, _Tract._ 1 _in Ev. Joan._; _Omnia ratione sunt condita_, _83 qu._, qu. 46, n. 2; _Non enim quidquam fecit ignorans. Nota ergo fecit, non facta cognovit_, _De Gen. ad litt._ V 8; _De trin._ XV 13; _De civ._ XI 10. Creation is a realization of God’s thoughts. Therefore a close connection also exists between the ideas and creatures. The ideas are the exemplars and forms of things, _principales formae, rationes rerum, stabiles atque incommutabiles_, _83 qu._, qu. 46, n. 2, and thus not only of species but of all individual things. _Cum omnia ratione sint condita, . . . singula igitur propriis sunt creata rationibus_, ibid.; and _De civ._ XI 10; _De Gen. ad litt._ III 12, 14; V 14, 15. But this view is not always maintained. Sometimes the ideas occur again in Augustine as types and exemplars that are gradually realized and will first be fully actualized in the new heaven and new earth, _De Gen. ad litt._ V 11. Nor is the connection clear in which, according to Augustine, ideas and creatures stand. The ideas are not only exemplars in God, but also active powers, immanent principles in things. He therefore preferred the translation of ἰδεαι by _rationes_ to Cicero’s translation by _formae_ or _species_, _83 quaest._, qu. 46; _De Gen. ad litt._ I 18; II 16; IX 18. The ideas are, as it were, deposited in the things themselves. Ideas and creatures do not stand alongside and over against one another dualistically as παραδειγματα and ὁμοιωματα, as true and untrue, as immutable and mutable; rather, the ideas are the soul and principle of things. Movement belongs to creatures _ex illis insitis rationibus_, _De Gen. ad litt._ IV 33. And further, the causality of the ideas is nevertheless always mediated by God’s will; this is the proper and ultimate cause of the being of things, _De civ._ XXI 8; cf. Gangauf, _Metaph. Psych. des h. Aug._, p. 77 f.; Ritter, _Gesch. der chr. Philos._ II 310 f.; Dr. E. Melzer, _Des Aug. Lehre vom Kausalitätsverhältniss Gottes zur Welt_, 1892, p. 5 f. Besides Augustine, we find this doctrine of ideas also in Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ IV, c. 25; V, c. 3; Tertullian, _De an._ 18; Eusebius, _Praep. evang._, l. 14, c. 44; Origen on John 1:1; Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 5; and later in Anselm, _Monol._, c. 8 sq.; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 35; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 15; qu. 44, art. 3; _S. c. G._ 54; _Sent._ I, dist. 36; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 35; Petavius, _Theol. dogm._ IV, c. 9–11; Scheeben, _Kath. Dogm._ I 657 f.; Simar, _Lehrb. der Dogm._, 3rd ed., Freiburg, 1893, p. 147 f.; and also among Reformed theologians such as Polanus, _Synt. theol._, pp. 267, 268; Zanchius, _Op._ II, col. 201; Voetius, _Disp._ I 258 sq.; Twisse, _De scientia media_, 1639, p. 312 sq. Later this doctrine of ideas almost entirely disappeared from dogmatics. In modern philosophy the word _idea_ also changed in meaning. Formerly one understood by _idea_ the exemplar of things in God’s creating mind, while a concept was the imprint of things in the knowing mind. But in modern philosophy _idea_ became the designation for those concepts that are obtained not from sensory perception but by pure thought (Descartes), of which there is no experience and thus no scientific certainty (Kant), and which can be recognized immediately only in feeling or reason (Jacobi). Yet usage still frequently recalls the old meaning, at least in our language and in German. The word still always includes, especially in the sphere of art, the thought of an objective exemplar, παραδειγμα, and therefore of an ideal perfection. One then speaks of the idea of God, freedom, art, science, the true, the good, the beautiful, and so forth. Applied to God, the idea expresses that God made everything with wisdom, that wisdom is the firstfruits of his way, ἀρχη της κτισεως, Prov. 8:22; Col. 1:15; Rev. 3:14. God is no less than an artist. Just as an artist realizes his idea in the work of art, so God creates all things according to the thoughts he has formed. The world is God’s work of art. He is artist and architect of the entire universe. God does not work unconsciously but is guided in all his works by wisdom, by his ideas. Yet there is also a distinction between God and an earthly artist. God’s thoughts are absolutely original, taken from his own being, eternal, immutable; indeed, they are one with his divine being. The ideas in God are the _essentia Dei_ itself, insofar as it is the exemplar of created things and can be expressed and imitated in finite creatures. Every creature is a revelation of God and participates in the divine being. The nature of this participation does not consist in creatures being modifications of God’s being or in creatures having received that being of God _realiter_ into themselves. Rather, every creature has its own being because in its existence it is an ectype of the divine being. Because of the multiplicity and richness of God’s ideas realized in the created order, God’s wisdom is to be called πολυποικιλος, Eph. 3:10. Yet God’s wisdom is one, and his idea of the world is one, gradually unfolding in the course of the ages and leading the real toward the intended goal. On account of this archetypal character of the ideas in God, there is also no proper idea of sin as such. Sin, after all, has no being of its own but is a diminution of being, a deformation. It is indeed the object and content of God’s knowledge; it is indeed made serviceable to his glory by God’s wisdom; but in itself it is no idea of his wisdom, no ray of his light, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 15, art. 3. This wisdom does, however, come to expression in the creation, ordering, direction, and government of all things. It is and remains the אָמוֹן, _opifex_, Prov. 8:30, ἡ των παντων τεχνιτις, Wis. 7:21, which creates and governs all things and leads them to their destination, that is, to the glorification of God’s name.
