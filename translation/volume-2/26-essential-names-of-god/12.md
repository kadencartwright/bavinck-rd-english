## 12.

God’s holiness is most closely related to his goodness.
It was formerly described as _ab omni scelere libera et omnino perfecta et omni ex parte immaculata puritas_, Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 12, §2; cf. further Suicerus, s.v. ἁγιος.
It is frequently not treated separately alongside the _bonitas, perfectio_, and _pulchritudo Dei_; neither Lombard nor Thomas discusses it.
Among Protestant theologians the description of God’s holiness was essentially the same: it consisted in _moralis perfectio, puritas_, Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 28; _Synopsis_ VI 40; Mastricht, _Theor. pract. theol._ II, c. 19; L. Meyer, _Verh. over de Goddel. eigensch._ III 115 f., and was connected sometimes more with God’s righteousness, at other times with his goodness, and also with his truthfulness and wisdom, Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, S. 73; cf. Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._ 382 f.; Hase, _Hutt. Rediv._ §63.
The investigation of the biblical concept holy, however, gradually gave rise to a different conception.
All now acknowledge that the concept of holiness in the Old and New Testaments expresses a relation of God to the world.
But there is disagreement about the nature and character of that relation.
With an eye to texts such as Hos. 11:9; Isa. 57:15; Ezek. 20:9 f., Menken thought here of God’s condescending goodness and grace, _Versuch einer Anleitung zum eigenen Unterricht in den Wahrh. der h. S._, 3te Aufl., 1833, cap. 1, §9.
Baudissin, however, thought that God’s absolute exaltation and power above all creatures was instead expressed in his holiness, _Stud. zur semit. Religionsgesch._, 2tes Heft, Leipzig, 1878, S. 3–142, and was supported in this opinion by Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II² 89 f., and also by Prof. H. P. Smith, _Presb. and Ref. Rev._, Jan. 1890, p. 42; etc., with an appeal to passages such as Num. 20:13; Isa. 5:16; Ezek. 20:41; 28:25; 36:20–24, and the association of glorious and exalted with holy in passages such as Isa. 63:15; 64:11; Jer. 17:12; Ezek. 20:40; etc.
Related to this is the conception of Schultz, who in connection with God’s holiness thinks of God’s consuming majesty, his inaccessibility and inviolability, and the infinite distance that separates him from every creature, and for this refers to Ex. 15:11; 1 Sam. 2:2; 6:20; Isa. 6:3; 8:14; 10:17, _Altt. Theol._⁴ 554 f.; cf. also Kuenen, _G. v. Isr._ I 47 f.; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I² 83; and, in the main, Smend, _Altt. Religionsgesch._ 333 f.
Because there was such great disagreement over which attribute of God was actually designated by holiness, others thought that this concept denotes no inward, essential quality at all but only a relation and is therefore purely and solely a _Verhältnissbegriff_.
Holiness was first understood in this way by Diestel, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1859, S. 3–62, and since then many have accepted this conception as correct, especially Otto Schmoller, _Die Bedeutung von קדשׁ im A. T._, in _Festgruss an Rudolf von Todt zum Dr. jubiläum, von seinen Freunden u. Schülern_, Stuttgart, 1893, S. 39–43; cf. also Delitzsch, art. “Heiligkeit” in Herzog²; Cremer, _Wörterbuch_, s.v.; Issel, _Der Begriff der Heiligkeit im N. T._, Leiden, 1887; R. Schröter, _Der Begriff der Heiligkeit im A. u. N. T._, Leipzig, Fock, 1892; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ II 260–280.

The root קדשׁ, related to הדשׁ, is usually derived from the root קד with the meaning to cut, to separate, and thus expresses being cut off or set apart.
The verb occurs in the Niphal, Piel, Hiphil, and Hithpael; the adjective is קָדוֹשׁ and the substantive קֹדֶשׁ; its _oppositum_ is חֹל, κοινος, from חִלֵּל, _κοινον facere_, Lev. 10:10; 1 Sam. 21:5, 6; Ezek. 48:14, 15.
It is related to but nevertheless also clearly distinguished from טָהוֹר, clean, which has טָמֵא as its _oppositum_, Lev. 10:10.
The word holy is now used first of all of all kinds of persons and things that are set apart from common use and placed in a special relation to God and his service.
Thus there is mention of holy ground, Ex. 3:5; a holy assembly, Ex. 12:16; a holy Sabbath, Ex. 16:23; a holy people, Ex. 19:6; a holy place, Ex. 29:31; holy anointing oil, Ex. 30:25; a holy robe, Lev. 16:4; a holy year of jubilee, Lev. 25:12; a holy house, Lev. 27:14; a holy field, Lev. 27:21; a holy tithe, Lev. 27:30; holy water, Num. 5:17; holy vessels, Num. 16:37; a holy cow, Num. 18:17; a holy camp, Deut. 23:14; holy gold, Josh. 6:19; holy bread, 1 Sam. 21:4; the holy ark, 2 Chron. 35:3; holy seed, Ezra 9:2; a holy city, Neh. 11:1; a holy covenant, Dan. 11:28; a holy word, Ps. 105:42; the temple as sanctuary, Ex. 15:17, with its holy place and holy of holies; and also the angels or the children of Israel as the holy ones, Deut. 33:2, 3; Job 5:1; 15:15; Ps. 16:3, 10; 32:6; 89:6, 8, 20; Prov. 9:10; 30:3; Dan. 4:17; 7:18, 22, 25, 27; 7:21; Hos. 12:1; Zech. 14:5.
In all these cases the concept holy does not yet express an inward, moral attribute but indicates only that the persons or objects so designated are consecrated to the Lord and stand in a special relation to his service and are thus set apart from the common sphere.
The persons and things called holy, however, do not of themselves stand in this distinctive relation to God.
Rather, by nature there is a distance and estrangement, a distinction and contrast, between God and his creature.
In itself the whole world is חֹל, profane, not standing in fellowship with God and not fit for his service.
Nor can persons and things sanctify themselves and place themselves in that special relation to God which is expressed by the word holy.
Sanctification proceeds from God alone.
It is he who sanctifies Israel, the priesthood, the temple, the altar, particular places, persons, and objects, brings them into his service and fellowship, and separates them from what is unholy.
I am the Lord who sanctifies you, Ex. 31:13; Lev. 20:8; 21:8, 15, 23; 22:9, 16, 32; Ezek. 20:12; 37:28.
This sanctification of persons or things by the Lord now occurs in two ways: negatively, by choosing a people, person, place, day, or object and separating it from all others; and positively, by consecrating these persons or things according to definite rules and causing them to live accordingly.
God sanctifies the Sabbath not only by setting it apart from the other days of the week but also by resting on it and blessing it, Gen. 2:2, 3; Ex. 20:11; Deut. 5:12.
He sanctified the whole people of Israel by choosing it from all the peoples of the earth, admitting it into his covenant, and making his laws known to it, Ex. 19:4–6.
God’s holiness is the principle of the whole legislation, of the moral and ceremonial commandments, of the entire revelation of salvation to Israel, for this intends nothing other than that Israel should be holy, Ex. 19:4–6; Lev. 11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:26.
Israel is holy because God takes it as his possession, comes to the people, dwells among them, and is a God to them, Ex. 19:4–6; 29:43–46.
And within this sphere he now particularly sanctifies in turn the firstborn by appropriating them to himself, Ex. 13:2; the people by having them wash themselves and thus prepare to meet God, Ex. 19:10, 14; the mountain by setting bounds around it, Ex. 19:23; the priesthood by anointing, sacrifice, sprinkling with blood, and special clothing, Ex. 28:3, 41; 29:1 f., 21; the tabernacle and the altar by anointing, Ex. 29:37; 40:9 f.; Lev. 8:10, 11; Num. 7:1; the anointing oil by having it prepared in a special way, Ex. 30:22 f.; the Nazirites by causing them to live according to definite rules, Num. 6:2 f.; etc.
And what is holy in this way shares in a life of its own, bears a character of its own, and is distinguished from common life and common law; it may not, for example, be touched, Ex. 19:23, 24; eaten, Ex. 29:33; or used, Ex. 30:32 f.; and it sanctifies that which comes into contact with it, Ex. 30:29; Lev. 10:2 f.; Num. 1:51, 53; 3:10, 38; Isa. 8:14.
Now the positive action by which something becomes holy is not always expressed; sometimes sanctification appears to consist in nothing other than separation, Lev. 25:10; 27:14; Josh. 7:13; 20:7; Judg. 17:3; 1 Sam. 7:1; 2 Sam. 8:11; 1 Chron. 18:11; etc.
But to sanctify is nevertheless something more than merely to separate; by means of washing, anointing, sacrifice, sprinkling with blood, and so forth, it is to divest something of the character it has in common with all things and to impress upon it another distinctive stamp that it must bear and display everywhere.
Now the ceremonies necessary for sanctification clearly indicate that the impurity and sinfulness of the creature also come into consideration here and had to be removed in precisely that way.
Washing, sacrifice, sprinkling with blood, and anointing served for cleansing from sin and consecration, Lev. 8:15; 16:16; Job 1:5; etc.
Holy and clean are therefore synonyms, Ex. 30:35; Lev. 16:19.
But for this reason the concept holy is not exhausted by this moral purity.
The latter is indeed not excluded, but it is neither the only nor even the first meaning.
Holy in the Old Testament, especially in the law, has a much broader sense.
The whole distinction and contrast between outward and inward purity, and so forth, has been transferred from a later standpoint to that of the Mosaic legislation.
But holy is that which has been chosen and set apart by YHWH, which through special ceremonies has been divested of its common character and received a character of its own, and which now lives in this new state according to the laws established for it.
Israel is a holy people because it was chosen and set apart by God, admitted into a covenant, and must now live according to all his laws, including the ceremonial laws and so forth.
Holy is that which in every respect answers to the special laws God has given for it; holiness is perfection not only in a moral sense but in that whole sense in which Israel’s distinctive legislation conceives it—in a religious, ethical, ceremonial, inward, and outward sense.

This concept of holiness, however, first becomes fully clear when we examine the sense in which it is applied to God.
Cremer rightly pointed out that holiness does not first of all designate a relation from below to above but from above to below, and that it belongs in the first place to God and afterward in a derivative sense also to creatures.
Creatures are not holy in themselves.
Nor can they sanctify themselves.
All sanctification and all holiness proceed from God.
YHWH is holy, and therefore he wills a holy people, a holy priesthood, a holy dwelling, and so forth, Ex. 19:6; 29:43; Lev. 11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:26; 21:8; Deut. 28:9, 10.
This predicate is repeatedly attributed to YHWH, Lev. 11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:26; 21:8; Josh. 24:19; 1 Sam. 2:2; 6:20; Ps. 22:4; 99:5, 9; Isa. 5:16; 6:3; etc.
Isaiah frequently uses the name Holy One of Israel, Isa. 29:23; 40:25; 43:15; 49:7; 62:12; cf. 2 Kings 19:22; Ezek. 39:7; Hab. 1:12; 3:3.
Further, there is also mention of God’s holy name, Lev. 20:3; 22:32; 1 Chron. 16:35; Pss. 99:3; 103:1; 111:9; etc.; his holy arm, Isa. 52:10; and holy majesty, 2 Chron. 20:21.
Now YHWH is called this first of all because he placed himself in a special relation to Israel; in God too, holy is first of all a _Verhältnissbegriff_.
YHWH’s holiness reveals itself in the fact that he adopted Israel as his people, gave himself to Israel, and went to dwell among them, Ex. 29:43–46; Lev. 11:44, 45; 20:26; Ps. 114:1, 2.
But this relation is no abstraction; it is rich in content.
God himself regulated this relation in the laws he gave to Israel.
The entire legislation of Israel has its principle in YHWH’s holiness and its goal in the sanctification of the people.
What YHWH’s holiness contains is revealed in the whole law, and the people are holy when they answer to it.
As the Holy One he is the one who gave himself to Israel and dwells among Israel, and who thereafter also keeps his word, remains faithful to his covenant, Ps. 89:35 f., and repeatedly delivers and redeems Israel.
God is the Holy One of Israel, who belongs to Israel and is as his law makes him known.
From his holiness there flow for Israel salvation, Pss. 22:4, 5; 89:19; 98:1; 103:1; 105:3; 145:21; answer to prayer, Pss. 3:5; 20:7; 28:2; comfort, Isa. 5:16; Hab. 1:12; and confidence, Pss. 22:4, 5; 33:21; Isa. 10:20.
His holiness does not permit him to destroy Israel.
As the Holy One he is Israel’s Creator, Redeemer, and King, Isa. 43:14, 15; 49:7; 54:5; 62:12.
And so he is thanked and praised as the Holy One by his redeemed people, Pss. 30:5; 71:22; 97:12; 1 Chron. 16:10, 35.
But at the same time this holiness of God is also the principle of punishment and chastisement.
When Israel breaks his covenant, profanes his name, and transgresses his laws, it is precisely God’s holiness that impels him to punish; his holiness requires that Israel be holy and hallow him, Lev. 11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:7, 26; 21:8.
In the event of disobedience he chastises Israel, 1 Kings 9:3–7; 2 Chron. 7:16–20.
The same holiness that is the principle of deliverance and the object of praise is for transgressors the principle of destruction and the object of fear.
Holy is then synonymous with jealous, Josh. 24:19; great and fearsome, Ex. 15:11; Pss. 99:3; 111:9; and glorious and exalted, Isa. 6:3; 57:15.
No one is like him as the Holy One, Ex. 15:11; 1 Sam. 2:2; Isa. 40:25.
To hallow him is to fear him, Isa. 8:13; 29:23.
When human beings profane his name and his covenant, he sanctifies himself through justice and righteousness, Isa. 5:16; Ezek. 28:22.
But even then he does not forget his people.
His holiness remains for Israel the cause of deliverance, Isa. 6:13; 10:20; 27:13; 29:23, 24; 43:15; 49:7; 52:10; etc.; Jer. 51:5; Hos. 11:8, 9, and will finally reveal itself in his causing the Gentiles to know that he is the Lord, Jer. 50:29; Ezek. 36:23; 39:7, and in his redeeming Israel and cleansing it from all iniquities, Ezek. 36:25 f.; 39:7.
This last point leads directly to holiness in the New Testament sense.
The very choice of the Greek word is significant.
Σεμνος, from σεβομαι, designates what is venerable, Phil. 4:8; 1 Tim. 3:8, 11; Titus 2:2; ἱερος expresses only a relation to the Deity, 1 Cor. 9:31; 2 Tim. 3:15; Heb. 8:2; 9:8; etc.; ἁγνος has the meaning pure, chaste, 2 Cor. 11:2; Titus 2:5; etc.
These words are never used of God.
In the New Testament God is called only ὁσιος, Rev. 15:4; 16:5; cf. Heb. 7:26, and especially ἁγιος, Luke 1:49; John 17:11; 1 John 2:20; 1 Pet. 1:15, 16; Rev. 4:8; 6:10.
In the Old Testament God’s holiness is not yet clearly defined alongside and in distinction from all his other perfections.
It still designates there the entire relation in which the Lord stands to Israel and Israel to the Lord.
Therefore YHWH can be called the Holy One of Israel, who has given himself wholly and entirely to Israel and along all manner of ways maintains and preserves it as his possession.
For this reason the sanctification on the people’s side is not only religious and ethical but also ceremonial, civil, and political in nature.
Just as holiness in God is not yet delimited alongside other excellencies, so on Israel’s side it also encompasses the whole people in every respect.
But when in the New Testament ὁ ἁγιος του θεου appears, Mark 1:24; Luke 4:34; Acts 3:14; 4:27, who forms the most profound antithesis to the world, John 15:18, and sanctifies and consecrates himself to God in the absolute sense, John 17:19, then God’s holiness ceases to be the principle of punishment and chastisement and, in the Holy Spirit—who in the Old Testament is called this only a few times, Ps. 51:13; Isa. 63:10, but now regularly bears this name—becomes the principle of the sanctification of the church.
This church is now the ἐθνος ἁγιον, 1 Pet. 2:5, 9; Eph. 2:19; 5:27, consisting of ἐκλεκτοι, ἁγιοι, ἀμωμοι, Eph. 1:1, 4; Col. 1:2, 22; 3:12; 1 Cor. 7:24, wholly freed and cleansed from sin and eternally consecrated to God in soul and body.
The holiness in which YHWH gives himself to Israel and claims Israel wholly for his service finally reveals itself most highly in the fact that God gives himself in Christ to the church and redeems and cleanses it from all iniquities.
