## 16.

Although God wills himself and creatures with one and the same simple act, nevertheless we must distinguish the will of God with a view to its different objects.
In pantheism this distinction is impossible because there the world is the very essence of God.
But Scripture ascribes to creatures a being that, though not independent, is nevertheless their own and distinct from God’s being; and therefore creatures cannot be the object of his will in the same sense and in the same manner as he himself is with his own being.
The will of God by which God wills himself is _propensio in se tanquam finem_; the will that has creatures as its object is _propensio in creaturas tanquam media_.
The former is _necessaria_, just like the _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_; God cannot do otherwise than love himself; he delights in himself eternally and with divine necessity.
This will of God is therefore exalted above all arbitrariness but is nevertheless not unfree and compelled; freedom and necessity coincide here.
The case is entirely different, however, with the will of God that has creatures as its object.
Scripture speaks of this will as absolutely as possible.
God does all that pleases him, Ps. 115:3; Prov. 21:1; Dan. 4:35.
He owes an account to no one and answers for none of his deeds, Job 33:13.
Human beings are in his hand like clay in the hand of the potter, Job 10:9; 33:6; Isa. 29:16; 30:14; 64:8; Jer. 8:1 f.; nations are regarded as a drop, a speck of dust, as nothing, Isa. 40:15 f.
It is as foolish for a human being to exalt himself against God as for an ax to boast against the one who hews with it or for a saw to vaunt itself against the one who draws it, Isa. 10:15!
The human being has no right before God; no one can ask him, What are you doing?
Shall the clay say to the one who forms it, What are you making? Job 9:2 f.; 11:10; Isa. 45:9.
Let the human being therefore be silent and lay his hand upon his mouth, Job 39:37.
And the New Testament teaches no differently.
God can do with what is his whatever he wills, Matt. 20:15.
The being and being-such of things depend solely upon God’s will, Rev. 4:11.
This will is the ultimate ground of everything.
Mercy and hardening have their cause in it, Rom. 9:15-18.
In the church the Holy Spirit distributes gifts as he wills, 1 Cor. 12:11.
And a human being has nothing to bring against God’s free dispositions, Matt. 20:13 f.; Rom. 9:20, 21.
On these grounds, in Christian theology the _voluntas Dei_ that had creatures as its object, like the _scientia visionis_, was always called _libera_.
Augustine said that God’s will was the ultimate and deepest ground of all things, _De civ._ XXI 8; _De trin._ III 7, 19; there is no deeper ground.
To the question why God created the world, there is only one answer: _quia voluit_; whoever then asks further for a cause of that will _majus aliud quaerit, quam est voluntas Dei; nihil autem majus inveniri potest_, _De gen. c. Man._ I 2; _De civ._ V 9.
And with him the whole Christian church and theology spoke in this way, for example, Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 19 art. 5; Calvin, _Inst._ III 23, 2.
All taught, not indeed that God could have acted _plus_ and _melius_ than he did, for he always acts in a divine, perfect manner, but that he could nevertheless have made things _plura_ and _majora_ and _meliora_ than he made them, Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 44; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 25 art. 5 and 6; Zanchi, _Op._ II 184-187; Voetius, _Disp._ I 426, 564; V 151; Burman, _Synopsis_ I 41, § 41.
The moral law was indeed called eternal and natural by some and regarded as an expression of God’s being; but within it a distinction was nevertheless also made between what was essential and what was incidental, and dispensation from many commandments could be granted in particular circumstances, Voetius, _Disp._ I 347 sq.; Moor II 614 sq.
According to many, the incarnation and satisfaction were not absolutely necessary; God could also have forgiven without satisfaction if he had willed, and he does not necessarily have to punish sin, Athanasius, _C. Ar._ III; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or._ 9; John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ III 18; Augustine, _De trin._ XIII 10; Lombard, _Sent._ III dist. 20; Thomas, _S. Th._ III qu. 1 art. 2; Petavius, _De incarn._ II c. 13; Calvin, _Inst._ II 12, 1; _Comm._ on John 15:13; Zanchi VIII 144-146; Martyr, _Loci_, cl. 2 c. 17; Pareus on Rom. 5; Twisse, _Vindic. gr._, 1652, I 153 sq.; and others, such as Vossius, Roell, Vogelsang, B. S. Cremer; cf. M. Vitringa I 170; Schultens, _Waersch. op den catech. van Comrie_, pp. 198-222.
This wisdom of God emerged even more strongly in election and reprobation, for which no ground whatever can be given and which rest solely in God’s sovereign good pleasure.
There can be no question of a right of any creature over against God; there is no _justitia commutativa_, Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 21 art. 1; Polanus, _Synt._ II c. 26; Voetius, _Disp._ II 358.
Since a creature can have no merits before God, neither is there any _justitia remunerativa_ in the proper sense, Thomas I qu. 21 art. 1; Belgic Confession, art. 24; Westminster Confession, c. 16; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ X 288.
Some even held that God, although he never makes use of this right, could punish the innocent temporally or eternally, Perkins, _Werken_ I 772; Twisse, _Vind._ I 165, 179, 324 sq.; Amyraut, _Synt. thesium_ I 237.
Thus many theologians, proceeding from the absolute freedom of God, came onto the path of medieval nominalism.
It was Duns Scotus who consistently applied to God the Pelagian concept of freedom of the will as absolute indifference.
In _Sent._ I dist. 8 qu. 5, he argues against Aristotle and Avicenna that nothing besides God is necessary.
The world is contingent in its whole and in its parts; it is unnecessary for God.
To the question why God willed this and willed one thing rather than another, Scotus gives the answer: _indisciplinati est quaerere omnium causas et demonstrationem.... Principii enim demonstrationis non est demonstratio; immediatum autem principium est voluntatem velle hoc ita quod non est aliqua causa media inter ista; sicut immediatum est, calorem esse calefactivum, licet hoc sit naturalitas, ibi autem libertas, et ideo hujus, quare voluntas voluit hoc, nulla est causa, nisi quia voluntas est voluntas. Sicut hujus, quare calor est calefactivus, nulla est causa, nisi quia calor est calor, quia nulla est prior causa_, _Sent._ I dist. 8 qu. 5 n. 24.
One must stop at this statement: _Voluntas Dei vult hoc_; there is no cause _prior ratione voluntatis_, ibid.
Scotus remains faithful to this principle in everything that exists and occurs in time.
If the whole world is contingent, then it can retain this character only because God, the first cause, causes it contingently.
Now God causes the world through intellect and will.
The contingent cannot, however, lie in God’s intellect insofar as it precedes the will, for this intellect _intelligit mere naturaliter et necessitate naturali_.
It must therefore lie in the will, _Sent._ I dist. 39 qu. 5 n. 14.
This divine will _nihil aliud respicit necessario pro objecto ab essentia sua; ad quodlibet igitur aliud contingenter se habet, ita quod posset esse oppositi_, ibid., n. 22.
Now Scotus indeed acknowledges that God’s knowledge precedes the will and that the ideas in God, though distinct from his _essentia_, exist before his decree, _Sent._ I dist. 35 qu. unic. n. 7 sq.
But it is nevertheless the will that chooses from all those possible ideas and determines which shall attain reality.
The will is the cause of all actuality.
And only as a consequence of this decision of the will does the intellect also know what will become actual, ibid., dist. 39 qu. 5 n. 23 sq.
God therefore created the world with absolute freedom, even though he made the decree to do so from eternity, ibid., II dist. 1 qu. 2 n. 5.
Naturally, human freedom of the will was therefore also conceived by Scotus as absolutely as possible; the will is free _ad oppositos actus_ and _ad opposita objecta_, ibid., I dist. 39 qu. 5 n. 15 sq.; it is determined by nothing; it can choose a lower good just as well as a higher one; it alone is the complete cause of its actions; _non autem bonitas aliqua objecti causat necessario assensum voluntatis sed voluntas libere assentit cuilibet bono et ita libere assentit majori bono sicut minori_, ibid., I dist. 1 qu. 4 n. 16.
_Nihil aliud a voluntate est causa totalis volitionis in voluntate_, II dist. 25 qu. 1 n. 22.
The will even precedes the intellect, for although the intellect offers the will the object of its striving, the will nevertheless directs the intellect toward the object; and blessedness lies formally not in the intellect but in the will, IV dist. 49 qu. 4.
Further, Scotus indeed acknowledges that love for God, prescribed in the first table of the law, is necessary and natural, but the commandments of the second table are positive and could also have been otherwise, III dist. 37 qu. unica.
With respect to the incarnation, Scotus teaches that the Logos, if he willed, could also have assumed another nature than the human, for example, that of a stone, III dist. 2 qu. 1 n. 5 sq.
The incarnation would probably also have taken place apart from sin and was not absolutely necessary even in the state of sin; God could also have redeemed in another way, III dist. 7 qu. 3 n. 3; III dist. 20 qu. unica.
The merits of Christ were not sufficient in themselves but were accepted as such by God, III dist. 19 qu. unica.
Considered in itself, reckoned according to God’s _potentia absoluta_, God could also have granted blessedness to the sinner apart from the merits of Christ, and the sinner could also have made satisfaction for himself if God had only willed to reckon his works sufficient, IV dist. 15 qu. 1 n. 4 sq.; cf. also IV dist. 1 qu. 6 n. 4 sq.
Transubstantiation is possible, _quia una substantia potest totaliter incipere et alia totaliter desinere_, IV dist. II qu. 1 n. 4.
God can even cause a person or thing to cease to exist during a _tempus intermedium_ without thereby losing its identity, IV dist. 43 qu. 1 n. 4.
Yet Scotus did not go as far as some Muslim theologians, who had all things created from moment to moment by God’s will without any mutual connection, without laws of nature, without substances and qualities, Ritter, _Gesch. der christl. Philos._ III 732 f.
He acknowledges ideas in God that precede the will; considers love for God necessary and natural; believes in a natural knowledge of God, a world order, and natural law; and also says that incarnation and satisfaction were necessary according to the _potentia Dei ordinata_.
But Scotus nevertheless exalts freedom and omnipotence to such a degree that at least the means that must lead to the end become entirely arbitrary, Ritter, _Gesch. d. christl. Philos._ IV 388 f.; Stöckl, _Gesch. der Philos. des M. A._ II 832 f.; Baur, _Dreiein. u. Menschw. Gottes_ II 642 f.; Ritschl, _Jahrb. f. d. Th._ 1865, S. 298 f.; Dorner, in Herzog² 3, 735 f.
Others developed this principle of God’s freedom and omnipotence still further.
Entirely in the spirit of Scotus, Ockham maintained, for example, that according to his _potentia absoluta_ God can grant blessedness without regeneration and can damn the regenerate; that he can forgive without satisfaction and can regard the works of sinful human beings as sufficient; that the Father could have become human instead of the Son, and that the Son could also have assumed the nature of a stone or an ass; that God can grant dispensation from all the commandments of the moral law, and so forth, Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II 1018 f.
This nominalist standpoint was later adopted in the first place by the Jesuits but further also by the Socinians, Fock, _Der Socin._, 416 f.; the Remonstrants, _Apol. Conf._ c. 24; Episcopius, _Instit. theol._ V sect. 5 c. 3; by Descartes, _Resp. sextae_, Amsterdam, 1654, p. 160 sq.; by Cartesian theologians, such as Burman, _Synopsis_ I c. 20 § 6; c. 21 § 19 sq.; and in this century still by, among others, Charles Sécrétan, _Philosophie de la liberté_, 1849, I 305.
From this standpoint the will in God is completely detached from his being and from all his virtues; it consists in nothing other than formal arbitrariness.
Creation, incarnation, satisfaction, good and evil, true and false, reward and punishment—everything could just as well have been otherwise than it actually is.
Nothing is natural any longer; everything is positive.
But precisely this nominalism put Christian theology on its guard.
Even though it said with Augustine that God’s will was the ultimate ground of all things, it nevertheless guarded against divesting this will of all nature and allowing it to dissolve into pure arbitrariness.
First, in the doctrine of God it took its point of departure not in the _voluntas_ but in the _essentia Dei_.
The _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_ logically preceded the _scientia visionis_, just as the _voluntas necessaria_ preceded the _voluntas libera_.
Self-knowledge and self-love were absolutely necessary in God; and the knowledge and will that have creatures as their object are not dualistically separated from them but stand in the closest connection with them.
The _scientia visionis_ is bound to the _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_, and the _voluntas libera_ to the _voluntas necessaria_.
According to consistent nominalism, not only the existence but also the essence of things is exclusively determined and established by the formal will in God; properly speaking, therefore, there is no _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_ in God in which all possibilities are comprehended; there is in him only a _scientia visionis_ that follows upon the will.
But according to Christian theology, all possibilities lay enclosed in the _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_, and from it the _scientia visionis_ draws them and the will brings them to reality.
The existence of things thus depends upon God’s will but their essence upon his intellect.
Leibniz built upon this and said that God was indeed free whether or not to create the world, the human being, the animals, and the plants, but that if he created them, he had to create them according to the idea of them that his consciousness had formed independently of and prior to the will.
Given God’s will to create, the world must be precisely as it is.
It is therefore the best possible world, and all its parts cohere so intimately that _qui unum bene novit, omnia novit_, _Theod._ I 7, 52; II 169 f.; Pichler, _Die Theol. des Leibniz_ I 239 f.; cf. also Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, S. 57-62; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unb._ I⁹ 100 f.; II 435 f., 446 f.; Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I § 460 f.
Scripture further taught everywhere that God created and upheld the world through the word, through the Logos.
It therefore rested upon thoughts of God.
God acted not arbitrarily and accidentally but with the highest wisdom.
Even if we could not discover the reasons for his actions, they were nevertheless present to him.
And Christian theology therefore sought after those reasons.
Creation had its cause in his goodness or love; all kinds of reasons were given for the permission of sin; incarnation and satisfaction were perhaps not absolutely necessary, but this way of redemption was nevertheless the most fitting and excellent, Petavius, _De incarn._ II c. 5 sq.
And although many did not dare limit the _potestas absoluta_, all nevertheless acknowledged that he did not exercise this absolute right and thus came into complete agreement again in the _potestas ordinata_.
Finally, on concrete points nominalism was decisively rejected: the moral law can have no other content than it has, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2 qu. 100 art. 8; Becanus, _De lege naturae_ III c. 3 qu. 4; Suarez, _De lege_ I c. 15; Voetius, _Disp._ I 364-402; Maresius, _Syst. theol._ VII 4; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III qu. 18 and XI qu. 2; Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ III 89, 90; the punishment of sin is necessary, Canons of Dort II 1; Voetius, _Disp._ I 358 sq.; II 240 sq.; Moor I 683 sq., 996-1034; and so too are the incarnation and satisfaction, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ III 20; Anselm, _Cur Deus homo_; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 11, 40; _Avondmaalsform. der Ned. Ger. Kerken_; Trigland, _Antapol._ c. 4; Voetius, _Disp._ II 238 sq.; Turretin, _De satisf. Christi_ II § 16 sq.; Owen, _De justitia divina_; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ V, and so forth; and God’s power was so defined that it excluded the doing of contradictory things; see below at no. 18.

In this way nominalism was, if not overcome in Christian theology, nevertheless pushed back and limited.
When the _voluntas libera_ was defined as _propensio in creaturas propter se ut media_, this already contained a principled protest against God’s will as absolute arbitrariness, for the character of the means is determined by the nature of the final end.
Hence too, although theology placed the will of God first and always returned to it, it nevertheless also always sought motives for that will.
Realism strove to understand the world as one harmonious whole in which nothing was arbitrary and everything had its place, even sin, and which as a whole was serviceable to the glorification of God’s name.
If the nature of a science consists in _rerum cognoscere causas_, theology cannot withdraw from such an investigation either.
Only humility and modesty constitute a first duty for every practitioner of science, and especially for the theologian.
One may not be wise above what one ought to be wise.
All science is bound to its object; it may not falsify or deny the phenomena it observes for the sake of any preconceived theory.
Thus theology too is most strictly bound to the facts and testimonies that God makes known to it in nature and Scripture.
It must let these stand, unimpaired and unmutilated.
If it cannot explain them, it must confess its ignorance.
The will of God that expresses itself in those facts is for theology the end of all reasoning.
Ultimately it rests in his sovereignty.
In this sense the saying of Augustine remains true: _voluntas Dei cujusque creaturae natura est_.
It permits itself to be led astray neither by pantheism, which makes the world necessary, for there is a distinction between God and the world, between his _voluntas necessaria_ and _voluntas libera_; nor by deism, which makes the world a product of chance, for that entire world is a revelation of God’s wisdom.
And against both it maintains that the world is an act of God’s free, sovereign will and that God had his wise and good reasons for this will.
For us something is good solely and simply because God wills it; God himself can never will anything except because it is good in itself or because of something else, Voetius, _Disp._ I 387.
We almost never understand why God willed one thing and not another, and we must therefore represent it in such a way that he could have willed the one just as well as the other.
But properly speaking, no choice occurs in God, for choice always presupposes uncertainty, hesitation, and deliberation.
He knows eternally, firmly, immutably what he wills.
In his will there is no arbitrariness, no chance, no uncertainty, but only eternal determinacy and immutability.
Contingency is proper to the creature, and, reverently speaking, it is not possible even for God to take this character away from the creature.
In God alone are _existentia_ and _essentia_ one; every creature as such, by virtue of its own nature, includes the possibility that it did not exist.
But with an eternal and immutable will God willed all creatures as contingent.
Therefore it is neither possible nor even permissible for us to ascend higher than the will of God.
For every attempt to do so results in seeking a ground for the creature in God’s being, thus making it necessary and eternal and divine, and divesting it of its creaturely, that is, contingent character.
To this extent the will of God that has created things as its object is called free.
But this freedom does not exclude the other virtues of God—his wisdom, goodness, righteousness, and so forth.
After all, even among creatures, that is not true freedom of will which requires long periods of doubt, deliberation, and decision; rather, the highest freedom is that which at once, _uno intuitu_, establishes both the end and the means and knows no hesitation.
And such a freedom also exists in God: a freedom that is not to be conceived as though bound by the other virtues of God or as independent of them in the nominalist manner, but a freedom that is therefore free in the absolute sense because it is the freedom of a wise, righteous, holy, merciful, and almighty God.
Thus when Augustine, Thomas, Calvin, and others said that there was no _causa_ for the will of God, they meant that the will of God, as one with his being, has no _causa_ behind or above itself upon which that will would be dependent.
But they by no means intended to say that this will was without _ratio_, that it was a blind, alogical will in the sense of Schopenhauer.
On the contrary, the will of God is one with his being, with his wisdom, goodness, and all his virtues, Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 19 art. 5.
And therefore the human heart and mind can rest in that will, for it is the will not of a blind fate, an incalculable chance, a dark force of nature, but of an almighty God and a gracious Father.
His sovereignty is a sovereignty of unlimited power but also a sovereignty of wisdom and grace.
He is King and Father at the same time.
