### C. God as Light

## 8.

The whole of Scripture proceeds from the assumption that God possesses consciousness and knowledge. He is pure φως without any σκοτια, 1 John 1:5, dwelling in inaccessible light, 1 Tim. 6:16, and the source of all light in nature and grace, Pss. 4:7; 27:1; 36:10; 43:3; John 1:4, 9; 8:12; James 1:17, etc. This designation of light includes that God is perfectly conscious of himself, that he comprehends himself throughout his entire being, and that nothing in that being is hidden from his consciousness. God’s trinitarian life too is a perfectly conscious life, Matt. 11:27; John 1:17; 10:15; 1 Cor. 2:10. But further, God has consciousness and knowledge of all that exists outside his being. Nowhere in Scripture is it even intimated that anything would be unknown to him. The manner in which he takes cognizance is sometimes described in a very human way, Gen. 3:9 f.; 11:5; 18:21, etc., but he nevertheless knows everything. The thought that anything would be unknown to him is rejected as absurd: would he who plants the ear not hear, and he who forms the eye not see? Ps. 94:9. His חָכְמָה, גְבוּרָה, עֵצָה, תְבוּנָה, γνωσις, σοφια are repeatedly spoken of, Job 12:13; 28:12–27; Prov. 8:12 f.; Ps. 147:5; Rom. 11:33; 16:27; Eph. 3:10, etc. And this knowledge of God has every creature as its object. It extends to everything and is therefore omniscience in the strict sense. His eyes range throughout the whole earth, 2 Chron. 16:9; everything is known to him and lies naked and exposed before his eye, Heb. 4:13. The smallest and least things, Matt. 6:8, 32; 10:30; the most hidden things, heart and kidneys, Jer. 11:20; 17:9, 10; 20:12; Ps. 7:10; 1 Kings 8:39; Luke 16:15; Acts 1:24; Rom. 8:27; thoughts and deliberations, Ps. 139:2; Ezek. 11:5; 1 Cor. 3:20; 1 Thess. 2:4; Rev. 2:23; the human being in his origin and essence and in all his ways, Ps. 139; night and darkness, Ps. 139:11, 12; hell and destruction, Prov. 15:11; evil and sin, Ps. 69:6; Jer. 16:17; 18:23; 32:19; the conditional, 1 Sam. 23:10–13; 2 Sam. 12:8; 2 Kings 13:19; Ps. 81:14, 15; Jer. 26:2, 3; 38:17–20; Ezek. 3:6; Matt. 11:21; and the future, Isa. 41:22 f.; 42:9; 43:9–12; 44:7; 46:10, specifically also the end of life, Pss. 31:16; 39:6; 139:6, 16; Job 14:5; Acts 17:26, etc.—it is all known to God. He knows everything, 1 John 3:20. And he knows all this not _aposteriori_, through observation, but _apriori_, from eternity, 1 Cor. 2:7; Rom. 8:29; Eph. 1:4, 5; 2 Tim. 1:9. His knowledge is incapable of increase, Isa. 40:13 f.; Rom. 11:34, and is certain and determinate, Pss. 139:1–3; Heb. 4:13, so that all God’s revelations are true, John 8:26; 17:17; Titus 1:2; all God’s works make his wisdom known to us, Pss. 104:24; 136:5; Eph. 3:10; Rom. 11:33; and they dispose us to worship and admiration, Ps. 139:17 f.; Isa. 40:28; Job 11:7 f.; Rom. 11:33; 1 Cor. 2:11. God’s knowledge both of himself and of the world is taught so decisively and clearly in Scripture that it has been acknowledged within the Christian church at all times. Pantheism, however, has risen in opposition to it. It ascribed to God no being of his own distinct from the world and therefore could not assume in God a consciousness of his own either. Spinoza did indeed still speak of _cogitatio_ as an _attributum Dei_ and called God a _res cogitans_, _Eth._ II, prop. 1. But from this _cogitatio_ he distinguished the _intellectus_ as a _certus modus cogitandi_, and the latter falls not within the _natura naturans_ but only within the _natura naturata_, _Eth._ I, prop. 31; and all these _modi cogitandi_ together constitute God’s eternal and infinite intellect, _Eth._ V, prop. 40, schol. Through Fichte, Schelling, Hegel, and Schopenhauer, this pantheism led to Eduard von Hartmann’s _Philosophie des Unbewussten_. In the first part of his work Hartmann indicates at length the significance that belongs everywhere in the world to the unconscious and then says that this unconscious being is one in all things, II⁹ 155 f. And this Unconscious, although he prefers not to call it God, II 201, nevertheless fills the place of God in Hartmann. Now Hartmann sometimes conceives this Unconscious in such a way that it is not blind and alogical, like the will in Schopenhauer, but rather clairvoyant, like instinct in animals and human beings, and possesses an omniscient, all-wise, _überbewusste Intelligenz_, II 176 f. But he denies that consciousness belongs to the Unconscious. For consciousness consists in a division of subject and object, is thus always a limitation, and presupposes individualization, indeed even the body, II 177 f. As soon as consciousness is taken absolutely, it loses its form and automatically becomes unconscious, II 179. If the Absolute still had a consciousness of its own outside the individual consciousnesses, it would immediately swallow them up. Monism is incompatible with a conscious divine being. Such a being would not be immanent in but separated from the world. It is indeed the cause of all consciousness but is not itself conscious, II 178 f. And especially self-consciousness cannot be ascribed to the Absolute, for this presupposes a distinction of subject and object, a reflection upon oneself, which cannot occur in the Absolute, II 187 f. Pantheism thus denies to God both knowledge of the world—_Weltbewusstsein, innerweltliches Selbstbewusstsein_—and knowledge of himself, self-consciousness—_ausserweltliches_ or _innergöttliches Selbstbewusstsein_. Even where he comes to consciousness in the individual consciousnesses, he knows only the world but not himself, Hartmann II 176. Nearly everything Hartmann says about the power and significance of the unconscious in creatures can now be conceded. The unconscious does indeed play a large role in animal and human life. But it is remarkable that Hartmann does not even ask whether that extensive power of the unconscious does not precisely presuppose and require the intelligence of God. For there is certainly many an unconscious and yet purposive action. But the unconscious in animals and human beings does not itself act with a purpose; rather, the conscious human spirit discerns in that action a purpose toward which the unconscious has been directed by a higher consciousness standing above it, Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ I 44, 5. From ancient times, therefore, the observation of what is purposive in the world has led to the acknowledgment of a divine being. Anaxagoras therefore already posited a νους that possessed infinite knowledge in order to order everything in the best way. Socrates describes the Deity as one who sees and hears everything and is present everywhere. In Plato, God is the fashioner of the world who creates all things according to the ideas. Aristotle reasons that the Deity is the _primum movens_, absolute being, _actus purus_, and absolutely incorporeal. He is therefore thinking, νους, and has himself as his content. God cannot derive the content of his thought from outside himself; he can think only what is best, and that is himself. God thus thinks himself; he is the thinking of thinking; in him thinking and its object are one; God is νοησις νοησεως, _Metaph._ XII 9. And even the Stoa concluded teleologically from the purpose in the world to an intelligent cause. Zeno already reasoned as follows: _Nullius sensu carentis pars aliqua potest esse sentiens. Mundi autem partes sentientes sunt. Non igitur caret sensu mundus_, Cicero, _N. D._ II, 8, 22; cf. III, 9, 22: _quod ratione utitur melius est quam id quod ratione non utitur. Nihil autem mundo melius. Ratione igitur mundus utitur._ So also Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 28 and 44. Whoever acknowledges no purpose in the world has no need of a self-conscious God either and finds materialism sufficient. But whoever with von Hartmann defends the teleological view of the world must assume a consciousness in God and does not explain the conscious by the unconscious. After all, the effect cannot contain more than is present in the cause. Would he who plants the ear not hear, and he who forms the eye not see? A perfection in the creature points back to a perfection in God. If the rational being surpasses all others in value, if thought raises the human being—otherwise weak as a reed—above all creatures, God cannot be conceived as unconscious. Religion resists this, for it always and everywhere presupposes a personal, self-conscious God, but philosophy too lodges a protest. Pantheism may be able to appeal to Spinoza and Hegel; theism has all the philosophers of antiquity on its side and also finds support in more recent philosophy in Descartes, Leibniz, Kant, Schelling (2nd period), J. H. Fichte, Herbart, Lotze, Ulrici, Carrière, and others. That the divine self-consciousness differs specifically from that of the human being and that the latter affords only a faint analogy to it is certainly established beyond all doubt. To that extent there would be no objection to calling God _überbewusst_. But precisely for that reason no conclusion concerning divine knowledge can be drawn from the limitedness of human self-consciousness. Despite all the reasonings from the pantheistic side about the limitedness, individuality, and sensory basis that necessarily belong to self-consciousness, it cannot be seen why God’s self-knowledge would be a limitation of his being. In us self-consciousness is finite and limited because we ourselves are finite and because it never covers our being. Our being is much richer than our self-consciousness. But God is eternal, pure being. And his self-knowledge has nothing less than that full, eternal, divine being itself as its content. Being and knowing are one in God. He knows himself through his own being. Nor does he gradually come to consciousness, and he is not conscious of himself now more and then less. For in him there is no becoming, no process, no development. He is pure being, sheer light without darkness, Augustine, _De trin._ XV 14; Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 57–48.

God’s _Weltbewusstsein_ must be distinguished from this self-consciousness. Earlier theology therefore divided God’s knowledge into _scientia naturalis_ (_necessaria, simplicis intelligentiae_) and _scientia libera_ (_contingens, visionis_). The two are not identical, as pantheism teaches, for if the Absolute can be thought of as only logically and potentially prior to the world, it is wholly insufficient to explain the world. God does not need the world in order to attain personality and self-consciousness. He has neither, according to Hegel, a logical nor, according to Schelling, a historical _cursus_ to traverse in order to attain his full actuality (von Baader). Yet self-consciousness and _Weltbewusstsein_ in God cannot be separated so as to stand alongside each other in the way they were sometimes presented in earlier dogmatics. Rather, there is an organic connection between the two. From the infinite fullness of ideas present in his absolute self-consciousness, God did not arbitrarily select some to realize outside himself. In doing so he was guided by the goal of revealing himself, all his attributes and perfections, in the world. The _cognitio libera_ therefore comprises precisely those ideas that in their realization are suited to unfold God’s being in a creaturely manner. The _cognitio naturalis_ is by nature unsuited to be communicated to creatures. As God knows himself, he can never be known by creatures, neither as to content and extent nor as to manner. But the _cognitio libera_ comprises everything capable of realization and suited to reveal God’s attributes in and for creatures. It stands to the _cognitio naturalis_ as the ectype to the archetype. Yet with this _cognitio libera_ too it must be kept in mind that it is something altogether different in God than in creatures. Even if this _cognitio libera_ is communicable, it nevertheless differs greatly as it exists in God and in the rational creature not only in extent but also in nature and essence. First, Holy Scripture teaches very clearly that this _scientia Dei_ encompasses all that exists; nothing is withdrawn from his omniscience, nothing hidden from his all-seeing eye. Past, present, and future; the least and the most hidden; all is naked and exposed before him with whom we have to do. This too was acknowledged in Christian theology at all times. The only one who spoke otherwise was Jerome. In his commentary on Hab. 1:13, he called it absurd to bring the divine majesty down so far, _ut sciat per momenta singula quot nascantur culices, quot ue moriantur; quae cimicum et pulicum et muscarum sit in terra multitudo, quanti pisces in aqua natent, et qui de minoribus majorum praedae cedere debeant_. But this restriction of God’s omniscience finds no support in Scripture and was unanimously rejected by Christian theology. Further, God knows everything not by observation from things, but he knows everything out of and from himself. Our knowledge is posterior; it presupposes being and is derived from it. With God the reverse is true: he knows everything before it is. Scripture also clearly expresses this when it says that God knows everything before it happens, Isa. 46:10; Amos 3:7; Dan. 2:22; Ps. 139:6; Matt. 6:8, etc. God is, after all, the Creator of all things; he thought them before they existed. _Iste mundus nobis notus non esse posset, nisi esset; Deo autem, nisi notus non esset, esse non posset_, Augustine, _De civ._ XI 10; _De Gen. ad lit._ V 18. He exists of himself and also in his consciousness and knowledge cannot depend upon or be determined by anything outside him. From his _aseitas_ follows of itself the absolute independence of his knowing. He does not know all things from the things once they exist, for then they would, as in Schopenhauer and Hartmann, have proceeded from the unconscious, cf. Augustine, _De civ._ XI 10; _Conf._ XIII 16; _Gen. ad litt._ V 15, but he knows all things in, through, and from himself. And therefore his knowledge is also one, simple, immutable, eternal; he knows everything at once, simultaneously, from eternity; and everything stands eternally present before his eye, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I 12; Augustine, _De trin._ XI 10; XII 18; XV 7, 13, 14; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 35, 36; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 14; _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 44 sq.; Zanchius, _Op._ II 195 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 18, etc.

Accordingly, in God there can be no _praescientia_ in the proper sense. In him there is no _differentia temporis_, Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ III 5. He calls the things that are not as though they were and sees what is not as though it already existed. _Quid est enim praescientia, nisi scientia futurorum? quid autem futurum est Deo, qui omnia supergreditur tempora? Si enim scientia Dei res ipsas habet, non sunt ei futurae sed praesentes; ac per hoc non jam praescientia sed tantum scientia dici potest_, Augustine, _Ad Simpl._ II 2. _Omne quod nobis fuit et erit, in ejus prospectu praesto est_, Gregory the Great, _Mor._, l. 20, c. 23. _Quidquid tempora volvunt, praesens semper habet_, Marius Victor, in Petavius, _De Deo_ IV 4. The distinction of God’s omniscience into _praescientia, scientia visionis_ (of the present), and _reminiscentia_ is entirely a human conception, Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 67. Yet Scripture frequently represents it as though God’s omniscience temporally precedes the being of things. And without this auxiliary conception we too cannot speak of God’s omniscience. But from this there arose in theology the question of how this omniscience of God could be reconciled with human freedom. For if God foreknows everything, then everything is fixed from eternity and there remains no room for free and contingent acts. Cicero therefore already came to deny God’s omniscience because he could not reconcile it with free will, _De fato_, c. 14; _De div._ II 5–7. Marcion denied God omniscience along with omnipotence and goodness because God had allowed the first human being to fall into sin, Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 5. The same was later taught by the Socinians. God knows everything, but everything according to its nature. He knows what may happen in the future, the contingent, therefore not with absolute certainty—for then it would cease to be contingent—but as possible and contingent; that is, he does not certainly and infallibly foreknow the future insofar as it depends on human beings. For otherwise freedom of the will would be lost, God would become the author of sin, and God himself would be subjected to necessity, Fock, _Der Socin._, pp. 437–446. But such a restriction of God’s omniscience conflicted too greatly with Holy Scripture to find acceptance except among a few. Christian theology usually seeks the solution in another direction. Two paths presented themselves for this. On the one hand, Origen distinguished between _praescientia_ and _praedestinatio_. God does indeed foreknow things, but this _praescientia_ is not the cause and ground of their occurrence; rather, God foreknows them with certainty only because in time they will occur through the free decision of human beings: ὀυ γαρ ἐπει εγνωσται γινεται, ἀλλ’ ἐπει γινεσθαι ἐμελλεν ἐγνωσται, _In Gen._ I 14; _C. Cels._ II 20. On the other side stands Augustine, who also wishes to maintain both _praescientia_ and freedom of the will. _Religiosus animus utrumque eligit, utrumque confitetur, et fide pietatis utrumque confirmat_, _De civ._ V 9, 10. But he recognizes that if God foreknows something with certainty, it must also necessarily occur; otherwise the whole of _praescientia_ would collapse again. _Praescientia, si non certa praenoscit, utique nulla est_, _De lib. arb._ III 4. He therefore says that the human will, with its entire nature and all its decisions, is included in the _praescientia_ and is not destroyed by it but rather posited and maintained. _Cum enim sit praescius voluntatis nostrae, cujus est praescius ipsa erit. Voluntas ergo erit, quia voluntatis est praescius_, _De lib. arb._ III 3. _Voluntates nostrae tantum valent, quantum Deus eas valere voluit atque praescivit; et ideo quidquid valent, certissime valent; et quod facturae sunt, ipsae omnino facturae sunt; quia valituras atque facturas ille praescivit, cujus praescientia falli non potest_, _De civ._ V 9. Scholasticism, although making all kinds of distinctions, aligned itself in principle with Augustine: Anselm, _De concordia praescientiae et praedestinationis necnon gratiae Dei cum libero arbitrio_; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 38; Bonaventure, ibid.; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 14; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacram._ I 9, etc. But the Jesuits introduced a change, cf. vol. I, p. 91 f. In keeping with Semi-Pelagianism, they inserted between _scientia necessaria_ and _scientia libera_ the so-called _scientia media_ in order thereby to reconcile God’s omniscience with human freedom. By this they understood a knowledge of God, logically prior to the decrees, concerning the conditionally future. The object of this knowledge is not the merely possible, which will never become reality, nor that which will certainly occur as a consequence of a decree of God, but the possible that can become reality under one condition or another. God, namely, makes much in his government of the world depend on conditions and now knows beforehand what he will do in case the conditions are or are not fulfilled by the human being. God is therefore ready in all cases; he foresees and knows all possibilities and, with a view to all those possibilities, has taken his measures and made his decrees. He knew beforehand what he would do if Adam fell and also if he remained standing; if David went to Keilah or did not go there; if Tyre and Sidon did or did not repent, etc. The knowledge of the _futura conditionata_ therefore precedes the decree of the _futura absoluta_; the human being decides entirely freely and independently at every moment but nevertheless can never surprise God by one decision or another or frustrate his plans, for all possibilities have been reckoned with by God’s _praescientia_. This doctrine of the _scientia media_ was supported by many passages of Scripture that ascribe to God knowledge of what would happen in a given case if one condition or another were or were not fulfilled, such as Gen. 11:6; Ex. 3:19; 34:16; Deut. 7:3, 4; 1 Sam. 23:10–13; 25:29 f.; 2 Sam. 12:8; 1 Kings 11:2; 2 Kings 2:10; 13:19; Ps. 81:14, 15; Jer. 26:2, 3; 38:17–20; Ezek. 2:5–7; 3:4–6; Matt. 11:21, 23; 24:22; 26:53; Luke 22:66–68; John 4:10; 6:15; Acts 22:18; Rom. 9:29; 1 Cor. 2:8. It was indeed opposed by the Thomists and Augustinians, for example Bannez, the Salmanticenses, and Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae_, 1747, I 464 sq., but it was also zealously defended by the Molinists and Congruists—Suarez, Bellarmine, Lessius, and others. Fear of Calvinism and Jansenism benefited the doctrine of _scientia media_ in the Roman Catholic Church, and gradually, in a more pronounced or moderate sense, it found acceptance among almost all Roman Catholic theologians; cf. vol. I, p. 93; Karl Werner, _Der h. Thomas v. Aq._ III 389–442; idem, _Gesch. der kath. Theol._, pp. 98 f.; G. Schneemann, _Die Entstehung der thomistisch-molinistischen Controverse_, _Ergänzungshefte zu den Stimmen aus Maria Laach_, nos. 9, 13, 14; Schwane, _D. G. der neuern Zeit_, pp. 37–59; Petavius, _De Deo_ IV, c. 6, 7, etc. The line of Augustine was thereby abandoned and that of Origen taken up again. Greek theology had done so from the beginning, John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ II, c. 30. Roman Catholic theology followed. The Lutherans too—Gerhard, _Loc._ II, c. 8, sect. 13; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 316; Buddeus, _Instit._ 217; Reinhard, _Dogm._ 116, etc.—and the Remonstrants—Episcopius, _Inst._ IV, sect. 2, c. 19; Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ II, c. 8, §29—were not disinclined toward the _scientia media_. In more recent times many teach, in approximately the same way, that God also actually acquires knowledge from the world as a _medium scientiae_. He does indeed foreknow the contingently future as possible but learns only from the world whether it does or does not become actual. In every case, however, he knows _sein dem Thun der Creatur, wie es auch ausfalle, angemessenes Thun_; he did fix the schema of the world plan but leaves its _Ausfüllung_ to the creature, Dorner, _Gl._ I 319–323; Rothe, _Ethik_ §42; Martensen, _Dogm._ §116; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 343; Grétillat, _Theol. syst._ III 237 s.; Sécretan, _La civilisation et la croyance_, 1887, p. 260 s., etc. By contrast, in the footsteps of Augustine, such a _nuda praescientia_ and _scientia media_ were rejected by the Reformed: Voetius, _Disp._ I 254–258; Twisse, _De scientia media_, _Op._ III, p. 1 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III, qu. 13; Moor I 659 sq.; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ IV 281 f.

Now with the _scientia media_ the question is not whether things frequently stand in a conditional relation to one another, a relation that is known to God and willed by him. If this alone were meant by the _scientia media_, it could be accepted without objection, just as Gomarus, _Disp. theol._ X 30 sq., and Walaeus, _Loci Comm._, _Op._ I 174–176, adopted and acknowledged it in this sense; cf. Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. r. K._, p. 64. But the doctrine of _scientia media_ aims at something else; it wishes to bring the Pelagian conception of freedom of the will into agreement with omniscience. The will of creatures, namely, is by nature indifferent. It can do the one as well as the other. It is determined neither by its nature nor by the many circumstances in which it is placed. These can exercise influence upon the will, but in the end the will remains free and chooses as it pleases. This freedom of the will naturally cannot be reconciled with a decree of God; it consists essentially in _independentia a decreto Dei_. God has not determined that will but has left it free; he could not determine it without destroying it. God must assume a waiting posture over against the will of rational creatures. He watches to see what it will do. But now he is omniscient. He therefore knows all possibilities, all contingently future things, and also foreknows the absolutely future things. He has formed his decrees accordingly. If a human being in particular circumstances will accept grace, God has elected him to life; if he will not believe, God has rejected him. Now it is clear that such a doctrine differs in principle from that of Augustine and Thomas. For according to them, foreknowledge precedes the things, and nothing can come into being except through the will of God. _Non fit aliquid nisi omnipotens fieri velit_, Augustine, _Enchir._ 95; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 14, art. 9, ad 3. Not the world but the decrees are the medium from which God knows all things, Thomas, I, qu. 14, art. 8; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II, dist. 37, art. 1, qu. 1. And the contingent and free can therefore be known certainly and infallibly in its connection and order. It is true that on this point scholasticism sometimes expressed itself differently from Augustine. Anselm, for example, said that _praescientia_ entailed not a _necessitas interna_ and _antecedens_ but only a _necessitas externa_ and _consequens_, _Cur Deus homo_ 16. And Thomas judged that God indeed knows the _futura contingentia_ eternally and certainly _secundum statum, quo actu sunt, secundum suam praesentialitatem_, but that in their _causae proximae_ they were nevertheless contingent and undetermined, _S. Th._ I, qu. 14, art. 13. But this does not take away the fact that with a view to the _causa prima_ they are entirely certain and thus cannot be called contingent; and elsewhere Thomas also says again, _omne quod est, ex eo futurum fuit antequam esset, quia in causa sua erat ut fieret_; cf. Billuart I 440 sq. But in the doctrine of _scientia media_, contingent and free things are also contingent and free over against God, and indeed not only over against his _praedestinatio_ but also over against his _praescientia_. For, just as in Origen, here the things are not because God knows them, but God foreknows them because they will be. The order is therefore not _scientia necessaria, scientia visionis, decretum creandi_, etc., but _scientia necessaria, scientia media, decretum creandi_, etc., _scientia visionis_, Jansen, _Prael._ II 110 sq. God knows the free acts of human beings not from his own being, from his decrees, but from the will of creatures, Schwane, _D. G._ IV 52. God thus becomes dependent on the world, receives knowledge from the world that he could not have or obtain from himself, and therefore ceases in his _scientia_ to be one, simple, independent—that is, to be God. Conversely, the creature becomes for a large part entirely independent of God; it did indeed once receive _esse_ and _posse_ from God, but now has _velle_ entirely in its own hand. It decides entirely sovereignly and does or does not bring something into actuality without a decree of God preceding it. Something can thus come into existence without God’s will; the creature is creator, autonomous, sovereign; and the whole history of the world is withdrawn from God’s government and placed in human hands. The human being decides, and then God comes with his plan corresponding to that decision. If such a decision occurred only once, for example in Adam, that would still be conceivable; but such decisions of a more or less serious nature occur thousands of times in every human life. What, then, are we to think of a God who always waits for all those decisions and has all possible plans ready for all possibilities? What even remains of the schema of the world plan whose _Ausfüllung_ has been left to the human being? And what is a government worth in which the King is the slave of his subjects? And this is the case with God here in the _scientia media_. God watches, and the human being decides. It is not God who makes a distinction among human beings; they distinguish themselves. Grace is distributed according to merits; predestination depends on good works. What Scripture combats everywhere and Augustine rejected in Pelagius is certified by Jesuitism as orthodox Roman Catholic teaching, Billuart I 479. For the advocates of _scientia media_ do indeed appeal to many passages of Holy Scripture, but entirely without justification. Scripture certainly acknowledges that things are placed by God in all kinds of relations to one another and that this relation is frequently also of a conditional nature, so that the one cannot occur unless something else has first been fulfilled. Without faith there is no salvation, without work no food, and so forth. But all the texts that the Jesuits cited for the _scientia media_ do not prove what had to be proved. They do speak of condition and fulfillment, of obedience and promise, of supposition and consequence, of what will occur in one case or another. But none of them says that God, even though he speaks humanly to human beings and deals and negotiates with them in a human manner, would not in each particular case have known and determined what certainly had to occur. Between the merely possible, which is in God only as an idea and will never become actual, and the certainly actual, which is established by God’s decree, there is no further domain that can be governed by the human will. Something always belongs either to the one or to the other. If it is only possible and never becomes actual, it is the object of the _scientia necessaria_; and if it does in fact one day become actual, it is the content of the _scientia libera_. Nothing lies between them; there is no _scientia media_. But moreover, the _scientia media_ does not achieve what it wishes to achieve. It wishes to reconcile freedom of the will in the sense of _indifferentia_ with _praescientia_. Now it says that the latter, understood as _scientia media_, does not make human actions necessary but leaves them entirely free. And that is indeed so, but then it also ceases to be _praescientia_. For if God knows with certainty beforehand what a human being will do in a given case, then he can foresee this only if the motives determine the will in one direction and the will therefore does not consist in indifference. Conversely, if it consists in _indifferentia_, then no knowledge or calculation beforehand is possible; then there is only _scientia post factum_. God’s _praescientia_ and the will as arbitrariness exclude each other. _Si enim scit_, Cicero therefore already said, _certe illud eveniet; sin certe eveniet, nulla fortuna est._ The solution of the problem must therefore be sought with Augustine in another direction. As will become evident later, freedom of the will consists not in _indifferentia_, in arbitrariness or chance, but in _lubentia rationalis_. And this is so little in conflict with the _praescientia Dei_ that it is rather posited and maintained by it. The will, with its nature, its antecedents and motives, its decisions and consequences, is included in the _ordo causarum, qui certus est Deo ejusque praescientia continetur_, Augustine, _De civ._ V 9. In the knowledge of God, things lie in relation to one another in precisely the same connection in which they occur in reality. It is not _praescientia_ and not _praedestinatio_ that now and then falls in from above and compels, but every decision and action is motivated by what precedes it. And thus, in that connection, it is included in the _scientia Dei_. The contingent and free is incorporated, in accordance with its own nature as known and willed by God, into the _ordo causarum_ that is gradually revealed to us in the history of the world.
