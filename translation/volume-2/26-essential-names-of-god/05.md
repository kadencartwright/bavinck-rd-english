### B. God as Spirit

## 5.

This being, however, must not be conceived in God as an abstract being without content, but as a rich, full, divine life.
In order to indicate the fullness of God’s life, Holy Scripture makes use not only of adjectives but also of substantives and calls God truth, righteousness, life, light, love, wisdom, Jer. 10:10; 23:6; John 1:4, 5, 9; 1 Cor. 1:30; 1 John 1:5; 4:8.
And theology treated this doctrine under the name _simplicitas Dei_.
In Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I 12; II 13, 28; IV 4, God is called _totus cogitatus, totus sensus, totus oculus, totus auditus, totus fons omnium bonorum_; cf. Clement, _Strom._ V 12; Origen, _De princ._ I 1, 6; Athanasius, _De decr. Nic. syn._ c. 22; _C. Ar._ II 38.
The three Cappadocians had especially to vindicate, over against Eunomius, the legitimacy of the different divine names.
But Augustine returned repeatedly to the _simplicitas Dei_.
God is pure _essentia_, without _accidens_, _De trin._ V 4.
Compared with him, all created being is nonbeing, _De trin._ VII 5; _Enarr. in Ps._ 134; _Conf._ VII 11; XI 4.
In creatures there is a difference between being, living, knowing, and willing; there is a difference of degree among them; there are creatures that only exist, others that also live, and still others that also think.
But in God all this is one, _De civ._ VIII 6; _De trin._ XV 5.
God is everything that he has, _De civ._ X 10.
He is his own wisdom, his own life.
Being and life are one in him; cf. above, pp. 88 f., 92 f., and Gangauf, _Des h. Aug. specul. Lehre von Gott dem Dreieinigen_, 1883, S. 147-157.
Further, we find this doctrine in John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ I 9, who adduces various proofs for it; in Anselm, _Monol._ c. 15; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 8 n. 4-9; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 3, who briefly expresses it in art. 3 as follows, _Deus est Deitas_; idem, _S. c. Gent._ I c. 16 sq.; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I dist. 8 art. 3; and further in all Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed theologians: Petavius, _De Deo_ lib. II c. 1-8; _Theol. Wirceb._ III p. 64; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II 92 sq.; Jansen, _Prael. theol. dogm._ II 60 sq.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I 183 f.; _Philos. der Vorzeit_ II 742 f.; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ loc. 2 cap. 8 sect. 3; Baier, _Comp. theol. posit._ I c. 1 § 9; Buddeus, _Inst. theol._ II c. 1 § 17; Hyperius, _Meth. theol._, pp. 88, 89; Zanchi, _Op._ II 63-73; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ lib. 2 c. 8; Trigland, _Antapologia_, cap. 4; Voetius, _Disp._ I 226-246; H. Alting, _Theol. Elenct._, p. 119 sq.; Moor I, pp. 604-618; L. Meijer, _Verhand. over de Godd. eigensch._ IV 517-552, and so forth.
On the other hand, this simplicity of God was contested from various sides.
Eunomius did indeed teach God’s absolute simplicity, but inferred from it that all the names of God were mere sounds and that God’s essence coincided with _ἀγεννησια_.
This one attribute rendered all the others superfluous and useless, Schwane, _D. G._ II² 21.
The Anthropomorphites of earlier and later times dispute the simplicity of God because they ascribe a body to God.
The Arabic philosophers taught the simplicity of God but used it to oppose the Christian doctrine of the Trinity, since the three persons were _denominationes additae super substantiam_, Stöckl, _Gesch. der Philos. des M. A._ II 89.
Duns Scotus contradicted the simplicity of God, which he otherwise teaches expressly, _Sent._ I dist. 8 qu. 1-2, insofar as he assumed that the attributes were distinguished _formaliter_ from one another and from God’s essence, ib. qu. 4.
Nominalism went much further still and accepted a real distinction among the attributes.
In the century of the Reformation, the Socinians followed in these footsteps.
In the interest of human autonomy, they came to make the divine being finite and thus did not know what to do with God’s simplicity.
Socinus doubted whether simplicity could be ascribed to God according to Scripture.
The Racovian Catechism omits it entirely.
Schlichting, Volkelius, and others denied that the attributes were one with God’s essence and maintained that a fullness of attributes did not conflict with his unity, Hoornbeek, _Socin. conf._ I, pp. 368-369.
Vorstius concurred and said, especially on the ground of the doctrine of the Trinity, that a distinction must be made in God between _materia_ and _forma_, essence and attributes, _genus_ and _differentiae_.
Scripture therefore also says that God swears by his soul, Jer. 51:14, and that the Spirit is in him, 1 Cor. 2:11.
There is a distinction between knowing and willing, between the subject who lives and the life by which he lives; cf. Trigland, _Kerk. Gesch._ IV 576, 585 f.; Schweizer in _Theol. Jahrb._ 1856, S. 435 f.; 1857, S. 153 f.; Dorner, _Gesammelte Schriften_, 1883, S. 278 f.
The Remonstrants were of the same opinion.
In their _Apol. Conf._ cap. 2 they said that there was not one iota about the _simplicitas Dei_ in Scripture, that it was a purely metaphysical doctrine and by no means necessary to believe.
It was objected especially that the freedom of God’s will and the change in his disposition could not be reconciled with his simplicity.
Episcopius still included the _simplicitas Dei_ among the _attributa_ and thought that the _relationes_, the _volitiones_, and the _decreta libera_ could indeed be reconciled with it, _Instit. Theol._ lib. 4 sect. 2 cap. 7, but Limborch no longer mentioned this attribute.
In rationalism it receded completely into the background or was even left undiscussed, Wegscheider, _Inst. Theol._ § 61; Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 33; Bretschneider, _Handb. der Dogm._ I⁴ 486.
The last-named says that Scripture does not know these subtleties of philosophy.
Pantheism likewise could neither acknowledge nor appreciate the doctrine of God’s simplicity.
It identified God and the world and, through the mouth of Spinoza, even ascribed the attribute of extension to God himself.
Thus the _simplicitas Dei_ has almost entirely disappeared from modern theology; its significance is not understood; sometimes it is vigorously opposed.
Schleiermacher did not place the simplicity of God on the same level as the other attributes and regarded it only as _das ungetrennte und untrennbare Ineinandersein aller göttlichen Eigenschaften und Thätigkeiten_, _Chr. Gl._ § 56.
In Lange, Kahnis, Philippi, Ebrard, Lipsius, Biedermann, F. A. B. Nitzsch, Oosterzee, and others, this attribute is no longer mentioned.
It is vigorously opposed by Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 208 f.; Dorner, _Glaub._ I 221 f.; idem, _Gesammelte Schriften_, S. 305; Frank, _Syst. de chr. Wahrh._ I² 124; Ritschl, _Theol. u. Metaph._, S. 12 f.; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III², S. 2 f.; _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ 1865, S. 275 f.; 1868, S. 67 f.; S. 251 f.; Doedes, _Ned. Geloofsbel._, p. 9, especially on these two grounds: that it is a metaphysical abstraction and conflicts with the doctrine of the Trinity; cf. against this Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ I 256-259; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 338; Kuyper, _Ex ungue leonem_, 1882.

Nevertheless, this _simplicitas Dei_ is of the greatest significance for the knowledge of God.
It is not only taught in Scripture where God is called light, life, love, and so forth, but it also follows of itself from the idea of God and is necessarily given with the other attributes.
Here simple is the opposite of composite.
If God is composed of parts, like a body, or also of _genus_ and _differentia_, _substantia_ and _accidentia_, _materia_ and _forma_, _potentia_ and _actus_, _essentia_ and _existentia_, then his perfection, his unity, his independence, and his immutability cannot be maintained.
For then he is not the highest love, since in him the subject who loves would be one thing and the love by which he loves another, and so also with respect to all the other attributes.
God would then not be the one _quo melius nihil cogitari potest_.
But God is of himself and has nothing above him; he is therefore completely one with wisdom, grace, love, and so forth; he is absolutely perfect; nothing higher than God can be conceived, Augustine, _De trin._ V 10; VI 1; Hugh of St. Victor, _De trin._ I 12.
In creatures all this is otherwise.
Here there is a distinction between existence, essence, life, knowing, willing, acting, and so forth; _omne quod compositum est creatum est_; no creature can be absolutely _simplex_, for every creature is finite.
But God is the infinite one, and whatever is in him is infinite.
All his attributes are divine and thus infinite, one with his essence.
Therefore he is, and he alone can be, self-sufficient, all-blessed, and all-glorious in himself, Petavius, _De Deo_ lib. II 2.
From this it is already evident that this simplicity of God is by no means a metaphysical abstraction.
It is essentially distinct from the philosophical idea of _το ὀν, το ἑν, το μοναχον, το ἁπλουν_, or of substance, the Absolute, and so forth, by which Xenophanes, Plato, Philo, Plotinus, and later Spinoza and Hegel designate God.
It was not found by abstraction, by going beyond all oppositions and distinctions in creatures and defining the idea of God as being exalted above all oppositions.
Rather, this attribute was reached precisely by placing all the perfections of creatures in God in a perfect, divine manner.
The description of God as _simplicissima essentia_ indicates that he is perfect, infinitely full being, that he is an infinite and boundless _πελαγος οὐσιας_.
And far from promoting pantheism, as Baur, _Chr. Lehre van d. Dreiein. Gottes_ II 635, supposes, this doctrine of the _simplicissima essentia Dei_ stands in direct opposition to it.
For in pantheism God has no existence and life of his own apart from and without the world.
In Hegel, for example, the Absolute, pure being, thought, the idea, does not exist before the world, but only logically and potentially earlier than the world.
All the determinations of the Absolute are without content; they are nothing but abstract, logical forms of thought, Drews, _Die deutsche Spekulation_ I 249.
But the description of God as _simplicissima essentia_ in Christian theology maintains precisely that God has a life of his own, infinite and all-blessed in himself, even though we can designate that essence of God only with names borrowed from creatures.
The substance, the highest being, the Absolute, and so forth, by which pantheistic philosophy preferably names the divine being, is obtained by abstraction; one proceeded by thinking everything away from things until nothing remained but the final, most universal concept, namely pure being, bare existence.
This being is indeed an _abstractum_, a concept to which no reality corresponds and which may not be further determined.
Every further determination would render it finite, make it something particular, and thus destroy its universality.
_Omnis determinatio est negatio._
But the being that theology ascribes to God is a proper, particular being, distinct from the world.
It designates God not as a being of which we can think absolutely nothing except that it is, but as a being in whom everything is essence, as the absolute fullness of being.
This simple essence therefore does not exclude the many names, as Eunomius maintained, but requires them.
God is so rich that only with many names can we obtain some conception of his riches.
Every name designates the same full, divine essence, but each time from another side, according to which it reveals itself to us in its works.
God is therefore _simplex_ in his _multiplicitas_ and _multiplex_ in his _simplicitas_ (Augustine).
Every determination, every name by which we designate God, is therefore not a negation but an enrichment of the knowledge of his essence.
_Divinum esse est determinatum in se et ab omnibus aliis divisum per hoc quod sibi nulla additio fieri potest_, Thomas, _Sent._ I dist. 8 qu. 4 art. 1 ad 1; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I² 204 f.
Understood in this way, this simplicity of God is also not in conflict with the Trinity.
For simple, _simplex_, is opposed here not to twofold or threefold, _duplex, triplex_, but to composite, _compositus_.
And the divine essence is not composed of three persons, nor is each person composed of the essence and the _proprietas personalis_; rather, the one and the same simple essence exists in the three persons; each person or personal property is distinguished from the essence not _re_ but only _ratione_; each personal property is indeed a _relatio realis_, but still adds no _aliquid reale_ to the _essentia_.
The personal properties _non componunt sed solum distinguunt_, Petavius, lib. 2 cap. 3-4; Zanchi, _Op._ II col. 68, 69.
