### D. God as the Holy One

## 11.

Among the ethical virtues, the first place belongs to the goodness of God.
This too is known from nature.
In Plato the idea of the good was one with the Godhead.
But the word _good_ has different meanings.
The original and primary meaning of _good_ appears to indicate not an intrinsic quality but a relation to something else.
For Socrates the good was identical with the useful, with what was good, serviceable, useful for another, Xenophon, _Memor._ IV 6, 8 f.; there is therefore no absolute good, but only a relative good; benefit and harm are the standard of good and evil.
Greek ethics remained at this standpoint; the question concerning the highest good coincides with that concerning happiness; good is that which all desire, Plato, _Symp._ 204 E f.; Aristotle, _Nic. Eth._ I 2.
Hence the customary definition: _bonum est id quod omnia appetunt_, for example, in Dionysius, _De div. nom._ c. 4; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 5 art. 1; qu. 16 art. 1 and 3; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 38, 40; _Sent._ I dist. 8 art. 3.
Utilitarian morality adheres to this meaning of the word _good_ and grounds its teaching upon it; cf. Paulsen, _System der Ethik_, 1889, S. 171 f.; Wundt, _Ethik_, 1886, S. 18 f.
And Friedrich Nietzsche built his whole revolution in morality upon it; _good_ originally meant as much as noble, strong, powerful, beautiful, and _bad_ was the designation of the _schlichten, gemeinen Mann_, _Zur Genealogie der Moral_, S. 6.
Now the word _good_ does indeed often have this meaning, as when we speak of a good house, a good friend, and so forth, and thereby indicate that a person or thing possesses certain properties and is fit for something.
_Good_ then has no content of its own but receives it from the end that someone or something must serve, and therefore varies in meaning among different peoples.
The Greek thinks of beauty, καλοκαγαθια; the Roman, of noble birth and wealth; the Germanic person, of what is fitting and _tüchtig_; and in connection with this the meaning of ἀρετη, _virtus_, virtue, also differs.
This good in the general sense includes under it the useful, the pleasant, the aesthetically good, and the ethically good, _bonum utile, bonum delectabile_, and _bonum honestum_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 5 art. 6; I 2 qu. 99 art. 5, and so forth.
In all these meanings, _good_ is still a relative concept and designates _id quod omnia appetunt_.
But the word is nevertheless not exhausted by this meaning.
We also speak of a _bonum in se_.
The meaning of _good_ as _bonum morale_ or _honestum_ forms the transition to this.
The morally good is good in itself, apart from all advantageous or harmful consequences; it has absolute value.
According to Scripture, God is the sum of all perfections.
All virtues are present in him in an absolute sense.
Scripture calls him good in an absolute sense only a few times, Οὐδεις ἀγαθος ¡ει μη εἱς ὁ θεος, Mark 10:18; Luke 18:19; he is τελειος, Matt. 5:48.
But whatever virtue it ascribes to God, it always proceeds from the assumption that this belongs to him in an absolute sense.
Knowledge, wisdom, power, love, righteousness, and so forth, belong to him in an entirely unique, that is, divine manner.
This goodness of his is therefore one with his absolute perfection.
In him idea and reality are one.
He is pure εἰδος, _actus purissimus_.
He does not need to become anything but eternally is what he is.
He has no end outside himself but is self-sufficient, ἐξαρκης, παναρκης, αὐταρκης, Ps. 50:9 f.; Isa. 40:28 f.; Hab. 2:20.
He receives nothing but only gives.
Everything needs him; he needs nothing and no one.
He always aims at himself because he cannot rest in anything less than himself.
Since he himself is the absolutely good, the perfect one, he cannot and may not love anything else except in and for himself.
He cannot and may not be satisfied with less than absolute perfection.
Where he loves others, he loves himself in them—his own virtues, works, and gifts.
On the goodness of God in the sense of perfection, cf. Augustine, _De nat. boni c. Manich._ 1; _De trin._ VIII 3; Pseudo-Dionysius, _De div. nom._ c. 13; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 4-6; _C. Gent._ I 28; Petavius, _De Deo_ VI c. 1 sq.; Gerhard, _Loc._ II c. 8 sect. 10, 17; Zanchi, _Op._ II 138 sq., 326 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II c. 9, and so forth.
Therefore he is also absolutely blessed in himself, as the sum of all good, of all perfection.
Aristotle already said that God was therefore the blessed one because he was the unity of thinking and thought and was completely exalted above all desiring, striving, and willing, Zeller III³ 367 f.
And at all times those who placed the primacy in the intellect joined Aristotle and sought blessedness in thinking, knowing, contemplation; cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2 qu. 3 art. 4.
There was truth in this insofar as absolute blessedness is a state of rest, is incompatible with any striving toward an end, and also presupposes consciousness.
Blessedness belongs only to rational beings.
The unconscious in von Hartmann, like the will in Schopenhauer, is absolute wretchedness that itself needs deliverance, Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._ II⁹ 434; _Religionsphilosophie_ II 152 f.
Drews accordingly says that the attribute of perfection cannot be ascribed to God; a perfect God would be a _totes Abstraktum_ and could not explain the world; a God who had everything, who was perfectly blessed and sufficient unto himself, has no need of change or a world, _Die deutsche Spekulation seit Kant_ II 593 f.
But precisely this doctrine of God’s absolute wretchedness warns us against ascribing primacy to the will in God and the human being, as Duns Scotus and many after him did.
Much more correct, therefore, is the position of Bonaventure, who placed blessedness in both, in intellect and will, _Sent._ IV dist. 49 pars 1 art. 1 qu. 4, 5.
Just as human blessedness will encompass soul and body and all one’s faculties, so in God it consists not only in his perfect knowledge but equally in his perfect power, goodness, holiness, and so forth.
_Beatitudo status est omnium bonorum aggregatione perfectus_, Boethius, _De cons. philos._ 4.
On the blessedness of God, Augustine, _De civ. Dei_ XII 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 26; Gerhard, _Loc._ II c. 8 sect. 19; Zanchi II 155 sq.; Polanus II c. 17.
But what is good in itself is also good for others.
And as the perfect and blessed one, God is the highest good for his creatures, _bonum summum quod omnia appetunt; fons bonorum omnium; bonum omnis boni; bonum unum necessarium et sufficiens, finis bonorum omnium_, Ps. 4:7, 8; 73:25, 26.
He alone is the _bonum quo sit fruendum_, but creatures are _bona, quibus sit utendum_, Augustine, _De doctr. chr._ I c. 3 sq.; _De trin._ X 10; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 1; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I dist. 1.
Augustine especially often presented God in this way as the _summum bonum_, _De trin._ VIII 3; _Enarr. in Ps._ 26; _De doctr. chr._ I 7; cf. Anselm, _Prosl._ c. 23-25.
In him is everything, and in him alone is what all creatures seek and need.
He is the _summum bonum_ for all creatures, although for them in different measure, according as they participate more or less in his goodness and can enjoy him more or less.
He is the one toward whom all creatures consciously or unconsciously, willingly or unwillingly, strive, the object of the desire of all.
And the creature finds rest in God alone, Augustine, _Conf._ I 1.
Thus Christian theology at all times placed the highest good in God, and it never occurred to it to seek that highest good in any moral deed or virtue of the creature, in duty (Kant), in the kingdom of God (Ritschl), in love (Drummond), or in any other creature.
Further, as _summum bonum_, God is also the overflowing fountain of all good, Belgic Confession, art. 1.
_Deus, cum perfecte bonus sit, perpetuo beneficus est_, Athenagoras, _Leg. pro christ._ n. 26.
There is no good in any creature except from and through him.
He is the _causa efficiens, exemplaris et finalis_ of all good, however variously this may be distinguished in creatures.
All natural, moral, and spiritual good has its origin in him.
Holy Scripture is a hymn of praise to the goodness of the Lord.
From it are derived creation and all life and blessing for human being and beast, Pss. 8; 19; 36:6-8; 65:12; 147:9; Matt. 5:45; Acts 14:17; James 1:17.
It extends over all his works, Ps. 145:9, and endures forever, Ps. 136.
Again and again the whole creation is summoned to praise God’s goodness, 1 Chron. 16:34; 2 Chron. 5:13; Pss. 34:9; 106:1; 107:1; 118:1; 136:1; Jer. 33:11, and so forth; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._, 545 f.; Dionysius, _De div. nom._ c. 4, who portrays the goodness of God in an exalted manner and compares it to the sun that illumines everything; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 6, especially art. 4; _C. Gent._ I c. 40, 41; Petavius, _De Deo_ VI c. 3; Gerhard, _Loc._ II c. 8 sect. 10; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ II c. 20; Zanchi, _Op._ II cols. 326-342, and so forth.
This goodness of God appears in different forms according to the objects toward which it directs itself.
Closely related to it is _lovingkindness_, חֶסֶד, derived from חסד, _stringere_, to bind, χρηστοτης, related to πραυτης, 2 Cor. 10:1.
Sometimes it is used in a general sense, 1 Chron. 16:34, but for the most part it denotes God’s special favor toward his people, the affection by which he is bound to his favored ones: to Joseph, Gen. 39:21; Israel, Num. 14:19; David, 2 Sam. 7:15; 22:51; Ps. 18:51; 1 Chron. 17:13; the godly, Ps. 5:8.
It is connected with God’s covenant, Neh. 1:5; is the principle of forgiveness, Pss. 6:5; 31:17; 44:27; 109:26; Lam. 3:22; of grace, Ps. 51:3; of comfort, Ps. 119:76; endures forever, Isa. 54:8, 10; and is better than life, Ps. 63:4.
In all its riches it has revealed itself in Christ, Rom. 2:4; 2 Cor. 10:1; Eph. 2:7; Col. 3:12; Titus 3:4, and now manifests itself to believers, leading them to repentance, Rom. 2:4; 11:22; Gal. 5:22; cf. Delitzsch on Ps. 4:4; Cremer, s.v. ἐλεος.
The goodness of God, when shown to the wretched, is called mercy, רָחֲמִים, σπλαγχνα, _viscera, misericordia_, in the New Testament ἐλεος, οἰκτιρμος.
Scripture speaks at every moment of this mercy of God, Ex. 34:6; Deut. 4:31; 2 Chron. 30:9; Pss. 86:15; 103:8; 111:4; 112:4; 145:8, and so forth, in contrast with human beings, 2 Sam. 24:14; Prov. 12:10; Dan. 9:9, 18.
It is manifold, 2 Sam. 24:14; Ps. 119:156; great, Neh. 9:19; Ps. 51:13; without end, Lam. 3:22; tender as that of a father, Ps. 103:13; is shown to thousands, Ex. 20:6; and returns after chastisement, Isa. 14:1; 49:13 f.; 54:8; 55:7; 60:10; Jer. 12:15; 30:18; 31:20; Hos. 2:22; Mic. 7:19, and so forth.
In the New Testament God, the Father of mercies, 2 Cor. 1:3, revealed his mercy in Christ, Luke 1:50 f., who is a merciful high priest, Matt. 18:27; 20:34, and so forth; Heb. 2:17; and he further shows the riches of his mercy, Eph. 2:4, in the salvation of believers, Rom. 9:23; 11:30; 1 Cor. 7:25; 2 Cor. 4:1; 1 Tim. 1:13; Heb. 4:16, and so forth; cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 21 art. 3; Zanchi, _Op._ II 370 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II c. 23; Gerhard, _Loc._ II c. 8 sect. 11.
The sparing goodness of God toward those deserving punishment is called _long-suffering_, רוּחַ or אֹרֶך אַפַּיִם, μακροθυμια, ἀνοχη, χρηστοτης.
This virtue of God too is mentioned repeatedly in Scripture, Ex. 34:6; Num. 14:18; Neh. 9:17; Pss. 86:15; 103:8; 145:8; Jonah 4:2; Joel 2:13; Nah. 1:3.
It manifested itself throughout the whole time before Christ, Rom. 3:25, and is even now, after the example of Christ, 1 Tim. 1:16; 2 Pet. 3:15, frequently shown to sinners, Rom. 2:4; 9:22; 1 Pet. 3:20; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ II c. 24.
The goodness of God is much richer where it is shown to those who have deserved no good but every evil; then it bears the name _grace_, חֵן, תְחִנָּה, derived from חָנַן, to bend down, incline, χαρις, from χαριζομαι.
This word also denotes the favor that one human being finds with another or that one bestows upon another, Gen. 30:27; 33:8, 10; 47:29; 50:4, and so forth; Luke 2:52.
When used of God, however, it never has creatures in general or even the Gentiles as its object, but only his people.
It is shown to Noah, Gen. 6:8; Moses, Ex. 33:12, 17; 34:9; Job, ch. 8:5; 9:15; Daniel, ch. 1:9; to the meek and wretched, Prov. 3:34; Dan. 4:27; and then especially to Israel as a people.
Its election and guidance, its deliverance and redemption, and all the benefits it received in distinction from other peoples are due to God’s grace alone, Ex. 15:13, 16; 19:4; 33:19; 34:6, 7; Deut. 4:37; 7:8; 8:14, 17; 9:5, 27; 10:14 f.; 33:3; Isa. 35:10; 42:21; 43:1, 15, 21; 54:5; 63:9; Jer. 3:4, 19; 31:9, 20; Ezek. 16; Hos. 8:14; 11:1, and so forth.
In history and law, in psalmody and prophecy, the keynote is always: not to us, O Lord, but to your name give glory, Ps. 115:1.
He does everything for his name’s sake, Num. 14:13 f.; Isa. 43:21, 25 f.; 48:9, 11; Ezek. 36:22, and so forth.
And therefore that grace too is repeatedly praised and glorified, Ex. 34:6; 2 Chron. 30:9; Neh. 9:17; Pss. 86:15; 103:8; 111:4; 116:5; Jonah 4:2; Joel 2:13; Zech. 12:10.
In the New Testament that grace proves to be still richer and deeper in content.
Χαρις means objectively beauty, charm, grace, Luke 4:22; Col. 4:6; Eph. 4:29, and subjectively favor, affection on the part of the giver, and thanks, honor on the part of the recipient.
In God it denotes his voluntary, unowed, undeserved affection, which is shown to guilty sinners and, instead of the sentence of death, grants them δικαιοσυνη and ζωη.
As such it is a virtue and attribute of God, Rom. 5:15; 1 Pet. 5:10, which manifests itself in the sending of Christ, who is full of grace, John 1:14 f.; 1 Pet. 1:13, and further in the bestowal of all kinds of spiritual and physical benefits, which are all gifts of grace and themselves grace, Rom. 5:20; 6:1; Eph. 1:7; 2:5, 8; Phil. 1:2; Col. 1:2; Titus 3:7, and so forth, and completely exclude all merits on the human side, John 1:17; Rom. 4:4, 16; 6:14, 23; 11:5 f.; Eph. 2:8; Gal. 5:3, 4; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._, 425 f.; Cremer, s.v.
The doctrine of grace was first developed in the Christian church by Augustine, but in this connection people usually thought not of grace as a virtue of God but of the benefits that God out of grace bestowed in Christ upon the church.
Grace is not usually treated among the attributes of God.
This concept of grace, namely as _virtus Dei_, is not lacking, to be sure; Thomas says, for example, _quandoque tamen gratia Dei dicitur ipsa aeterna dilectio, secundum quod dicitur etiam gratia praedestinationis, in quantum Deus gratuito et non ex meritis aliquos praedestinavit sive elegit_, _S. Theol._ II 1 qu. 110 art. 1; cf. Schwetz, _Theol. dogm. cath._ I, 1851, p. 193; Gerhard, _Loc._ II c. 8 sect. 11 qu. 4; Musculus, _Loci Communes_, 1567, p. 317 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II c. 21; Zanchi, _Op._ II 342-358; but grace is immediately taken in a broader sense, as it must first be discussed later in dogmatics, in the _locus de salute_.
Cf. also Lange, art. “Gnade,” in Herzog²; _Dogm._ II § 18.
Further, the goodness of God appears as _love_ when it gives not merely particular benefits but itself.
In the Old Testament this love is not yet as often spoken of as an attribute of God, but it is by no means lacking, Deut. 4:37; 7:8, 13; 10:15; 23:5; 2 Chron. 2:11; Isa. 31:3; 43:4; 48:14; 63:9; Jer. 31:3; Hos. 11:1, 4; 14:5; Zeph. 3:17; Mal. 1:2.
And not only virtues and attributes, such as right and righteousness, Pss. 11:7; 33:5; 37:28; 45:8, but also persons are its object, Pss. 78:68; 146:8; Prov. 3:12; Deut. 4:37; 7:8, 13; 23:5; 2 Chron. 2:11; Jer. 31:3; Mal. 1:2.
This love of God comes to light much more clearly in the New Testament, now that God has given himself in the Son of his love.
The Hebrew אַהֲבָה is rendered not by ἐρως, which is the ordinary name for sensual love, nor by φιλια, the word for love among blood relatives, but by ἀγαπη, which is foreign to ordinary Greek and also does not occur in Philo and Josephus but is precisely suited to designate full, pure, divine love, just like the Latin _caritas_ (_dilectio_) in distinction from _amor_.
The relation of Father and Son is depicted as a life of love, John 3:35; 5:20; 10:17; 14:31; 15:19; 17:24, 26.
But in Christ, who himself loves and has demonstrated his love in his self-surrender, John 15:13, that love also reveals itself toward human beings, not only toward the world or the church in general, John 3:16; Rom. 5:7; 8:37; 1 John 4:9, but also individually and personally, John 14:23; 16:27; 17:23; Rom. 9:13; Gal. 2:20.
Indeed, God does not merely love, but he is love, 1 John 4:8, and his love is the foundation, source, and exemplar of ours, 1 John 4:10.
Now one can indeed speak of God’s love in general toward creatures and toward human beings, φιλοκτισια, _amor complacentiae_, and φιλανθρωπια, _amor benevolentiae_, but Scripture nevertheless uses the word _goodness_ chiefly for this and as a rule speaks of God’s love, like his grace, only in relation to the people or the church that he has chosen, ἐλεκτοφιλια, _amor amicitiae_, Oehler, _Theol. des Alt. Test._ § 81; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._, 545 f.; Smend, _Altt. Religionsgesch._, 197 f.; Cremer, s.v.
This love is indeed not the essence of God in the sense that it constitutes the center and heart therein and that the other attributes are its _modi_, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 167; Schoeberlein, _Princip u. System der Dogm._, 1881, S. 129; Dorner, _Glaub._ I 437; Oosterzee, _Dogm._ § 50, for all the attributes are his essence in an equal sense; in God there is no higher and lower, no less and more, Lange, _Dogm._ II 203; Thomasius, _Christi Person und Werk_ I³, S. 105; Doedes, _Leer van God_, 231.
But love is nevertheless most certainly identical with the divine essence.
It is independent, eternal, immutable, like God himself.
It has its origin in him and also returns to him through creatures.
Pseudo-Dionysius therefore spoke of divine love _tamquam sempiternus circulus, propter bonum, ex bono, in bono, et ad bonum indeclinabili conversione circumiens, in eodem et secundum idem et procedens semper et manens et remeans_, _De div. nom._ c. 4 § 14; cf. Augustine, _De trin._ VIII 6-12; IX 6; X 1; _Conf._ IV 12; _Solil._ I 2; _De vera relig._ c. 46; Lombard, _Sent._ III dist. 32, 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 20; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 91; Scheeben, _Dogm._ I 692 f.; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ loc. II c. 8 sect. 11 qu. 2; Zanchi, _Op._ II 359 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II c. 22.
