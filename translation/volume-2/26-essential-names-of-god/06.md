## 6.

God’s simplicity includes his spiritual nature, for everything corporeal is composite.
Scripture does indeed always speak of God in a human manner and attributes to him all kinds of bodily organs and activities, but even here it nevertheless observes a certain limit.
Of the internal organs of the human body, only the heart and inward parts are ascribed to him.
Organs of nutrition, digestion, and reproduction are never transferred to him.
God is indeed said to see and hear, and also to smell, but not to taste or touch.
A body is nowhere attributed to him.
Although the Old Testament nowhere says in so many words that God is spirit, this conception nevertheless underlies its entire description of God.
God is YHWH, who descends to his people and reveals himself in a human manner, but from the beginning he is also Elohim, who is far exalted above everything creaturely.
He exists of himself, Ex. 3:13; Isa. 41:4; 44:6; is eternal, Deut. 32:40; Ps. 90:1 f.; 102:28; omnipresent, Deut. 10:14; Ps. 139:1 f.; Jer. 23:23, 24; incomparable, Isa. 40:18, 25; 46:5; Ps. 89:7, 9; invisible, Ex. 33:20, 23; incapable of being imaged, Ex. 20:4; Deut. 5:8, because he is without form, תְּמוּנָה, Deut. 4:12, 15.
God and human beings stand over against one another as spirit and flesh, Isa. 31:3.
Although he repeatedly reveals himself in appearance, dream, and vision and to that extent makes himself visible, Gen. 32:30; Ex. 24:10; 33:11; Num. 12:8; Deut. 5:24; Judg. 13:22; 1 Kings 22:19; Isa. 6:1, it is nevertheless through his Spirit, and thus in a spiritual manner, that he is present in creation and creates and sustains all things, Ps. 139:7; Gen. 2:7; Job 33:4; Pss. 33:6; 104:30; etc.; Oehler, _Theol. des A. T._ §46; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._ 496–504.
God’s spiritual essence comes to light more clearly in the New Testament.
This is evident not only from the fact that God is eternal, Rom. 16:26; 1 Tim. 6:16; 1 Pet. 1:23; Rev. 1:8; 10:6; 15:7; omnipresent, Acts 27:27, 28; Eph. 4:6; and incomparable, Acts 17:29; Rom. 1:22, 23, but Jesus also states it directly when he calls God πνευμα and therefore requires a spiritual and true worship of God, John 4:24.
And although this statement is not repeated literally by the apostles, they nevertheless have the same conception of God’s essence when they call him invisible, John 1:18; cf. 6:46; Rom. 1:20; Col. 1:15; 1 Tim. 1:17; 6:16; 1 John 4:12, 20—something with which the promise of the vision of God in the _status gloriae_ does not conflict, Job 19:26; Ps. 17:15; Matt. 5:8; 1 Cor. 13:12; 1 John 3:2; Rev. 22:4.
This spiritual essence of God has been misunderstood and denied in paganism, Rom. 1:23.
Even philosophy has been unable to free itself from sensory representations of God.
Materialism regards material atoms as the ultimate ground of being.
Deism finitizes God and makes him essentially like human beings; in Epicurus the gods are a company of reasoning philosophers, with a subtle ethereal body and distinguished by sex, Zeller IV³ 433.
But pantheism too impairs this spiritual essence of God.
According to Heraclitus and the Stoa, the first cause is to be conceived as material and corporeal; God is at the same time the original force that in the course of time brings forth all things from itself and then takes them back into itself, Zeller IV³ 133–146.
All these movements also continued to exert their influence in the Christian church and down to the present day.
In the realistic-eschatological conceptions that prevailed widely in the second half of the second century, the thought of a certain corporeality of God was not far removed.
It is not entirely certain whether Tertullian ascribed a material body to God.
For he does indeed say, _quis enim negabit, deum corpus esse, etsi deus spiritus est? spiritus enim corpus sui generis in sua effigie_, _Adv. Prax._ 7, but he appears to understand _corpus_ in the sense of substance when he says, _omne quod est, corpus est sui generis, nihil est incorporale nisi quod non est_, _De carne_ 11, and elsewhere speaks just as realistically of the soul, _De an._ 6.
Nevertheless, the identification of _corpus_ and _substantia_ is noteworthy; it proves that Tertullian could conceive no substance except as _corpus_.
And elsewhere he also says that God is no more free of affections than the human being, although they are present in him in a perfect manner.
He even goes so far that he wishes to understand the hands and eyes attributed to God in Scripture not spiritually but literally, though in a perfect manner, _Adv. Marc._ II 16.
Tertullian can therefore be called the father of biblical realism.
The danger posed by the spiritualism produced by the allegorical exegesis of the Alexandrian school drove many in the direction of anthropomorphism.
According to Origen, Melito, bishop of Sardis, ascribed a body to God, although it cannot be determined to what extent this report is correct, Herzog² 9, 539.
In the fourth century a similar anthropomorphism was taught by Audius and his followers and by many Egyptian monks, who were opposed especially in the work _Contra anthropomorphitas_ attributed to Cyril.
Later this anthropomorphic representation of God was revived by Socinianism.
Here God is only a Lord who has power over us.
No mention is made of his simple, infinite, independent, spiritual essence, Socinus, _Christ. relig. instit._; _Catech. Racov._, qu. 53.
Crell maintained that the word spirit was used of God, angel, and soul not _aequivoce_ and _analogice_ but _univoce_, _De Deo et attrib._, lib. 1, c. 15.
And Vorstius said in Tertullian’s sense, _vere corpus Deo attribui, si per corpus vera et solida substantia intelligatur_, Dorner, _Ges. Schriften_, 279.
Further, theosophy too teaches that God most certainly has a body in a certain sense and that the human being was therefore also created bodily in God’s image.
In the Kabbalah the ten _sefiroth_—that is, the attributes or _modi_ in which God reveals himself—together bear the name Adam Kadmon, the first heavenly human being, because the human form is the highest and most perfect revelation of God and God is so presented in Scripture, Franck, _La Kabbale_, 1843, p. 179.
From this there developed among the Christian theosophists, in connection with the doctrine of the Trinity, the conception that God, who is not a resting being but an eternally becoming life, not only inwardly rises from darkness to light, from nature to spirit, but outwardly also surrounds himself with a certain nature, corporeality, glory, and heaven, and therein assumes form for himself and glorifies himself.
Thus we find it in Böhme; cf. Joh. Claassen, _Jakob Böhme, Sein Leben und seine theos. Werke_, 3 vols., Stuttgart, 1885, I, S. XXXII, 157; II 61; Oetinger, _Die Theologie aus der Idee des Lebens usw._, 1852, S. 109 f.; Baader; cf. Joh. Claassen, _Franz v. Baaders Leben und theos. Werke usw._, 2 vols., Stuttgart, 1887, II 110–112; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psychol._, S. 48 f.; Auberlen, _Die Philos. Oetingers_, 1847, S. 147; Hamberger, _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ VIII, 1863, S. 448; etc.
Finally, even in pantheism the spiritual nature of God does not receive its due.
Spinoza ascribed the attribute of _extensio_ to substance, _Eth._ II, props. 1, 2.
And in the philosophy of Hegel, Schleiermacher, Hartmann, and others, the world is an essential moment in the infinite.
_Ohne Welt ist Gott nicht Gott_, Hegel, _Werke_ XI 122.
God and world are _correlata_; they express the same being, first as unity and then as totality, Schleiermacher, _Dialektik_, S. 162.
This pantheism does indeed continue to speak of God as Spirit, and even with a certain fondness.
But it is difficult to understand what is to be meant by this.
Hegel uses the name spirit especially for the last and highest _Stufe_ that the idea reaches through nature in the human being, specifically in philosophy.
_Das Logische wird zur Natur und die Natur zum Geiste_, _Werke_ VII, 2, S. 468.
Hegel still seeks the essence of spirit in ideality, in _einfache Insichseyn_, in I-hood, ibid., S. 14 f., 18 f., but God nevertheless first becomes spirit after a long process through and in the human being.
Others have begun to use the word spirit for the ultimate immanent cause of things without thereby thinking of a proper, distinct existence and a personal, conscious life.
Hartmann designates the unconscious as pure, unconscious, impersonal, absolute spirit and therefore calls his philosophy spiritualistic monism and panpneumatism, _Philos. des Unbew._ II⁹ 457; _Philos. Fragen der Gegenwart_, 1885, S. 61.

Over against all these movements, the Christian church and theology have always maintained the _spiritualitas Dei_, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II, c. 13; Origen, _De princ._ I 1, 6; _C. Cels._ VII 33 sq.; VIII 49; Augustine, _De trin._ XV 5; _De civ._ VIII 5; _Conf._ VI 3; etc.; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I, c. 11; II, c. 3; etc.
The _spiritualitas Dei_ is frequently not treated separately but discussed under the _simplicitas Dei_, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 8, 4 sq.; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 3, artt. 1–2; _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 20; Petavius, _De Deo_ II, c. 1.
God’s spiritual essence is equally maintained and defended by Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed theologians, _Theol. Wirceb._ III 27–34; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 68; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ II, c. 8, sect. 1; Forbesius a Corse, _Instruct. histor. theol._, lib. 1, c. 36; Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ II, c. 6.
By this _spiritualitas Dei_ was understood that God was a substance of his own, distinct from the being of the world, and that this substance was immaterial, imperceptible to bodily senses, and without composition or extension.
The human being can form only some faint conception of the nature of such an immaterial substance from the analogy of the spiritual being he finds within himself.
But the essence of his own soul is positively unknown to the human being; he perceives within himself all kinds of psychical phenomena and from these concludes to a spiritual substance as their bearer, but this essence of the soul itself escapes his perception.
And even then the _spiritualitas Dei_ is of an entirely different and unique kind.
The designation spirit for God, angel, and soul is to be understood not _univoce_ and synonymously but _analogice_.
This is already evident from the fact that the spirit in angels and human beings is composite—not indeed of material parts, but nevertheless of _substantia_ and _accidentia_, of _potentia_ and _actus_—and is therefore subject to change.
In God, however, this composition is no more present than composition from bodily parts; he is pure being.
Further, this also comes clearly to light in the fact that God as πνευμα is not only the Πατηρ των πνευματων, Heb. 12:9, but just as much the Creator and Father of all visible things.
These too came into being from things that do not appear before the eyes, Heb. 11:3, and have their origin in God as πνευμα.
God as spiritual being is the author not only of everything among creatures called πνευμα and ψυχη, but also of everything called σωμα and σαρξ.
And thus _spiritualitas_ designates that perfection of God by which, negatively, in analogy with the spirit of angels and the human soul, he is to be conceived as immaterial and invisible, and also, positively, as the hidden, simple, absolute ground of all creaturely being, both somatic and pneumatic.
This was also perceived earlier, and hence some proposed that the spirits of angels and the souls of human beings should still be called bodies, while God alone would then be spirit in an entirely unique sense; cf. Augustine, _Epist._ 28; or that neither the predicate of corporeality nor that of incorporeality should be applied to God, since he is exalted above both, Petavius, _De Deo_ II, c. 1, §§14–16.
But this deserves no recommendation, because it promotes misunderstanding and also forgets that, although all knowledge of God is analogical, that analogy nevertheless emerges more clearly in one group of creatures than in another and speaks more strongly in the world of invisible things than in that of visible things.
It is equally incorrect, on the other hand, to locate, with Descartes, the essence of spirit in _cogitatio_, _De meth._, c. 4, and, with Cartesian theologians, to describe God’s essence as _res cogitans_, Wittichius, _Theol._, c. 15, §218, or as _perfectissima et purissima intellectio_, Burmannus, _Synopsis_ I 17, 4.
For thereby God’s spiritual nature is immediately identified with personality, as also occurred in Reinhard, _Dogm._ 111; Wegscheider, _Inst. theol._ §64; and others.
Pantheism therefore did not know what to do with this spiritual nature of God and turned it into a sound without meaning, a word without reality.
It is therefore important to maintain the _spiritualitas Dei_ as a designation of his _substantia incorporea_.
Personality, self-consciousness, and self-determination are not thereby automatically given—naturally they are in God, because he is a simple being, but not in the concept considered in itself.
Animal consciousness and unconscious representations in the human being sufficiently prove that _spiritualitas_ and _personalitas_ are not identical concepts.
That which is expressed in _personalitas_ will be discussed later in the doctrine of the _scientia_ and _voluntas Dei_.
Here, in connection with _spiritualitas_, the concern is to maintain God’s spiritual essence.
And this is of such great importance that the whole manner in which we must serve God rests upon it.
Worship in spirit and truth rests upon God’s spirituality.
Only through this is all image worship cut off in principle and forever.
