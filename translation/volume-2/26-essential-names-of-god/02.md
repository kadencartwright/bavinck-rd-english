## 2.

From God’s _aseitas_ his immutability follows of itself.
Yet this seems to find little support in Scripture.
For there God always stands in the most lively interaction with the world.
In the beginning he created heaven and earth and thus passed from not creating to creating.
And from that beginning he shares, as it were, in the life of the world and especially of his people Israel: he comes and goes, reveals and hides himself, turns his face away, and turns it toward them in grace.
He repents, Gen. 6:6; 1 Sam. 15:11; Amos 7:3, 6; Joel 2:13; Jonah 3:9; 4:2, and changes his intention, Ex. 32:10–14; Jonah 3:10.
He becomes angry, Num. 11:1, 10; Ps. 106:40; Zech. 10:3, and lays aside his anger, Deut. 13:17; 2 Chron. 12:12; 30:8; Jer. 18:8, 10; 26:3, 19; 36:3.
He is different toward the pious than toward the wicked, Prov. 11:20; 12:22; with the pure he shows himself pure, but with the crooked he shows himself a wrestler, Ps. 18:26, 27.
In the fullness of time he even becomes human in Christ and through the Holy Spirit takes up residence in the church; he rejects Israel and accepts the Gentiles.
And in the life of God’s children there remains a continual alternation between the sense of guilt and the consciousness of forgiveness, between experiences of God’s wrath and of his love, of his abandonment and of his nearness.
But at the same time that same Scripture testifies that under all this God is and remains the same.
Everything changes, but he endures; he remains who he is, Ps. 102:26–28.
He is YHWH, the One who is and always remains equal to himself; the first and, with the last, still the same God, Isa. 41:4; 43:10; 46:4; 48:12.
He is who he is, Deut. 32:39; cf. John 8:58; Heb. 13:8, ἀφθαρτος, ὁ μονος ἐχων ἀθανασιαν, always ὁ αἰτος, Rom. 1:23; 1 Tim. 1:17; 6:16; Heb. 1:11, 12.
And immutable in his existence and essence, he is also immutable in his thought and will, in all his purposes and decrees.
He is not a man that he should lie or repent.
What he says, he does, Num. 15:29; 1 Sam. 15:29.
His _charismata_ and calling are ἀμεταμελητα, Rom. 11:29.
He does not cast off his people, Rom. 11:1.
He completes what he began, Ps. 138:8; Phil. 1:6.
In a word, he, YHWH, does not change, Mal. 3:6; with him there is οὐκ ἐνι παραλλαγη ἢ τροπης ἀποσκιασμα, James 1:17.
On this Christian theology built the doctrine of the _immutabilitas Dei_.
Mythological theogony could not rise to this height, but philosophy repeatedly named and described God as the one, eternal, immutable, unmoved ruler of all things who remains equal to himself, Philolaus and others, in Zeller, _Phil. d. Gr._ I⁴ 425, 488 f.; II 928.
From the motion of the world Aristotle concluded to a _primum movens_, an αἰδιος οὐσια ἀκινητος, who was one and eternal, necessary, immutable, free of all composition, without any possibility, ὑλη, δυναμις, but solely ἐνεργεια, pure εἰδος, sheer being, absolute form, το τι ην εἰναι το πρωτον, ibid., 359–365.
Philo called God ἀτρεπτος, ἰσαιτατος ἑαυτῳ, ἀκλινης, παγιος, βεβαιος, ἀμεταβλητος, ἑστως, Dähne, _Gesch. Darst. der jüd.-alex. Rel. Philos._ I 118.
And Christian theology agreed with this.
According to Irenaeus, God is _semper idem, sibi aequalis et similis_, _Adv. haer._ IV, c. 11; cf. Origen, _C. Cels._ I 21; IV 14.
For Augustine, God’s immutability followed directly from the fact that he was the highest, perfect being.
In every rational creature it is _naturaliter et veraciter insitum, esse omnino incommutabilem et incorruptibilem Deum_, _De Gen. ad litt._ VII 11.
This concept of an eternal and immutable being cannot be obtained through the senses, for every creature, including the human being himself, is mutable; but inwardly the human being sees and finds that immutable reality which is better and greater than everything that changes, _De lib. arb._ II 6; _De doctr. chr._ I 9.
If God were not immutable, he would not be God, _Conf._ VII 4.
His name is _being_, and this name is a _nomen incommutabilitatis_.
Whatever changes ceases to be what it was.
But true being belongs only to him who does not change.
What truly is, remains: _Quod est, manet._
But what changes _fuit aliquid et aliquid erit; non tamen est, quia mutabile est_, _De trin._ V 2.
God, however, who is, cannot change, because every change would be a diminution of being.
And just as God is immutable in his essence, so he is also immutable in his knowing, willing, and decrees.
_Dei essentia, qua est, nihil mutabile habet, nec in aeternitate, nec in veritate, nec in voluntate_, _De trin._ IV, prooem.
As he is, so he knows and wills, in an immutable manner.
_Essentia tua scit et vult incommutabiliter et scientia tua est et vult incommutabiliter et tua voluntas est et scit incommutabiliter_, _Conf._ XIII 16.
Creation, revelation, incarnation, affections, and so forth brought about no change in God.
In God there never arose a _novum consilium_, _De ord._ II 17.
There has always been in God one immutable will.
_Una eademque sempiterna et immutabili voluntate res quas condidit, et ut prius non essent egit, quamdiu non fuerunt, et ut posterius essent, quando esse coeperunt_, _De civ._ XII, 17.
There is change only in the creature, from nonbeing to being, from good to evil, and so forth.
We then repeatedly find the same thought later in John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I, c. 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 9; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 8, 3; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 8, art. 2, qu. 1–2; Petavius, _De Deo_ III, c. 1–2; and likewise in Lutheran and Reformed theologians, Gerhard, _Loc._ II, cap. 8, sect. 5; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 77–83; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 13; cf. _Conf. Gall._, art. 1; _Conf. Belg._ 1; etc.

But this immutability of God met with much opposition, from both the deistic and the pantheistic sides.
In Epicurus the gods are entirely like excellent human beings who change in place, activity, thought, and so forth, Zeller IV 430 f.; and in the Stoa, as earlier in Heraclitus, the Deity as the immanent cause of the world was taken up into its unceasing change, ibid., IV 133 f.
The opposition to God’s immutability within Christian theology was of the same nature.
On the one side stand Pelagianism, Socinianism, Remonstrantism, and rationalism, which especially contest the immutability of God’s knowing and willing and make God’s will dependent upon, and thus cause it to change according to, human conduct.
Vorstius in particular contested God’s immutability in his work _De Deo et ejus attributis_.
He distinguished between God’s _essentia_, which is simple and immutable, and the _voluntas Dei_, which, as _libera_, neither wills everything eternally nor always wills the same, Dorner, _Gesammelte Schriften_, 278.
But the opposition to God’s immutability from the pantheistic side is much more serious.
Common to this opposition is that the idea of becoming is transferred to God and the boundary between Creator and creature is thereby completely erased.
The idea of God as _substantia_, as it occurs in Spinoza, proved to be an abstract concept without content.
To bring life into that concept, philosophy often replaced being with becoming.
Naturally, there is still a great difference here, according as one conceives this process by which God himself becomes unitarily or trinitarianly and allows it to unfold immanently in God’s essence or _transeunt_ in the world.
To this belong first of all Gnosticism; then the theosophy of the Kabbalah, of Böhme, Baader, Schelling, Rothe, Hamberger, and others, whose influence continued in the doctrine of kenosis; and finally the pantheistic philosophy of Fichte, Hegel, Schleiermacher, Schopenhauer, Hartmann, and others.
However differently elaborated, the fundamental thought is one.
God does not _exist_ but _becomes_.
_An sich_, in the first moment, he is βυθος ἀγνωστος, an abstract, purely potential being, mere nature, contentless thought, a dark impulse, a blind alogical will—in a word, a being that is nothing but can become everything.
But from that potential being God gradually brings himself to actuality in the form of a process.
He is his own Creator.
He produces himself.
He gradually arrives, whether in himself or in the world, at personality, self-consciousness, and spirit.
God is _causa sui_.
Under the influence of this philosophical idea of absolute becoming, God’s immutability has also repeatedly been denied or restricted in more recent theology, and God has with predilection been called _causa sui_, self-actualizing power, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ §§16–38; Martensen, _Chr. Dogm._, 1856, S. 86, 116; cf. _Chr. Ethik_ I 90 f.
Luthardt says, _Gott ist seine eigene That_, _Apol. Vortr. über die Grundwahrh. des Chr._, 1870, I 45; cf. Müller, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ II⁵ 171 f.
Others speak of a _Selbstsetzung Gottes_, Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ I 244, 245; Frank, _Syst. der chr. Wahrh._ I² 116 f.
In his essay _Ueber die richtige Fassung des dogm. Begriffs der Unveränderlichkeit Gottes_, _Ges. Schriften_, 1883, S. 188–377, Dorner seeks to avoid both deism and pantheism (acosmism) and to unite God’s immutability and _Lebendigkeit_ with each other.
He believes he attains this goal by locating God’s immutability in the ethical, S. 340 f.
Ethically God is immutable and always equal to himself; he remains holy love, S. 352.
But otherwise Dorner believes that creation, the incarnation, and satisfaction, and so forth, did bring about change in God, S. 309, 311 f., 358 f., 364; that he stands in a _Wechselverhältniss_ with the human being, S. 319, 362; that he knows reality only from the world, S. 320; that for him too there is a past, present, and future, S. 322; and that he becomes angry, justifies, and has a disposition corresponding to the human being, S. 324; etc.
And essentially the same is taught by Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I 573; F. A. B. Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 365; and others.
Many also do not express themselves concerning this important attribute in the doctrine of God but first make it evident, in connection with creation or incarnation or kenosis, and so forth, that they accept change in God, as, for example, Ebrard, Hofmann, Thomasius, and others.

Nevertheless, the doctrine of God’s immutability is of the highest importance for religion.
The distinction between Creator and creature lies in the contrast between being and becoming.
Everything that is creature is becoming.
It is mutable, continually strives, seeks rest and satisfaction, and finds that rest only in him who is pure being without becoming.
For this reason God is so often called the rock in Scripture, Deut. 32:4, 15, 18, 30, 31, 37; 1 Sam. 2:2; 2 Sam. 22:3, 32; Ps. 19:15; 31:3; 62:3, 7; 73:26; etc.
The human being can rely on him; he does not change in his essence, nor in his knowing or willing; he eternally remains who he is.
Every change is foreign to God.
In him there is no change of time, for he is eternal; nor of place, for he is omnipresent; nor of essence, for he is pure being.
Christian theology also repeatedly expressed this last point with the term _purus actus_.
Aristotle had conceived God’s essence in this way, as πρωτον εἰδος without any δυναμις, as absolute ἐνεργεια.
Scholasticism therefore began to speak of God as _actus purissimus et simplicissimus_, in order to indicate that he is perfect, absolute being without any _potentia_ toward nonbeing or toward being otherwise.
Thus Boethius says, _De cons. philos._, lib. III, that God does not change in essence (_forma_), _quia purus actus est_; cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 3.
For this reason the expression _causa sui_ was also avoided in speaking of God.
The idea of absolute becoming was first clearly expressed by Heraclitus and afterward repeatedly returned in philosophy.
Plotinus especially makes use of this concept, not only in application to matter but also to that which he regards as absolute being.
He teaches concerning God that he brought forth his own being, that he was active before he existed, Thilo, _Kurze pragm. Gesch. d. Philos._ I 352 f.
Christian theology did indeed speak of God as an _ens a se_, and thus of his _aseitas_.
And Lactantius, Synesius, and Jerome also used the expression _causa sui_; the last wrote: _Deus qui semper est nec habet aliunde principium et ipse sui origo est suaeque causa substantiae, non potest intelligi aliunde habere quod subsistit_, _Comm. ad Eph._, cap. 3:15.
But this expression was always understood to mean that God indeed existed of himself, not that he had become and been brought forth by himself, Klee, _Kath. Dogm._ II 43.
When Descartes later accorded God’s will priority over the understanding and made the _essentia_ of all things dependent upon that will, indeed even made God’s existence a product of his will, so that he said, _Deus se revera conservat_, God is _causa sui_ and _a se_ not in a negative but in a positive sense, God is _causa efficiens sui ipsius_, he has being _a reali potentiae immensitate_, _Resp. ad primas objectiones_, pp. 57, 58; then some followers did adopt this expression, such as Burmannus, _Synopsis theol._ I, c. 15, §2, but the Reformed theologians wished the expressions _causa sui_ and _existentia a se_ to be understood only in a purely negative sense, Ryssenius, _De oude rechtsinnige waerheyt_, etc., Middelburg, n.d., p. 24; Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ II, c. 3, §22; Moor I 590 sq.
A _causa sui_ in a positive sense is not possible, because the same thing would then be said at the same moment to exist insofar as it brings itself forth and not to exist insofar as it is brought forth.
Now it is understandable that monistic philosophy has taken refuge in this idea of absolute becoming in order to give at least the appearance of an explanation of reality.
But Herbart rightly subjected this idea to sharp criticism, and his followers Thilo, _Die Wissenschaftlichkeit der mod. Theol._, S. 25, and Flügel, _Die spekul. Theol. in der Gegenwart_, 1888, S. 201 f.; _Die Probleme der Philos. und ihre Lösungen_, 1888, S. 10 f., have not without reason expressed their amazement that this idea has found such favor in speculative theology.
There is, after all, nothing that can be done with it in theology as applied to the divine being.
Not only does Scripture testify that in God there is no change or shadow of turning, but thought leads to the same conclusion.
In the case of becoming, one must ask for a cause, because there is no becoming without a cause.
But being in the absolute sense no longer permits the question of a cause.
Absolute being is because it is.
The idea of God itself entails immutability.
No increase or diminution is conceivable in him.
He cannot change for the better or for the worse.
For he is absolute, perfect, true being.
Becoming is proper to the creature and is a form of change in space and time.
But God is who he is, eternally, far exalted above space and time, above every creature.
He rests in himself and is therefore the goal and resting point of all creatures, the rock of salvation whose work is perfect.
Whoever ascribes to God a change of essence, knowledge, or will impairs all his excellencies—his independence, simplicity, eternity, omniscience, and omnipotence.
He robs God of his divine nature and religion of its firm foundation and certain comfort.
Yet this immutability is no monotonous unity, no rigidly immobile being.
Scripture itself leads the way in describing God to us in the most manifold relations to all his creatures.
Immutable in himself, he nevertheless shares, as it were, in the life of his creatures and participates in their changes.
Scripture speaks of God in a human manner and can do no other.
But however humanly it speaks, at the same time it forbids us to posit any change in God himself.
There is change around him and outside him; there is change in the relation to him, but there is no change in God himself.
And precisely herein consists God’s incomprehensible greatness and at the same time the glory of the Christian confession: that, immutable in himself, God can nevertheless call mutable creatures into existence; that, eternal in himself, he can nevertheless enter into time; that, immeasurable in himself, he can nevertheless permeate every point of space with his being; that he who is absolute being can nevertheless also grant a place of its own to the creature that is becoming.
In God’s eternity no moment of time can be discovered, in his immensity no point of space, in his being no element of becoming.
But conversely, it is God who posits the creature, eternity that posits time, immensity that posits space, being that posits becoming, immutability that posits change.
There is no transition between the two; there is a deep chasm between God’s being and the being of every creature.
It is divine greatness that he can descend to the creature; that, transcendent, he can nevertheless dwell immanently in all that is created; that, preserving himself, he can give himself, and, absolutely maintaining his immutability, can nevertheless enter into an infinite number of relations with his creatures.
People sought to clarify this with several examples.
The sun remains the same whether it scorches or warms, causes pain or refreshes (Augustine).
A coin does not change, even though it is called now a prize and then a pledge (idem).
A pillar remains standing whether someone sees it now on his right hand and then on his left (Thomas).
An artist does not change when he gives form to his conception in word or tone, in voice or color, nor a scholar when he sets down his thoughts in a book.
All these comparisons limp, but they nevertheless give some idea of how something can remain the same in essence while changing in relation.
This applies all the more to God, since he, the immutable One, is himself the sole cause of everything that changes.
He does not place himself in relation to any creature as though it could exist in any way without him; rather, he himself places all things in those relations to himself that he eternally and immutably wills, and precisely so, in that manner and at that moment of time in which they actually occur.
No _prius_ or _posterius_ falls in God, but _in rebus prius non existentibus et posterius existentibus_, Augustine, _De civ._ XII 17.
It is immutable being itself that places mutable becoming outside itself with an order and law entirely its own and, as it were, brings it upon the stage before itself.
