## 13.

God’s glory also belongs to his holiness. Scripture uses for it the words כָּבוֹד and δοξα. כָּבוֹד, from the verb כבד, to be heavy, weighty, is the appearance of one who is weighty, eminent; alongside it הוֹד is also used, designating the glorious appearance of one whose name is widely spread, and הָדָר, which designates that appearance in its splendor and beauty, Delitzsch on Ps. 8:6. The Greek word for this in the LXX and New Testament is δοξα: the acknowledgment someone enjoys or to which he has a claim, subjectively therefore the acknowledgment due to or actually granted to someone, the fame or honor in which he shares, synonymous with τιμη and εὐλογια, Rev. 5:12, opposite to ἀτιμια, 2 Cor. 6:8; and objectively the appearance, form, aspect, magnificence, splendor, glory of a person or thing that presents itself, or the person or thing itself in its glorious appearance, and then related to εἰδος, εἰκων, μορφη, Isa. 53:2; 1 Cor. 11:7. The כָּבוֹד יהוה, δοξα του θεου, therefore signifies the splendor and glory inseparable from all God’s perfections and from his entire self-revelation in nature and above all in grace, the glorious form in which he everywhere appears over against creatures. This glory and majesty with which God is clothed and which characterizes all his doing, 1 Chron. 16:27; Pss. 29:4; 96:6; 104:1; 111:4; 113:4; etc., reveals itself in the whole creation, Ps. 8; Isa. 6:3, but is nevertheless seen especially in the domain of grace. It appears to Israel, Ex. 16:7, 10; 24:16; 33:18 f.; Lev. 9:6, 23; Num. 14:10; 16:19; etc.; Deut. 5:24. It filled the tabernacle and the temple, Ex. 40:34; 1 Kings 8:11, and communicated itself to the whole people, Ex. 29:43; Ezek. 16:14; etc. Above all it is beheld in Christ, the Only-Begotten, John 1:14, and through him in the congregation, Rom. 15:7; 2 Cor. 3:18, which awaits the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of the great God and of her Savior Jesus Christ, Titus 2:13. It is repeatedly connected with God’s glory, Ex. 29:43; Isa. 6:3, and therefore also described as a fire, Ex. 24:17; Lev. 9:24, and as a cloud, 1 Kings 8:10, 11; Isa. 6:4. Without doubt, by that cloud and fire Scripture thinks of sensibly perceptible, creaturely forms under which God’s presence made itself known; cf. Volume I, p. 249. It is otherwise, however, with the light to which God’s glory is frequently compared and under which it is repeatedly presented. Light is in Scripture the image of truth, holiness, and blessedness, Ps. 43:3; Isa. 10:17; Ps. 97:11. This comparison is so simple and natural that there is truly no need for the supposition that YHWH was originally a sun god, Kuenen, _G. v. I._ I 48 f., 240 f., 249, 267, any more than the designation _rock_ needs to point to an earlier period of stone worship, Kuenen, I 392–395. The light of the sun and the fire of heaven furnish the Israelite with the material for describing the perfections of YHWH, but he is clearly conscious that he is speaking in images. Just as thunder is his voice, Ps. 104:7; Amos 1:2; Isa. 30:30, so the light of nature is his garment, Ps. 104:2. What light is in nature—a source of knowledge, purity, joy—that God is in the spiritual domain. He is the light of the devout; his face, his Word, spreads light, Pss. 27:1; 44:4; 89:16; 119:105; only in his light do they see light, Ps. 36:10. He himself is pure light, without darkness, and the Father of all that is light, 1 John 1:5; 1 Tim. 6:16; James 1:17, and according to the promise, Isa. 9:1; 60:1, 19, 20; Mic. 7:8, appeared in Christ as the light, Matt. 4:16; Luke 2:32; John 1:4; 3:19; 8:12; 1 John 2:20, so that now his congregation is light in him, Matt. 5:14; Eph. 5:8; 1 Thess. 5:5, and goes to meet the full light, Rev. 21:23 f.; 22:5; Col. 1:12. Later the Jews understood this כָּבוֹד יהוה as a created, visible splendor, as a _Lichtleib_ through which he made his presence known in creation, and in the Shekinah they even conceived it as a personal subject, Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._, 1880, pp. 160, 179–184. From Jewish theology this opinion passed into theosophy. Böhme describes God’s glory as a _Leib des Geistes_, as a _Reich der Herrlichkeit Gottes, das ewige Himmelreich, worin die Kraft Gottes wesentlich ist, tingiert vom Glanz und Kraft des Feuers und des Lichts_, as an uncreated heaven, a paradise, Joh. Claassen, _Jakob Böhme, sein Leben und seine theos. Werke_ I 157, II 61; and so too Baader, Joh. Claassen, _Fr. v. Baaders Leben u. theos. Werke_ II 90 f.; Oetinger, _Die Theol. aus der Idee des Lebens_, ed. J. Hamberger, 1852, pp. 113 f., 117 f.; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._², p. 49 f.; Keerl, _Der Mensch das Ebenbild Gottes_ II 17 f., 113; idem, _Die Lehre des N. T. von der Herrlichkeit Gottes_, Basel, 1863. Among Lutheran theologians there was already formerly a controversy over whether God was called light in a proper (Dannhauer, Chemnitz) or improper (Musaeus and others) sense, M. Vitringa, _Doctr. chr. relig._ I 139. In the Eastern church the Council of Constantinople in 1431 even attached its approval to the doctrine of an uncreated, divine light that was distinguished from God’s essence, Kurtz, _Lehrb. der Kirchengesch._ §69, 2. Yet this opinion cannot be approved. Scripture clearly teaches God’s spirituality and invisibility. The acceptance of a glory of God as a place, מקום, a form, תמונה, εἰδος, μορφη, as a פנים, a body, a kingdom, a heaven that, even if uncreated, would nevertheless be distinguished from his essence, conflicts with the attributes named above and likewise with God’s simplicity. Even when Scripture speaks of God’s face, glory, and majesty, it speaks in images. But like all perfections, this one too comes to expression in his creatures. It is communicable. In the created there is a weak splendor of the ineffable glory and majesty God possesses. Just as creatures lead us to speak of God’s eternity and omnipresence, of his righteousness and grace, so they cause us to know something of God’s glory. Yet here too analogy is not identity. This emerges already in language. With creatures we speak of attractive, beautiful, fair; but for God’s beauty Scripture has a name of its own, that of glory. It is therefore also not advisable to speak with the church fathers, scholastics, and Roman Catholic theologians of the _pulchritudo Dei_. Augustine already spoke in this sense. He proceeded from the fundamental proposition: _Omne quod est, in quantum est, verum, bonum, pulchrum est._ Now in being, and thus also in the true, good, and beautiful, there is distinction, ranking, ascent. The more being something has, the more truth, goodness, and beauty it also has. Everything is beautiful in its kind. _Singula opera Dei . . . inveniuntur habere laudabiles mensuras et numeros et ordines in suo quaeque genere constituta_, _De Gen. c. Manich._ I 21. _Inest enim omnibus quoddam naturae sui generis decus_, _De Gen. ad litt._ III 14. All creatures therefore contribute to the beauty of the whole. But all creaturely beauty is perishable and mutable; creatures are not beautiful through themselves but by participation in a higher, absolute beauty. Question all creatures: _Respondent tibi omnia: ecce, vide, pulchra sumus. Pulchritudo eorum, confessio eorum_, _Serm._ 241. That highest beauty to which all creatures point is God. He is _summum esse, summum verum, summum bonum_, and also the highest immutable beauty. _Ista pulchra mutabilia quis fecit, nisi incommutabilis pulcher?_ Ibid. God is the highest beauty because in his essence there is absolute unity, measure, and order. Nothing is lacking in him, and there is nothing superfluous in him, _De ord._ I 26; II 51; _De beata vita_ 34; _C. Acad._ II 9; Ritter, _Gesch. der christl. Philos._ II 289 f. The influence of Neoplatonism cannot be denied in this conception of Augustine either, for it likewise regarded the deity as the highest beauty and the cause of all that is beautiful, Zeller, V² 483 f. Yet in this way this thought of Augustine found acceptance in Dionysius, _De div. nom._, c. 4, §7; Bonaventure, _Breviloquium_, pars I, c. 6; Petavius, _Theol. dogm._ VI, c. 8; Scheeben, _Kath. Dogm._ I 589 f.; etc. Protestant theologians, by contrast, preferred to speak of the _majestas_ and _gloria Dei_, Gerhard, _Loci theol._ I, c. 8, sect. 18; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 31; Mastricht, _Theor. pract. theol._ II, c. 22; _Synopsis pur. theol._ VI 43. In God’s glory his greatness and exaltedness come to expression, as they are so repeatedly portrayed in the psalms and prophets, Ps. 104; Isa. 40; Hab. 3. It is called greatness and exaltedness insofar as it awakens reverent admiration and worship in the creature. It is called glory insofar as it disposes to thanksgiving, praise, and honor. It is called majesty insofar as it stands in connection with his absolute _dignitas_ and demands submission from every creature.
