### E. God as Sovereign

## 15.

Finally, to all the virtues treated thus far must be added those that belong to God as Sovereign. God is the Creator and therefore the Owner, Possessor, and Lord of everything; nothing exists or possesses anything apart from him; he alone has absolute authority; his will always decides and decides about everything. Scripture therefore repeatedly speaks of this sovereign will, חֵפֶץ, צְבוּ רָצוֹן, Dan. 4:35; 6:18, θελημα, βουλημα. That will is the ultimate cause of all things, both of their being and of their being such as they are. Everything is derived from it: creation and preservation, Rev. 4:11; government, Prov. 21:1; Dan. 4:35; Eph. 1:11; the suffering of Christ, Luke 22:42; election and rejection, Rom. 9:15 f.; regeneration, James 1:18; sanctification, Phil. 2:13; the suffering of believers, 1 Pet. 3:17; our life and lot, James 4:15; Acts 18:21; Rom. 15:32; even the smallest and most insignificant things, Matt. 10:29; etc. Correspondingly, Christian theology also honored God’s will as the ultimate cause of all that exists and as the terminus of every contradiction. _Voluntas conditoris conditae rei cujusque natura est_, Augustine, _De civ._ XXI 8; _De trin._ III 6–9. Philosophy, however, has never been satisfied with this and has always sought another and deeper explanation of things. Plato tried to derive the visible world from the ideas, which were true being, but he did not succeed, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II⁴ 744–769. Aristotle conceived the deity as pure εἶδος, as νοησις νοησεως, which excluded all willing, all ποιησις and πραξις; and although he also recognized it as _primum movens_, the nature of motion and the relation between God and the world remained completely obscure for him, Zeller, ibid., III³ 357–384. In the Stoa this led to pantheism: God is the reason, spirit, and soul of the world, and the world is God’s body, garment, and appearance, Zeller, ibid., IV³ 133–149. Modern philosophy returned to this rationalism. Descartes saw in thinking the essence of spirit and the guarantee of being. By legitimate inference this led to Hegel’s philosophy of identity. The absolute is sheer thought, but not, as in Plato, a world of ideas, and just as little a genuine reality. It is thought without qualification, without any content; not being but becoming; actually nothing and potentially everything. From this principle, which was merely a logical abstraction, the world had to be explained. It soon became clear that this was impossible. Hegel left the problem just as unresolved as Plato and Aristotle. Multiplicity could not proceed from this abstract unity, nor being from this thought. Whatever semblance of truth it still possessed arose from a play on concepts. Hegel also called this absolute thought pure possibility. This logical possibility was, however, dialectically transformed into real power, into absolute δυναμις, and thus it indeed seemed capable of producing the universe. But this was a dialectical game that could not satisfy in the long run. Opposition to Hegel’s philosophy arose from various sides. Rationalism had had its day; idealism did not explain being; the primacy of the intellect went bankrupt; no existence, no world could be derived from absolute thought without will. Therefore an attempt was now made with the primacy of the will.

Schelling undertook this in his second period. He went back to the theosophy of Böhme and through him to the Kabbalah and Neoplatonism. Here Plotinus had already taught that God was _causa sui_, the product of his will and power, and that the will was therefore ultimate being and preceded the understanding. In the same way Schelling now made the will the ultimate principle of the existence both of infinite and of finite being. Already in his philosophical investigations concerning the essence of human freedom from the year 1809 he adopted this position. _Es gibt_, he said, _in der letzten und höchsten Instanz gar kein anderes Seyn als Wollen. Wollen ist Urseyn und auf dieses allein passen alle Prädicate desselben: Grundlosigkeit, Ewigkeit, Unabhängigkeit von der Zeit, Selbstbejahung. Die ganze Philosophie strebt nur dahin, diesen höchsten Ausdruck zu finden_, _Werke_ I 7, p. 350; cf. I 1, p. 401 f. In God and in creatures a distinction must be made between being insofar as it exists and being insofar as it is merely the ground of existence, ibid., p. 357. God has the ground of his existence in himself, that is, in a nature distinguished from God himself, ibid., p. 358. This nature is, as it were, that _Sehnsucht, die das ewige Eine empfindet, sich selbst zu gebären_; it is will, _aber Wille in dem kein Verstand ist, und darum auch nicht selbständiger und vollkommener Wille, indem der Verstand der Wille in dem Willen ist. Dennoch ist sie ein Willen des Verstandes, nämlich Sehnsucht und Begierde desselben; nicht ein bewusster, sondern ein ahnender Wille, dessen Ahndung der Verstand ist_, ibid., p. 359. But so it is now also with all things. They are something other than God and yet cannot exist outside God. This contradiction can be resolved only in this way: _dass die Dinge ihren Grund in dem haben, was in Gott selbst nicht Er selbst ist, d. h. in dem, was Grund seiner Existenz ist_, ibid., p. 359. Things have their ground in this dark nature, in this unconscious will of God. Order, regularity, and form can indeed now be observed in the world, just as in God too the light of spirit and personality arose out of the dark nature. _Aber immer liegt noch im Grunde das Regellose, als könnte es einmal wieder durchbrechen, und nirgends scheint es, als wären Ordnung und Form das Ursprüngliche, sondern als wäre ein anfänglich Regelloses zur Ordnung gebracht worden_, ibid., p. 359. Schelling derives the entire world-process from the opposition between nature and spirit, darkness and light, the real and the ideal principle, which together have been present in God himself from all eternity.

Through this position Schelling advanced beyond Hegel’s rationalism and became of the greatest importance for later philosophy. He did indeed develop further and in a theistic sense the thoughts he had already expressed in his investigation concerning human freedom in his later writings—the _Stuttgarter Privatvorlesungen_ of 1812, _Werke_ I 7, p. 417 f.; _Denkmal der Schrift von den göttlichen Dingen des Herrn F. H. Jacobi_ of 1812, _Werke_ I 8, p. 19 f.; and especially his _Philosophie der Offenbarung_, _Werke_ II, vols. 3 and 4. But he remained faithful to his primacy of the will and was followed in this by Schopenhauer and von Hartmann. It is true that for these philosophers the will is not will in the proper sense but nothing more than an unconscious desire, a blind urge, a dark nature. But this was in part acknowledged by them. Schelling expressly says that the will without understanding is not an independent and perfect will, _indem der Verstand der Wille in dem Willen ist_, _Werke_ I 7, p. 359. Schopenhauer did indeed reckon consciousness among the appearances of the will and considered it bound to individuality and brains, _Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung_ II⁶ 370, but nevertheless inserted between the will and the world of appearances the ideas that were the eternal forms and exemplars of things, ibid., I 154. And von Hartmann again speaks of the Unconscious as subject and as _überbewusst_, _Philos. des Unbew._ II⁹ 186. The existence of things is determined by the will, but their essence by the intellect, Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, pp. 57–62; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._ I 100 f.; II 435 f., 446 f. Yet even apart from this admission, their philosophy is of great significance for theism. It demonstrated the untenability of rationalistic and idealistic pantheism. God cannot be conceived without will, freedom, and power. It has indeed been objected that all willing was a desiring and striving and thus evidence of imperfection, dissatisfaction, and unrest, which cannot occur in God. Mysticism has therefore sung: _Wir beten, es geschehe, mein Herr und Gott, dein Wille; Und siehe, Er hat nicht Wille, Er ist eine ewige Stille_ (Angelus Silesius). But this objection rests on a mistaken conception of the will. Certainly, willing is often a striving for something not possessed; but when the will has obtained what was desired and rests and delights in it, that resting and delighting is also an act and activity of the will—indeed, its highest and most powerful activity; cf. later the doctrine of the will, and my _Beginselen der Psychologie_, 1897, p. 156 f. Such a will that rests and delights in what has been obtained also exists in creatures and is nothing other than love, which embraces its object and is blessed in it. If such rest and delight are not reckoned to the will, either they are unattainable for the creature, that is, no blessedness is possible for the creature, or, if that blessedness is nevertheless one day to be granted to the creature, it can consist in nothing other than the annihilation of the will, the stupefaction of consciousness, and the complete suppression of personality. In pantheistic mysticism, therefore, blessedness is conceived as the soul’s sinking, merging, and being swallowed up in God, that is, in a nirvana. But Scripture and Christian theology do not teach this. Blessedness does not kill the will but brings it to its highest activity, for love is the richest, most powerful energy of the will. Thus there is also a will in God. His willing is not a striving for a good that he would lack and without which he could not be blessed. For he is the All-Sufficient and All-Blessed in himself. He himself is the highest good, for his creatures and also for himself. He can rest in nothing other than himself; because he is God, he can be blessed only through and in himself. His love is self-love and therefore absolute, divine love. And this absolute self-love is nothing other than a willing of himself, the highest, absolute, divine energy of his will. The object of God’s will is therefore God himself. Not in the sense that he were the product of his will, that he had caused himself and was _causa sui_, cf. above, p. 126 f., for that would reintroduce becoming and striving, and thus imperfection, into God. Rather, God eternally wills himself with a _voluntas complacentiae_; he eternally loves himself with divine love and is perfectly blessed in himself. His will is _sapientissima ejus propensio in se ipsum tanquam summum bonum_; it is also no faculty or power in God, but subject, action, and object of that will are one with God’s being; θελημα θελητικον, θελησις, and θελημα θελητον coincide with his being, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 45; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 19, art. 1; _C. Gent._ I 72 sq.; Zanchius, _Op._ II 246; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 19; etc.

Scripture, however, also speaks of a will of God in relation to the created order. Just as God’s knowledge is twofold, _scientia necessaria_ and _libera_, so God’s will must also be distinguished as _propensio in se ipsum_ and as _propensio in creaturas_. But just as the _scientia libera_ does not make God dependent on creatures but is known to him from his own being, so the will of God that directs itself toward creatures must not be placed dualistically alongside the will that has his own being as its object. Scripture expressly teaches that God wills _alia a se_ and repeatedly speaks of his will in relation to creatures, but it adds, as it were in the same breath, that God does not will those creatures out of need but only for his own sake, for his name’s sake, Prov. 16:4. Creation must therefore not be conceived as an object that stands outside and over against him, which he lacks and strives to possess, or through which he hopes to obtain something he does not have. For from him and through him and also to him are all things. He does not find his end in creatures, but they find their end in him. _Quae extra se vult, ea etiam quodam modo sunt in Deo, in quo sunt omnia_, Zanchius, II 246. He does not will creatures because of something they are or that is in them, but he wills them for his own sake. He remains his own end. He never directs himself to creatures as such, but through them to himself. Going out from himself, he returns to himself. It is one _propensio in se ipsum tanquam summum finem et in creaturas propter se ut media_; his love for himself includes his love for creatures and through them returns to itself. Therefore his willing, also in relation to creatures, is never a striving for some good still to be obtained and thus is no sign of imperfection and wretchedness. Rather, his willing, always also in and through creatures, is absolute self-enjoyment, perfect blessedness, divine rest. Rest and activity are one in God; his self-sufficiency is at the same time absolute activity, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 19, art. 2; _C. Gent._ I 75, 76.
