## 10.

Finally, God’s truthfulness also belongs to the perfections of his understanding. The Hebrew words אמת, אמונה, adjective אמן, come from the verb אמן, to make firm, build, support; intransitively, to be firm; in the Hiphil, to hold fast to, trust in, be certain of something; and designate subjectively the holding fast to something, faith, πιστις, and objectively the firmness, reliability, truth of the person or thing upon which one relies. The Hebrew words are rendered in the LXX sometimes by ἀληθεια, ἐν ἀληθειᾳ, and sometimes by πιστοω, πιστευω, πιστις, πιστος, and thus in our language by true, truthful, faithful. In ordinary Greek, and so also in the LXX and New Testament, the concept ἀληθεια had too definite a meaning to render the Hebrew words sufficiently; it therefore had to be supplemented by the words πιστος, and so forth. The name YHWH already expresses that he remains the one he is. He is truth and without injustice, עָוֶל, perversity, crookedness, Deut. 32:4; Jer. 10:10; Ps. 31:6; 2 Chron. 15:3. This includes both that he is the true, real God over against the idols, which are הֲבָלִים, Deut. 32:21, and so forth, and that as such he at all times keeps and fulfills his words and promises, so that he is perfectly reliable. For he is not a man that he should lie or repent, Num. 23:19; 1 Sam. 15:29. All that proceeds from him bears the stamp of truth. His beneficence, חֶסֶד, and faithfulness are repeatedly spoken of, Gen. 24:49; 47:29; Josh. 2:14; 2 Sam. 2:6; 15:20; Ps. 40:11; his lovingkindness, חֶסֶד, and truth, Gen. 24:27; Ex. 34:6; Pss. 57:4; 61:8; 89:15; etc. His words, judgments, paths, works, commandments, and laws are all pure truth, 2 Sam. 7:28; Pss. 19:10; 25:10; 33:4; 111:7; 119:86, 142, 151; Dan. 4:37. His truth and faithfulness reveal themselves on earth so richly and gloriously that they reach to the clouds, Ps. 36:6; Ex. 34:6. He repeatedly confirms his Word by swearing by himself, Gen. 22:16, etc.; Heb. 6:13. He is therefore repeatedly called a rock who through his immutable firmness offers support to his favored ones, Deut. 32:4, 15, 18, 30, 37, and in many proper names, Num. 1:5, 6, 10; 3:35; 34:28, and further in 2 Sam. 22:3, 32; Pss. 18:3, 32; 19:15; 28:1; 31:3; 71:3; 144:1; Isa. 26:4. And as such a God of truth and faithfulness he keeps the covenant, Deut. 4:31; 7:9; Ps. 40:11; Hos. 12:1; etc., and is a perfectly reliable refuge for all his people, Pss. 31:6; 36:6 f.; 43:3; 54:7; 57:4; 71:22; 96:13; 143:1; 146:6; etc. Likewise, in the New Testament he is called the ἀληθινος θεος; that is, only that God is the true, real God who revealed himself in Christ, John 17:3; 1 John 5:20. And all that he reveals is pure truth. He is a θεος ἀληθης in contrast to all human beings, John 3:33; Rom. 3:4. His Word is the truth, his gospel is truth, Christ is the truth, John 14:6; 17:17; Eph. 1:13. Indeed, he is now still the one he always was. The New Testament is the fulfillment and confirmation of his promises in the days of the Old Covenant. He has remembered his covenant and oath, Luke 1:68–73. He is faithful, πιστος, in that he is and remains the God of the covenant and grants salvation perfectly, 1 Cor. 1:9; 10:13; 1 Thess. 5:24; 2 Thess. 3:3; Heb. 10:23; 11:11; 1 John 1:9. He cannot deny himself, 2 Tim. 2:13. All his promises are yes and amen in Christ, 2 Cor. 1:18, 20. Christ is ὁ μαρτυς ὁ πιστος, Rev. 1:5; 3:14; 19:11. And therefore he is and can be the immutable object of our πιστις. Cf. Wendt, “Der Gebrauch der Wörter ἀληθεια, ἀληθης und ἀληθινος im N. T.,” _Stud. u. Krit._, 1883, pp. 511–547; Cremer, _Wörterbuch_, 1895, pp. 109–126.

Scripture thus uses the word _truth_ in different senses. Philosophy too usually distinguishes a threefold concept of truth, namely _veritas in essendo_ (_in rebus_), _in significando_ (_in verbis_), _in cognoscendo_ (_in intellectu_), or metaphysical, ethical, and logical truth, Liberatore, _Inst. philos._, ed. 8, Rome, 1855, I 70 sq.; Schmid, “Der Begriff des Wahren,” in Gutberlet’s _Philos. Jahrbuch_, 1893, pp. 35–48, 140–150; Mastricht, _Theol._ II, 14, 5; Moor, _Comm. in Marck_ I 676. Metaphysical, ontological truth consists in the fact that some object, person, or thing is all that belongs to its essence. Thus gold that not only looks like gold but really is gold is genuine, true, real gold. Truth in this sense stands over against falsehood, spuriousness, vanity, nonbeing. It is an attribute of all being as being; it is one with substance. Augustine especially spoke frequently of truth in this meaning. All being as such is true and beautiful and good. There is indeed rich diversity in the measure of being; there is a difference of degree among creatures. Yet all things have received being from God _pro suo modo_ and participate in the divine being; cf., e.g., _De civ._ XI 23; _De nat. boni c. Manich._, c. 19. But from this creaturely being Augustine ascends to God. In Scripture God is called the true one over against the idols, which are vanity. And so in Augustine God is the true, one, simple, immutable, eternal being. Compared with his being, the being of creatures is to be regarded as nonbeing. God is _summum esse, summa veritas, summum bonum_. He is pure _essentia_. He does not have truth, but is truth. _O veritas quae vere es_, _Conf._ X 41; VII 10; XII 25. Further, God is also truth in the second sense named above, that is, in the ethical sense. By _veritas ethica_ is understood the agreement between someone’s essence and his revelation, in word or deed. Whoever says otherwise than he thinks is untruthful, a liar. Truth in this sense stands over against the lie. Now in God there is perfect agreement between his being and his revelation, Num. 23:19; 1 Sam. 15:29; Titus 1:2; Heb. 6:18. It is impossible for God to lie or deny himself. Finally, God is also truth in the logical sense. This consists in the agreement of thought with reality, _conformitas_ or _adaequatio intellectus et rei_. Our concepts are true when they are pure imprints of reality. In this sense truth stands over against error. God is also truth because he knows all things as they really are. His knowing is correct, immutable, adequate. Indeed, in his knowing he is truth itself, just as in his being he is ontological truth. God’s knowledge is living, absolute, adequate truth. It was not obtained from investigation and reflection concerning things but is essential in God and precedes things. It is one with God’s essence and is thus substantial truth. Therefore his Word, his law, his gospel are also pure truth. They are all as they ought to be. These three meanings of the concept _truth_ are indeed distinct, but they are nevertheless also one. This unity consists in the fact that truth in all three meanings is always agreement between thought and being, between ideal and real being. God is truth in the metaphysical sense, for he is the unity of thought and being. He is perfectly conscious of himself. He is God in the true sense, fully answering to the idea of God that is in himself. He is truth in the ethical sense, for he reveals himself, speaks, acts, and appears as he truly is and thinks. And he is truth in the logical sense, for he thinks things as they are—or rather, things are as he thinks them. He is truth in the full, absolute sense. And therefore he is the _prima veritas_, original truth, the source of all truth, the truth in all truth; the ground of truth, of the true being of all things, of their knowability and thinkability; the ideal and archetype of all truth, of all ethical being, of all rules and laws by which the essence and revelation of all things must be judged and normed; the source and origin of all knowledge of truth in every domain, the light in which alone we can see light, the sun of spirits. _Te invoco Deus veritas, in quo et a quo et per quem vera sunt, quae vera sunt omnia_, Augustine, _Solil._ I 1. Cf. further Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 16 and 17; I, 2, qu. 3, art. 7; _S. c. Gent._ I, capp. 59–62; III, c. 51; Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 8, art. 1; Scheeben, _Kath. Dogm._ I 578 f., 663 f.; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._ II, c. 8, sect. 16; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 27; Zanchius, _Op._ II, cols. 226–242; L. Meyer, _Verhand. over de Godd. eigensch._ IV, pp. 1–88.
