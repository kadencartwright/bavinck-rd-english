## 14.

Finally, to God as the Holy One belongs the virtue of righteousness. The words צַדִּיק, צֶדֶק, צְדָקָה designate the state of someone who conforms to a law. The primary meaning appears to be forensic: צַדִּיק is the one who is in the right in a proceeding before the judge and must therefore be acquitted, הִצְדִּיק, opposite הִרְשִׁיעַ, Deut. 25:1. It is also the word for one who is right in a dispute or war of words, Job 11:2; 33:12, 32; Isa. 41:26; and the substantive can therefore also designate the correctness or truth of an assertion or statement, Ps. 52:5; Prov. 16:13; Isa. 45:23. Next, it signifies in general that someone is in the right, even when no proceeding or court is involved; that the person has right on his side, is righteous and good, and conforms to the law, Gen. 30:33; 38:26; 1 Sam. 24:18; Ps. 15:2. From here it is also transferred to the religious sphere and applied to God. In the Pentateuch God is still called צַדִּיק only twice, Ex. 9:27; Deut. 32:4. God’s righteousness first comes to expression in history, in his government of the world and his direction of Israel, and is therefore developed most of all by the psalmists and prophets. It reveals itself throughout the whole world, even among the animals of the field, Ps. 36:7. God is Judge of the whole earth, Gen. 18:25. It consists in God repaying each according to his work, treating the righteous and the wicked differently, Gen. 18:25. But it is now remarkable that the one side of this righteousness, namely, that according to which God punishes the wicked, the _justitia vindicativa_, comes much less to the foreground in Scripture than the other, according to which he vindicates the righteous. Diestel, in his article “Die Idee der Gerechtigkeit vorzüglich im A. T.,” _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ V, 1860, issue 2, pp. 173–253, rightly drew attention to this and found agreement among many, especially Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II² 102–119. The thing itself that was later expressed in dogmatics as _justitia vindicativa_ is not absent. On the contrary, God by no means holds the guilty innocent, Ex. 20:7; Nah. 1:3. He does not spare, Ezek. 7:4, 9, 27; 8:18; 9:10. He shows no partiality and accepts no gift, Deut. 10:17, and judges impartially, Job 13:6–12; 22:2–4; 34:10–12; 35:6, 7. He is righteous and all his judgments are right, Pss. 119:137; 129:4; the punishment of the wicked is also connected more than once with his righteousness, Ex. 6:5; 7:4; Pss. 7:12; 9:5–9; 28:4; 62:13; 73; 96:10, 13; 2 Chron. 12:5–7; Neh. 9:33; Lam. 1:18; Isa. 5:16; 10:22; Dan. 9:14; Rom. 2:5; 2 Thess. 1:5–10. Nevertheless, it is true that the punishment of the wicked is usually derived from God’s wrath and that righteousness in Scripture appears above all as a principle of salvation for God’s people. God’s wrath is designated in Hebrew by many words: אַף, הָרוֹן, כַּעַס, זַעַם, רֹגֶז, קֶצֶף, usually translated by wrath or anger; חֵמָה, usually rendered by fury; עֶבְרָם, usually translated by indignation; and in the LXX and New Testament by θυμος, inward wrath, and ὀργη, wrath revealing itself outwardly, joined together in Rom. 2:8. This wrath—whose root words are partly connected with the concept of burning and partly express a violent, irrepressible movement of feeling—is frequently compared to a fire, Lev. 10:6; Deut. 32:22; Ps. 21:10; a conflagration, Deut. 32:22; 2 Kings 23:26; Ps. 2:12; Isa. 30:27; Jer. 15:14; 17:4; and is therefore called hot and fierce, Ps. 58:10; Deut. 13:17; 2 Chron. 28:11; Job 20:23; Isa. 13:9, 13, and smoking, Deut. 29:30; Ps. 74:1. It is aroused and kindled by Israel’s theocratic sins against God’s covenant, such as breach of oath, Josh. 9:20; profanation of the service of God, Lev. 10:6; Num. 1:53; 16:46; 18:5; idolatry, Deut. 9:19; the sin of Manasseh, 2 Kings 23:26; the sin of David, 1 Chron. 27:24; and above all by the sins of which the people made themselves guilty and through which they deserved all manner of punishments, Isa. 42:24, 25; Jer. 7:20; 21:5; 32:31; etc.; Lam. 2:2 f.; 3:43; Ezek. 5:13 f.; 7:3; 13:13; etc.; Zech. 7:12 f. This wrath is terrible, Ps. 76:8, and produces terror, Pss. 2:5; 90:7; pain, Job 21:17; Ps. 102:11; punishment, Pss. 6:2; 38:2; Jer. 10:24; devastation, Jer. 42:18; 2 Chron. 29:8; etc.; cf. Job 9:5; Pss. 21:10; 56:8; 85:5. According to Deut. 6:15; 29:20; 32:21; Job 16:9; Nah. 1:2, hatred, vengeance, and jealousy are related to this wrath. Hatred almost always has sinful deeds as its object, Deut. 16:22; Ps. 45:8; Prov. 6:16; Jer. 44:4; Hos. 9:15; Amos 5:12; Zech. 8:17; Rev. 2:6, and only a few times sinful persons, Ps. 5:6; Mal. 1:3; Rom. 9:13. The vengeance נְקָמָה, ἐκδικησις, ascribed to God, Nah. 1:2; 1 Thess. 4:6, and expressly reserved to him, Deut. 32:35; Rom. 12:19; Heb. 10:30, does indeed sometimes reveal itself now in judgment and retribution, Num. 31:2, 3; Judg. 5:2; 11:36; 16:28; 2 Sam. 4:8; 22:48; Pss. 18:48; 99:8, but will first reveal itself in all its power in the future, on the day of vengeance, Deut. 32:41, 42; Pss. 94:1; 149:7; Isa. 34:8; 35:4; 59:17; 61:2, 4; Jer. 46:10; 50:15, 28; 51:11; Ezek. 25:14 f.; Mic. 5:14. God’s jealousy, קִנְאָה, ζηλος, which occurs repeatedly, Ex. 20:9; 34:14; Deut. 4:24; 5:9; 6:15; Josh. 24:19; Nah. 1:2, is aroused because Israel, YHWH’s bride, violates his rights as bridegroom and husband by whoring after other gods, Deut. 32:16, 21; 2 Kings 14:22; Ps. 78:58; Ezek. 8:3, 5, and reveals itself in YHWH’s in turn provoking Israel to jealousy by choosing another people, Deut. 32:21; Ps. 79:5; Ezek. 5:13; 16:38; 23:25; Rom. 10:19. Alongside all these virtues, God’s righteousness is now usually taken _in bonam partem_ and described from that side according to which it vindicates the righteous and elevates them to honor and salvation. In this above all consists the צְדָקָה of YHWH: that he acknowledges the righteousness of the righteous, brings it to light, and causes it to triumph. He is righteous because he appoints salvation for the devout: he establishes them, Ps. 7:10; assists them, 31:2; answers them, 65:6; hears them, 143:1; delivers them, 143:11; makes them alive, 119:40; acquits them, 34:23; does them justice, 35:23; etc., while the wicked do not attain to his righteousness, 69:28, 29. The righteousness of YHWH therefore forms no contrast with his steadfast love, as wrath does, Ps. 69:25 f., but is related and synonymous with it, Pss. 22:32; 33:5; 35:28; 40:11; 51:16; 89:15; 145:7; Isa. 45:21; Jer. 9:24; Hos. 2:18; Zech. 9:9. The demonstration of God’s righteousness is at the same time a demonstration of his grace, Pss. 97:11, 12; 112:4; 116:5; 119:15–19. Even the forgiveness of sins is owed to that righteousness of God, Ps. 51:16; 103:17; 1 John 1:9. The revelations of that righteousness, צִדְקוֹת, are therefore saving benefits, deeds of rescue and deliverance, Judg. 5:11; 1 Sam. 12:7; Ps. 103:6; Isa. 45:24, 25; Mic. 6:5. Especially in Isaiah does this soteriological character of God’s righteousness come strikingly to expression. Israel is indeed a sinful people and has therefore also been severely punished, 43:26; 48:1; 53:11; 57:12; 59:4; 64:5, but nevertheless Israel is in the right over against the Gentiles; despite all its transgressions, it has a righteous cause; in the end, right is on its side. When it has been chastised enough, God’s righteousness will therefore awaken, acknowledge Israel in this its right, and deliver it from all its misery, 40:1 f.; 54:5, 7 f.; 57:15 f.; 61:1 f.; etc. And so it is with all the devout. Personally they are sinners, guilty of all manner of unrighteousness, and are a poor and afflicted people. But they advocate a righteous cause, trust in the Lord, and rely on God to do them justice, plead their cause, and crown them with his salvation, Pss. 17:1 f.; 18:21, 22; 34:16; 103:6; 140:13. In the New Testament too, God’s δικαιοσυνη consists particularly in this: that in Christ it provides a means of atonement through which he both proves himself δικαιος and can δικαιουν τον ἐκ πιστεως, and further grants his own forgiveness, 1 John 1:9, and appoints salvation, John 17:25; 2 Tim. 4:8. More still, in the end even wrath and jealousy, hatred and vengeance are made serviceable to the rescue and deliverance of his people. His wrath is fulfilled after a little time, Pss. 30:6; 78:38; 85:4; 103:9; Isa. 10:25; 48:9; 51:22; 54:8; Jer. 3:12 f.; 32:37; Ezek. 43:7–9; Dan. 9:16; Hos. 14:5; Mic. 5:18, and his jealousy against Israel comes to an end, Ezek. 16:42; 36:6 f.; Zech. 8:2 f. Then his wrath and jealousy and vengeance will turn against the enemies of his people on the great day of wrath and vengeance, Deut. 32:41, 42; Isa. 13:2 f.; 26:11; 30:27 f.; 34:8; 35:4; 42:11; 59:17; 61:2, 4; 63:3 f.; Jer. 10:25; 46:10; 50:15, 28; 51:11; Lam. 3:66; Ezek. 25:14 f.; 38:19; 39:25; Mic. 5:14; Nah. 1:2; Hab. 3:12; Zeph. 1:15 f.; 2:2; etc., and thereby serve as blessing and deliverance for Israel, 2 Kings 19:31; Isa. 9:6; 37:32; Joel 2:18; Zech. 1:14; 8:2. Likewise the New Testament says that although God’s wrath already rests on the wicked now, John 3:36; Eph. 2:3; 1 Thess. 2:16, that wrath in all its terror will first be revealed in the future, Matt. 3:7; Luke 3:7; 21:23; Rom. 5:9; 1 Thess. 1:10; 5:9; Eph. 5:6; 3:6; Rev. 6:16, 17; 11:18; 14:10; 16:19; 19:15. In dogmatics the concept usually acquired a broader sense. Sometimes it was taken so broadly that it also included God’s perfection or holiness; righteousness was then virtue itself and the sum of all virtues, consisting in God in perfect conformity with himself, _justitia divina_. Usually, however, following Aristotle it was understood in a narrower sense. In his ethics he described it as ἀρετη, δι’ ἡν τα αὑτων ἑκαστοι ἐχουσιν. It is possible only in a society of beings who can possess a larger or smaller quantity of goods; it does not exist among the gods, because there is no measure for their possessions, nor among irrational creatures, because among them there is no question of property. _Justitia_ therefore presupposes, first, that rights exist that are granted by the lawgiver, _justitia dominica_, _legislativa_, _dispositiva_; next, that those rights are mutually respected in agreements and contracts, _justitia commutativa_; and finally, that the rights that exist are maintained, _justitia distributiva_, whether by reward, _justitia remunerativa_, or by punishment, _justitia vindicativa_. In all these parts righteousness was _constans et perpetua voluntas jus suum cuique tribuendi_. All of this was transferred to God, and thus the concept of righteousness acquired a much broader sense in dogmatics than in Scripture. There is now no overriding objection to this, provided that the difference in usage is kept in view, for the matters themselves that are treated under the _justitia Dei_ in dogmatics all occur clearly in Holy Scripture. There is even an advantage attached to it, because it offers opportunity to uphold God’s righteousness in its whole extent against its opponents. The Gnostics in general, and especially Marcion, made a sharp contrast between law and gospel, works and faith, flesh and spirit, and likewise between the God of wrath, vengeance, and righteousness who revealed himself in the Old Testament and the God of love and grace who made himself known in Christ in the New Testament, Harnack, _D. G._ I 228 f. Later, essentially in the same sense, righteousness—particularly punitive righteousness—was denied to God by many as conflicting with his love. Now many difficulties arise in connection with the _justitia Dei_. No law can be conceived above God to which he has to conform, for his will is the supreme law; creatures have no rights over against him, for they have received everything from him and have repaid him nothing; they can make no claim to reward, for even when they have done everything they were obligated to do, they are unprofitable servants; and nothing in his nature seems to compel him to punish. Why could he, who is the Almighty, not forgive without satisfaction or punishment? In order, however, not to stray too far from the biblical concept of righteousness, it is preferable to discuss all these questions not here but presently under God’s will and freedom. Righteousness in Holy Scripture is not an attribute of God’s _dominium absolutum_ but rests on a moral foundation. Though a creature by nature can have no rights over against God, Rom. 11:35; 1 Cor. 4:7, and there is no question of a _justitia commutativa_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 21, art. 1, it is nevertheless God himself who, as it were, gives his creatures rights. Every creature received a nature of its own in creation; there are laws and ordinances for all created things; there are rights enclosed in the existence and nature of all that is. Above all, such rights have been given to rational creatures, and among these again for all the domains in which they move: for understanding and heart, soul and body, art and science, family and society, religion and morality. And when those rights have been forfeited and sinned away by humanity, God establishes with Noah a _foedus naturae_ and with Abraham a _foedus gratiae_, in which he once again graciously accords his creatures all kinds of rights and binds himself under oath to uphold those rights. Thus by God’s grace an entire legal order has been established, both in nature and grace, with all kinds of statutes, ordinances, and laws that he himself upholds and causes to rule. In Scripture, however, these statutes and rights are not derived from God’s righteousness—which righteousness, after all, would obligate him to do so?—but from his holiness and grace. This is certainly more correct than designating them by the name _justitia legislativa_. Yet that is not wrong, provided it is not thereby thought that by virtue of some righteousness God gave and had to give all these rights to his creatures. The truth in it, however, is precisely that God is the Supreme Lawgiver and that the whole legal order in every domain is rooted in him. Every right, whatever it may be, has its final and deepest ground not in a _contrat social_, nor in an independent natural law, nor in history, but only in God’s will—and in that will conceived not as _dominium absolutum_ but as a will of goodness and grace. God’s grace is the source of every right. Further, God also upholds this legal order in every domain of life; he who is righteousness itself and the source of every right is also the _judex_, the _vindex justitiae_. His _justitia legislativa_ includes the _justitia judicialis_. Right is no right when it is not upheld, if necessary with coercion and punishment. The latter is admittedly not given of itself with the legal order. Without sin the legal order would nevertheless have existed, but it would have been obeyed without coercion, voluntarily and in love, by every creature. It is sin that compels the law, in accordance with its nature, to cause itself to be respected with force and coercion. Not the law itself, but indeed the character of coercion that it must now bear, has been made necessary by sin. Yet this character of coercion is so little accidental or arbitrary that now no right without that character can be conceived, and our own conscience itself bears witness to it. The moral order is so little in conflict with the legal order that it instead bears, requires, and supports it. Right is an important part of morality. Righteousness is precisely the way in which God’s grace and love are maintained and raised to triumph. Those who with Marcion posit an opposition between righteousness and grace fail to recognize the connection between the moral and legal orders and do not understand the majesty and glory of right. The _justitia Dei_ must therefore by its nature be a _justitia judicialis_ and thus, on the one hand, a _justitia remunerativa_ and, on the other, a _justitia vindicativa_. This is not as though the creature could ever claim any reward of itself or could not in itself receive forgiveness without punishment; but God owes it to his covenant, to the right once instituted by him, to his name and honor, to bring his people to salvation and punish the wicked. Only in this way can right come to dominion and triumph.
