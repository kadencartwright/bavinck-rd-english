## 3.

God’s immutability is called eternity with respect to time and omnipresence with respect to space.
Both were sometimes comprehended under the name _infinitas Dei_.
The concept of infinity, however, is in itself unclear.
First, it can be understood negatively in the sense of endlessness.
Something is then called infinite because it in fact has no end, though it could have one.
God has frequently been called infinite in this sense in philosophy.
Neoplatonism conceived God in this sense as without boundary and form, without any determinacy, _οὐ πεπερασμενος, ὁρος οἰκ ἐχων, ἀπειρος_, and so forth, an overflowing fullness from which the world emanated, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ V 485 f., 497.
In the same sense the Kabbalah spoke of God as _אֵין טוֹף_, the infinite one, without boundary and form, but who in the ten _sefiroth_ created a transition from the infinite to the finite, Frank, _La Kabbale_, 179.
Later Spinoza’s philosophy gave currency to this concept of God’s infinity.
In Spinoza substance, God, is not a being of its own distinct from the world; it is that which constitutes reality in creatures and is therefore of itself infinite, _ens absolute infinitum_, _Eth._ I prop. 8.
Every determination is therefore negation, a privation, a lack of existence, _Epist._ 50 and 41; God, however, is free from every limitation and boundary; he is _substantia non determinata_.
Extension is one of his attributes.
In Hegel the concept of infinity acquired yet another meaning because he conceived Spinoza’s substance not as an eternally immutable being but as an absolute becoming.
God was therefore called infinite because he could become everything, in approximately the same way as the _ἀπειρον_ of Anaximander, which is itself indeterminate but can produce all sorts of things.
The error of this conception consists in identifying the most universal, which the intellect obtains by abstraction from finite being, with the infinite.
For it was precisely the endeavor of the philosophy of identity to have the particular proceed from the universal, the determinate from the indeterminate, and the finite from the endless by means of a process.
God is _an sich infinitus potentiâ_; he becomes _finitus_, personal, conscious, determinate, in creatures as his revelation.
But this conception is untenable.
Infinite is not a negative but a positive concept; it indicates not that God has no essence of his own distinct from creatures but that he is limited by nothing finite and creaturely.
Now such a creaturely limitation can naturally be denied of God in various ways.
If one means that God _non tempore finiri potest_, then _infinitas_ coincides with _aeternitas_.
If one wishes to indicate that God _non loco finiri potest_, then _infinitas_ is the same as _omnipraesentia_.
And the _infinitas Dei_ is also frequently defined in this way, Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II c. 10, 11; Zanchi, _Op._ II 90.
But _infinitas_ can also be understood in the sense that God is infinite in his perfections, that every perfection is present in him in an absolute manner; then _infinitas_ is equivalent to _perfectio_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 7.
But even then the infinity of God must be understood correctly.
God’s infinity is not an _infinitas magnitudinis_, as one sometimes speaks of the infinite or endless magnitude of the world, for God is not a body and has no extension.
Nor is it an _infinitas multitudinis_, as _mathesis_ speaks of the infinitely small and the infinitely great, for God is one and simple.
Rather, it is an _infinitas essentiae_.
God is infinite in his essence, absolutely perfect, infinite in an intensive, qualitative, positive sense.
But understood in this way, God’s infinity coincides completely with his perfection and need not be discussed separately.

Infinity in the sense of not being determined by time is the eternity of God.
Scripture nowhere speaks of a beginning or an end of God’s existence.
Although he is frequently portrayed very vividly as entering into time, he is nevertheless exalted above it.
He is the First and the Last, Isa. 41:4; Rev. 1:8, existing before the world, Gen. 1:1; John 1:1; 17:5, 24, and remaining despite every change, Ps. 102:27, 28.
He is God from eternity to eternity, Ps. 90:2; 93:2.
There is no searching out the number of his years, Job 36:26.
A thousand years are as little to him as yesterday is to us, Ps. 90:4; 2 Pet. 3:8.
He is the eternal God, Isa. 40:28; Rom. 16:26, dwelling in eternity, Isa. 57:15, living forever, Deut. 32:40; Rev. 10:6; 15:7, who swears by his life, Num. 14:21, 28, the _θεος ζων και μενων_, 1 Pet. 1:23, incorruptible, Rom. 1:23, immortal, 1 Tim. 6:16, who is and was and shall be, Ex. 3:14; Rev. 1:4, 8.
To be sure, here too Scripture speaks of God in a human manner and of eternity in the forms of time, but at the same time it nevertheless indicates clearly that God is exalted above time and cannot be measured or defined by that standard.
The deism of earlier and later times, however, understands eternity as time extended endlessly forward and backward; the difference between time and eternity lies only in quantity, not in quality; it is gradual, not essential.
The distinction does not lie in eternity’s having no succession of moments but in its being without beginning and end.
Past, present, and future exist not only for the human being but also for and in God.
So taught the Socinians, Socinus, _Prael. theol._ c. 8; Crell, _De Deo et ejus attributis_ c. 18; _Catech. Racov._ qu. 60; cf. Fock, _Der Socinianismus_, S. 427 f.; likewise Reinhard, _Dogm._, S. 106; Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ § 63; Dorner, _Glaub._ I 132; _Ges. Schriften_, 322, and so forth.
From the other side, eternity and time are likewise mixed by pantheism.
God and world are related here as _natura naturans_ and _natura naturata_, Spinoza, _Eth._ I prop. 29.
Eternity is not essentially distinguished from time but is the substance, the immanent cause of time, and time is the _modus_, the _accidens_ of eternity, just as the motion of waves is the phenomenal form of the ocean; God himself is drawn down into the stream of time and only in time attains his full reality, Spinoza, _Cog. Metaph._ I c. 4, II c. 1; Hegel, _Encycl. der philos. Wiss._ § 257 f.; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbew._ I⁹, S. 281 f., 298 f.; Biedermann, _Dogm._ II 518 f.
Strauss expressed it clearly in his _Glaub._ I 562: _Ewigkeit und Zeit verhalten sich wie die Substanz und ihre Accidentien_.
And Schleiermacher cautiously defined God’s eternity as _seine mit allem Zeitlichen auch die Zeit selbst bedingende schlechthin zeitlose Ursächlichkeit Gottes_.
Christian theology must now avoid both deism and pantheism also with respect to this perfection of God.
Naturally, the difference between eternity and time does also lie in the fact that time has, or at least can have, a beginning and end, while eternity does not.
But the whole difference is not exhausted in this.
There are three _notae_ of the concept eternity, namely, that it excludes _principium, finem_, and also _successionem_.
God is _ἀγεννητος, ἀφθαρτος_, but also _immutabilis_, Petavius, _de Deo_ III c. 3 § 6.
Between eternity and time there is a difference not only in quantity and degree but also in quality and essence.
For Aristotle already observed that time, though it was not identical with motion, nevertheless stood in the closest connection with motion, with becoming, that is, with the transition from _potentia_ to _actus_, although he believed that motion in the world without a beginning was conceivable.
Augustine expressed this in another manner by saying that there is time only where the present becomes past and the future becomes present.
_Quid est ergo tempus? Si nemo ex me quaerat, scio; si quaerenti explicare velim, nescio; fidenter tamen dico, scire me. Quod si nihil praeteriret, non esset praeteritum tempus, et si nihil adveniret, non esset futurum tempus, et si nihil esset, non esset praesens tempus_, _Conf._ XI 14.
Time is not a substance alongside other substances, no _reale quid_, but a mode of existence of things.
If there were no creatures, there would be no time.
Time began _a creatura_ much more than _creatura coepit a tempore_, Augustine, _de Gen. ad litt._ V 5; _de civ._ XI 6.
On the other hand, time is also not merely a subjective form of perception, as Kant thought, _Kr. d. r. V._ 81 f.
There is indeed truth in this too, and Augustine reasoned that in order to measure and calculate time, a thinking spirit is indeed needed that retains the past by memory, stands in the present, and awaits the future, and that to this extent measures the times within itself, _Conf._ XI 23-28.
Augustine did not thereby deny, however, that there would be measurable and divisible motion of things if there were no thinking spirit to count and measure it.
But a distinction must be made between external and internal time, _tempus extrinsecum_ and _intrinsecum_.
By external time we understand the standard by which we measure motion.
This standard is in a certain sense accidental and arbitrary.
We derive it from the motion of the heavenly bodies, which is constant and known to all human beings, Gen. 1:14 f.
This time will one day cease, Rev. 10:6; 21:23 f.
But the case is different with internal time.
This is that mode of existence of things by which they have a past, present, and future, and thus parts that can be measured or counted by whatever standard.
What can be measured or counted is subject to measure and number and thereby limited, for there is always another measure or number greater than what has been measured or counted.
The essence of time therefore lies not in its being finite or endless _a parte ante_ and _a parte post_, but in its containing a succession of moments, in there being an earlier, a now, and a later within it.
But from this it follows that time, _tempus intrinsecum_, is the mode of existence of everything created and belongs essentially to everything finite.
Whoever says time says motion, change, measurability, countability, limitation, finitude, creature.
Time is the duration of creaturely being: _tempus est mensura motus in re mobili_.
But for that very reason there can be no time in God.
He is who he is from eternity to eternity.
In him there is no change or shadow of turning.
He is not a becoming but an eternal being.
He has no beginning and no end, but also no earlier and later.
He cannot be measured or counted in his duration.
A thousand years are to him as one day.
He is the eternal _ἐγω εἰμι_, John 8:58.
The eternity of God is therefore to be conceived rather as an eternal present, without past or future.
_Omne praesens est apud Deum. Hodiernus tuus (dies est) aeternitas. Aeternitas ipsa Dei substantia est, quae nihil habet mutabile_, Augustine, _Conf._ XI 10-13; _de vera relig._ c. 49.
Boethius defined God’s eternity by saying that he _interminabilis vitae plenitudinem totam pariter comprehendit ac possidet_, _de cons. philos._ V, and Thomas as _interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio_, _S. Theol._ I qu. 10 art. 1.
And so speak all the theologians: Tertullian, _adv. Marc._ I 8; Gregory of Nyssa, _adv. Eunomium_ I; Gregory of Nazianzus, _orat._ 38, 11; Dionysius, _de div. nom._ c. 5 § 4; Anselm, _Monol._ c. 18-24; _Prosl._ c. 18; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 8, 2; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 10; Petavius, _de Deo_ III c. 3-6; Gerhard, _Loci_ II c. 8; Zanchi, _Op._ II 73-77; Alting, _Theol. problem._, loc. 3, probl. 20, and so forth.
Yet God’s eternity may not therefore be conceived as an eternally stationary, immovable moment of time.
Rather, it is one with God’s essence and therefore a fullness of being.
God is not only eternal, but he himself is _sua aeternitas_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 10 art. 2.
Its analogy is found not in the empty existence of the human being for whom, through idleness or boredom, pain or anxiety, minutes stretch into hours and the days do not pass but crawl, but rather in the rich, full life of the human being for whom, through labor and enjoyment, time as it were does not exist and the hours and days fly by.
Therefore there is truth in the assertion that in hell there is no eternity but only time, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 10 art. 3 ad 3, and that the more a creature is akin to God and is his image, the more it loses the imperfection of time and approaches eternity, ib., artt. 4 and 5.
Therefore God’s eternity also does not stand abstractly and transcendently above time but is present and immanent in every moment of time.
There is an essential difference between eternity and time, but there is nevertheless also analogy and kinship, so that the former can dwell and work in the latter.
Time was created with things; it has no origin in itself; an eternal time, a time without beginning, is inconceivable.
God, the eternal one, is the sole absolute cause of time.
But that time also has no subsistence in itself; it is a continual becoming and must rest in an immutable being.
It is God who with his eternal power upholds time in its entirety and in every moment.
God permeates time and every moment of time with his eternity.
In every second the pulse of eternity beats.
God therefore stands in relation to time.
With his eternity he enters into time.
Time is also objective for him.
In his eternal consciousness he knows the whole of time and the succession of all its moments.
He himself does not thereby become temporal or subject to time, measure, and number; he remains eternal and dwells in eternity.
But he uses time to bring his eternal thoughts and perfections to revelation within it; he makes time serviceable to eternity and thereby proves himself to be the _βασιλευς των αἰωνων, Rex seculorum_, 1 Tim. 1:17.
