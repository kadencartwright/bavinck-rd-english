## 7.

From this _spiritualitas Dei_ his _invisibilitas_ follows directly. Scripture teaches this expressly, above, p. 145 f. And in the Christian church there was therefore little disagreement over whether God could be beheld with bodily eyes. This was generally denied both for the present dispensation and for that of glory; only a few—the Audians, the Socinians, Vorstius, and also some Lutherans, such as Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 554 sq., 566; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 458; Hulsemann, Majus, Jaeger, and others—affirmed it and also appealed to the Reformed Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 849, and Bucanus, _Instit. theol._, 1648, p. 443. Gerhard, _Loc._ XXXI §144, however, left it undecided; and Baier, _Comp._ I, c. 6, and Buddeus, _Inst. theol._ II, c. 3, §4, restricted it to _cognitio intellectualis_. Otherwise all taught that God, as spirit, was invisible to bodily senses, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 6; IV 20; Origen, _C. Cels._ VII 33 sq.; Augustine, _De civ._ XXII 29; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 12, art. 3, ad 3; Martyr, _Loci_, pp. 8, 9; Zanchius, _Op._ I 225; etc. If there can be any seeing of God, it is possible only with the eyes of the soul or spirit. This could, however, raise the question whether a vision of God is possible directly, without the medium of creatures, face to face. Can God, as scholasticism put it, be seen _ut est in se per essentiam, in substantia vel essentia sua_? It was now generally taught that such a vision of God _per essentiam_ was in any case possible only in the _status gloriae_. Here on earth such a sight of God was unattainable for anyone. At most, by a special grace and only for a moment, it had been granted to Moses, Ex. 34, Isaiah, ch. 6, and Paul, 2 Cor. 12, Augustine, _Epist._ 112; _De Gen. ad litt._ XII 27; Basil, _Hexaem._, hom. 1; Hilary, _De trin._ V. But by far the majority judged, also with respect to the vision of God that had fallen to patriarchs, prophets, and apostles, that it had always occurred mediately—through a cloud, in some sign, under one form or another, Petavius, _De Deo_ VII, c. 12. Strictly speaking, therefore, only the question whether there was a _visio Dei per essentiam_ in the _status gloriae_ was under discussion. The church fathers do indeed speak frequently of a sight of God reserved for believers in heaven, for example, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV 20; Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ V, c. 1; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Orat._ 34; Augustine, _De civ._ XXII 29; _De Gen. ad litt._ XII 26, but they do not mention a sight of God _per essentiam_. They say only with Scripture that the blessed will see God, not through a mirror, but face to face. And they add that God is invisible but that he can make himself visible and reveal himself to humanity, Irenaeus, ibid. _In potestate nostra non est eum videre, sed in potestate illius est apparere_, Ambrose, in Petavius, VII, c. 7. Others therefore also denied that God would be beheld _per essentiam_ hereafter, such as Chrysostom, Gregory of Nyssa, Cyril of Jerusalem, Theodoret, Jerome, Isidore, and others. The Roman Catholic theologians are embarrassed by the statements of these fathers. Vásquez candidly acknowledged: _hos patres, si sincere loqui volumus, vix possumus in bonum sensum interpretari_. But others try to weaken these statements and explain them as dealing with the bodily vision of God, or the vision of God in this life, or the absolute knowledge of God, which even in heaven is and remains unattainable, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 12, art. 1, ad 1; _Sent._ IV, dist. 49, qu. 1, art. 1, ad 1; Petavius, _De Deo_ VII, c. 5; _Theol. Wirceb._ III, pp. 82–86. Gradually, however, under the influence of Neoplatonic mysticism, which worked upon Pseudo-Dionysius, another conception of the _visio Dei_ arose. It was now understood as a beholding of God in his being, _ut in se est_. God no longer descended to humanity; rather, humanity was raised up to God by a supernatural gift and thus deified. Gregory the Great, _Moral._, l. 18, c. 28, already said that God will be seen not only in his glory but also in his nature, for _ipsa ei natura sua claritas, ipsa claritas natura est_. Prosper, _De vita contempl._ 1, c. 4, spoke of the blessed as _sui creatoris substantiam mundis cordibus contemplantes_. Bernard, _Serm._ 4 _in festum omnium sanctorum_, says that God will be seen in all his creatures but that the _trinitas_ will also be seen _in semetipsa_. And the Council of Florence declared that souls _in coelum mox recipi et intueri clare ipsum Deum trinum et unum sicuti est, pro meritorum tamen diversitate alium alio perfectius_, cf. Denzinger, _Enchir. symb. et defin._, nos. 403, 456, 588, 870, 875. Scholasticism found here ample material for all kinds of speculation and made abundant use of it. In connection with the doctrine of the _dona superaddita_, it was taught that humanity, with its natural gifts, was incapable of this vision of God, but that a _divinum auxilium_ was needed, which supplemented, elevated, and perfected the natural gifts. This supernatural aid was usually designated by the name _lumen gloriae_. But great disagreement arose over the nature of this _lumen_: whether it was objective or subjective; whether it was the Word or the Spirit, a gift of the Holy Spirit or the Spirit himself, although it was usually assumed to be _aliquid creatum, intellectui datum in ratione habitus_. Otherwise it became the general teaching that God in his being was the object of the vision and that the blessed therefore saw in God everything that could not be separated from his being, thus both the attributes and the persons. But the Thomists claimed that the essence could not be beheld without the persons, nor one attribute without the other, while the nominalists and Scotists maintained the contrary. Further, in everyone’s opinion, God himself and all that was _formaliter_ in him, that is, that belonged to his being, was the object of the vision; but there was again disagreement whether this also included what was _eminenter_ in him, that is, what is called into existence by God in accordance with his decrees. Usually this was maintained in order thereby to justify the invocation of angels and the blessed, who saw in God everything that would happen and thus knew the needs of believers on earth. Even though it was added that the _visio Dei_ was not _comprehensio_ and differed among the blessed according to the measure of their merits, this doctrine nevertheless issued in the deification of humanity. On the ground of a supernatural gift, the creature raises itself upward by its own merits and becomes like God, Lombard, _Sent._ IV, dist. 49; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 12; I 2, qu. 3, especially art. 12; qu. 4, art. 2; _S. c. Gent._ III, c. 38–63; _Sent._ IV, dist. 49, qu. 1, 2; Bonaventure, _Brev._ VII, c. 7; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacr._, l. 2, pars 18, c. 16; Petavius, _De Deo_ VII, c. 1–14; Becanus, _Theol. Schol._ I, c. 9; _Theol. Wirceb._ III, pp. 67–90; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ III, pp. 266–276; Jansen, _Prael. theol. dogm._ III, pp. 913–935; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ III, pp. 23–44; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorz._ II, pp. 122–134; Scheeben, _Dogm._ I 562 f.; II 294 f.

The Reformation adopted varying positions toward this _visio Dei_. The Lutherans inclined to affirm a _visio Dei per essentiam_, and one that was not only _mentalis_ but even _corporalis_. God’s power was so great that he could grant the blessed an illumination by which they could see him in his being even with the eyes of the body, above, p. 151. Some Reformed theologians too considered such a _visio Dei per essentiam_ not impossible, Bullinger on John 1:18; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, pp. 9, 518; _Synopsis pur. theol._ LII 11–24; Forbes of Corse, _De visione beatifica_, _Op._, Amsterdam, 1703, I 282–289; etc. But most, holding to the glory surpassing all knowledge that is prepared for believers, set aside the _spinosae quaestiones_ of scholasticism or rejected the _visio Dei per essentiam_ altogether, Calvin, _Inst._ III 25, 11; Pareus on 1 Cor. 13:12; Rivetus on Ps. 16:11, _Op._ II 68; Danaeus on 1 Tim. 6:16; Gomarus on John 1:18, _Op._, pp. 249–252; _Maccovius redivivus_, _Theol. pol._ 162; Hoornbeek, _Instit. theol._ XVI 20; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XX, qu. 8; Heidegger, _Corpus theol._ III 37; XXVIII 138; Moor VI 720; M. Vitringa IV 18–25; especially Voetius, _Disp._ II 1193–1217; etc.; cf. also Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV, sect. 2, c. 4; Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ VI 13, 6. This modesty is certainly in accord with Scripture, which does speak of a vision of God by the blessed but explains nothing further concerning it and elsewhere expressly calls God invisible. The vision awaiting believers is called by Paul a knowing as we are known. If God, as all acknowledge, cannot be known perfectly in his being but is incomprehensible, neither can he be seen in his being. _Visio per essentiam_ and _comprehensio_ are, after all, entirely the same. Moreover, God is infinite and humanity remains finite, even in the _status gloriae_. God can therefore always be seen by humanity only in a finite, human manner. Thus the _objectum_ may be _infinitum_, but the _repraesentatio_ in human consciousness is and remains _finita_. But then the seeing of God is not _per essentiam_ either. From God’s side a συγκαταβασις, a revelation by which he descends to us from his side and makes himself knowable to us, is therefore always necessary; Matt. 11:27 remains in force even in glory. The vision of God in his being would entail that humanity was deified and the boundary between Creator and creature erased. This does indeed lie in the line of Neoplatonic mysticism, which Rome adopted, but not in that of the Reformation, and certainly not in that of the Reformed church and theology. For in Rome humanity is raised above its own nature by the _donum supernaturale_; thereby it actually becomes another being, a _homo divinus et supernaturalis_, Bellarmine, _De justif._ V 12. But however highly and gloriously the Reformed thought of the _status gloriae_, there too humanity nevertheless remained humanity, indeed raised _supra gradum suum naturalem_, but not _supra speciem suam et objectum illi analogum_, Turretin, _Th. El._ XX, qu. 8, 12. Human blessedness certainly lies in the _visio Dei beatifica_, but always in such a _visio_ as the finite and limited human nature is capable of. A θεωσις such as Rome teaches fits within the system of the Pseudo-Dionysian hierarchy but finds no support in Scripture; cf. later the locus on the image of God, and also Stuckert, “Vom Schauen Gottes,” _Zeits. f. Th. u. K._, ed. Gottschick, VI 6, pp. 492–544.
