# § 26. The Essential Names of God

### A. God as the One Who Is

## 1.

In some theologians the _locus de trinitate_ precedes that concerning the perfections of God: John of Damascus, _de fide orthod._ I 6 sq.; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 2 sq.; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ I c. 2 sq.; Martyr, _Loci Comm._, p. 36 sq., and so forth; and Frank even has serious objections to the reverse order, _Syst. der chr. Wahrh._ I 151 f.
If treating the perfections of God before the doctrine of the Trinity were intended to ascend gradually from _theologia naturalis_ to _theologia revelata_ and from the natural to the Christian concept of God, it would undoubtedly deserve disapproval.
But this is by no means the case.
In the doctrine of God’s perfections, the divine nature is treated as it is revealed to us in Scripture, confessed by Christian faith, and, as will presently become evident in the _locus de trinitate_, exists in a threefold manner.
In order to understand in that _locus_ that Father, Son, and Spirit partake of the same divine nature, it is necessary to know what that divine nature contains in itself and how it differs from every created nature.
Scripture also precedes us in this.
God’s essence is taught to us there earlier and more clearly than his trinitarian existence; the Trinity first comes to clear revelation in the New Testament; the names YHWH and Elohim precede those of Father, Son, and Spirit.
The first thing Scripture makes known to us about God is that he has a being and life of his own, distinct from all creatures, subsistent and independent.
He has an essence of his own, a _φυσις, natura, substantia, essentia_, not in distinction from his perfections but coming to meet us and becoming knowable in all his perfections and excellencies.
He bears proper names that belong to no creature.
Among these the name YHWH stands foremost, Ex. 3:14.
By this name he is designated as the one who is and will be what he was, who eternally remains the same in relation to his people.
He exists of himself.
He existed before all things, and they exist only through him, Ps. 90:2; 1 Cor. 8:6; Rev. 4:11.
He is _אָדוֹן, κυριος, δεσποτης_ in the absolute sense, Lord of the whole earth, Ex. 23:17; Deut. 10:17; Josh. 3:13.
He is from no one, while everything depends upon him, Rom. 11:36.
He kills and makes alive, creates light and darkness, peace and evil, Deut. 32:39; Isa. 45:5-7; 54:16.
He does according to his will with the host of heaven and the inhabitants of the earth, Dan. 4:35, so that human beings are in his hand like clay in the potter’s hand, Isa. 64:8; Jer. 18:1 f.; Rom. 9:21.
His counsel, his good pleasure, is the final ground of all that exists and occurs, Ps. 33:11; Prov. 19:21; Isa. 46:10; Matt. 11:26; Acts 2:23; 4:28; Eph. 1:5, 9, 11.
He therefore also does everything for his own sake, for the sake of his name and his glory, Deut. 32:27; Josh. 7:9; 1 Sam. 12:22; Ps. 25:11; 31:4; 79:9; 106:8; 109:21; 143:11; Prov. 16:4; Isa. 48:9; Jer. 14:7, 21; Ezek. 20:9, 14, 22, 44.
He also needs nothing and is all-sufficient, Job 22:2, 3; Ps. 50:19 f.; Acts 17:25, and has life in himself, John 5:26.
Thus he is the first and the last, the alpha and the omega, who is and who was and who is to come, Isa. 41:4; 44:6; 48:12; Rev. 1:8, and so forth; absolutely independent, not only in his existence but consequently also in all his perfections and excellencies, in all his decrees and deeds.
He is independent in his understanding, Rom. 11:34, 35; in his will, Dan. 4:35; Rom. 9:19; Eph. 1:5; Rev. 4:11; in his counsel, Ps. 33:11; Isa. 46:10; in his love, Hos. 14:5; in his power, Ps. 115:3, and so forth.
And thus, all-sufficient in himself and receiving nothing from outside himself, he is instead the sole source of all being and life, of all light and love, the abundant fountain of all good things, Ps. 36:10; Acts 17:25.
This independence of God is acknowledged to some extent by all human beings.
The pagans indeed draw the divine down into the creaturely and teach a theogony, but behind and above their gods they nevertheless frequently assume a power to which everything is subject in the absolute sense.
Many speak of nature, chance, fate, or fortune as a power exalted above everything, and philosophers prefer to designate God by the name the Absolute.
In Christian theology this perfection of God bore the name _αὐυταρκεια, aseitas, omnisufficientia, independentia, magnitudo_.
In the East theologians spoke of _θεος ἀναρχος, ἀναιτιος, ἀγεννητος_ and preferred to call God _αὐτογεννητος, αὐτοφυης, αὐτουσιος, αὐτοθεος, αὐτοφως, αὐτοσοφια, αὐτοαρετη, αὐταγαθος_, and so forth; cf. Suicerus, _Thes. Eccl._, s.v.
God is all that he is through himself.
He is _ipsa per se bonitas, sanctitas, sapientia, vita, lux, veritas_, and so forth.
As was said earlier, in their description of God the church fathers, following Philo, usually proceeded from the name YHWH.
This was his essential name preeminently.
God was the One Who Is.
Everything is enclosed in this name: I will be who I will be.
All God’s other perfections were derived from it.
God is _summum esse, summum bonum, summum verum, summum pulchrum_.
He is the perfect, the highest and best being, _quo esse aut cogitari melius nihil possit_.
He has the whole of being in himself; he is an infinite and boundless sea of being.
_Si bonum, si magnum, si beatum, si sapientem vel quidquid tale de Deo dixeris, in hoc verbo instauratur quod est Est. Nempe hoc est ei esse, quod haec omnia esse. Si et centum talia addas, non recessisti ab esse; si ea dixeris, nihil addixisti; si non dixeris nihil minuisti_, Bernard, _de consid._ I c. 6.
Scholasticism followed this: Anselm, _Monol._ 6; Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 2 art. 3, qu. 3, and qu. 13 art. 11; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 16 sq.; and it also treated this perfection under the name _infinitas_ or _spiritualis magnitudo Dei_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 7; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 43, or under that of the _aseitas Dei_, by which it was indicated that God as _summa substantia per se ipsam aut ex se ipsa est quidquid est_, Anselm, _Monol._ 6.
Later Roman Catholic theologians likewise usually proceed from this _aseitas_ or _independentia_, Petavius, _de Deo_ I c. 6; _Theol. Wirceb._ III p. 38 sq.; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II p. 88-90; Jansen, _Prael. theol. dogm._ II 26 sq.
The Reformation brought no change in this.
Luther too, on the ground of the name YHWH, defines God as the absolutely existent one; he is pure being.
Yet Luther does not dwell long upon abstract, metaphysical descriptions and soon passes from the _Deus absconditus_ to the _Deus revelatus_ in Christ, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 302 f.
In his _Loci_, Melanchthon defines God as _essentia spiritualis_.
The Lutherans mostly adopted this definition but then frequently added the further qualification _infinita, a se subsistens_, or _independens_, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. Luth. K._ § 17.
Among the Reformed this perfection of God comes still more strongly to the foreground, although the name _aseitas_ is later usually exchanged for that of _independentia_.
_Aseitas_ expresses only God’s independence in his existence, but _independentia_ has a broader sense and includes that God is independent in everything—in his being, in his perfections, in his decrees, and in his works.
While earlier theologians therefore usually proceed from the name YHWH, Hyperius, _Meth. Theol._, pp. 87, 135; Sohnius, _Op._ II 48, III 261; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, p. 135, and so forth, later _independentia_ occurs mostly as the first of the incommunicable attributes, Mastricht, _Theol._ II c. 3; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ III § 30; Maresius, _Syst. theol._, loc. 2 § 17; Marck, _Merg der Godg._ IV § 20; L. Meyer, _Verhand. over de Godd. eigensch._, 1783, I, pp. 39-110, and so forth.
Now when in Scripture God ascribes this _aseitas_ to himself, he thereby makes himself known as absolute being, as the One Who Is in the absolute sense.
Through this perfection he is immediately, essentially, and absolutely distinguished from every creature.
For every creature is not _a se_ but _ab alio_, and therefore is and has nothing of itself; it is absolutely dependent in its origin and thus also in its entire subsequent existence and development.
But as this name shows, God alone is of himself, _a se_, not in the sense that he caused himself, but as the one who from eternity to eternity is who he is—being, not becoming, absolute being, the fullness of being—and therefore also eternally and absolutely independent in his existence, in his perfections, and in all his works, the first and the last, the sole cause and final goal of all things.
In this _aseitas_ of God all the other perfections are enclosed; they are given with it and are its rich, many-sided unfolding.
And yet, while in this perfection the immeasurable distinction between Creator and creature comes clearly and plainly to light all at once, there is nevertheless a faint likeness of this perfection of God in every creature.
Pantheism cannot acknowledge this, but theism confesses that the creature, although absolutely dependent, nevertheless has a being of its own, distinct from God.
And in this being there is implanted persistence in existence, the _conatus in suo esse perseverandi_, the _Selbsterhaltungstrieb_, the drive to self-preservation.
Every creature, insofar as it exists, fears death; and even the smallest atom resists every attempt at annihilation.
It is a shadow of the independent, immutable being of our God.
