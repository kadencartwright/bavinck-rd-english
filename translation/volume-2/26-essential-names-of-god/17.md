## 17.

Creation, however, raises still more and other difficulties for the doctrine of God’s will. Just as God and the world are distinct, so that for us God’s will divides into _propensio in se_ and _in creaturas_, so too within the world there is again every sort of distinction among creatures. And that distinction rests on a different relation in which God places himself, with his being, knowing, and willing, toward creatures. God does not will everything in the same way, in the same sense, with the same energy of his being, for then no diversity among creatures would be possible; everything would be a monotonous uniformity. But while he wills all creatures _propter se ut media_, he wills these more than those, according to the measure in which they are more direct and suitable means to his glorification. God is a Father to all his creatures, but he is so in a special sense to his children. His affection for all he created stands below that for the church, and that for the church below his affection for Christ, the Son of his good pleasure. A _providentia generalis_, _specialis_, and _specialissima_ are distinguished. So too, within God’s will directed toward creatures, we again make as many distinctions as there are creatures. For the _voluntas Dei libera_ is as rich as that whole world. It must therefore not be conceived as an indifferent potency, as a blind power, but as rich, full, living, divine ενεργεια, the fountainhead of all the rich life that creation displays to us. But in that world there is one thing that causes special difficulty for the doctrine of God’s will, and that is evil, both as _malum culpae_ and as _malum poenae_, in the ethical and in the physical sense. However completely evil may stand under God’s government, it nevertheless cannot in the same sense and in the same way be the object of his will as the good. In view of these entirely different and directly opposed objects, we must again make distinctions within God’s will itself. Scripture leads us in this. There is a great difference between the will of God that prescribes what we are to do, Matt. 7:21; 12:50; John 4:34; 7:17; Rom. 12:2, and the will of God that says what he does and will do, Ps. 115:3; Dan. 4:17, 25, 32, 35; Rom. 9:18, 19; Eph. 1:5, 9, 11; Rev. 4:11. The prayer that God’s will may be done, Matt. 6:10, has an entirely different meaning from the childlike submission, “Your will be done,” Matt. 26:42; Acts 21:14. And so in history we repeatedly see this will of God appearing in two senses. God commands Abraham to sacrifice his son and yet does not let it happen, Gen. 22. He wills that Pharaoh let Israel go and yet hardens his heart so that he does not do so, Ex. 4:21. He has Hezekiah told that he will die and yet adds fifteen years to his life, Isa. 38:1, 5. He forbids the condemnation of the innocent, and yet Jesus was delivered up according to God’s determinate counsel and foreknowledge, Acts 2:23; 3:18; 4:28. God does not will sin; he is far from wickedness; he strictly forbids and punishes it, and yet it exists and stands under his government, Ex. 4:21; Josh. 11:20; 1 Sam. 2:25; 2 Sam. 16:10; Acts 2:23; 4:28; Rom. 1:24, 26; 2 Thess. 2:11; etc. He wills the salvation of all, Ezek. 18:23, 32; 33:11; 1 Tim. 2:4; 2 Pet. 3:9, and yet has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills, Rom. 9:18. The distinction between this twofold will of God arose early in theology. Tertullian already speaks of a hidden, higher will and a lower or lesser will, _De exhort._ 2 sq. Augustine points out that God often fulfills his good will through the evil will of human beings, _Enchir._, c. 101. Later this twofold will was given the names _voluntas εὐδοκιας_, _beneplaciti_, _arcana_, _decernens_, _decretiva_, and _voluntas εὐαρεστιας_, _signi_, _revelata_, _praecipiens_. The name _voluntas signi_ is derived from the fact that this will _significat quid Deo gratum sit et nostri sit officii_ and is known by us from the five _signa_: _praeceptio_, _prohibitio_, _consilium_, _permissio_, _operatio_. Developed at length by scholasticism, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 45; Hugh of St. Victor, _De Sacram._, lib. 1, pars 4; idem, _Summa Sent._, tract. 1, c. 13; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 19, arts. 11, 12; etc., it was generally adopted by Roman Catholic theology, Petavius, _De Deo_, lib. V, c. 4, 6; _Theol. Wirceb._ III 160; Perrone II 199; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 156; etc. But it was treated with special predilection in Reformed theology, Calvin, _Inst._ I, c. 18, §§3, 4; Musculus, _Loci Comm._, p. 978 sq.; Martyr, _Loci Comm._, p. 63b sq.; Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, p. 237 sq.; Zanchius, _Op._ II 251 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ II, c. 19; etc.; cf. Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. ref. K._ I 359–368; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._ 68 f. Alongside it arose still other distinctions in God’s will, especially that into _voluntas antecedens_ and _consequens_, which occurs already in Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 11; Chrysostom, _Ep. ad Eph._ 1:5; and John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ II, c. 29; and that into _voluntas absoluta_ and _conditionata_, _efficax_ and _inefficax_, which is already found in Augustine, _Enchir._, c. 102 sq.; _De civ._ XXII, cc. 1 and 2. These distinctions too can be understood well, namely in this sense: that God antecedently and conditionally wills many things—for example, the salvation of all human beings—that he nevertheless consequently and absolutely does not will and therefore does not cause to happen. Zanchius, at the place cited, therefore also says that all these distinctions amount to the same thing; Hyperius, _Meth. theol._, pp. 155–159; Walaeus, _Op._ I 180; Voetius, _Disp._ V 88; etc., judge similarly. But although Luther in his book _De servo arbitrio_ had made a very sharp distinction between the _Deus absconditus_ and _revelatus_, the Lutherans rejected this distinction into _voluntas beneplaciti_ and _signi_, at least in the Calvinistic sense, Gerhard, _Loc._ 2, c. 8, sect. 15; Baier, _Comp. theol. pos._ I, c. 1, §22; Buddeus, _Instit. theol. dogm._ II, c. 1, §29. The Arminians followed that example, Episcopius, _Instit. theol._ IV, sect. 2, c. 21; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II, c. 9, §9. And the Roman Catholic theologians indeed retained the distinction in name but explained it so that God’s will was always _voluntas beneplaciti_, divided into _voluntas antecedens_ and _consequens_, and that the _voluntas signi_ was nothing but a partial revelation of that will, _Theol. Wirceb._ III 160; C. Pesch, _Prael._ II 156; Kleutgen, _Theol. d. Vorz._ I 564 f. Thus it happened that, on the one hand, the Roman Catholics and others really retained only the distinction into _voluntas antecedens_ and _consequens_ (_absoluta_ and _conditionata_), and, on the other hand, the Reformed only that into _voluntas beneplaciti_ and _signi_ (_decernens_ and _praecipiens_, _arcana_ and _revelata_), while rejecting the distinction of _voluntas antecedens_ and _consequens_, Maccovius, _Collegia theol._, disp. 1–8; Nic. Gürtler, _Instit. theol._, 1732, c. 3, §105; Moor I 576; Mastricht, _Theor. pract. theol._ II, c. 15, §24 and §27 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III, qu. 15, 16. The difference amounts to this: the Roman Catholics, Lutherans, Remonstrants, and others proceed from the _voluntas signi_. This is the will proper, consisting in God’s not willing sin but only willing to permit it, willing the salvation of all, offering grace to all, and so on; and when the human being has then decided, God adapts himself to it and determines what he wills—the salvation of the one who believes, the perdition of the one who does not believe. The _voluntas consequens_ follows upon the human decision and is not God’s proper, essential will but God’s action occasioned by human conduct. By contrast, the Reformed proceeded from the _voluntas beneplaciti_ and held this to be God’s proper, essential will. That will always succeeds, always reaches its goal, and is eternal and immutable; the _voluntas signi_, by contrast, is God’s prescription in law and gospel that applies as the rule for our conduct.

Now it is the pervasive teaching of Scripture, in both Old and New Testaments, that God’s will is eternal, immutable, independent, and efficacious. This is not merely stated now and then, for example, Pss. 33:11; 115:3; Dan. 7:25; Isa. 46:10; Matt. 11:26; Rom. 9:18; Eph. 1:4; Rev. 4:11; etc.; the whole of Scripture testifies to it, all God’s virtues demand it, and the entire history of church and world furnishes proof of it. Correspondingly, Christian theology, especially since Augustine, taught that God’s will is simple, eternal, and immutable because it is one with his being, Augustine, _Conf._ XII 15. The _voluntas antecedens_ is not properly a will in God, _magis potest dici velleitas quam absoluta voluntas_, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 19, art. 6. The _voluntas signi_ is called _voluntas_ in God only in a metaphorical sense, _sicut cum aliquis praecipit aliquid, signum est quod velit illud fieri_, ibid., art. 11. The proper will in God is the _voluntas beneplaciti_, and this is one with God’s being, is immutable, and is always fulfilled, ibid., arts. 6 and 7; _C. Gent._ I 72 sq.; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 45–48, and the commentaries of Thomas, Bonaventure, and others. Pelagianism wrongly abandoned this line and elevated a powerless desire, an unfulfilled wish in God, into his will. By this it failed to do justice to God’s whole being and all his virtues. For if _velleitas_ is God’s proper and essential will, then he is deprived of his omnipotence, wisdom, goodness, immutability, independence, and so forth; the entire government of the world is then withdrawn from his providence; an irreconcilable dualism is created between God’s intention and the result of world history; the outcome is then an eternal disappointment for God; the world-plan has failed, and Satan triumphs at the end. Pelagianism does indeed claim that it acts this way in the interest of God’s holiness and that it upholds this better than Paul and Augustine, than Thomas and Calvin, because in their system God becomes the author of sin. But this is no more than appearance. Sin remains just as inexplicable from the standpoint of Pelagius as from that of Augustine; indeed, holiness comes much better into its own here. For it is more in accord with Scripture and the whole Christian faith to assume that for wise reasons—though unknown to us—God has in a certain sense willed sin than that, not willing it in any respect, he nevertheless tolerates and permits it. The latter, after all, precisely detracts from his holiness and omnipotence; cf. later in the doctrine of sin. In addition, Scripture, although theologically giving priority to the _voluntas beneplaciti_, at the same time maintains in the _voluntas signi_ how and in what manner he does not will sin. In the _signa_ of prohibition, admonition, warning, chastisement, punishment, and so forth, he descends to us and says what he requires of us. Because the human being is a rational, moral being, God does not deal with him as with a stick and a block but speaks and acts with him according to his nature. Just as a father forbids a child to use a sharp knife and yet handles it himself without injury, so God forbids sin to his rational creatures, while he himself can and actually does use it for the glorification of his name. The _voluntas beneplaciti_ and _voluntas signi_ therefore do not conflict with one another either, as the common objection runs. For, first, the _voluntas signi_ is not properly God’s will but only his command and prescription, which applies as the rule for our conduct. In the _voluntas signi_ he does not say what _he_ will do; it is no law for _his_ action; it does not prescribe what God must do. Rather, in it he says what _we_ are to do; it is a rule for _our_ conduct, Deut. 29:29. It is therefore called _voluntas Dei_ only in a metaphorical sense. It is indeed objected to this that the _voluntas signi_ is nevertheless called such because it is a _signum voluntatis in Deo_ and thus must agree with his _voluntas beneplaciti_. Second, therefore, it should be noted that this is indeed so: the _voluntas signi_ is an indication of what God wills us to do. The _voluntas beneplaciti_ and _voluntas signi_ do not stand directly opposed to each other, such that according to the first God does will sin and according to the second does not will sin, or according to the first does not will the salvation of all and according to the second does, and so forth. According to the _voluntas beneplaciti_ too, God takes no delight in sin; it is no object of his good pleasure; he does not afflict from a desire to afflict. And conversely, according to neither the _voluntas signi_ nor the _voluntas beneplaciti_ does he will that _all_ human beings, head for head, be saved. In view of history, no one can seriously teach that; in fact, the _all_ in 1 Tim. 2:4 is limited by everyone to a larger or smaller circle. The two stand so little in opposition that the _voluntas signi_ is precisely the way in which the _voluntas beneplaciti_ is fulfilled. In the way of admonitions and warnings, prohibitions and threats, conditions and demands, God carries out his counsel. And the _voluntas beneplaciti_ maintains only that the human being, in transgressing God’s command, does not become independent of God for a single moment but in that very moment serves God’s counsel and becomes—even if unwillingly—an instrument of his glory. Not only the _voluntas signi_ but also the _voluntas beneplaciti_ is holy and wise and good and will at the end be revealed as such precisely in the way of law and justice. Therefore, finally, the distinction between the two must also be maintained. It is the problem of right and fact, of idea and history, of the moral and the actual, of what ought to happen and what actually happens, that confronts us here. Whoever denies the _voluntas signi_ detracts from God’s holiness, the majesty of the moral law, and the gravity of sin. Whoever, by contrast, denies the _voluntas beneplaciti_ comes into conflict with God’s omnipotence, wisdom, independence, and sovereignty. From both standpoints one runs the danger either of closing one’s eyes to reality with a superficial optimism and calling everything actual rational, or of cursing existence with a one-sided pessimism and despairing of world and fate. Theism, however, does not seek a solution by deleting one of the two terms of the problem but recognizes and maintains them both. It sees the lines of the rational and the actual intersecting and crossing each other at every moment in history. It traces both back to God’s sovereignty and has so high a conception of that sovereignty that, through even the irrational and sinful, it will carry out its holy and wise counsel to the glory of the Lord’s name. For his divine sovereignty shines forth splendidly in this: that in human foolishness he glorifies his wisdom, in their weakness his power, in their sin his righteousness and grace.
