# § 30. The Spiritual World

## 1.

According to Holy Scripture, creation divides into a spiritual and a material world, into heaven and earth, τα ἐν τοις οὐρανοις και τα ἐπι της γης, τα ἀορατα και τα ὁρατα, Col. 1:16.
The existence of such a spiritual world is acknowledged in all religions.
Besides the gods proper, all kinds of demigods or heroes, demons, _genii_, spirits, souls, and so forth, are also objects of religious worship.
Especially in Parsism was this doctrine of angels strongly developed.
A whole host of good angels, Jazada, is there gathered around Ahura Mazda, the god of light, just as Ahriman, the god of darkness, is surrounded by a number of evil angels, Daevas.
According to Kuenen, _G. v. I._ II 254 f., and many others, the Jews borrowed their doctrine of angels chiefly from the Persians after the Babylonian Exile.
But there is very great exaggeration in this.
For, first, Kuenen too acknowledges that belief in the existence and activity of higher beings is ancient Israelite.
Second, there is a great difference between angelology in the canonical writings and in Jewish popular belief.
And finally, so much remains uncertain in the mutual relation of Judaism and Parsism that James Darmesteter, in his work on the Zend-Avesta in 1893, derives the Persian doctrine of angels, precisely conversely, from Judaism; cf. Dr. Geesink in _De Heraut_ 830, and against Darmesteter, Tiele, _Versl. en Meded. v. d. Kon. Ak. van Wet. Letterk._, 1895, p. 364 f.
According to Acts 23:8, however, the existence of angels was denied by the Sadducees, who therefore probably regarded the angelic appearances in the Pentateuch as momentary theophanies.
Josephus leaves various angelic appearances unmentioned and attempts to explain some naturally, for example, _Ant._ VIII 13, 7.
According to Justin, _Dial. c. Tr._ 128, angels were regarded by some as temporary emanations from the divine being that returned to God after completing their task.
In later times the existence of angels was denied by the followers of David Joris, cf. Hoornbeek, _Summa Controv._, 1653, p. 413; by the Libertines, cf. Calvin, _Opera_, ed. Schippers, VIII 383; and by Spinoza, _Tract. Theol. pol._ II 56, and Hobbes, _Leviathan_ III c. 34, both of whom saw in them simply revelations and operations of God.
Balthasar Bekker did not go this far in his _Betoverde Werelt_ II c. 6-15, but he nevertheless restricted the activity of angels, frequently regarded them as human beings, and taught, like Spinoza, that Christ and his apostles repeatedly accommodated themselves in the doctrine of angels to the belief of their contemporaries.
Leibniz, Wolff, Bonnet, Euler, and the supernaturalists sought to maintain their existence chiefly on moral grounds; from the human being there could be no _vacuum formarum_ in the ascending series of creatures, either upward or downward, Reinhard, _Dogm._ 184; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I 746 f.
Even Kant did not consider the existence of other thinking beings besides human beings impossible, Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez. zw. Theol. u. Naturw._ II 69, 249.
The eighteenth century erased the distinction between angels and human beings, just as the nineteenth did that between human beings and animals.
Swedenborg, for example, had learned from the angels themselves that they were actually human beings; the inner being of the human is an angel, and the human being is destined to become an angel, _Die wahre christl. Religion_, 2nd ed., Stuttgart, 1873, S. 42, 178.
In modern theology, however, little has remained of the angels.
Rationalists such as Wegscheider do not indeed deny the existence of angels but nevertheless deny their appearance, _Inst. theol._ § 102.
In the second edition of his dogmatics, Marheineke omitted the paragraph on angels.
Strauss thought that the modern worldview had deprived angels of their dwelling; they owe their origin solely to popular legends, to the desire to counterbalance the mass of matter in the world with somewhat more spirit, _Gl._ I 671 f.
In Lipsius they are only _bildliche Veranschaulichung des lebendigen Wirkens der göttlichen Vorsehung_ and belong solely in religious symbolism, _Dogm._ § 518 f.; cf. Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ II² 510 f.
And Schleiermacher too indeed did not consider their existence impossible but nevertheless judged that Christ and the apostles taught nothing positive concerning them because they had accommodated themselves to the people and had spoken of angels as we do of fairies and elves, and that dogmatically and religiously they had no significance for us, _Chr. Gl._ §§ 42, 43; cf. Bovon, _Dogm. Chrét._, Lausanne, 1895, I 297.
Even those who still maintain their existence frequently alter their nature.
Schelling regarded the good angels as _Potenzen, Möglichkeiten_ that, because of the fall, had not entered into actuality and are now nothing other than the idea or _Potenz_ of an individual or people, _Werke_ II 4, S. 284 f.; cf. Martensen, _Dogm._ §§ 68, 69.
Others have transformed the angels into inhabitants of the planets.
Already early on we find the opinion that the planets were inhabited.
It already occurs in Xenophanes and some Stoics.
After modern astronomy had given some conception of the immense space of the universe, this thought again found acceptance among Descartes, Wittich, Allinga, Wilkins, Harvey, Leibniz, Wolff, Bonnet, Kant, Reinhard, Bretschneider, Swedenborg, and others; cf. Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._ II 55 f.; Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._, 431; Büchner, _Kraft und Stoff_, 55; Strauss, _Alte u. neue Gl._, 165; C. du Prel, _Die Planetenbewohner_, Leipzig, 1879; C. Flammarion, _Les terres du ciel_, 7th ed., Paris, 1881; idem, _Zijn de hemellichamen bewoond?_, Dutch trans. by Dr. H. Blink, 1891.
And some theologians thus came to think of angels as those inhabitants, Kurtz, _Bibel und Astronomie_, 3rd ed., S. 173 f., 346 f.; Keerl, _Der Mensch das Ebenbild Gottes_ I 278 f.; Splittgerber, _Tod, Fortleben und Auferstehung_, 5th ed., 1879, S. 150; Lange, _Dogm._ II 362 f.; K. Keerl, _Die Fixsterne und die Engel_, _Bew. d. Gl._, June 1896, S. 230 f., and so forth.
And finally, over against materialism, a reaction has arisen in spiritualism, which not only acknowledges the existence of departed spirits but also considers communion between them and human beings possible, and has thereby once again paved the way for belief in a spiritual world among thousands.
Special literature on the angels: Suarez, _De angelis_, Lugd., 1620; Jac. Ode, _Comment. de angelis_, Traj., 1739; Voetius, _Disp._ V 241-268; Kuyper, _De Heraut_, nos. 858-915; Oswald, _Angelologie_, Paderborn, 1883.
