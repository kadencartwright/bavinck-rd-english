## 3.

The name angel, under which we usually comprehend the whole class of higher spiritual beings, is in Scripture not a _nomen naturae_ but a _nomen officii_.
The Hebrew מלאך simply means messenger, envoy, and can also designate a human being, whether sent by human beings, Job 1:14; 1 Sam. 11:3, and so forth, or by God, Hag. 1:13; Mal. 2:7; 3:1.
And the same is true of ἀγγελος, which is repeatedly used of human beings, Matt. 11:10; Mark 1:2; Luke 7:24, 27; 9:52; Gal. 4:4; James 2:15; it is therefore incorrectly rendered in our States Translation by angel instead of envoy, for example in Mal. 3:1; Rev. 1:20, and so forth.
There is in Scripture no common, distinctive name for all spiritual beings together, although they are frequently called sons of God, spirits, holy ones, and watchers.
There are different kinds and classes of angels, all of which bear names of their own.
The world of angels is as rich as the material world; just as in the latter there are all kinds of creatures that nevertheless together form one whole, so it is also with the world of spirits.
The class of cherubim is the first to be mentioned in Scripture.
They appear in Gen. 3:24 as guardians of the garden.
In tabernacle and temple they are depicted above the ark with faces turned toward the mercy seat and with wings covering the mercy seat, Ex. 25:18f.; 37:8; 1 Chron. 28:18; 2 Chron. 3:14; Heb. 9:5, and between them the Lord dwells, Ps. 80:2; 99:1; Isa. 37:16.
When God descends to earth, he is represented as traveling, riding upon the cherubim, 2 Sam. 22:11; Ps. 18:11; cf. Ps. 104:3; Isa. 66:15; Heb. 1:7.
In Ezekiel 1 and 10 they occur under the name חַיּוֹת, four in number, in human form, each with four wings and four faces, namely those of a human being, a lion, an ox, and an eagle; while finally in Rev. 4:6f., as the four ζωα, each with one face and each with six wings, they surround the throne of God and sing the thrice-holy day and night.
The name כְרוּבִים is variously derived: sometimes from כְ and רֹב, as “many” (Hengstenberg); then from רלוב, “chariot”; or also from the Arabic כרב, “to terrify,” thus meaning something like terrible beings; but most often from a root meaning to grasp, to hold; cf. γρυψ; Delitzsch on Gen. 3:24 and Ps. 18:11.
There is equally great disagreement concerning the nature of the cherubim.
Some regard them as mythical beings, others as symbolic figures, still others as powers of God in creation, or also as an original designation of thunderclouds or storms; Smend, _Altt. Rel._, 21 f.
But in Gen. 3:24; Ezek. 1; Rev. 4, they are nevertheless clearly presented as living and personal beings; indeed, the human form predominates in them, Ezek. 1:5.
Since, however, they are beings of extraordinary, superhuman power and glory, Scripture calls symbolic representation to its aid to give us some conception of their spiritual nature.
They are portrayed as ζωα, in whom God’s power and strength come to expression far better than in weak humanity.
They have the strength of an ox, the majesty of the lion, the speed of the eagle, and in addition the reason of the human being.
The wings they bear and the sword with which they guard the garden point to these same qualities.
From this representation, which is not an image but a symbol, it appears that even among the angels they are highly placed beings who, more than any other creatures, reveal the power, majesty, and glory of God and who are therefore also charged with the task of guarding his holiness at the garden of Eden, in the tabernacle and temple, and also at God’s descent to earth; Dr. Joh. Nikel, _Die Lehre des A. T. über die Cherubim und Seraphim_, Breslau, 1890.
Further, in Isa. 6 the שְׂרָפִים are mentioned, probably from the Arabic root _sarufa, nobilis fuit_; they too are symbolically represented there in human form but with six wings, two of which serve to cover the face, two to cover the feet, and two for the swift execution of God’s commands.
In distinction from the cherubim, they stand as servants around the King who sits upon his throne, sing his honor, and await his commands.
Seraphim are the noble ones, cherubim the powerful ones, among the angels.
Finally, in Daniel two angels with proper names also occur, namely Gabriel, cap. 8:16; 9:21, and Michael, c. 10:13, 21; 12:1; contrary to many earlier and later interpreters, such as the Van den Honerts, Burman, Witsius, Hengstenberg, Zahn, and others, both are to be regarded as created angels and may not be identified with the Son of God.
According to the New Testament too, there are different classes of angels.
The angel Gabriel appears in Luke 1:19, 26.
Michael occurs in Jude 9; Rev. 12:7; 1 Thess. 4:16.
And further, among the angels there is mention of αρχαι, ἐξουσιαι, Eph. 3:10; Col. 2:10; κυριοτητας, Eph. 1:21; Col. 1:16; θρονοι, Col. 1:16; δυναμεις, Eph. 1:21; 1 Pet. 3:22, all of which point to a distinction in rank and dignity among the angels; while finally in the Apocalypse of John seven angels repeatedly come very clearly to the fore, c. 8:2, 6; 15:1, and so forth.
There is in addition the fact that the number of the angels is very great.
The name Tzebaoth, cf. above, p. 111f.; Mahanaim, Gen. 32:1, 2; legions, Matt. 26:53; and host, Luke 2:13; and the numbers of a thousand times thousands indicate this, Deut. 33:2; Ps. 68:18; Dan. 7:10; Heb. 12:12; Jude 14; Rev. 5:11; 19:14.
Such a great number already requires of itself distinction of order and rank, all the more because the angels do not, like human beings, stand in family relations to one another and are therefore in that respect much more alike.
Scripture accordingly also clearly teaches that among the angels there are all kinds of distinctions—of rank and station, of dignity and service, of office and honor, even of class and kind.
This beautiful, rich thought of diversity in unity may not be surrendered, even though it has been fantastically elaborated by the Jews and the Roman Catholics.
The Jews made all kinds of distinctions among the angels; Weber, _System_, 161 f.
In the Christian church, at first people remained with the data of Scripture; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 54; Origen, _De princ._ I 5.
Augustine still said that he did not know how the _societas_ of the angels was ordered, _Enchir._ 58.
But Pseudo-Dionysius gave a schematic classification in his writings _De coelesti_ and _De ecclesiastica hierarchia_.
Proceeding from the thought that in creation God had, as it were, stepped out of his unity into multiplicity, _De div. nom._ II 11, he teaches that all things proceed from God in an ever-descending series and thus successively return to him.
God is the center; creatures arrange themselves peripherally around him.
There is a hierarchy of things, _De coel. hier._ III 1.
Such a hierarchy is twofold, a heavenly and an ecclesiastical.
The heavenly is formed by three classes of angels.
The first and highest class serves God exclusively; it comprises the seraphim, who ceaselessly contemplate the divine being; the cherubim, who ponder his counsels; and the thrones, who adore his judgments.
The second class serves the whole visible and invisible creation; it comprises the dominions, which order what must occur according to God’s will; the powers, which carry out what has been decreed; and the forces, which bring it to completion.
The third class serves the earth, individual human beings, and peoples; it comprises the principalities, which promote the general welfare of human beings; the archangels, which guide particular peoples; and the angels, which watch over individual human beings, _De coel. hier._ VI sq.
The ecclesiastical hierarchy is an image of that heavenly hierarchy, in its mysteries (baptism, Eucharist, ordination), organs (bishop, priest, deacon), and laity (catechumens, Christians, monks), _De eccl. hier._ 2 sq.
And this entire hierarchy has its origin and head in Christ, the incarnate Son of God, _De eccl. hier._ I 1, and its end in deification, ib. I 3.
This classification of Pseudo-Dionysius, which reveals the hierarchy in heaven and on earth as the intimate thought of the Roman Catholic system, found fertile soil; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II c. 3; Lombard, _Sent._ II dist. 9; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 108; Petavius, _De angelis_ lib. II; Oswald, _Angelologie_, Paderborn, 1883, p. 57 f., and so forth.
Now Scripture does indeed also clearly teach a distinction and rank ordering of the angels.
Some wrongly think that the different names always mean the same angels, considered only from different points of view; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I 301.
It must even be acknowledged that this rank ordering has not sufficiently come into its own in Protestant theology.
There is rank and order among those thousands of beings.
God is a God of order in all the congregations, 1 Cor. 14:33, 40.
The world of spirits is no less rich and beautiful than the world of material beings.
But the hierarchy of Roman Catholic doctrine goes far beyond God’s revelation in his Word.
It was therefore unanimously rejected by the Protestants; Calvin on Eph. 1:21; Voetius, _Disp._ I 882 sq.; Rivetus, _Op._ III 248 sq.; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 443, 450; Gerhard, _Loc._ V cap. 4 sect. 9.
And likewise all calculations of the number of angels were considered vain and fruitless: for example, that of Augustine, who had the number of the angels after the fall of some of them filled up by the _praedestinati homines_, _Enchir._ 29; _De civ._ XXII c. 1; Anselm, _Cur Deus homo_ I 18; or that of Gregory, who thought that as many human beings were saved as angels had remained standing, cf. Lombard, _Sent._ II 9; or that of William of Paris, who called the number of the angels infinite; or that of Hilary and many others, who on the basis of Matt. 18:12 thought that the number of human beings stood to that of the angels as one to ninety-nine, cf. Petavius, _De angelis_ I c. 14; or that of G. Schott, who fixed the number of the angels at a thousand times a thousand millions, in Busken Huet, _Het Land van Rembrand_ II 2, 37.
And likewise little attention was given to the question whether the angels also differed among themselves in being and kind, _essentia_ and _specie_; Thomas taught this decisively, _S. Theol._ I qu. 50 art. 4, but the church fathers mostly held another opinion, John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 3; Petavius, _De angelis_ I c. 14; Voetius, _Disp._ V 261.
However many kinds of distinction may exist among the angels, Scripture does not enter into the matter here and offers only a few data.
With respect to us, unity comes much more to the fore than diversity; they all have a spiritual nature, are all called ministering spirits, and all find their principal activity in the glorification of God.
