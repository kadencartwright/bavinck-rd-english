## 6.

The unity of the angels is further manifested in the fact that they are all rational beings, endowed with intellect and will.
Both these faculties are repeatedly ascribed in Scripture to angels, both evil and good, Job 1:6 f.; Zech. 3:1 f.; Matt. 24:36; 8:29; 18:10; 2 Cor. 11:3; Eph. 6:11, and so forth.
All kinds of personal attributes and activities occur in them, such as self-consciousness, Luke 1:19; speaking, ibid.; desiring, 1 Pet. 1:12; rejoicing, Luke 15:10; worshiping, Heb. 1:6; believing, James 2:19; lying, John 8:44; sinning, John 3:8, and so forth.
In addition, great power is also ascribed to them; the angels are not timid beings but an army, a host of mighty heroes, Ps. 103:20; Luke 11:21; Col. 1:16; Eph. 1:21; 3:10; 2 Thess. 1:7; Acts 5:19; Heb. 1:14.
On this ground it is incorrect to regard the angels, with Schelling and others, as attributes or powers.
Nevertheless, in describing the personality of the angels one should remain with the simplicity of Holy Scripture.
Augustine distinguished a twofold knowledge of the angels: one that they obtained, as it were in the morning of creation, _a priori_, through the vision of God, _cognitio matutina_, and another that they received, as it were in the evening of creation, _a posteriori_, from creatures, _De Gen. ad litt._ V 18; _De civ._ XI 29.
The scholastics not only adopted this distinction, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 54, but also attempted to define more precisely the nature and measure of the angels’ knowledge.
Their knowledge is not, as in God, identical with their being and essence.
But they do not know through sensory perception; the distinction between _intellectus possibilis_ and _agens_ is not present in them; the _intellectus_ in them is never merely a capacity and never at rest but always active; they cannot exist without knowing; they know themselves, their own essence, through themselves, perfectly and immutably; they know created things not from their appearance but through innate ideas, not through abstraction and discursively but intuitively and intellectually.
Indeed, with their natural powers the angels know not immediately but _per speciem impressam_, through and simultaneously with their own being; but in the supernatural order to which the good angels have been elevated, they nevertheless know God through immediate vision, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 54-58; _S. c. Gent._ II c. 96-101; III c. 49; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 3 art. 4 and dist. 4 art. 3; Petavius, _De angelis_ I c. 6-9; Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorzeit_ I² 196 f.; Oswald, _Angelologie_, 43-51.
Some even taught, in order to defend prayer to the angels and also to the saints, that because they see God, who sees everything, they saw everything in him and thus knew all our needs and wants, Gregory, _Moral._ lib. 12 c. 13; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 83 art. 4; III qu. 10 art. 2; Bellarmine, _De sanct. beat._ I c. 26.
The Protestants were more cautious; they admonished modesty.
There is so much that is mysterious in the manner in which we come to knowledge; how much more in that of the angels, Zanchi, _Op._ III 108 sq.; Voetius, _Disp._ V 267; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ V c. 4 sect. 5.
This alone can be said: that they are richer in knowledge than we are here on earth, Matt. 18:10; 24:36; that they obtain their knowledge from their own nature, John 8:44; from the contemplation of God’s works, Eph. 3:10; 1 Tim. 3:16; 1 Pet. 1:12; and from revelations that God communicates to them, Dan. 8, 9; Rev. 1:1.
But they are nevertheless bound to the objects, Eph. 3:10; 1 Pet. 1:12.
They do not know our hidden thoughts of the heart, nor one another’s, 1 Kings 8:39; Ps. 139:2, 4; Acts 1:24, so that they also need a language among themselves to communicate their thoughts, 1 Cor. 13:2, and in general can glorify God in their own manner and according to the nature of their being by speaking and singing, Petavius, _De angelis_ I c. 12.
They do not know the future, nor the _futura contingentia_, except _per conjecturam_, Isa. 41:22, 23.
They do not know the day of judgment, Mark 13:12.
And their knowledge is capable of increase, Eph. 3:10.
To this it may certainly also be added that knowledge and power differ greatly among the angels.
In this respect too there is diversity and order.
Even from the few names that occur in Scripture it may be inferred that the angels are distinguished not only into classes but also as persons and that each bears a particular individuality, even though the opinion of some scholastics that every angel constitutes a particular _species_ must be rejected, Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 3 art. 2; Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ II 52.
