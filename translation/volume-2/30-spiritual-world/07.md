## 7.

Finally, the angels are also one in that they are all moral beings.
This is evident both from the good angels, who serve God night and day, and from the evil angels, who did not remain standing in the truth.
Scripture says very little about the _status originalis_ of the angels.
It testifies only that at the end of the work of creation God saw all things, and behold, they were very good, Gen. 1:31.
Moreover, in John 8:44; Jude 6; 2 Pet. 2:4, a good state of all the angels is presupposed.
And the same is demanded by the theism of Scripture, which wholly excludes Manichaeism.
Precisely because Scripture reveals so little, however, imagination and reasoning found ample scope here.
Augustine thought that at the very moment of their creation the angels in part fell and in part remained standing; to the latter, therefore, God gave the grace of perseverance simultaneously with their nature, _simul eis et condens naturam et largiens gratiam_, _De civ._ XII 9; cf. _De corr. et gr._ c. 11 n. 32.
Scholasticism later appealed to this for its doctrine of the _dona superaddita_ also among the angels.
According to Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 4 art. 1 qu. 2; Halesius, Lombard, Scotus, and others, the angels first existed for a time _in puris naturalibus_ and later received the _auxilium gratiae actualis_.
Thomas, however, thought with others that the distinction between nature and grace was to be understood only logically and that the grace to be able to remain standing was also granted in different measure to the different angels, _S. Theol._ I qu. 62 arts. 3 and 6.
Equipped with that grace, the angels could merit the highest, indefectible blessedness, which consists in the vision of God; Petavius, _De angelis_ I 16; Becanus, _Theol. Schol._, _De ang._ c. 2, 3; _Theol. Wirceb._, 1880, III 466 sq.; Pesch, _Prael._ III 204 sq.; Oswald, _Angelologie_, 81 f.; Jansen, _Prael._ II 361 sq.
In the _locus de homine_, the doctrine of the _dona superaddita_ will demand our particular attention.
Here let it only be pointed out that among the angels, at any rate, it finds not the slightest ground in Scripture.
Protestant theology therefore unanimously rejected it.
It contented itself with the teaching that the angels who remained standing were confirmed in the good.
And with Augustine and scholasticism it maintained this over against Origen, _De princ._ I 5, 3, 4, and the Remonstrants, who still regarded the will of the good angels as mutable.
Indeed, the good angels are also always presented to us in Holy Scripture as a faithful host that immutably does the will of the Lord.
They are called angels of the Lord, Pss. 103:20; 104:4; ἐκλεκτοι, 1 Tim. 5:21; ἁγιοι, Deut. 33:3; Matt. 25:31; angels of light, Luke 9:26; Acts 10:22; 2 Cor. 11:14; Rev. 14:10.
They see God’s face daily, Matt. 18:10; are set before us as an example, Matt. 6:10; and believers will one day become like them, Luke 20:36.
