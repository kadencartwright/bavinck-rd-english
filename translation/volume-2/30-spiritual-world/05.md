## 5.

Next, the unity of the angels appears from the fact that they are all spiritual beings.
On this, however, there has at all times been great difference of opinion.
The Jews attributed to them aerial or fiery bodies, Weber, _System_, 161 f.
Most church fathers judged similarly, including Justin, _C. Tryph._ 57; Origen, _De princ._ I 6; Basil, _De Sp. S._ c. 16; Tertullian, _De carne Chr._ 6; Augustine, _De trin._ II 7; etc.
At the Second Synod of Nicaea in 787, Patriarch Tarasius read aloud a dialogue by a certain John of Thessalonica in which the latter argued that angels had subtle bodies and therefore could also be depicted.
Tarasius added that they were limited in space and had appeared in human form and were therefore capable of depiction.
The synod expressed its agreement with this, Schwane, _D. G._ II² 235.
But gradually the boundary between matter and spirit was drawn more sharply, and many ascribed to the angels a purely spiritual nature, including Pseudo-Dionysius, _De coel. hier._; John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ II, c. 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 50, art. 1; qu. 51, art. 1.
In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council called the nature of the angels _spiritualis_, Denzinger, _Enchir._ n. 355, and most Roman Catholic and Protestant theologians agreed with this.
Nevertheless, a certain corporeality of the angels continued repeatedly to be taught even later: by Roman Catholics such as Cajetan, Eugubinus, and Bannez; by a few Reformed theologians such as Zanchius, _Op._ III, col. 69, and Vossius, _De idol._ I 2, 6; further, by Episcopius, _Inst. Theol._ V, c. 2, p. 347; Vorstius, Poiret, Böhme, Leibniz, Wolff, Bonnet, Reinhard, and others; and in more recent times by Kurtz, _Bibel und Astronomie_, 3rd ed., S. 152; Beck, _Lehrwiss._ I 176; Lange, _Dogm._ II 578; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 443 f.; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 306; Keerl, in _Bew. d. Gl._, June 1896, S. 235–247; and others; cf. Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._² 65 f.
The principal ground for this opinion is that the concept of a purely spiritual, incorporeal nature is metaphysically inconceivable and also incompatible with the concept of a creature.
God is pure Spirit, but he is also simple, omnipresent, and eternal.
The angels, however, are limited, also with respect to time and place; if they really move from one place to another, they must be corporeal in their own way.
Likewise, the angels are not simple as God is but are composed of _materia_ and _forma_, and for this reason too a certain corporeality must be ascribed to them—not indeed gross and material, but nevertheless subtle and ethereal.
To this consideration was added the exegesis that understood the sons of God in Gen. 6 to be angels.
This interpretation of Philo, Josephus, the Jews, and the LXX was adopted by many church fathers, Justin, _Apol._ I 1; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV 16, 2; V 29, 2; Clement, Tertullian, Lactantius, Cyprian, Ambrose, and others; it also gained acceptance with Luther and in more recent times was defended again by Ewald, Baumgarten, Hofmann, Kurtz, Delitzsch, Hengstenberg, Köhler, Kübel, and others.
In support of angelic corporeality, appeal is further made to their appearances, to several particular texts in Holy Scripture, such as Ps. 104:4; Matt. 22:30; Luke 20:35; 1 Cor. 11:10, and sometimes also to the claim that as inhabitants of the stars they must indeed be corporeal.
Over against all these arguments, however, stands the clear declaration of Holy Scripture that the angels are πνευματα, Matt. 8:16; 12:45; Luke 7:21; 8:2; 11:26; Acts 19:12; Eph. 6:12; Heb. 1:4; that they do not marry, Matt. 22:30; are immortal, Luke 20:35, 36; and are invisible, Col. 1:16; that a legion of them can be present within a single confined space, Luke 8:30; and that as spirits they do not have flesh and bones, Luke 24:39.
Further, the interpretation of the בְנֵי־הָאֱלֹהִים in Gen. 6:2 as angels rather than human beings is untenable, for although this designation is repeatedly used of angels, Job 1:6; 2:1; 38:7, it can also very well designate human beings, Deut. 32:5; Hos. 2:1; Ps. 80:16; 73:15; and in any case it cannot be applied to the evil angels, who under this interpretation must nevertheless have committed the sin here; furthermore, the expression לקח אשה in Gen. 6:2 is always used of a lawful marriage and never of fornication; and finally, the punishment for the sin is carried out upon human beings alone, for they are the guilty ones, and there is no mention of angels, Gen. 6:3, 5–7.
Nor do the other passages of Scripture prove the corporeality of the angels.
Ps. 104:4, cf. Heb. 1:7, says only that God uses his angels as servants, just as wind and fire also serve to carry out his commands, but it by no means indicates that the angels are changed into wind or fire.
Matt. 22:30 means that after the resurrection believers will be like the angels in that they no longer marry, but it contains nothing about the corporeality of the angels.
And 1 Cor. 11:10 says that, as a sign of their subjection to the man, women must also be covered in the congregation so as not to displease the good angels who are present in the congregation; there can be no thought here at all of evil angels who otherwise would be seduced by the women.
As for the angelic appearances, it is indeed certain that they always took place in a corporeal, visible form, just as symbolism too always represents the angels in all kinds of visible forms.
But from this nothing follows concerning their corporeality.
For God is Spirit and yet is beheld by Isaiah in chapter 6 as a King sitting upon his throne.
Christ appeared in the flesh and yet is true God.
Both in appearance and in symbolism the angels repeatedly occur in different forms; the representations of the cherubim in Gen. 3:24, above the ark of the covenant, in Ezekiel, and in the Apocalypse differ very greatly from one another; and the forms in which angels appear are far from alike, Gen. 18; Judg. 6; 13:6; Dan. 10:11; Matt. 28:3; Luke 2:9; Rev. 22:8.
How these bodies are to be conceived is another question.
Whether they were real bodies or apparent bodies cannot be said, John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 51, art. 1–3; Turretin, _Theol. El._ VII 6, 5; etc.
The strongest proof for the corporeality of the angels, however, is derived from philosophy, as was said above.
But all kinds of misunderstanding are involved here.
If corporeality means only that the angels are limited in both time and space and also are not simple as God is, in whom all his attributes are his very being, then corporeality would certainly have to be ascribed to the angels.
But corporeality usually includes a kind of materiality, even if of a subtler nature than in human beings and animals.
And in this sense one cannot and may not speak of angels as having bodies.
Matter and spirit exclude each other, Luke 24:39.
It is pantheistic philosophy of identity to mix the two and erase the distinction.
Scripture, however, always maintains the distinction between heaven and earth, angels and human beings, spiritual and material, invisible and visible things, Col. 1:16.
If, therefore, the angels are to be conceived as spirits, they stand in a different, freer relation to time and space than human beings do.
On the one hand, they are not exalted above all time and place as God is, for they are creatures and therefore finite and limited.
They possess neither an _ubi repletivum_—omnipresence—nor eternity.
On the other hand, they also have no _ubi circumscriptivum_ as our bodies do, for the angels are spirits and therefore have no dimensions of length and breadth, and thus no extension and no diffusion through space.
It was therefore commonly said that the angels possess an _ubi definitivum_; that is, as finite and limited beings they are always somewhere; they cannot be in two places at once; their presence is not extensive but point-like; and they are so free with respect to place that they can move with the greatest speed and cannot be stopped by material objects; their change of place is instantaneous.
Such speed of movement and such _Zeit- und Raumfreiheit_, which nevertheless is not _Zeit- und Raumlosigkeit_, is indeed inconceivable to us.
But Scripture clearly indicates it, and in the speed of thought and imagination, of light and electricity, we have a by no means negligible analogy, Augustine, _De civ._ XI 9; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 52 and 53; Voetius, _Disp._ V 252 sq.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 302; Oswald, _Angelologie_, S. 23–43.
