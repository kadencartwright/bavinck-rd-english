## 2.

The existence of angels cannot be demonstrated philosophically. Leibniz’s reasoning that, starting from the human being, there must be all kinds of creatures above just as there are below, so that there may be no _vacuum formarum_ and no _saltus naturae_, is unacceptable, since, if consistently followed, it would erase the distinction between Creator and creature and lead to a Gnostic pantheism. Yet philosophy is still less able to bring forward any argument against the possibility of such an existence. As long as we ourselves are psychical beings and cannot explain the life of the soul from metabolism but must assume a spiritual substance for it, the existence of a spiritual world conflicts with no argument of reason and no fact of experience. Not only Leibniz and Wolff but also Schleiermacher and Kant fully acknowledged its possibility. Moreover, the universality of belief in such a spiritual world proves that this acknowledgment contains something other and something more than arbitrariness and chance. Strauss’s explanation that the angelic world is a compensation for the quantity of matter in creation is not worth refuting. Nor does Daub’s reasoning have any value, namely, that because the human being was placed between good and evil, he fashioned types in both directions and thus arrived at the ideas of angel and devil, in Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ I 536. Belief in a spiritual world is not philosophical but religious in nature. It is most closely connected with revelation and miracle. Religion is inconceivable without revelation, and revelation cannot exist unless above and behind this visible world there is a spiritual world that stands in communion with it. In all religions angels are not factors in the religious-ethical life itself but in the revelation upon which that life is built. Religion itself entails the belief that its deepest causes do not lie within the circle of visible things. Good and evil, both in the religious and in the ethical sense, are rooted in a world other than the one that appears before our eyes. Belief in angels gives expression to this. It does not constitute the essence and center of religion, but it is nevertheless connected with it. God’s transcendence, belief in revelation and miracle, and the nature of religion naturally entail belief in spiritual beings. The _Diesseits_ does not satisfy the human being. He thirsts again and again for another world that is no less rich than this one. Materialism calls forth spiritualism by reaction. But whereas in all religions and spiritualist theories the doctrine of angels appears in falsified and corrupted form, erases the boundary between God and creature, and fails to recognize the distinction between revelation and religion, in Scripture this doctrine occurs in such a way that it detracts nothing from God’s honor and leaves religion untouched in its purity. For the Christian, the revelation in Scripture is the firm ground of his belief in angels as well. In earlier times people sought to prove historically the existence of the good and especially also of the evil angels from oracles, apparitions, ghosts, and the possessed, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 50, art. 1; Zanchius, _Op._ II 2; Vossius, _De orig. et progr. idololatriae_ I, c. 6; Voetius, _Disp._ I 985–1017; V 241; etc. But these proofs were no more convincing than those from reason. By contrast, the existence of angels is taught very clearly in Scripture. Spinoza and Schleiermacher did indeed object that Christ and the apostles spoke about angels by accommodation to popular belief and themselves taught nothing positive about them. But Jesus and the apostles themselves unequivocally and repeatedly express their belief in angels, for example, Matt. 11:10; 13:39; 16:27; 18:10; 24:36; 26:53; Luke 20:36; 1 Cor. 6:3; Heb. 12:22; 1 Pet. 1:12; etc. When we speak of elves and fairies, everyone knows that this is meant figuratively. But in Jesus’s time belief in angels was universal; if Jesus and the apostles spoke of angels, everyone had to think that they themselves also believed in them. The ultimate ground for our belief in angels therefore lies in revelation. Christian experience as such teaches us nothing about them. The object of true faith is the grace of God in Christ. Angels are not factors in the religious life, not an object of our trust, not an object of our worship. Nowhere in Scripture are they any of these, and therefore they may not be so for us either. The Protestant confessions therefore speak very little about angels, Belgic Confession, art. 12; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 112, 117; Niemeyer, _Coll. Conf._, pp. 315, 316, 476. The Reformed in particular sinned more _in defectu_ than _in excessu_. At Rome angelology occupies a much larger place, but there it also corrupts religion and detracts from God’s honor. Yet although angels are neither factors nor objects of our religion, they are nevertheless of great significance in the history of revelation and derive from it above all their value for the religious life.
