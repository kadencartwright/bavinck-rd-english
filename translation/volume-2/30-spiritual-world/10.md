## 10.

Finally, this doctrine of guardian angels and of their intercession also had the disadvantage that in practice it soon led to veneration and worship of the angels.
Col. 2:18 teaches that such a θρησκεια των ἀγγελων occurred already in the apostolic age.
In his commentary on that passage, Theodoret remarks that such worship of angels still existed in Phrygia in his day and that the Synod of Laodicea had forbidden the veneration of angels lest God be forsaken, Schwane, _D. G._ II 245.
Many church fathers warn against the veneration and worship of angels, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 32; Origen, _C. Cels._ V 4, 5; VIII 13; Athanasius, _C. Ar._ II 23; Augustine, _De vera relig._ 55; _Conf._ X 42; _De civ._ VIII 25; etc.
The conviction was still universal that God alone might be worshiped and that angels were entitled only to civil honor.
_Honoramus eos caritate, non servitute_, Augustine, _De vera relig._ 55.
They are _potius imitandi quam invocandi_, _De civ._ X 26.
And Gregory the Great still says in his exposition of the Song of Songs, chapter 8, that since Christ came to earth, _ab ipsis etiam angelis ecclesia honoratur_;
under the Old Testament Joshua did worship the angel, Josh. 5:19, but in the New Testament the angel refuses John’s worship, Rev. 22:9.
Yet these warnings prove precisely that in practice the boundaries between the worship of God and reverence for the angels were erased.
The invocation of angels is first stated clearly by Ambrose, _De viduis_, cap. 9, §55, where he says, among other things: _obsecrandi sunt angeli, qui nobis ad praesidium dati sunt_.
Eusebius had already made the distinction between the τιμαν that is fitting for us toward the angels and the σεβειν that belongs to God alone, _Praep. Ev._ VII 15, and substantially we already find it in Origen, _C. Cels._ VIII 13, 57.
Augustine adopted it precisely in order to prevent the religious veneration of angels, _De civ._ V 15; VII 32; X 1.
But soon this distinction was used to justify the invocation of angels.
This occurred already at the Council of Nicaea in 787 and then further among the scholastics, Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 9; Thomas, _S. Th._ II 2, qu. 103, art. 3.
Trent called the invocation good and useful, sess. 25; the _Catech. Rom._ III, cap. 2, qu. 4, n. 3 grounded it in the fact that the angels always see God’s face and have undertaken the _patrocinium salutis nostrae_; the _Breviarium Romanum_ also included prayers _in festo angelorum_; and the Roman Catholic dogmaticians unanimously defend it, Bellarmine, _De sanct. beatit._ I, c. 11–20; Petavius, _De angelis_ II, c. 9 and 10, although they ordinarily treat it later under the _cultus sanctorum_.
Lutherans, the Reformed, and almost all Protestants, however, took a firm stand when they rejected this religious veneration of angels, just as they did that of the saints, Luther, in Köstlin, _L. Theol._ II 23 f.; Zwingli, _Op._ I 268 sq., 280 sq.; III 135 sq.; etc.; Calvin, _Inst._ I 14, 10–12; cf. III 20, 20–24; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ XXVI §370–480, on the angels especially §427; Quenstedt I 486 sq.; Turretin, _Th. El._ VII, qu. 9; idem, _De necessaria secessione nostra ab ecclesia Romana_, Geneva, 1692, disp. 2–4, _De idololatria Romana_, pp. 33–109.
For, first, there is not a single example of it in Scripture.
Roman Catholics do indeed appeal to a few passages of the Old Testament, such as Gen. 18:2; 32:24; 48:16; Ex. 23:20; Num. 22:31; Josh. 5:14; Judg. 13:17, but everywhere these speak not of a created angel but of the Angel of YHWH, and in the New Testament not even the semblance of any proof can be found.
But that is not all.
The veneration of angels is not only without precept and without example in Scripture, so that Rome might claim with some semblance of reason that the veneration of angels and saints is not forbidden in Scripture and is therefore permitted, and for that reason in fact does not impose or command it but only permits it and regards it as useful, Jansen, _Prael. theol._ III 1008; it is also positively forbidden there, Deut. 6:13; 10:20; Matt. 4:10; Col. 2:18, 19; Rev. 19:10; 22:9.
According to Scripture, religious honor may be shown to God alone and belongs to no creature.
Roman Catholics have not dared to deny this entirely but have nevertheless sought to justify the veneration of angels by the distinction between _latria_ and _dulia_.
Now at Rome this is not a distinction between religious and civil honor, which would be defensible; no, the veneration of angels and saints at Rome very definitely bears a religious character, even though it is relative.
The _dulia_ is a _cultus religiosus_.
But understood in this way, it is condemned both by Scripture and by practice.
Scripture knows no twofold religious veneration, one lower and one higher.
Roman Catholics accordingly admit that _latria_ and _dulia_ are by no means distinguished in Scripture as they distinguish them and that their etymology provides no ground for doing so either.
The Hebrew עבד is translated at one time by δουλεια and at another by λατρεια; cf. Deut. 6:13 and 1 Sam. 7:3; 1 Sam. 12:20 and Deut. 10:12.
Israel is forbidden both to δουλευειν and to λατρευειν other gods, Ex. 20:5; Jer. 22:9.
Likewise, the Hebrew שׁרת is rendered by both Greek words, Ezek. 20:32; Isa. 56:6.
Frequently δουλευειν is used of God, Matt. 6:24; Rom. 7:6; 14:18; 16:18; Gal. 4:9; Eph. 6:7; Col. 3:24; 1 Thess. 1:9, and λατρευειν is also used of service toward human beings.
Neither etymologically nor scripturally are the two words distinguished as Rome teaches.
The entire distinction is arbitrary.
Indeed, monotheism entails that there is and can be only one religious veneration; all veneration of creatures is either merely civil, or it detracts from monotheism and ascribes a divine character to creatures.
Practice teaches this most clearly as well.
Even if it is repeatedly said that angels and saints are only mediators, that they themselves are not directly invoked but that God is invoked in them, and that God’s honor is not diminished but increased by their invocation, all this in fact makes no difference; experience shows only too clearly that the Roman Catholic Christian puts his trust in creatures.
Moreover, even if the distinction were correct in itself, it still could not justify the religious veneration of angels.
For if this reasoning were sufficient as a defense, neither idolatry nor image worship could any longer be condemned.
The pagans who worshiped animals and images knew very well that these were not the gods themselves, Rom. 1:23.
The Jews did not regard the golden calf as YHWH himself, Ex. 32:4; 1 Kings 12:28.
Satan, when tempting Christ, by no means demanded that Christ regard him as God, Matt. 4:9.
And John certainly did not think that the angel who appeared to him was God, Rev. 19:10.
Yet Jesus answers: “You shall worship the Lord your God and serve him alone.”
This “alone” is exclusive, just as the one Mediator, Christ Jesus, excludes all other angels or human beings as mediators.
But this is precisely what Rome denies.
Just as Matt. 19:17; 23:8; John 9:5; 1 Tim. 2:5; 6:16; etc. do not exclude the possibility that human beings too may be called good, master, light, mediator, and immortal, so according to Rome Matt. 4:10 also does not prove that God alone may be worshiped.
For according to Rome the angels and saints partake of the divine nature itself; the _dona supernaturalia_ are indeed granted and derived, but they are of the same nature as the divine being itself.
And in this lies Rome’s deepest ground for the worship of saints and angels.
In the _justitia supernaturalis_ God communicates himself, his own being, to creatures; and therefore they too may be venerated in a religious manner.
And Rome’s proper thought is this: _tot esse species adorationis, quot sunt species excellentiae_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 103, art. 3; Bellarmine, _De sanct. beatit._ I, c. 12; Petavius, _De angelis_ II, c. 10; Jansen, _Prael. Theol._ III 1017; Möhler, _Symb._ §§52, 53.

By rejecting the religious veneration of angels, Protestantism acknowledged that angels are not an indispensable element in the religious life of Christians.
They are not the agents of our salvation, not the ground of our trust, not the object of our veneration; we stand in communion not with them but with God; indeed, they no longer appear to us now, and all special revelation through angels has ceased.
In Protestant churches and confessions the angels cannot and may not occupy the place assigned to them in Roman Catholic churches and confessions.
But this does not deny all significance of the angelic world for religion.
This significance lies first of all in the fact that God wills to make use of the service of angels in his works in the realm of grace.
The angels are of extraordinary significance for the kingdom of God and its history; we encounter them at every turning point; they are mediators of the resurrection, witnesses of God’s great deeds.
Their significance is much more objective than subjective in nature.
In our religious experience we know nothing of communion with the angelic world.
The angels have no influence upon either our religious or our moral life that can be put into clear words.
Their value lies in the history of revelation as Scripture makes it known to us.
In the second place, the angels therefore cannot be the object of reverential homage such as we show to human beings.
There certainly is a civil honor that we have to show them.
But this too is different from the honor shown to human beings whom we personally know and encounter.
Roman Catholics argue for the veneration of angels especially with the reasoning that, as envoys of the Most High, they are entitled to our homage, just as in the ambassadors of rulers those rulers themselves are honored.
And this is in itself entirely correct.
If an angel appeared to us, we would have to greet him with reverential homage.
And so it also happened when angels appeared to human beings in the days of revelation.
But such appearances no longer occur.
For us there can be no question of homage such as the patriarchs, prophets, and apostles showed to the angels who appeared to them.
It is not possible to show them such reverence and homage.
In the third place, therefore, there is indeed still an honor that the angels must receive from us.
But this honor is in no respect religious; it is only civil in nature, in essence equal to that which we accord to human beings, to creatures.
And this _honor civilis_ consists in our thinking and speaking of them with reverence; in our not despising them in the little ones, Matt. 18:10; in our being mindful of their presence, 1 Cor. 11:10; in our proclaiming to them the manifold wisdom of God, Eph. 3:10; in our causing them to gain insight into the mysteries of salvation, 1 Tim. 5:21; in our bringing them joy through our conversion, Luke 15:10; in our imitating them in doing God’s will, Matt. 6:10; in our feeling and living as one with them in the expectation of going to them, Heb. 12:22; and in our forming with them and all creatures one choir for the magnification of the name of the Lord, Ps. 103:20, 21.
In this consists the true veneration of angels.
And if this is correctly understood, then finally, in the fourth place, the doctrine of angels can also be a comfort and encouragement to us.
God has also revealed this doctrine to us so that he might strengthen us in our weakness and lift us up in our discouragement.
We do not stand alone in the spiritual struggle.
We are connected with a whole cloud of witnesses around us.
There is another, better world than this one, where God is served in a perfect manner.
It is for us an example, a spur and incentive, and at the same time an object of longing and our final destination.
Just as the angelic world descended to us in revelation, so the church ascends to it in Christ.
We will be like the angels and daily behold the face of our Father who is in heaven, Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pr._ III 7, 25; Love, _Theol. practica_, p. 205; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 320 f.; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ I 353; van Oosterzee, _Chr. Dogm._ §57, 10.
