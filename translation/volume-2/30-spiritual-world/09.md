## 9.

The service and activity of the angels correspond to this nature of theirs.
Here Scripture clearly distinguishes between an extraordinary and an ordinary service of the angels.
The extraordinary service first begins after the fall and became necessary through sin.
It is an important element in special revelation.
We see the angels appear first to guard the garden, Gen. 3:24, but then they appear and convey revelations, acting with blessing or punishment in the history of the patriarchs and the prophets and throughout the whole Old Testament.
They appear to Abraham, Gen. 18; Lot, Gen. 19; Jacob, Gen. 28:12; 32:1; they serve at the giving of the law, Heb. 2:2; Gal. 3:19; Acts 7:53; they take part in Israel’s struggle, 2 Kings 19:35; Dan. 10 v. 13, 20; they proclaim God’s counsel to Elijah and Elisha, to Ezekiel, Daniel, and Zechariah.
As if to refute the claim that they are remnants of polytheism and belong to a prehistoric period, their extraordinary service increases still further in the days of the New Testament.
They occur at the birth of Jesus, Luke 1:13, 30; 2:10; at his temptation, Matt. 4:11; accompany him throughout his entire earthly life, John 1:52; and appear especially at his suffering, Luke 22:43; resurrection, Matt. 28; and ascension, Acts 1:10.
Afterward they appear a few more times in the history of the apostles, Acts 5:19; 12:7, 13; 8:26; 27:23; Rev. 1:1, then cease their extraordinary service and first appear again at Christ’s return, Matt. 16:27; 25:31; Mark 8:38; Luke 9:26; 2 Thess. 1:7; Jude 14; Rev. 5:2, and so forth, when they wage war against God’s enemies, Rev. 12:7; 1 Thess. 4:16; Jude 9; gather the elect, Matt. 24:31; and cast the wicked into the fire, Matt. 13:41, 49.
The extraordinary service of the angels thus consists in their accompanying salvation history at its turning points.
They do not themselves bring about that salvation, but they nevertheless take part in its history.
They convey revelations, protect God’s people, fight his enemies, and render all kinds of service in the kingdom of God.
In this they are always active in the sphere of the church; even where they receive power over the forces of nature, Rev. 14:18; 16:5, or intervene in the fortunes of nations, this occurs in the interest of the church.
In doing so they never displace God’s sovereignty, nor are they mediators of fellowship between God and the human being.
Rather, they are ministering spirits for the sake of those who will inherit salvation.
They serve God in the kingdom of grace, not in that of nature.
This extraordinary service therefore also ceased of itself with the completion of revelation.
Whereas formerly they repeatedly had to convey special revelations and descended to earth, they are now rather examples to us, and we ascend to them.
As long as special revelation had not yet been completed, heaven approached earth and God’s Son descended to us.
Now Christ has appeared, and the Word of God has been spoken to us completely.
Thus the angels now look toward the earth in order to learn from the church the manifold wisdom of God.
What could angels still give us now that God himself gave us his own Son?

But Scripture also speaks of an ordinary service of the angels.
To this belongs first of all that they praise God day and night, Job 38:7; Isa. 6; Pss. 103:20; 148:2; Rev. 5:11; and Scripture gives the impression that they do so in audible sounds, even though we can form no conception of the speaking and singing of angels.
But to this ordinary service also belongs that they rejoice over the conversion of the sinner, Luke 15:10; watch over believers, Pss. 34:8; 91:11; protect the little ones, Matt. 18:10; are present in the church, 1 Cor. 11:10; 1 Tim. 5:21; follow it on its paths through history, Eph. 3:10; allow themselves to be instructed through it, Eph. 3:10; 1 Pet. 1:12; and carry believers into Abraham’s bosom, Luke 16:22.
They are thus active _coram Deo consistendo, hominibus piis assistendo, atque diabolis et hominibus malis resistendo_, Hollaz, p. 390.
Scripture usually remains with this general description of the ordinary service of angels and does not descend into particulars.
But theology was not satisfied with this; it specialized that service in all kinds of ways.
This occurred especially in the doctrine of guardian angels.
The Greeks and Romans had something similar in their δαιμονες and _genii_; not only did they ascribe a good and an evil _genius_ to every human being, but they also had genii of houses, families, societies, cities, lands, peoples, of earth, sea, world, and so forth.
The Jews accepted seventy angels of the nations with an appeal to Deut. 32:8 and Dan. 10:13, and further gave each Israelite an angel as guide, Weber, _System_, 161 f.
Christian theology soon adopted this.
The _Pastor_ of Hermas, _Mand._ VI 2, assigns two angels to every human being, εἱς της δικαιοσυνης και εἱς της πονηριας, and further placed the whole creation and the entire structure of the church under the care of angels, _Vis._ III 4.
This doctrine of guardian angels was developed with special fondness by Origen.
Sometimes in him every human being has a good and an evil angel; sometimes he adds that only the good angels of baptized Christians see the face of God; or he also says that only Christians and the virtuous have a guardian angel and that according to their merit they receive a lower or higher angel as guide.
But further, he also assumes particular angels for churches, lands, peoples, for arts and sciences, for plants and animals; thus Raphael is the angel of healing, Gabriel of war, Michael of prayer, and so forth; cf. _De princ._ I 8; III 3; _C. Cels._ V 29; VIII 31, and so forth.
And substantially all the church fathers taught in this way, although there was disagreement as to whether all human beings or only Christians had a guardian angel; whether every human being had only a good angel or also an evil one; when the guardian angel was given to the human being, at birth or at baptism, and when he was taken from one, upon attaining perfection or only at death.
It was also established among all that there are guardian angels not only of human beings but also of lands, peoples, churches, dioceses, provinces, and so forth, Schwane, _D. G._ II² 244.
Later this guardianship of the angels was in part restricted and in part still further extended.
It was restricted insofar as some, following Pseudo-Dionysius, _De coel. hier._ c. 13, taught that the three chief classes of angels—cherubim, seraphim, and thrones—served God alone in heaven, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 112.
It was extended insofar as scholasticism conceived the whole providence of God in nature and history, especially in the motion of the stars, as mediated by angels, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 70 art. 3; qu. 110 art. 1; _S. c. Gent._ III 78 sq.; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II dist. 14 p. 1 art. 3 qu. 2.
Guardian angels of human beings are generally accepted by Roman Catholic theologians and are also acknowledged in the _Catech. Rom._ IV c. 9 qu. 4 and 5.
For the rest, however, there is great difference of opinion among them concerning all the points named above; cf. Petavius, _De ang._ II c. 6-8; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ tract. III _De angelis_ c. 6; _Theol. Wirceb._, Paris, 1880, III 480; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ III 210; Oswald, _Angelologie_, 120 f., and so forth.
We find the same doctrine in Luther; cf. Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 345; but Lutheran theologians were for the most part more cautious, Gerhard, _Loci theol._ V cap. 4 sect. 15; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. pol._ I 450; Hollaz, _Syst. theol._, p. 390.
Calvin rejected the _angeli tutelares_, _Inst._ I 14, 7; _Comm._ on Ps. 91; Matt. 18:10, and most of the Reformed followed him; see especially Voetius, _Disp._ I 897 sq.
Only a few accepted guardian angels of human beings, Zanchi, _Op._ III 142 sq.; Bucanus, _Inst. theol._ VI 28; Maccovius, _Loci Comm._, p. 394; Rivet, Alsted, Vossius, and also Hugo Grotius on Matt. 18:10; cf. Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, 155; M. Vitringa II 117.
In modern times the doctrine of guardian angels again found support in Hahn; Weiss, _Bibl. Theol. des N. T._, 594; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 239; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 310; Martensen, _Dogm._ § 69; and others.
Further, the ordinary service of angels was also defined more precisely in this respect: that through their intercession they worked in heaven for the good of believers on earth.
This too was already taught by the Jews and also by Philo, and was then adopted by Origen, _C. Cels._ VIII 64, and the church fathers and established in the Roman Catholic symbols, _Catech. Rom._ III c. 2 qu. 5 n. 2.
The Lutheran confessional writings too still speak of this intercession, _Apol. Conf._ art. 21; _Art. Smalc._ II 2, and likewise the Lutheran dogmaticians, Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II 324.
By contrast, it was unanimously rejected by the Reformed.

For this special service of protection and intercession, an appeal is made to a few passages of Scripture, especially Deut. 32:8; Dan. 10:13, 20; Matt. 18:10; Rev. 1:20; 2:1, and so forth; Job 33:23; Zech. 1:12; Luke 15:7; Rev. 18:3; and especially also Tobit 12:12-15.
In itself, this doctrine of the protection and intercession of angels is not objectionable.
That God frequently or even regularly makes use of angels in general revelation as well as in special revelation is not impossible in itself; and neither is it absurd that the angels offer up their prayers for human beings to God, since they take an interest in their lot and follow the courses of the kingdom of God in the history of humanity.
But however little may be brought against it in itself, Scripture observes a restraint with respect to the protection and intercession of angels that must be our rule.
In Deut. 32:8 we read that at the division of the nations God had already thought of his people Israel and determined their dwelling לְמִסְפַּר בְּנֵי־יִשְׂרָאֵל, according to the number of the children of Israel, that is, in such a way that Israel received an inheritance sufficient for its number.
The LXX, however, translated these words with κατα ἀριθμον ἀγγελων and thereby occasioned the doctrine of angels of the nations.
The original text, however, teaches nothing of this here and therefore falls away entirely as a proof-text.
The case is different with Dan. 10:13, 20.
There it is said that the figure appearing to Daniel in v. 5 stood over against the prince of Persia and, assisted by Michael, who is called one of the first princes in v. 13 and the great prince for the children of Israel in 12:1; 10:21, drove away that prince of Persia and took his place with the kings of Persia.
Calvin and the Reformed usually thought of the Persian kings in connection with that prince of Persia.
But it appears that something else, namely, the guardian spirit of Persia, must be understood under that prince.
For, first, there is no doubt that Israel has such a guardian angel in Michael, who is called its prince, 10:13, 21; 12:1; second, the kings of Persia are clearly distinguished from that prince in 10:13; and third, the analogy requires that the spiritual power on one side wage war against a spiritual power on the other.
The book of Daniel therefore actually gives us the conception that the struggle between the kingdom of God and the kingdoms of the world is fought not only here on earth but also in the realm of spirits among the angels.
No more may or can be inferred from it.
It by no means implies that every land and people has its own angel.
But in the mighty conflict waged between Israel and Persia—that is, here essentially between the kingdom of God and that of Satan—angels appear on both sides who take part in the conflict and support the peoples.
Much less can it be inferred from Rev. 1:20, and so forth, that every church has its angel, for by the ἀγγελοι of the seven churches nothing other than their teachers are to be understood; they are after all regarded entirely as representatives of the churches; it is their works that are praised or blamed; the letters are written to them.
The doctrine of guardian angels finds its greatest support in Matt. 18:10, which undoubtedly contains the teaching that a particular class of angels is charged with the protection of the little ones, but which does not say in the least that an angel is assigned to every elect person.
This is found only in the apocryphal book Tobit.
But with this the doctrine of guardian angels also betrays its origin.
It is essentially of pagan origin.
It also leads to all kinds of subtle questions and vain disputes.
It does not know whether an angel is assigned to every human being—for example, even to the Antichrist, as Thomas thought, I qu. 113 art. 4—or only to the elect; whether only a good angel or also an evil angel accompanies everyone; when he is given to someone and taken from one; what his service is; and so forth.
Therefore we may go no further than to say that the care of particular interests on earth is also entrusted to particular classes of angels.
And so it is with the intercession of angels; it is taught in Tobit 12:16 but does not occur in Scripture.
In Job 33:23 the _Angelus increatus_ is spoken of.
Luke 15:7 indeed teaches that the angels rejoice over the conversion of a sinner and thus also certainly presupposes that they desire it, but it nevertheless speaks of no intercession in the proper sense.
And in Rev. 8:3 an angel indeed receives a censer with incense in order to make the prayers of the saints, sinful in themselves, sweet and pleasing to the Lord, but here too intercession is not mentioned in a single word.
The angel is simply a servant; he does not erect the altar; he does not himself prepare the incense but receives it and only allows the prayers to ascend to God with that incense.
He performs a service similar to that of the seraphim in Isa. 6:6, 7.
