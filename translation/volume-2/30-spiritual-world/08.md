## 8.

In all these properties of createdness, spirituality, rationality, and morality, the angels agree with human beings.
Precisely because in Scripture the unity of the angels comes to the fore and their mutual diversity recedes, there is a danger of overlooking the difference between angel and human being.
The resemblance between the two seems far to exceed the distinction.
Both human beings and angels are personal, rational, moral beings; both were originally created in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness; both received dominion, immortality, and blessedness; both are called sons of God in Scripture, Job 1:6; Luke 3:38.
And yet their distinction is most strictly maintained in Scripture by the fact that the human being, but never the angel, is said to have been created in God’s image.
In theology this distinction has been neglected in many ways.
According to Origen, the angels and human souls are of the same _species_; the union of the soul with the body is a punishment for sin and thus actually accidental.
Origen arrived at this because he taught that all inequality came from the creature.
In the beginning God created all things equal; that is, he created only rational beings and created them all equal—angels and souls.
Inequality arose among them through free will; some remained standing and received reward, others fell and received punishment; the souls were assigned to be bound to bodies.
The whole material world and all the diversity present in it therefore exist because of sin and the varying degree of sin; the world exists not to reveal God’s goodness but to punish sin.
This doctrine of Origen was indeed rejected by the church, and theology maintained the specific difference between human being and angel; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 75 art. 7.
Yet the thought has not been entirely overcome that, because angels are purely spiritual, they stand higher than human beings and therefore have even more, or at least as much, right to the name of image-bearers of God; John of Damascus, _De fide orth._ II 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 93 art. 3; commentators on _Sent._ II dist. 16; Oswald, _Angelologie_, 25, and so forth.
In the hierarchy of creatures, the angels, as purely spiritual beings, stand closest to God.
_Tu eras et aliud nihil, unde fecisti coelum et terram, duo quaedam unum prope te, alterum prope nihil; unum quo superior tu esses, alterum quo inferius nihil esset_, Augustine, _Conf._ XII 7.
_Non tantum substantiam a se longinquam, scil. naturam corpoream, producere debuit (Deus), verum etiam propinquam et haec est substantia intellectualis et incorporea_, Bonaventure, _Brevil._ II 6.
But Lutherans and Reformed too have lost sight of this distinction between human being and angel to too great a degree and have called the angels image-bearers of God; Calvin, _Inst._ I 15, 3; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ V c. 10; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XII 7; XIII 17; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ IX 187; Moor II 335; Gerhard, _Loc._ V c. 4 sect. 5; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._, 63.
Only a few, such as Theodoret, Macarius, Methodius, Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 8, and others contested this; Petavius, _De sex. dierum opif._ II c. 3 § 4–8.
Augustine expressly says, _Deus nulli alii creaturae dedit quod sit ad imaginem suam nisi homini_, in Thomas, I qu. 93 art. 3; cf. also Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ V 37.
However much resemblance there may be between human being and angel, there is no less great a difference.
There are indeed all kinds of features of the image of God in the angels, but the human being alone is the image of God.
That image of God lies not only in what human being and angel have in common but equally in what distinguishes them.
The principal points of difference are these.
First, the angel is spirit and, as spirit, complete; the human being, by contrast, is soul and body together; the soul without the body is incomplete.
The human being is therefore a rational but equally a sensory being; through the body the human being is bound to the earth, belongs to the earth, and the earth belongs to the human being.
And of that earth the human being is head and lord.
After the angels had already been created, God says that he will create the human being and give him dominion over the earth, Gen. 1:26.
Dominion over the earth is essentially proper to the human being, a part of the image of God, and is therefore also restored by Christ to his own, whom he makes not only prophets and priests but also kings.
The angel, however, however strong and mighty, is a servant in God’s creation, not lord of the earth, Heb. 1:14.
Second, as purely spiritual beings the angels are not bound to one another by ties of blood.
Among them there is no father and son, no physical coherence, no community of blood, no _consanguinitas_; however intimately connected ethically, they nevertheless stand separately alongside one another, so that many could fall while others remained standing.
In the human being there is therefore a reflection of the divine being, in which there are also persons who are one not only through will and affection but also through being and nature.
Third, there is therefore a humanity, but in this sense no “_Engelheit_.”
In one human being all fell, but in one the human race is also saved.
In humanity an Adam, and therefore also a Christ, is possible.
The angels are witnesses; human beings are the objects of God’s most glorious deeds, of the works of his grace.
The earth is the theater of God’s wonders; here the battle is fought, here the triumph of the kingdom of God is achieved, and the angels turn their eyes toward the earth and desire to look into the mysteries of salvation, Eph. 3:10; 1 Pet. 1:12.
Fourth, the angel may be the more powerful spirit, but the human being is nevertheless the richer spirit.
In understanding and power the angel far surpasses the human being.
But through the wonderfully rich relations in which the human being stands to God, the world, and humanity, he is deeper in soul and richer in feeling.
The relations entailed by sexual and family life, life in family, state, and society, and the life of labor, art, and science make every human being a world in miniature, which far surpasses the personality of the angels in versatility, depth, and richness.
Therefore the richest and most glorious attributes of God are also knowable and enjoyable only by the human being.
Angels experience his power, wisdom, goodness, holiness, and majesty; but the depths of God’s eternal mercies disclose themselves only to the human being.
Therefore only in the human being, or better still in humanity, is the full image of God unfolded in a creaturely manner.
Finally, let it be added here that the angels therefore also stand in an entirely different relation to Christ than human beings do.
That a relation exists between Christ and the angels is beyond doubt.
First, various passages of Scripture teach that all things—Ps. 33:6; Prov. 8:22f.; John 1:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; Eph. 3:9; Heb. 1:2—and specifically also the angels, Col. 1:16, were created through the Son, and thus he is the _Mediator unionis_ of all that has been created.
But in the second place, Eph. 1:10 and Col. 1:19, 20 contain the profound thought that all things also stand in relation to Christ as Mediator of reconciliation.
God has, namely, reconciled all things to himself through Christ and gathers them all under him as the Head.
The relation does not consist, as many have thought, in Christ having acquired _gratia_ and _gloria_ for the good angels, cf. Voetius, _Disp._ II 262 sq.; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._ XXXI c. 4 § 42; and see later under the doctrine of satisfaction.
Nor does it consist, as others judged, in angels being able to be called _membra ecclesiae_, cf. Gerhard, _Loci Theol._ XXII c. 6 sect. 9.
But it does consist in this: all things that have been disrupted and scattered by sin are again reconciled with one another in Christ, restored to their original relation, and gathered together under him as the Head.
Christ is thus Lord and Head of the angels, but not their Reconciler and Savior.
All things were created through him, and therefore they were also created unto him, so that he might give them back, reconciled and restored, to the Father.
But human beings alone form the church of Christ; it alone is his bride, the temple of the Holy Spirit, the dwelling of God.
