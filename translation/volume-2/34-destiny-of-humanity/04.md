## 4.

The covenant of works therefore includes another beautiful thought.
It not only realizes the true, full idea of religion; it also expresses that before the fall the human being, although created in God’s image, did not yet possess the highest.
On this point there is disagreement chiefly with the Lutherans.
For them, creation in the image of God is the realization of the highest idea of the human being; the ideal was fully attained in Adam; a higher state is not possible.
Adam had nothing to become but only to remain what he was; he partook of the full _gratiosa inhabitatio S. Trinitatis_.
He therefore also did not stand under a law that commanded him to do something positive; the law that applied to him had only a negative content; sin first brought him under the dominion of the law.
Hence among the Lutherans, as among the church fathers, the original state is often portrayed in greatly exaggerated terms; and the state to which believers are elevated in Christ is conceived as essentially equal to that of Adam before the fall.
For the Lutheran, everything concerning the believer centers on justification; if someone partakes of this, then he has enough, is completely satisfied and blessed.
Blessedness coincides entirely with forgiveness; there is no need to connect it backward with eternal election and forward with the whole Christian life, good works, and eternal life; neither predestination nor perseverance is necessary here; the Lutheran believer enjoys the present and is content with it; Luther in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 361; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst. des luth. u. ref. Lehrbegriffs_ I 90 f., 120 f.; II 185 f.; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 340; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I 375.
The Reformed, however, who walked in the footsteps of Augustine, thought differently.
According to them, Adam did not possess the highest.
The highest is, namely, material freedom and consists in no longer being able to err, sin, or die; in being elevated absolutely above all fear and anxiety, above every possibility of falling.
Believers immediately receive this highest good by grace through Christ.
They can no longer sin, 1 John 3:9; they can no longer die, John 3:16; through faith they immediately possess eternal, indefectible life; theirs is the _perseverantia sanctorum_; they can no longer be lost.
Christ therefore does not return his own to the state of Adam before the fall; he acquired and grants much more, namely, also that which Adam would have received if he had remained standing.
He places us not at the beginning but at the end of the path Adam had to traverse; he fulfilled not only passive but also active obedience, not only frees from guilt and punishment but also immediately grants by grace the right to eternal life.
Adam, however, did not yet possess this highest good; he did not yet have eternal life; he did indeed receive the _posse stare_ but not the _velle stare_; the _posse si vellet_ but not the _velle, quod posset_; he had the _posse non errare, peccare, mori_, but not yet the _non posse errare, peccare, mori_.
He still existed in the possibility of sin and death, and thus also in some fear and anxiety; his was not yet the perfect, immutable love that excludes all fear.
The Reformed therefore rightly said that this possibility, this mutable being-good, this still being able to sin and die, was no part, component, or content of the image of God but its boundary, limitation, and circumference; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, 181; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 104, 150.
It was therefore necessary for the image of God to unfold, completely overcome and nullify this possibility of sin and death, and shine in imperishable glory.
As a consequence of this conception of the _status integritatis_, the Reformed, in distinction from others, observed a praiseworthy sobriety in describing the state of paradise.
Adam was not Christ, the natural was not the spiritual, paradise was not heaven.
However much naturalism, which fails to recognize the power of sin and regards death as natural, must be resisted, supernaturalism, which makes the image of God consist in a supernatural addition to nature, must no less be avoided on the other side.
According to the Reformed, sin corrupted and devastated everything, but because it is not a substance it could not alter the essence, the substance, of creation.
The human being remained human as a sinner; and likewise all other creatures—earth, heaven, nature, plant, animal—despite the curse of sin and the dominion of corruption remained essentially and substantially the same.
Just as we saw above with religion, so it is also with everything else: sin took away no substance; grace returns no substance.
The _materia_ of all things is and remains the same, but the _forma_ given in creation was deformed by sin in order to be wholly reformed again in grace; Voetius, _Disp._ I 776.
This serious yet healthy Reformed conception of the state of paradise comes to expression on numerous points.
Against the Lutherans and Remonstrants, they maintained that Adam was indeed bound not only by the probationary command but also by the moral law.
He was not _exlex_, even though he fulfilled it without any compulsion, willingly and out of love.
That moral law was known to Adam by nature and therefore did not, like the probationary command, need to be revealed in a special manner.
It is essentially identical with the Ten Commandments, yet it bore a different form, for the law given at Sinai presupposes sin and therefore almost always speaks negatively: you shall not; whereas the moral law before the fall was much more positive.
But precisely because the moral law for Adam was by its nature wholly positive, it did not make the possibility of sin clear to Adam’s consciousness.
Alongside the _commands_, therefore, there had to come a _prohibition_; alongside the moral laws, a positive law; alongside the commands whose naturalness and reasonableness Adam perceived, a command that was in a certain sense arbitrary and contingent.
In the probationary command, the entire moral law was put at stake for Adam in a single throw; it embodied for him the dilemma: God or the human being, his authority or one’s own insight, unconditional obedience or independent inquiry, faith or doubt.
It was a dreadful test, which opened the way either to eternal blessedness or to eternal perdition.
Against the Cocceians, the Reformed maintained that the Sabbath commandment also belonged to that moral law; the human being before the fall did not yet enjoy the eternal, heavenly Sabbath; just as he was subject to the alternation of day and night, so too was he subject to that of six days of labor and rest on the seventh day; the day of rest and the workdays were therefore distinct even before the fall, and religious life even then required its own form and worship and day alongside the life of culture.
The magical, theosophical opinion that the two trees in the garden possessed in themselves the power to kill or to give life, whether by nature—Thomas, Suarez, Pererius, Reinhard, _Dogm._ 279, 289—or in a supernatural manner—Augustine, Bonaventure; whether already upon a single use—Bellarminus, _de gr. pr. hom._ c. 14—or upon repeated use—Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 97 art. 4; cf. also Heraut 941—was rejected by the Reformed with ever greater unanimity and decisiveness, even though some, such as Pareus, Rivetus, and Zanchius, _Op._ III 501, at first still assumed that eating the fruit also had an effect on physical life; Calvin, on Gen. 2:9, 3:22; _Inst._ IV 14, 12; 18; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ I c. 17; literature in M. Vitringa II 220 sq.
This conception is indeed in agreement with the Roman Catholic doctrine of the sacraments, but for that very reason also presents a serious objection for the Reformed; it makes life and death independent of the ethical condition, that is, of whether or not God’s command is obeyed; it presupposes that the human being would nevertheless have remained alive after the fall if only he had eaten _ex opere operato_ from the tree of life; it entails that eternal life could have been effected in the human being, either at once or gradually, by eating a physical fruit, and thus fails to recognize the distinction between the natural and the spiritual.
The Reformed therefore preferred to see in the tree of life a sign and seal of the covenant of works that bestowed life in a sacramental manner.
Likewise, all theosophical speculations about a male virgin, about the absence of sexual desire, and about magical generation were unanimously rejected by the Reformed as contrary to Scripture; Marck, _Hist. Parad._, p. 279 sq.
And just as the creation of the woman does not already presuppose a certain fall of Adam, neither was a _nova species_ added to the plant or animal kingdom after the entrance of sin; according to Voetius, wild and creeping animals were not first created after the fall but already on the sixth day; _Disp._ V 191.
And finally, Calvin, on Gen. 1:29, 9:3, and most of the Reformed—cf. Heidegger, _de libertate christianorum a re cibaria_, 1662; Voetius, _Disp._ IV 387, V 194; Coccejus, _S. Theol._ XX 17; Marck, _Hist. Parad._, p. 341; Moor, III 35–38, etc.—judged that eating meat had been permitted to the human being even before the flood and before the fall.
That Gen. 1:29 does not expressly speak of it cannot serve as an _argumentum e silentio_; in Gen. 1:30 only the plant world is divided between the human being and the animal, but the human being’s dominion and right over the animal world is not discussed there; these had already been granted to the human being in Gen. 1:28 and certainly include, especially also with a view to the fish, the right to kill and use animals.
Immediately after the fall, God himself makes animal skins, and Abel brings a sacrifice that was certainly also accompanied by a sacrificial meal.
Moreover, creophagy was certainly practiced before the flood and, if God first gave the right to it in Gen. 9:3, would have been unlawful and sinful before that time.
Gen. 9:1–5 gives no new command but renews the blessing of creation; the only new element is the prohibition against eating the flesh with its soul, that is, with its blood.
The ground for the prohibition, in Gen. 9:5–7, against killing a human being is not present in the case of animals; they, after all, were not made in God’s image.
And finally, it cannot be seen why the consumption of meat would have been permitted by God to the human being precisely after the fall and after the flood; conversely, one would expect that the human being’s right and dominion would have been restricted after the fall; that the consumption of meat would have been abolished to counteract brutalization; and that vegetarianism would have been regarded as much more in harmony with the condition into which the human being entered after the fall and the flood than creophagy; cf. Zöckler, _Lehre vom Urstand_, 273 f.; Köhler, _Bibl. Gesch._ I 33 f.
Reformed theology could judge so soundly in all these questions because it was deeply imbued with the thought that Adam did not yet possess the highest.
Sin undoubtedly has a cosmic significance; as is evident from death, it also works upon the physical realm and has brought the whole earth under the curse.
Without it, the development of humanity and the history of the earth would have been entirely different, even though we are unable to form any conception of this.
But on the other hand, the _status integritatis_ is not to be identified with the _status gloriae_; the former may not be inferred from the latter; Isa. 11:6, 65:25 are no more applicable to the state before the fall than Mark 12:25, Luke 20:36, 1 Cor. 6:13, etc.
The _forma_ has changed, but the _materia_ of the human being, plant, animal, nature, and earth is the same before and after the fall.
All the essential components that exist now also existed before the fall.
The distinction and inequality between man and woman, parents and children, brothers and sisters, relatives and friends; the many institutions and relations in social life, such as marriage, family, education, and so forth; the alternation of day and night, workdays and the day of rest, labor and recreation, months and years; the human being’s dominion over the earth through science and art, and so forth—all these have undoubtedly been modified and changed in appearance by sin, but they nevertheless have their origin and foundation not in sin but in creation, in the ordinances of God.
Socialism and communism, including as found among many Christian sects, rightly contend against the dreadful consequences of sin, especially also in the social sphere; but they do not stop there; they also come into conflict with the nature of things, with the ordinances of creation, and thereby consistently bear not a reformational but a revolutionary character.
