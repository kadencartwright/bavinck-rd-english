## 7.

It is all the more remarkable that Greek, Roman Catholic, and Reformed theologians declared themselves almost unanimously for creationism, while traducianism found acceptance only among the Lutherans.
This cannot be accidental; there must be a reason for it.
And that reason lies in the different conceptions of the essence and destiny of the human being.
First, Lutheran theology locates the image of God solely in certain moral qualities, in the _justitia originalis_.
As always, so here too it restricts its view to the ethical-religious and feels no need to connect this with the whole of cosmic existence and to understand it as a link in the entire counsel of God.
As a result, the essence of the human being receives its due neither over against the angels nor over against the animals.
For if the human being possesses this image of God, then he is nearly equal to the angels; the difference is insignificant in comparison with what they have in common; the angels too bear the image of God.
And if the human being lacks this image of God, then he sinks to the level of the animals and becomes a stick and a block; whatever then still distinguishes him from the animals has so little theological and religious value that it scarcely comes into consideration any longer; after all, the great difference lay in the image of God, and he has totally lost that.
The boundaries between human being and angel and between human being and animal are thus not sharply drawn here; the _justitia originalis_ is the be-all and end-all, while everything else in the human being is subordinate and theologically of almost no value.
For that reason it is also immaterial to Lutheran theology how the human being originates; or rather, what the human being has in common with the angel, namely, the image of God, the _justitia originalis_, must and can arise only by creation—that is a gift in the absolute sense—but everything else the human being possesses is transmitted in the same way as among the animals.
Roman Catholic and Reformed theologians, however, even though they sometimes still called the angels the image of God, from the beginning sought the image of God in the whole and entirely distinctive essence of the human being.
It certainly also consisted in the virtues of knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, but even in human beings these bore a character different from that which they bore in the angels; and it consisted not only in those virtues but also in all that is human in the human being.
It thus also consisted in this: that the πνευμα in the human being was organized from the outset for union with a σωμα, and that his σωμα was by nature adapted to the πνευμα.
Before and after the fall, in the _status integritatis_ and _corruptionis_, in the _status gratiae_ and _gloriae_, the human being always is and remains essentially distinct from angel and animal.
If he has the image of God, he does not become an angel; if he loses it, he does not become an animal; he always and eternally remains human and, insofar as this is so, always and eternally remains God’s image.
And this is sufficiently maintained only in creationism.
Because the human being _exists_ as a wholly unique being, he also _comes into existence_ in an entirely distinctive manner.
He is related to angel and animal and yet essentially distinct from both; in his dying he differs from both and therefore also in his origin; Adam’s creation differs from that of the animals and also from that of the angels.
Creationism alone adequately maintains the specific distinctiveness of the human being; it wards off both pantheism and materialism and respects the boundaries between human being and animal.

In the second place, it follows from the Lutheran conception of the image of God that the moral unity of the human race must recede behind physical descent.
Through the fall, human beings have lost, in and with the whole image of God, all spiritual and moral unity as well.
Natural religion, morality, and so forth are of almost no significance.
Physical descent alone holds them together, and it is at the same time the cause of their moral corruption.
Sin, which has deprived the human being of all religion and morality and of the whole image of God, can therefore become the portion of all not in an ethical way but only through physical descent.
It is indeed not a substance, although Luther and others, especially Flacius, used very strong expressions, but it is nevertheless in the first place a stain, a corruption, that has affected the whole human being and kills precisely everything religious and ethical in him.
Roman Catholic and Reformed theology maintained over against this, although each in its own way, that the unity of humanity was not only physical but also ethical in nature.
Physical descent, after all, is not enough; if it were, the animal species too would already form a unity.
Mere correspondence in moral virtues is also insufficient, for then the angels among themselves, and the angels together with human beings, would also form a unity.
Animal species do indeed physically descend from one blood, but they are not a _corpus morale_; and the angels are indeed a unity but are not related by blood.
The distinctiveness of the human being therefore requires that the unity of humanity be both physical and ethical.
And because original sin is not physical but only ethical, it too can be grounded only in the ethical, federal unity of humanity.
Physical descent is insufficient to explain it and runs the risk of materializing it.
The so-called realism of Shedd, for example, is unsatisfactory both in explaining sin from Adam and in explaining righteousness from Christ.
Another unity among human beings is needed to make them act together as a _corpus morale_, organically connected and mutually solidary also in an ethical respect.
And that is federal unity.
Upon the basis of physical unity an ethical unity must be erected.
Adam as progenitor is not enough; he must also be the covenant head of the human race, just as Christ, although not our progenitor in a physical sense, can nevertheless precisely as Covenant Head grant righteousness and salvation to his church.
And this moral unity of the human race can be maintained only in creationism.
For it bears a character of its own, is distinct both from the unity among animals and from that of the angels, and therefore also comes into being in a manner of its own, through physical descent and through a creative act of God, the two in connection with one another.

In the third and final place, by virtue of its conception of the image of God, Lutheran theology concerns itself little with the destiny of the human being.
Adam had everything he needed; he only had to remain what he was; the distinction between the _posse non_ and the _non posse peccare_ is of little importance; the _perseverantia_ is no higher good granted in Christ to his own.
And thus Adam had nothing higher to acquire for his descendants; he had only to transmit what he had; traducianism is sufficient for this; there is no place for the covenant of works and creationism.
But once again, Roman Catholic and Reformed theology proceeded from a different thought.
The destiny of the human being lies in heavenly blessedness, eternal life, the vision of God.
But he can attain this only in the way of obedience.
There is no proportion between this obedience and that life.
How, then, can that heavenly blessedness be granted to the human being as a reward for his works?
Rome says: because in the image of God he is granted a supernatural grace that can enable him to merit eternal life _ex condigno_.
The Reformed say: because God establishes a covenant with the human being and wills to grant him eternal life, not according to the worthiness of his works but according to God’s own gracious ordinance.
Both agree, however, that the destiny of the human being lies in eternal life, that this can be attained only in the way of moral obedience, and that God placed the decision in this matter for the human race into the hands of Adam.
And therefore both also arrived at creationism.
For this required both that all human beings were comprehended in the covenant head Adam and that they at the same time remained persons, individuals, human beings in their own right and with responsibility.
Physical descent alone would have made the sin we received from Adam a fate, a natural process, a disease that operates outside our will and therefore apart from our guilt.
But sin is not such a thing.
Nor does the righteousness that Christ, the last Adam, grants us bear that character.
Both presuppose a federal relation of humanity to its heads.
And so creationism maintains that every human being is an organic member of the great whole and at the same time occupies a distinct, independent place within that great whole.
It maintains the unity of the whole human race and at the same time the independent significance of every individual.
Human beings are not specimens, not numbers belonging to a species; neither are they individuals standing loosely alongside one another like the angels.
They are both at once: parts of the whole and individuals, _living stones_ in God’s building.
Creationism preserves the organic unity of humanity, both physical and moral, and at the same time respects the mystery of personality.
Every human being a member of the body of humanity and yet at the same time a thought of God all his own, with an eternal significance and an eternal destiny!
Every human being himself an image of God, and yet that image first unfolded fully in humanity as a whole!
By virtue of this unity, all humanity did indeed fall in Adam, its progenitor and head; but this fall is nevertheless no fate, no natural process; on the contrary, it rests upon a sovereign disposition of God.
And this disposition, however sovereign, is nevertheless so far from arbitrary that it instead presupposes the physical coherence of humanity, establishes and maintains its ethical unity, and can reveal and display in the most glorious manner not only God’s severity but also the riches of his grace.
For when Adam falls, Christ stands ready to take his place.
The covenant of grace can replace the covenant of works because both rest upon the same ordinances.
If we could not be condemned in Adam, we could not be acquitted in Christ.
However the first human being may choose, therefore, creation will not miss its destiny.
In Genesis, cosmogony immediately passes into geogony, and geogony into anthropogony.
World, earth, and humanity are one organic whole.
They stand, they fall, they are raised up together.
The _vestigia Dei_ in creation and the _imago Dei_ in the human being may be devastated and mutilated through the sin of the first Adam; through the last Adam and his re-creating grace they come to an all the more glorious manifestation.
Through the fall, or apart from the fall, the _status integritatis_ prepares the _status gloriae_, in which God will place his glory upon his creatures and be all in all.
