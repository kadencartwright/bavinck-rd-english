## 6.

From this vantage point new light falls upon the question of the propagation of the human race.
Opinions about it have been divided at all times.
The preexistence theory of Pythagoras, Plato, Plotinus, Philo, and the later Jews found acceptance among only a few Christians, Origen, _De princ._ I 6, 2; 8, 3; II 9, 2; _C. Cels._ I 32, 33; Henry More, _Mysterium pietatis_, 1660; Kant, _Relig._, ed. Rosenkranz, 44 f.; Schelling, _Werke_ I 7, S. 385 f.; Müller, _Sünde_ II⁵ 99 f., 504 f.
Scripture offered not the slightest ground for it; our soul is utterly unconscious of such a preexistence and so little regards the body as a prison and place of punishment that it shudders at death; moreover, the theory is rooted in a pagan dualism of spirit and matter, destroys the unity of the human race, and erases the distinction between human being and angel.
By contrast, in Christian theology the dispute between traducianism and creationism remained unresolved.
The former had many advocates in ancient times, such as Tertullian, _De an._ 19, 27; Rufinus, Macarius, Eunomius, Apollinaris, Gregory of Nyssa, and, according to Jerome, even the _maxima pars occidentalium_; but later, with a few exceptions, it was embraced only by the Lutherans—by Luther himself, who was at first a creationist, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 365, and then by Melanchthon, Gerhard, _Loc._ VIII, c. 8; Quenstedt I 519; Hollaz, _Ex._ 414; Philippi, _K. Gl._ III 103; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 348; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ I 400; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._ 106 f.; Cremer in Herzog² 14, 27; etc.
In ancient times creationism was defended by Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ IV 26; Lactantius, _Inst._ III 18; Hilary, Pelagius, Cassian, Gennadius, Theodoret, Athanasius, Gregory of Nazianzus, Cyril of Alexandria, Ambrose, and others.
Jerome already spoke of it as the doctrine of the church.
Greek, scholastic, and Roman Catholic theologians are therefore all creationists, Lombard, _Sent._ II 17, 18; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 90 and 118; _C. Gent._ II 86–89; Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ IV 2; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 172 f.; Kleutgen, _Philos._ II 583 f.; and only a few, such as Klee, _Dogm._ II² 313 f., show sympathy for traducianism.
With few exceptions, such as Sohnius, _Op._ II 563; Martyr, _Loci_, p. 81; and in modern times especially Shedd, _Dogm. Th._ II 22, 75; III 250, the Reformed likewise sided with creationism, Calvin on Heb. 12:9; Zanchius, _Op._ III 609; Polanus V 31; Voetius, _Disp._ I 798; Moor II 1064; III 289; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ II 4, § 7–9; etc.
Some prefer to leave the question undecided, Augustine, _De anima et ejus orig._; _De Gen. ad litt._ X; _Epist._ 166 _ad Hieron._; _Retract._ II 45, 46; Gregory the Great, Leo the Great, Isidore, Chemnitz, Buddeus, Musculus, Piscator, Maresius; or seek a mediation, Leibniz, _Theod._ I 91; Rothe, _Ethik_ § 136; Ebrard, _Dogm._ I 327 f.
In fact, traducianism and creationism are fairly evenly matched in the strength of their arguments.
Traducianism appeals to the creation of Eve, whose soul is not mentioned separately and who is therefore called ἐξ ἀνδρος, Gen. 2:21; 1 Cor. 11:8; to Holy Scripture’s manner of speaking, according to which descendants are comprehended in the loins of their fathers and have come forth from their thigh, Gen. 46:26; Heb. 7:9, 10; to the word ידע, “to know,” which would at the same time designate a spiritual act; to the completion of creation on the seventh day, Gen. 2:2; to the fact that animals too can bring forth their own kind, Gen. 1:28; 5:3; 9:4; John 3:6; and above all also to the inheritance of sin and of all kinds of psychical characteristics.
Creationism, by contrast, finds support in the creation of Adam’s soul, Gen. 2:7; in many texts such as Eccl. 12:7; Zech. 12:1; especially Heb. 12:9; cf. Num. 16:22; 17:16, of which even Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._ 114, says: _eine klassischere Beweisstelle für den Creatianismus kann es kaum geben_; and then above all in the spiritual, simple, indivisible, immortal nature of the soul.
And just as traducianism and creationism are fairly evenly matched in their arguments, so both are equally incapable of resolving the difficulties.
Traducianism explains neither the origin of the soul nor the transmission of sin.
As regards the former, it must arrive either at the doctrine that the child’s soul already existed beforehand in the parents and ancestors, and thus at a kind of preexistence theory; or at the view that the soul is potentially comprehended in the seed of the man or the woman or both, that is, at materialism; or at the view that it is in some way produced by the parents, that is, at creation, now not by God but by the human being.
And as regards the latter, traducianism can contribute nothing to the explanation, because sin is not material and is no substance but a moral quality, moral guilt, and moral corruption.
Creationism stands equally powerless before these problems.
Even if it conceives the creation of the soul as organically as possible and says with Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 17, _creando infundit eas Deus et infundendo creat_, it nevertheless remains perplexed by the question of when the soul is created in the embryo, cf., for example, Polanus V 31; Bucanus VIII 26, and how the _anima rationalis_ replaces the _anima vegetativa_ and _anima sensitiva_ that had already animated the embryo, cf. Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 118, art. 2; _C. Gent._ II 59, 68.
And creationism can explain original sin only by saying either that the soul, first created pure by God, is contaminated by the body, which, however, makes sin material, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 31; or that at the same moment in which God creates the soul, he also holds it guilty and makes it unclean, Voetius, _Disp._ I 1097; Turretin, _Th. El._ IX 12; Moor III 289.
No wonder, therefore, that many Pelagians and Roman Catholics denied original sin in a positive sense precisely on the ground of creationism, Gangauf, _Met. Psych. des h. August._ 259; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch._ 155 f., 163 f., 189 f.
Arguments and objections therefore do not decide the choice between traducianism and creationism; they would rather compel us to end with a _non liquet_.
