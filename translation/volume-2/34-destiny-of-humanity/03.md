## 3.

It can certainly be objected against the doctrine of the covenant as it was developed in Reformed theology that it descended too far into details and was treated too scholastically. The later theologians still defended the doctrine, but they no longer felt its significance and theological and religious importance. Its soul had departed, and opposition was therefore easy. But the doctrine of the covenant of works rests on a scriptural foundation and is of outstanding value. All higher life among rational and moral creatures bears the form of a covenant. In general, a covenant is an agreement between persons by which, for the avoidance of some evil or the acquisition of some good, they voluntarily obligate and bind themselves to one another under definite conditions. Such an agreement, whether entered into tacitly or expressly stipulated, is the ordinary form of human beings’ life and work together. Love, friendship, marriage, and all social cooperation in commerce, industry, science, art, and so forth ultimately rest on the foundation of a covenant, that is, on mutual faithfulness and all kinds of generally acknowledged moral obligations. It therefore cannot surprise us that the highest, richest life of the human being, that is, religion, also bears this character. In Scripture the covenant is the fixed form in which the relation of God to his people is represented. Even where the name does not occur, we nevertheless always see the two, as it were, in conversation and negotiation with one another: God inviting the human being to conversion, reminding him of his obligations, and binding himself to bestow every good. Later, in the doctrine of the covenant of grace, the biblical concept of בְרִית will be brought to light. Here it is enough to recall this general notion. Even if the name _covenant_ never occurred in Scripture for Adam’s religious relation to God, not even in Hos. 6:7, the religious life of the human being before the fall nevertheless bears the character of a covenant. The Reformed were never so narrow as to insist upon the name provided the matter itself was established: _de vocabulo dubitetur, re salva_. But behind opposition to the name there was usually concealed opposition to the thing itself. And this neither can nor may be surrendered, because covenant is the essence of true religion.

For, first, God is Creator and the human being creature, and with this an infinite distance is posited between them. No communion, no religion, seems possible between them; there is only difference, distance, infinite separation. If God in his sovereign highness and majesty remains exalted above the human being, then no religion is possible, at least no religion as communion with God. The relation between them is then completely represented by the names lord and servant. Even the image of the potter and the clay is far too weak to designate that relation, for the clay still possesses a being and thus rights independently of and over against the potter, whereas the human being has nothing that he is or has outside and over against God. Thus if there is truly to be religion, if there is to be communion between God and the human being, if their relation is indeed but not solely to be that of a lord to his servant, of a potter to his clay, but also that of a king to his people, of a father to his son, of a mother to her child, of an eagle to its young, of a hen to her chicks, and so forth—that is, if not one single relation but every kind of relation of dependence, submission, obedience, friendship, love, and so forth among human beings is to have its archetype in religion and receive its fulfillment there—then religion must bear the character of a covenant. For then God must descend from his highness, stoop down to his creature, communicate and reveal and give himself to the human being; then he who inhabits the high and exalted and eternal place must also dwell with the one who is of a humble spirit, Isa. 57:15. This is nothing other than a covenant. When religion is called a covenant, it is thereby designated as true, genuine religion. No religion has ever understood this. All peoples either pantheistically draw God down into the creaturely or deistically exalt him infinitely above it; in neither case does it come to true communion, covenant, genuine religion. But Scripture maintains both: God is infinitely great and condescendingly good; he is Sovereign but also Father; he is Creator but also archetype; in a word, he is the God of the covenant.

Second, it is clear that a creature can bring and possess no right whatever over against God. This is impossible by the nature of the case. A creature as such owes everything it is and has to its Creator; it can claim nothing from God; it can boast of nothing; it has no rights or demands whatever. There is never any question of merit on the creature’s part before God, nor can there be. The relation of _Creator_ and _creatura_ excludes every merit of the latter in principle and altogether. This applies not only after but equally before the fall. Then too the human being was a creature, without claim, without rights, without merit. When we have done all that is commanded of us, we are still δουλοι ἀχρειοι, Luke 17:10. The religion of Holy Scripture is nevertheless of such a nature that in it the human being can, as it were, assert rights over against God. For he has liberty to come to him with prayer and thanksgiving, to address him as his Father, to trust in him in every need and in death, to desire every good from him, even to expect blessedness and eternal life from him. This is possible solely because in condescending goodness God grants rights to his creature. Every right of creatures is a bestowed good, a gift of grace, unmerited and unobligated. Every reward is by grace from God’s side; no _meritum de condigno_ or even _de congruo_ is possible. True religion can therefore be nothing other than a covenant; it has its origin in condescending goodness, in the grace of God. Religion bears that character both before and after the fall. Religion is one, just as the moral law and the destiny of the human being are one. The covenant of works and the covenant of grace differ not in their final end but only in the way that leads to that end. In both there is one Mediator—then of union, now of reconciliation; in both there is one faith—then in God, now in God through Christ; in both there is one hope, one love, and so forth. Religion is always essentially the same; it differs only _in forma_.

Third, the human being is a rational and moral being. God created him in this way and therefore also treats him in this way; he sustains what he created. God therefore does not compel the human being, for compulsion conflicts with the nature of a rational creature. He does not treat him as an irrational creature, as a plant or an animal, as a stick and a block, but deals with him as a rational, moral, self-determining being. He wills that the human being be free and serve him in freedom, willingly, in love, Ps. 110:3. Religion is freedom, is love, which does not allow itself to be compelled. And therefore from the outset it too must bear the form of a covenant in which God appears not as compelling but as counseling, admonishing, warning, inviting, entreating, and in which the human being serves God not by compulsion or force but willingly, with his own free consent, moved by love to reciprocal love. Religion is at bottom not a duty but a privilege. It is not a work by which we benefit God and bring him something and have a claim to reward. Rather, it is grace to be permitted to serve him. God is never indebted to us; we are always indebted to him for the good works we do, Article 24, Belgic Confession. From his side there is always the gift; from our side always and only thanksgiving. Religion can therefore be conceived only in the form of a covenant; only in this does it come to its full realization.

God also made such a covenant with the first human being. The splintered elaboration of this doctrine may be set wholly aside. But the matter itself is established: after the human being was created in God’s image, God indicated his destiny to him and the way in which alone he could attain it. For the human being could know the moral law without extraordinary revelation; it was written in his heart. But the probationary command is positive; it is not given with human nature itself and could become known to him only because God communicated it to him. Neither was it self-evident that keeping that command bestowed eternal life. In this sense the _foedus operum_ is not a _foedus naturae_. At first this was not yet seen so clearly; cf. Gomarus, _De foedere_. But gradually it was clearly taught that God was in no respect obligated to bestow heavenly blessedness and eternal life upon the human being who kept his law and thereby did nothing other than what he was obligated to do. Here there is no natural connection between work and reward, Cocceius, _De foedere_ II 23 sq.; Burman, _Synops. Theol._ II 8, 2, 4; Marck, _Hist. Parad._, p. 479; Cloppenburg, _Exerc. theol._ VI, disp. 5; _De foed._ I 8 sq.; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ I 4, § 10–23; Leydecker, _Fax Verit._, p. 399 sq.; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ IX 227 f., 261 f.; X 288 f., 318 f.; Brahe, _Aanm. over de vijf Walch. art._, p. 125 f. And this is the truth contained in Rome’s doctrine of the _donum superadditum_. Eternal life is and remains an unmerited gift of God’s grace. But because Rome does not know the doctrine of the covenant of works, from this gracious gift of eternal life it infers that the image of God in the human being must also be supernatural and then, through the supernatural power granted in that image of God, nevertheless has eternal life merited again _ex condigno_. Under the appearance of honoring grace, it thus reintroduces the meritoriousness of good works. The Reformed, however, maintained on the one hand that the image of God in the human being was natural and that the human being who was God’s image could know the moral law without supernatural revelation and keep it without supernatural power. On the other hand, they maintained that a higher state of blessedness than the one in the paradise on earth could by the nature of the case never be merited but could only be bestowed by God’s free disposition. And they united both thoughts in the _foedus operum_. It rests upon a free, particular, gracious disposition of God; it proceeds from him; he determines all its parts—condition and fulfillment, keeping and reward, transgression and punishment. It is monopleuric in origin; it is added to creation in God’s image. The human being on his side, because he was created in God’s image, rests in it and sees in this covenant a way opened to higher blessedness. The _foedus operum_ thus does justice to both: the sovereignty of God, which includes the creature’s dependence and the unmeritoriousness of all his works, and the grace and goodness of God, which nevertheless wills to bestow upon the creature a blessedness higher than the earthly. As the probationary command stands in relation to the moral law, so the covenant of works stands in relation to creation in God’s image. With the probationary command the whole moral law stands or falls; with the covenant of works the entire image of God in the human being stands or falls. The covenant of works is the way for the human being created in God’s image and not yet fallen to heavenly blessedness.
