# § 34. The Destiny of the Human Being

## 1.

Although Adam was created in God’s image, he was not immediately that image in its fullest sense, nor was he so in himself alone.
Only when we include in it the destiny of the human being, both for this life and for the life to come, does the image of God stand before us in all its richness.
In 1 Cor. 15:45–49 Paul sets the two covenant heads, Adam and Christ, over against one another and compares them, not so much, as in Rom. 5:12–21 and 1 Cor. 15:22, with respect to what they effected, but with regard to their nature and person.
Here the comparison descends to the greatest depth and penetrates to the root of the difference between them.
The whole Adam, both before and after the fall, is set over against the whole Christ, both after and before the resurrection.
The first human being is by virtue of creation ψυχη ζωσα, ψυχικος, ἐκ γης χοικος; but the second human being is through his resurrection πνευμα ζωοποιουν, πνευματικος, ἐξ οὐρανου; cf. W. Lütgert, _Der Mensch aus dem Himmel_, _Greifswalder Studien, H. Cremer zum 25 j. Prof. dargebracht u. s. w._, Gütersloh, 1895, S. 207–228.
Although Adam was created in God’s image, since he was earthly, from the earth, he was dependent on the earth; after all, he needed food and drink, light and air, day and night, and thus did not yet possess a pneumatic, glorified body elevated above all those needs; his psychical body had not yet fully become an organ of the spirit.
As such Adam stands below Christ: Adam is the first, Christ is the second and the last, who presupposes Adam and follows him; Adam is the lesser and lower, Christ the greater and higher; Adam thus pointed forward to Christ, and even before the fall he was already a type of Christ; Christ was already in view in Adam’s creation; the whole creation, including that of the human being, was infralapsarian; the natural comes first, afterward the spiritual.
What Paul expounds here in breadth and depth is grounded in Gen. 1–2 itself.
Although the human being bore within himself πνευμα and a breath of life, he became, like the animals, a living soul.
He was given fruit from plant and tree for food, Gen. 1:29; a paradise for a dwelling place, Gen. 2:8 f.; a woman as helper, Gen. 2:18 f.; a command as rule, Gen. 2:16, 17; and a threat of punishment in case of transgression, Gen. 2:17.
From all this it is evident that the first human being, however highly placed, did not yet possess the highest.
There is a very great difference between the psychical and the pneumatic, between the _status integritatis_ and the _status gloriae_.
After the resurrection the belly and food will be done away with, 1 Cor. 6:13, but in Adam both were present; in heaven the children of God will no longer marry but will be like the angels, Matt. 22:30, but Adam needed the help of a woman.
Adam therefore stood not at the end but at the beginning of the way; his condition was provisional and temporal, one that could not remain as it was and had to pass either into higher glory or into a fall into sin and death.
Death was attached to the transgression of the command, but by contrast life—and indeed eternal life—was attached to keeping it.
For not only does the conscience of us all testify that in keeping God’s commandments there is great reward and that transgressing them brings punishment, but Holy Scripture also repeatedly declares this; it sums up under the name of life, of eternal life, all blessedness connected with doing God’s commandments, and in both the covenant of works and the covenant of grace it acknowledges only one highest ideal for the human being, namely eternal life, Lev. 18:5; Ezek. 20:11; Ps. 19:12; Matt. 19:17; Luke 10:28; Gal. 3:12.
Thus Adam still stood at the entrance; he did not yet possess this reward, eternal life, but had to acquire it; he could still err, sin, fall, and die.
His relation to God was such that he could increase more and more in his fellowship with God but could also still fall away from it.
This distinctive relation is perhaps compared with a covenant in one place in Scripture.
In Hos. 6:7 the Lord says of Israel and Judah that, despite all the labor expended upon them, they כְּאָדָם עָבְרוּ בְרִית, LXX ὡς ἀνθρωπος, Vulg., _sicut Adam_.
The translation “like a human being” is burdened by the objection that it is said precisely of human beings in general that they transgressed the covenant; and the rendering “as a human being’s (covenant)” would in any event require that the word כאדם be placed not after the subject הֵמָּה but after the word it qualifies, בְרִית.
Thus only the translation “like Adam” remains.
This then implies that the command given to Adam was in essence a covenant, because its purpose, like that of God’s covenant with Israel, was to grant Adam eternal life in the way of obedience.
This is further strengthened by the parallel Paul draws between Adam and Christ in Rom. 5:12–21.
Just as the obedience of one human being, namely Christ, and the grace granted in him brought acquittal, righteousness, and life for humanity, so also the one transgression and offense of the one human being became for humanity the cause of condemnation, sin, and death.
With Adam it is as with Christ.
We in fact stand in the same relation to him.
He is a type of Christ, our head, from whom, on account of his transgression, guilt and death come to us.
He is the cause of the death of us all; we all die in Adam, 1 Cor. 15:22.
Here too Adam’s relation to God is a covenant relation, but now it is described not so much from God’s side as from the side of those who are included under Adam as head in that covenant.
