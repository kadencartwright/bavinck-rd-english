## 5.

Finally, the doctrine of the _foedus operum_ contains a third thought of the richest religious and ethical significance. Adam was not created _alone_; as man he was incomplete in himself. He lacked something for which no lower creature could compensate him, Gen. 2:20. As man alone, therefore, he was not yet the fully unfolded image of God. The creation of the human being in God’s image is not completed on the sixth day until God creates both, man and woman in union with each other, cf. אותם, Gen. 1:27, in his image. Yet this creation of man and woman together in God’s image is also not the end but the beginning of God’s way with humanity. It is not good for the man; neither is it good for man and woman to be alone. God immediately pronounces over both of them the blessing of multiplication, Gen. 1:28. Not the individual human being, and not man and woman together, but the whole of humanity is the fully unfolded image of God, his son, his offspring. The image of God is far too rich to be realized completely in one single human child, however richly gifted. Only in a humanity of millions of members can it be unfolded to some degree in its depth and riches. Just as the _vestigia Dei_ are spread across many, many works, both in space and time, so the _imago Dei_ can be drawn at full length only in a humanity whose members exist both successively and alongside one another. But just as the cosmos is a unity and receives its head and lord in the human being, and just as the _vestigia Dei_ scattered throughout the whole world are comprehended in and elevated to the _imago Dei_ of the human being, so this humanity in turn must be conceived as an organism which precisely as such is the fully unfolded image of God. Not as a heap of souls on a piece of ground, not as an incoherent aggregate of individuals, but as created from one blood, as one household, as one family, humanity is the image and likeness of God. To this humanity belong also its development, its history, its ever-expanding dominion over the earth, its progress in knowledge and art, its subjection of all creatures. All this too is an unfolding of the image and likeness of God in which the human being was created. Just as God did not reveal himself only once at creation but continues and increases that revelation from day to day and from age to age, so the image of God is not an immutable magnitude but explains and unfolds itself in the forms of space and time. It is at once _Gabe_ and _Aufgabe_; it is an unmerited gift of grace bestowed immediately in creation upon the first human being, but it is at the same time the principle and seed of an exceedingly rich, glorious development. Only humanity as a whole, as one perfect organism comprehended under one head and spread over the whole earth, proclaiming the truth of God as prophetess, consecrating itself to God as priestess, and ruling the earth and all creation as queen—only this is the fully finished image, the most eloquent and striking likeness of God. Scripture teaches all this clearly when it says that the church is the bride of Christ, the temple of the Holy Spirit, the dwelling place of God, the new Jerusalem into which all the glory of the nations is gathered. For this indeed portrays the _status gloriae_ that will now be attained through sin, but religion, moral law, and final destiny are essentially the same in the covenant of works and the covenant of grace. Both concern a kingdom of God, a holy humanity in which God can be all in all.

Only one point in this representation still requires some further discussion. Humanity cannot be conceived as a perfect organism unless it is joined and comprehended in a head. In the _foedus gratiae_ Christ occupies that rank; he is the head of the church. In the _foedus operum_ that place was occupied by Adam. Eve was therefore already created from Adam so that he would be the _principium totius speciei_ and the unity of the human race would be rooted in the unity of its origin. The woman therefore most certainly shares in human nature and the image of God and represents both in a distinctive way and according to her own nature, but she shares in both not over against others but alongside them and in connection with the man. She is ἐκ ἀνδρος, δια τον ἀνδρα, δοξα ἀνδρος; she is not without the man. And the man too, although head of the woman and εἰκων και δοξα θεου because he is in the first place bearer of dominion, is nevertheless not complete without the woman either; he too is not without the woman, for she is the mother of all living, 1 Cor. 11:7–12; Eph. 5:22 f. Above all, however, Paul points out this unity of humanity when he places Adam over against Christ, Rom. 5:12–21; 1 Cor. 15:22, 45–49. Humanity is not only physically from one blood, Acts 17:26; that would not be enough for humanity, for it also applies to all those animal species created in the beginning. Moreover, Christ, the antitype of Adam, is not our progenitor; we did not physically proceed from him, but he himself is from Adam according to the flesh. In this respect Adam and Christ are not alike. The correspondence lies in the fact that in an ethical sense humanity stands in the same relation to Adam as it does to Christ. In the same way that Christ is the cause of our righteousness and life, Adam is the cause of our sin and death. God reckons and judges the whole human race in one human being. The Reformed expressed this in their doctrine of the _foedus operum_. Only in it does not merely the physical but the ethical unity of humanity come into its own. And this is necessary for humanity as an organism. In general, the architectonic law everywhere requires the monarchical system. A work of art must be governed by one thought; a sermon must have one theme; a church is completed in its tower; the man is head of the family; in a kingdom the king is bearer of authority. The human race as one organic whole, as an ethical _Gemeinschaft_, cannot be conceived without a head. In the _foedus operum_ Adam occupied that place. The probationary command proves that he held an entirely exceptional position. He was not only progenitor but also head, representative of the whole human race; his act was decisive for all. Just as the fate of the whole body rests with the head, which thinks, judges, and decides for all the members; just as the welfare of a family depends on the man and father; just as a sovereign can be a blessing or a curse to thousands and millions of subjects, so the fate of humanity was placed in Adam’s hands. His transgression became the fall of his whole race, but his obedience would also have been life for all his descendants, as the antitype Christ proves. If without our knowledge we could not be subjected to condemnation in Adam, neither could we be received into grace in Christ without our action. The covenant of works and the covenant of grace stand and fall together. The same law applies to both. On the basis of physical descent an ethical unity is built that enables humanity, in accordance with its nature, to act as one organism and most closely binds its members to one another not only by bonds of blood but also by common participation in blessing and curse, sin and righteousness, death and life.
