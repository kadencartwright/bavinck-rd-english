## 2.

In Christian theology this rich thought of Scripture has not always received its due.
The naturalistic conception located the image of God solely in the disposition, the _nuda potentia_, freedom of the will, and formal personality, and even regarded death as natural.
The image of God, or at least the likeness, consisted far more in what the human being had to acquire through the exertion of his own strength than in what was immediately granted him at creation.
Over against this, the supernaturalistic conception went to the opposite extreme and ascribed an entirely supernatural character to the _status integritatis_.
Not only was the _justitia originalis_ regarded as a _donum supernaturale_, immortality viewed as a special _beneficium conditoris_, and all susceptibility to suffering and pain denied to Adam, Augustine, _De civ._ XIV 26; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 97, art. 2; but some, such as Gregory of Nyssa, John of Damascus, Böhme, and others, judged that because the human being before the fall was immortal, he had no need of food, Petavius, _De sex dier. opif._, c. 7; excretion would in any event have taken place without any _indecentia_, Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 97, art. 3; according to most church fathers, scholastics, Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Remonstrant theologians, and also according to some Reformed theologians such as Zwingli, Musculus, Martyr, Zanchius, Junius, Piscator, and others, the human diet would have consisted solely of plants and not of meat; generation would have occurred without any sensual desire, and children would not have been born speechless and helpless and would have grown up very quickly, Augustine, _De pecc. mer. et rem._ I 37, 38; Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 20; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 98, art. 1; many went further still and believed that propagation would not have taken place _per coitum_ at all, Augustine, _Retract._ I 10; Gregory of Nyssa, _De hom. opif._ 16, 17; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 30; the human being was first created androgynously, and the creation of woman is already evidence of the fall, the Jews in Weber, _Syst._ 202 f.; Erigena, _De div. nat._ II 6, 10, 23; IV 12; Böhme, Oetinger, Baader, Schelling, Lange, _Dogm._ II 324 f.; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._ 102 f.; Hofmann, _Weiss. u. Erf._ I 65 f.; _Schriftbew._ I² 403 f.; and others; woman was thus properly neither the image of God nor a participant in human nature, cf. Augustine, _De trin._ XII 7; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 93, art. 4; qu. 99, art. 2; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II, dist. 16, art. 2, qu. 2; dist. 20, art. 1, qu. 6; Gerhard VIII, c. 6; Quenstedt II, p. 15; Jansen, _Gesch. des deutschen Volkes_ VI, 1888, S. 395-397.
Origen even derived corporeality and all inequality among human beings from a fall of preexistent souls, _C. Cels._ I 32, 33; _De princ._ II 9; or a body entirely different from ours was ascribed to the human being before the fall, cf. the Ophites in Hagenbach, _D. G._ § 62; Böhme, Antoinette Bourignon, Baader, and others.
And in connection with all this, paradise was frequently understood very idealistically or even allegorically; animals are said not to have died, no wild or unclean animals are said to have existed, the rose would have bloomed without thorns, the air was much purer, the water much softer, and the light much brighter, for example, Luther on Gen. 3; cf. Strauss, _Gl._ I 700 f.

Yet everyone acknowledged that Adam did not yet possess the highest good.
This was naturally implied in the probationary command, freedom of choice, and the possibility of sin and death.
Augustine in particular clearly distinguished between the _posse non peccare_ and _posse non mori_ that Adam possessed and the _non posse peccare_ and _non posse mori_ that, with glorification, would have been granted to the first human being in the event of obedience and are now bestowed by grace upon the elect, _De civ._ XXII 30; _De corr. et grat._ 11; _Enchir._ 104-107; _De Gen. ad litt._ III 2; VI 25; _Op. imp. c. Jul._ V 58; VI 5; and so forth.
Augustine even spoke of the relation in which Adam originally stood as a covenant, _testamentum_, _pactum_, _De civ._ XVI 27; and the translation of the words כאדם as “like Adam” led many to a similar conception, Marck, _Histor. Parad._ II 6, 7.
Substantially, therefore, the doctrine of what was later called the covenant of works is already found among the church fathers.
In the state of Adam as it was conceived by scholastic, Roman Catholic, and Lutheran theologians lie all the elements that were later summarized particularly by the Reformed in the doctrine of the _foedus operum_; cf. Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 19, 20.
Scripture frequently uses the name covenant for the relation into which believers come to stand toward God through Christ.
Zwingli and Bullinger already took up these thoughts of Scripture in order to defend the unity of the Old and New Testaments against the Anabaptists; cf. later in the doctrine of the _foedus gratiae_.
Now when, following the example of Scripture, the Christian religion was presented as a covenant, Paul’s parallel between Adam and Christ gave occasion to conceive the _status integritatis_ as a covenant too.
In distinction from the _foedus gratiae_, this was then called the _foedus naturae_ or _operum_.
It was called the covenant of nature not as though it followed of itself and naturally from the nature of God or of the human being.
Rather, it was so called because the foundation upon which the covenant rested, that is, the moral law, was known to the human being by nature, and because it was established with the human being in his original state and could be kept by the human being with the powers granted him in creation, without supernatural grace.
When later the name gave rise to misunderstanding, it was preferably replaced by _foedus operum_, covenant of works; and it bore this name because eternal life in this covenant could be obtained only in the way of works, that is, by keeping God’s commandments.
As the parallel of the _foedus gratiae_, this covenant was now taught and developed by the Reformed with special predilection.
It already occurs in Ursinus, _Catech. major_, qu. 9; Olevianus, _De subst. foed. grat._, 1585, I § 8, 9; II § 5; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ VI 33; Junius, _Theses theol._, c. 25; Wollebius, _Theol. Christ._ I, c. 8; and so forth; in this country first in Trelcatius, _Loci Comm._, 1587, p. 276; Gomarus, _Oratio de foedere Dei_, 1594; Trelcatius Jr., _Instit. Theol._, 1604, p. 131; Cloppenburg, _Disp. de foedere Dei_, 1643; Coccejus, _Summa doctr. de foedere_, 1648; and thereafter in all theologians, for example, Mastricht, _Theol._ III, c. 12; Marck, _Historia Parad._, 1705, II, c. 6 sq.; Moor III 52-108; M. Vitringa, c. 8; Comrie, _Verhandeling over het verbond der werken_, prefixed to his edition of Boston’s _Beschouwing van het verbond der genade_, 1741; Brahe, _Aanm. over de Walch. artikelen_, 1758, p. 123 f.; _Examen van het Ontw. v. Toler._, 10th dialogue; J. van den Honert, preface to Ursinus’s _Schatboek_, pp. 112-144; and so forth.
The confessional documents do not mention it in so many words, but substantially it is already contained in articles 14 and 15 of the Belgic Confession, where it is taught that through Adam’s transgression of the commandment of life the whole human nature was corrupted; in Lord’s Days 3 and 4 of the Heidelberg Catechism, where the human being is said to have been created in God’s image _so that_ he might live with God in eternal blessedness but is also said to have been entirely corrupted through Adam’s fall; and in chapter 3, 2 of the Canons of Dort, where it is said that Adam’s corruption passes to us “according to God’s righteous judgment”; formally, the covenant of works was included in the Irish Articles, the Westminster Confession, and the Walcheren Articles of 1693.
Further, the doctrine of the _foedus operum_ later also found acceptance among some Roman Catholics, Scheeben II 500; Pesch, _Prael._ III 136; and Lutherans such as Pufendorf, Jaeger, Buddeus, _Instit._, p. 527, and others; cf. Vitringa II 242; by contrast, it was opposed by Episcopius, _Instit. theol._ II, c. 2; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ III, c. 2; J. Alting on Heb. 8:6 and _Op._ V 392; Venema, _Korte verdediging van zijn eer en leer_, 1735; N. Schiere, _Doctrina test. et foed. div. omnium_, 1718, p. 491 sq.; and others.
Rationalism would have nothing to do with it, and even Van Oosterzee, _Chr. Dogm._ § 75, saw in it only a juridical contrivance.
Nevertheless, it was taken up again by Dr. Kuyper, _Heraut_ 161 f.; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 117-122; Dr. Vos, _De Verbondsleer in de Geref. Theologie_, 1891.
