## 2.

Scripture frequently speaks of the name of God in a very broad sense. The Jews therefore counted no fewer than seventy, Eisenmenger, _Entdecktes Judenthum_ I 455; and in Christian theology God’s perfections were also initially included among the names of God. But gradually a distinction arose. Jerome already restricted the names of God to ten, namely El, Elohim, Eloah, Tsebaoth, Elyon, _Ehyeh asher ehyeh_, Adonai, Yah, YHWH, Shaddai, and was followed in this by many, Alsted, _Theol. schol._, 71 sq.; Moor, I 511. The simplest name by which God is designated in Holy Scripture is אֵל. There is disagreement concerning its derivation. Lagarde connects the word with the root אלי and the preposition אל and thinks that the word designates God as the one who is the goal and object of human longing. But this derivation is as improbable as the one that connects the word with אֵלָה, the sacred tree. According to by far the majority, the word comes from the root אוּל, whether in the sense of being at the front, being the first or lord (Nöldeke), or in that of being strong, mighty (Gesenius); literature in M. Vitringa, I 132; Moor, I 515 sq.; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._, 508; Smend, _Altt. Theol._, 26. The name אֱלוֹהַּ, plural אלהים, is derived from the same root אול or from אלה, to fear, and thus makes God known either as the strong one or as the object of dread and fear. The singular is little used and poetic, e.g., Ps. 18:32; Job 3:4; the plural, by contrast, is the common name for God. This plural cannot be explained as a _pluralis majestatis_, since this is nowhere customary of God in Scripture. Nor can it be seen as a designation of the Trinity, as many did from Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 2, onward, Zanchius, _Op._ I 25; Voetius, _Disp._ V 27; M. Vitringa, I 209 sq., for _Elohim_ almost always has the adjective and verb with it _in singulari_, Augustine, _De trin._ II 11; Bellarmine, _De Christo_, c. 6; Calvin, _Inst._ I, 13, 9; Gomarus, _Theses Theol._, disp. 5; Moor, I 796. Modern critics usually see in it a remnant of earlier polytheism, but this explanation not only encounters the same objection as the earlier Trinitarian one; later investigations have also shown that outside Israel it was customary as a name for one single god, Noordtzij, _Oost. Lichtstralen over West. Schriftbeschouwing_, 1897, p. 41 f. This plural is therefore better conceived as a plural of abstraction (Ewald), or as a plural of quantity which, like מים and שׁמים, designates unlimited greatness (Oehler), or as an intensive plural signifying a fullness of power (Delitzsch). A few times _Elohim_ is construed with an adjective or verb _in plurali_, Gen. 20:13; 28:13 f.; 35:7; Ex. 32:4, 8; Josh. 24:19; 1 Sam. 4:8; 17:26; 2 Sam. 7:23; 1 Kings 12:28; Pss. 58:12; 121:5; Job 35:10; Jer. 10:10; and a similar plural can also be observed in the personal pronoun, Gen. 1:26; 3:22; 11:7; Isa. 6:8; 41:22; in קדשׁים, Prov. 9:10; Hos. 12:1; עשׂים, Job 35:10; Isa. 54:5; בראים, Eccl. 12:1; Ps. 149:2; and in אֲדֹנָי. All these plural forms designate God as the fullness of life and power. _Durch Elohim wird das göttliche Wesen in seinem Urverhältniss und in seinem stetigen Grundverhältniss zur Welt bezeichnet. Es ist eine Verhältnissbestimmung, nicht eine unmittelbare innere Wesensbestimmung, und zwar ist es Bestimmung des absoluten Hoheitsbegriffs in Bezug auf die ganze Welt_, Beck, _Gl._ II 22; cf. literature in M. Vitringa, I 133; Moor, I 518; Oehler, _Theol._ §36; Schultz, _Altt. Th._, 516. The name עֶלְיוֹן, LXX ὑψιστος, designates God as the High One, exalted above all. It occurs in the mouth of Melchizedek, Gen. 14:18; of Balaam, Num. 24:16; of the king of Babylon, Isa. 14:14; cf. also Mark 5:7; Luke 1:32, 35; Acts 16:17, and further especially in poetry. אֲדֹנָי, alternating with האדון, which is intensified in turn to אדון אדונים or אדון כל הארץ, makes God known as the Lord to whom everything is subject and over against whom man stands as a servant, Gen. 18:27. In earlier times God was also called בַּעַל in this same sense, Hos. 2:18, but later this name acquired an idolatrous character and therefore fell out of use, Robertson, _Israels oude godsdienst_, p. 200 f. All these names are not yet _nomina propria_ in the narrower sense; they are also used of idols, of human beings, Gen. 33:10; Ex. 7:1; 4:16, and of authorities, Ex. 12:12; 21:5, 6; 22:7; Lev. 19:32; Num. 33:4; Judg. 5:8; 1 Sam. 2:25; Pss. 58:2; 82:1, but they are nevertheless the ordinary names by which God is named and addressed. They are also generally Semitic names that make God known in his exaltation above every creature. The Semites gladly call God Lord, King; they feel themselves deeply dependent on him and bow humbly before him as servants. Yet in those names they express no philosophical view concerning God’s essence but place his relation to creatures, particularly to human beings, in the foreground.
