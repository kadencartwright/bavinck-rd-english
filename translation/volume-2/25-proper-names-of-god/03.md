## 3.

This high and exalted God, however, also descends from his transcendence to the creature. He reveals himself not only in general through creation to all peoples but has also made himself known in a special sense to Israel. The first name under which God now appears in special revelation is שַׁדַּי or אֵל שַׁדַּי. As such God reveals himself to Abraham when he appoints him father of many nations and seals his covenant with circumcision, Gen. 17:1. In the time of the patriarchs this name therefore occurs repeatedly, Gen. 28:3; 35:11; 43:14; 48:3; 49:25; Ex. 6:3; Num. 24:4, but further also in Job, in a few psalms, and a few times among the prophets. In the New Testament it is rendered by παντοκρατωρ, 2 Cor. 6:18; Rev. 4:8, etc. The origin of the name is still obscure. Nöldeke derived it from שַׁד, lord, and pointed it as שֵׁדַי, but in view of Gen. 43:14; 49:25; Ezek. 10:5, the name is undoubtedly an _adjectivum_. Formerly it was derived from שׁ, for אשׁר, and דַי, ἱκανος, and then translated as _qui est sufficiens_, all-sufficient; or from שׁדד in the sense of being strong, destroying; or also from שׁדה or אשׁד, to pour out, so that God is thereby designated as the one who abundantly imparts all things. Wherever this name occurs, however, the idea of power and invincible strength stands in the foreground; and Isa. 13:6 connects this name with שׁדד, to destroy, even if only by way of a wordplay; cf. Joel 1:15. This name therefore makes God known to us as the one who possesses all power and thus can break all resistance and make everything subservient to his will. Whereas Elohim is the God of creation and nature, El Shaddai is the God who subordinates all the powers of that nature to grace and places them in its service; cf. the marginal note on Gen. 17:1; Zanchius, _Op._ II 43; M. Vitringa, I 132; Moor, I 522 sq.; Oehler, _Theol._ §37; Delitzsch on Gen. 17:1, etc. In this name God’s θειοτης and αιδιος δυναμις are no longer objects of terror and fear but a source of salvation and consolation. God gives himself to his people and with his invincible power guarantees the fulfillment of his promises and the maintenance of his covenant. Therefore from this point onward he is also repeatedly called the God of Abraham, Gen. 24:12; of Isaac, Gen. 28:13; of Jacob, Ex. 3:6; the God of the fathers, Ex. 3:13, 15; the God of the Hebrews, Ex. 3:18; the God of Israel, Gen. 33:20; and frequently in Isaiah the Holy One of Israel. God is the Exalted One, Creator of heaven and earth, the Almighty, but at the same time he stands in a special, gracious relation to his people.

As the God of grace, however, he appears above all in the name יהוה. The Jews called this name the name _par excellence_, the name of essence, the proper name, the glorious name, the name of four letters, τετραγραμματον, etc., and inferred from Lev. 24:16 and Ex. 3:15, where לְעֹלָם was read as “to conceal,” that it was forbidden to pronounce it. It is unknown when this opinion arose among the Jews. But it is certain that the LXX already regularly read Adonai and therefore rendered the name by κυριος; other translations followed this example and translated the name by _Dominus_, _The Lord_, _der Herr_, LORD, Synod of Dort, sess. 12, etc.; the French has _l’Éternel_. As a result, the original, correct pronunciation was also lost. The church fathers called the name ἀπορρητον, ἀλεκτον, ἀφραστον, probably not because they considered the pronunciation of this name unlawful but because the Jews judged it so and the pronunciation had in fact been lost; Moor, I 534; Buddeus, _Inst. theol. dogm._ I 188. In Greek the four letters were written as ΠΙΠΙ, or, according to Diodorus Siculus and Origen, also rendered by Ιαω or Ιαη; according to Jerome, by Jaho; according to Philo of Byblos, by Ιευω; according to Clement of Alexandria, by Ιαου. Theodoret relates that the Jews pronounced Αϊα and the Samaritans Ιαβε. All this points back to an ancient pronunciation, Yahweh; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴ 523. Appealing to the tradition among the Jews, some pronounced the name Jeve, for example Joachim of Fiore in his _Evangelium aeternum_. This pointing is indeed found in Samuel b. Meir and was later still defended by Hottinger, Reland, and others; Delitzsch, _Neuer Comm. über die Genesis_, 1887, p. 546 f. The pronunciation Jehovah is of recent date: it gained currency especially through the Franciscan Petrus Galatinus, who, however, was opposed by many, including Genebrardus; Petavius, _De Deo_ VIII, c. 9; Herzog² 4, 173. Later men such as Drusius, Amama, Scaliger, Vriemoet, and others likewise still maintained that the pronunciation Jehovah could not be the true one but had borrowed its vowels from Adonai; Voetius, _Disp._ V 55; cf. the literature in M. Vitringa, I 130. In fact, this pointing faces very serious objections. First, YHWH in the Hebrew Bible is a _keri perpetuum_ and has at one time the vowels of Adonai, at another those of Elohim; further, the form Jehovah is un-Hebrew and inexplicable; and finally, this pointing dates from a time when the Jews had long considered the pronunciation of the name forbidden. If this vocalization is incorrect, the question arises how the name is then to be explained. The claim that it is of Egyptian origin (Voltaire, Schiller, Wegscheider, Heeren, Brugsch) is contradicted by Ex. 5:2 and now finds almost no defenders. The opinion of Hartmann, Bohlen, Colenso, Dozy, and Land that it is Canaanite or Phoenician and was adopted by the Israelites after their entrance into Canaan has likewise proved untenable and has been conclusively refuted; Kuenen, _G. v. I._ I 397–401; Valeton, “De Isr. Godsnamen,” _Theol. Stud._ VII, 1889, pp. 173–221, especially p. 176 f. The Israelite origin of the name may therefore be regarded as established. But then no doubt is possible that it comes from the stem הוה or היה, and since the pronunciation Jaho advocated by von Hartmann, _Religionsphilos._ I 370 f., cannot be considered, Kuenen, _Volksgodsd. en Wereldgodsd._, 261 f., the only remaining disagreement is whether it is a 3rd-person imperfect of the _qal_ or of the _hiphil_. The latter explanation was defended by Gesenius, Schrader, Lagarde, Schultz, Land, and Kuenen, _G. v. I._ I 275, really only on the ground that so exalted a conception of God as would be expressed in the _qal_ form was not yet conceivable in the time of Moses. The name Yahweh would then signify not “He who is” but “He who causes to be,” who gives life, Creator. But Smend, _Altt. Rel. Gesch._, 21, rightly says from his standpoint that even this name is far too lofty for that time; and he also calls this explanation improbable because the _hiphil_ of the verb הוה never occurs. Thus only the derivation given for the name in Ex. 3 remains. Yet even then there is still disagreement about the meaning of the name. The church fathers thought in connection with this name especially of the _aseitas_: God was the one who is, eternal, immutable being over against the οὐκ Ὀν of the idols and the μη ὀν of the creatures. Others, such as W. R. Smith and Smend, loc. cit., p. 21, appeal to Ex. 3:12 and understand the name as “He who will be _with you_.” Both interpretations are unacceptable: the latter because the addition “with you,” עִמָּך, could not then be omitted, and the former because it sounds too philosophical and also finds no support in Ex. 3. Indeed, in verses 13–15 the meaning of the name is clearly indicated. In full it reads אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה; and by this the Lord says that he who now calls Moses and wills to save his people is the same as the one who appeared to their fathers. He is who he is, yesterday and today the same forever. This meaning is further brought to light in verse 15: YHWH, the God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob sends Moses, and that is his name forever. God does not here call himself simply the one who is and give an explanation of his _aseitas_, but he says explicitly what and how he is. What and how, then, will he be? That cannot be said in one word; it cannot be described by a single addition, but he will be who he will be. Everything is contained in this; this addition is general and indefinite but for that very reason also so rich and profound in meaning. He will be who he was for the patriarchs, who he is now, and who he will remain; he will be everything for his people. It is no new or strange God who comes to them through Moses, but the God of the fathers, the immutable, the constant, the faithful, the one who always remains the same, who does not fail or forsake his people but always seeks them out again and saves them, immutable in his grace, in his love, in his help, who will be what he is because he is always himself. He therefore calls himself אני הוא in Isaiah, the first and the last, chapters 41:4; 43:10, 13, 25; 44:6; 48:12. Naturally, _aseitas_ does underlie this, but it nevertheless does not stand in the foreground and is not directly expressed in the name. From this explanation it is also evident whether and to what extent the name YHWH was known already before the time of Moses. Ex. 6:3 does not say that the name as a name was then communicated to Moses but that the Lord had not been known to the fathers with or in respect to his name. The name is also found repeatedly before Ex. 6, occurs in many proper names, such as Jochebed, Ahijah, Abijah, 1 Chron. 2:24, 25, and for that reason too could not have been an entirely new name, since Moses, in order to gain a hearing among his people, had to appear precisely not with a strange name but in the name of the God of the fathers, Ex. 3:12. The intent of Ex. 6:3 can therefore be no other than that the sense and meaning of this name was first made known to Moses at that time by the Lord himself. And this is indeed so. Only in Ex. 3 does the Lord himself give an explanation of this name; here he says how he wishes this name to be understood. The name existed earlier, was even then repeatedly used by the Lord himself, Gen. 15:7; 28:13, and used as a name of address, Gen. 14:22; 24:3; 28:16; 15:2, 8; 32:9. But nowhere is an explanation of that name given. Considered in the abstract, it is entirely possible that the name YHWH originally, according to its derivation, meant something quite different from what is said in Ex. 3. Ex. 3 gives no etymology, no explanation of the word, but an explanation of the matter. Just as God in his special revelation to Israel adopted all kinds of religious practices—circumcision, Sabbath, sacrifice, priesthood, etc.—and gave them a special meaning, so he also did with this name. Apart from its origin and original meaning, the Lord says in Ex. 6 how and in what sense he is Yahweh, the “I will be who I will be.” From this point onward the name YHWH is the description and guarantee that God is and remains the God of his people, immutable in his grace and faithfulness. And this could first come to expression only now, in Moses’ time. A long time had to elapse precisely in order to prove that God was faithful and immutable; a person’s faithfulness can be tested only over time and especially in times of misery. So it was with Israel. Centuries had elapsed since the time of the patriarchs; Israel was oppressed and sat in misery. And now God says: I am who I am, Yahweh, the immutably faithful one, the God of the fathers, still your God now and to eternity. Now God invests an old name with an entirely new meaning, which only now can be understood by the people. And therefore YHWH has been Israel’s God from the land of Egypt onward, Hos. 12:10; 13:4.
