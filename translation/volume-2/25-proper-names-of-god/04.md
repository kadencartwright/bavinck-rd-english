## 4.

The name YHWH is the highest revelation of God in the Old Testament. No new names are added to it. YHWH is God’s proper name, Ex. 15:3; Ps. 83:19; Hos. 12:6; Isa. 42:8; it is therefore never used of anyone other than Israel’s God and also never occurs _in statu constructo_, _in plurali_, or with _suffixa_. This name is repeatedly modified in form or strengthened by some addition. Through abbreviation arose the form יַהֲוְ, יָהוּ, יָה, which is used especially in compounds, and from it arose in turn the independent _nomen_ יָהּ. This shortened name is regularly used in the exclamation הַלְלוּ־יָהּ, but further also occurs repeatedly on its own, Ex. 15:2; Pss. 68:5; 89:9; 94:7, 12; 118:14; Isa. 12:2; 38:11, sometimes in connection with יהוה, Isa. 26:4. The combination Adonai YHWH is also very common, e.g., Ezek. 23:12. The name YHWH also receives a particular strengthening through the addition Tsebaoth, יהוה צבאות, Pss. 69:7; 84:2; Hag. 2:7–9; once יהוה הצבאות, Amos 9:5, properly abbreviated from יהוה אלהי צבאות, 1 Sam. 1:3; 4:4; Isa. 1:24, or יהוה אלהים צבאוח, Pss. 80:5; 84:9. Because Tsebaoth was connected with YHWH, which permits no _status constructus_, and sometimes with Elohim _in statu absoluto_, Origen, Jerome, and others inferred that Tsebaoth was an apposition. They were further strengthened in this by the fact that the word remained untranslated in the LXX, especially in 1 Samuel and Isaiah, and also in Rom. 9:29; James 5:4, Moor, I 512 sq. But this opinion lacks all ground. Elsewhere Tsebaoth is rendered by παντοκρατωρ or κυριος των δυναμεων, and the name YHWH who is Tsebaoth, that is, armies, hosts, makes no sense. It is difficult, however, to say what is to be understood by these Tsebaoth. Some have thought of Israel’s military armies and believe that the name Lord of hosts designates God as the god of war. But most passages cited for this, such as 1 Sam. 1:3, 11; 4:4; 15:2; 17:45; 2 Sam. 5:10; 6:2, 18; 7:8, 26, 27; 1 Kings 17:1; LXX 18:15; 19:10, 14; 2 Kings 19:31; Ps. 24:10, prove nothing. Only three passages, 1 Sam. 4:4; 17:45; 2 Sam. 6:2, furnish an appearance of proof; and 2 Kings 19:31 conflicts with it rather. Further, the plural _Tsebaoth_ is indeed in use for the hosts of the people of Israel, Ex. 6:25; 7:4; 12:17, 41, 51; Num. 1:3; 2:3; 10:14; 33:1; Deut. 20:9, but Israel’s military army is always designated by the singular _tsaba_, Judg. 8:6; 9:29; 2 Sam. 3:23; 8:16; 10:7; 17:25; 20:23; 1 Kings 2:35. Finally, all concede that among the prophets the name Lord of hosts no longer has this meaning of god of war, but they then leave unexplained how and by what this expression changed so much in meaning. Others understand the word _hosts_ to mean the stars, with an appeal to texts such as Deut. 4:19; Ps. 33:6; Jer. 19:13; 33:22; Isa. 34:4; 40:26; Neh. 9:6. Smend extends this still further and understands by it the powers and elements of the cosmos, on the ground of passages such as Gen. 2:1; Ps. 103:21; Isa. 34:2. And indeed Scripture repeatedly speaks of the stars as the host of heaven, Deut. 4:19, and of all creatures as the host of heaven and earth, Gen. 2:1. But, first, then the plural is never customary but only the singular; further, the stars are indeed called the host of heaven but never the host of God; and finally, all creatures are indeed designated by the name _host_, but never so abstract a concept as powers and elements of the cosmos. The unacceptability of these modern interpretations raises the value of the old explanation that understood the hosts as angels. And this explanation finds abundant support in Scripture. The name Lord of hosts is repeatedly connected with the angels, 1 Sam. 4:4; 2 Sam. 6:2; Isa. 37:16; Hos. 12:5, 6; Pss. 80:2, 5 f.; 89:6–9, and the angels are repeatedly presented as an army surrounding God’s throne, Gen. 28:12, 13; 32:2; Josh. 5:14; 1 Kings 22:19; Job 1:6; Pss. 68:18; 89:8; 103:21; 148:2; Isa. 6:2. Even though _tsaba_ usually stands _in singulari_ for the army of angels, this can be no objection, because Scripture repeatedly mentions many hosts of angels, Gen. 32:2; Deut. 33:2; Pss. 68:18; 148:2. The meaning of the name agrees with this. It bears no warlike or martial character whatever; this cannot be inferred even from 1 Sam. 4:4; 17:45; 2 Sam. 6:2. Rather, this name everywhere expresses the glory of God as King, Deut. 33:2; 1 Kings 22:19; Ps. 24:10; Isa. 6:2; 24:23; Zech. 14:16; 1:14. The angels belong to the δοξα of God or of Christ; they increase and spread it, Matt. 25:31; Mark 8:38; 2 Thess. 1:7; Rev. 7:11. Throughout Scripture YHWH Tsebaoth is the solemn royal name of God, full of majesty and glory. Elohim designates God as Creator and Preserver of all things; El Shaddai makes him known as the Strong One who makes nature serviceable to grace; YHWH describes him as the one who keeps faithfulness in his grace eternally; YHWH Tsebaoth portrays him as the King full of glory who, surrounded by his ordered hosts, receives honor and homage from all his creatures in his temple.
