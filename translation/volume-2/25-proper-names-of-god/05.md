## 5.

In the New Testament all these names are retained. El and Elohim are rendered by θεος; Elyon is translated by ὑψιστος θεος, Mark 5:7; Luke 1:32, 35, 76; 8:28; Acts 7:48; 16:17; Heb. 7:1; cf. ἐν ὑψιστοις θεος, Luke 2:14. The designation of God as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob or as the God of Israel also passes over into the New Testament, Matt. 15:31; 22:32; Mark 12:26; Luke 1:68; 20:37; Acts 3:13; 7:32, 46; 22:14; 24:14; Heb. 11:16. But most often these appositions are replaced by the _genitivi_ μου, σου, ἡμων, ὑμων, for in Christ God has become the God and Father of his people and of each of his children, Heb. 8:10; Rev. 7:12; 19:5; 21:3. The name YHWH is explicated several times in the New Testament by το ἀλφα και το ω, ὁ ὠν και ὁ ἠν και ὁ ἐρχομενος, ἡ ἀρχη και το τελος, ὁ πρωτος και ὁ ἐσχατος, Rev. 1:4, 8, 11, 17; 2:8; 21:6; 22:13. Otherwise, in the footsteps of the LXX, which already read _Adonai_, it is translated by κυριος, from κυρος, power. κυριος makes God known as the Mighty One, Lord, Owner, Ruler, who by right possesses power and authority, in distinction from δεσποτης, the lord who actually exercises power, and is used in the New Testament sometimes of God and sometimes of Christ. The combinations YHWH Elohim, YHWH Elohim Tsebaoth, are also found again in the New Testament as κυριος ὁ θεος, Luke 1:16; Acts 7:37; 1 Pet. 3:15; Rev. 1:8; 22:5, and κυριος ὁ θεος παντοκρατωρ, Rev. 4:8; 11:17; 15:3; 16:7; 21:22, while in Rom. 9:29; James 5:4, _Tsebaoth_ remains untranslated. One new name seems to be added in the New Testament, the name Πατηρ. Yet this name too is already used of God several times in the Old Testament, Deut. 32:6; Ps. 103:13; Isa. 63:16; 64:8; Jer. 3:4, 19; 31:9; Mal. 1:6; 2:10, just as Israel too is repeatedly called his son, Ex. 4:22; Deut. 14:1; 32:19; Isa. 1:2; Jer. 31:20; Hos. 1:10; 11:1. The name here expresses the special theocratic relation in which God stands to his people Israel; he formed that people from Abraham in a wondrous manner. In the more general sense of Origin and Creator of all things, the name Father is used in the New Testament in 1 Cor. 8:6; Eph. 3:15; Heb. 12:9; James 1:18; cf. Luke 3:38; Acts 17:18. But otherwise the name expresses the ethical relation in which God now stands through Christ to all his children. The relation that existed between God and Israel in the Old Testament is the type and example of this; it has now been deepened and extended, ethicized and individualized. The name Father now becomes the ordinary name of God in the New Testament. The designation YHWH is inadequately rendered by κυριος; it is, as it were, supplemented by the name Father. This is the highest revelation of God. God is not merely the Creator, the Almighty, the Faithful One, the King and Lord; he is also the Father of his people. The theocratic kingdom under Israel passes over into a kingdom of the Father who is in heaven. The subjects are at the same time children; the citizens are members of the household. Both law and love, state and family, come to perfect fulfillment in the New Testament relation of God to his people. Here is perfect kingship, for here is a King who is at the same time Father, who does not subject his subjects by force but himself creates and preserves his subjects. As children they are born from him, they bear his image, they are his family. This relation became possible in the New Testament through Christ, who is the Father’s own, only-begotten, and beloved Son. And believers partake of this sonship and also become conscious of it through the Holy Spirit, John 3:5, 8; Rom. 8:15 f. In the name Father, Son, and Spirit God has revealed himself most richly. The fullness enclosed in Elohim from the beginning gradually unfolded and in the Trinitarian name of God displayed itself most gloriously and completely.
