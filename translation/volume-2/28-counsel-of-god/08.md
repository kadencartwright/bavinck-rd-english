## 8.

Yet this gentler form of the doctrine of predestination also soon encountered opposition.
In the century of the Reformation it was already contested by Erasmus, Bibliander, Pighius, Bolsec, Trolliet, Castellio, Ochinus, and others.
The Socinians denied predestination altogether, taught only a decree of God to grant eternal life to those who keep his commandments and to punish the others, and sacrificed even God’s omniscience to human freedom of the will; Socinus, _Praelect._ c. XIII; Crell, _De Deo et ejus attrib._ c. 32; Fock, _Der Socin._, 653 f.
In this country it met with objection from many, such as Coolhaes, Duifhuis, Coornhert, Sybrants, Herberts, Wiggerts, and then especially Arminius.
The latter understood by predestination the eternal decree of God to save, in and for and through Christ, those of whom he foresaw that they would believe by virtue of the _gratia praeveniens_ and persevere by virtue of the _gratia subsequens_, and to punish the others, who would not believe or persevere; _Arminii Opera_, 1629, pp. 119, 283, 389, and so forth.
Arminius still intended to maintain the necessity of grace and faith as a gift; and his followers attempted the same in their Remonstrance of the year 1610, arts. 3 and 4, in Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 458.
But this grace was nevertheless always resistible, arts. 4, 5; and the universal will of God to save all human beings, the universal satisfaction of Christ, the universal offer of the sufficient means of grace, and the difficulty posed by the nevertheless always fixed and certain _praescientia Dei_ concerning who would and would not believe compelled them increasingly to place the decision in human hands, Scholten II 472 f.
In the later Remonstrant writings this comes clearly to expression: in the letter of Episcopius to the Reformed churches abroad; in the second Remonstrance of the year 1617, in Schweizer, _Centr._ II 89 f., 106 f.; in the confession and apology of the confession in Episcopius, _Opera_ II 69 f., 95 f.; in the dogmatic works of Uytenbogaert, _Onderwijzing in de christ. leer_, 2d ed., 1640; Episcopius, _Opera_ I 410 sq.; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II c. 18 sq. and IV.
Remonstrantism prepared the way for rationalism.
It was indeed condemned at the Synod of Dort, but as an intellectual tendency it gained increasing acceptance in all churches and countries during the 17th and 18th centuries.
It even received support from the Reformed side in the school at Saumur.
Here Amyraldus taught a double decree.
God first decreed in general that all without distinction who believed in Christ would be saved; but, knowing by his _praescientia_ that no one could believe of himself, he added to the first, universal and conditional decree a second, particular and absolute decree to grant the grace of faith to some and to save them; _Traité de la prédestination_, 1634; _Synt. thesium in acad. Saumur._, 1665, pars II, p. 107 sq.; cf. Rivetus, _Opera_ III 828–852; Spanhemius, _De gratia univers._, 18, 40; _Examen v. h. Ontwerp v. Toler._ VI 195 f.; VII 314 f., 450 f.; Schweizer, _Centr._ II 225 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 612 f.; Walch, _Bibl._ II 1028.
If the first, universal decree meant anything, it pushed the second entirely aside.
Despite its condemnation in the _Formula consensus_ of 1675, can. 4–6, it prevailed and led in Pajon to the denial of the _gratia efficax_.
An Arminian tendency gained ground in all Reformed churches.
All the movements and sects that arose in the 17th and 18th centuries—Neonomianism, deism, Quakerism, Methodism, and so forth—showed a weaker or stronger affinity with Arminius.
Only a few theologians held their ground here and there, such as Comrie, Holtius, and Brahe in this country; Boston and the Erskines in Scotland; and especially Jonathan Edwards, 1703–1758, in America.

More profound study of nature, history, and the human being has in this century demonstrated the untenability of deistic Pelagianism.
And in its place there has arisen a pantheistic or materialistic determinism, with a more ethical or a more physical cast.
Naturally, despite an appearance of agreement between this determinism and the doctrine of predestination, there is a principial difference.
In pantheism and materialism there is no place at all for a counsel of God; room remains only for an unconscious fate, a blind nature, an alogical will.
Nevertheless, many have understood and explained the church’s doctrine of predestination in such a deterministic sense; Biedermann, § 847 f.; Strauss II 362 f., 462 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._, passim, especially II⁴ 453–605; _De vrije wil_, 1859, p. 385 f.; Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_ II 174 f., 216 f., 271 f.
From this standpoint it is said: _so gewiss jeder Mensch zum Bösen nicht bloss prädisponirt sondern auch prädeterminirt ist, so gewiss ist auch jeder zum Guten nicht bloss prädisponirt sondern prädeterminirt.... so gewiss es keinen schlechthin Verworfenen giebt, so gewiss keinen schlechthin Erwählten, denn auch der Verworfenste trägt die Gnade in gewissem Grade in sich und auch der Begnadeste ist nicht vom aktuellen Bösen eximirt_, Hartmann, ib. 217.
At bottom Schleiermacher too agrees with this, _Chr. Gl._ II § 117–120; for although he proceeds from the church’s doctrine and holds fast to revelation in Christ, he distinguishes election and reprobation only with respect to time; there are no reprobates in the strict sense, and everyone would under other circumstances already have been converted here or will come to conversion later.
All the ideas of earlier and later times return in more recent theology.
First, many attempt to evade the whole doctrine of predestination by observing that eternity is not a time before time, that God’s decrees therefore cannot be conceived as finished and ready many centuries beforehand, but that the whole of predestination with election and reprobation is nothing other than God’s eternal immanent acting and governing as it reveals itself in time; the decrees are nothing other than the facts of history themselves; Schweizer, _Christl. Glaub._ II 254 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 540.
In this way, however, the entire distinction between eternity and time, between God and the world, is erased and theism is exchanged for pantheism.
Others therefore do indeed accept an eternal decree of God, but one that consists in nothing other than the _voluntas antecedens_, by which God sincerely desires and wills the salvation of all.
In history, however, this universal decree becomes particular, at least temporarily.
For God proceeds historically in executing it.
The object of election is not individual persons but the church.
God calls nations, and he does so successively, as in the course of the ages they have under his direction become receptive and ripe for the higher religion of Christianity.
And among those nations he in turn calls particular persons successively and in connection with their nation, their disposition, and their upbringing.
The election of one nation and of one person is therefore not at the expense but for the benefit of others.
Not all can be the first.
The nations and persons now temporarily passed over—that is, reprobated—come later, in this life or probably after this life, to true conversion.
In any case, there is no decree of reprobation on God’s part that would exclude definite persons from salvation; at most, on humanity’s part, a persistent resistance and a positive hardening are possible that cause a person to perish eternally; Martensen § 206 f.; Lipsius § 541–548; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 112 f.; Luthardt, _Komp. der Dogm._ § 32 f.; Nitzsch, _Evang. Dogm._, 607.
Still others go one step further and teach that, alongside that universal decree, another particular decree is added in which God determined to save those whose faith and perseverance he foresaw and knew, and, by contrast, to punish the others eternally.
But all agree in assigning the decision for eternity to human power.
Yet they do so in different ways.
According to one, the power to accept or reject grace already belongs to the human being from creation or from the pedagogical direction of God’s general providence; J. Müller, _Dogm. Abhandl._, 243 f.; idem, _Lehre v. d. Sünde_ II 313 f.; Nitzsch, _Syst. d. Chr. Lehre_ § 144; Kübel in Herzog² 12, 161, cf. 15, 112; Ebrard, _Dogm._ §§ 325, 344; also Ch. de la Saussaye in my _Theol. van ch. d. l. S._, 64f.
According to another, it comes from the _gratia praeparans_ and _antecedens_, which is granted to the human being in baptism or in the preaching of law and gospel; Kliefoth, _Acht Bücher von der Kirche_, 1854; cf. Müller, _Dogm. Abhandl._, 247 f.; Sartorius, _Lehre v. d. h. Liebe_ I³ 174 f.; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ II³ 278, 286; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ §§ 40, 41; Dorner, _Glaub._ III 703–724; Lipsius § 541.
Or again, reconciliation is sought in the view that grace is granted to those who use the natural powers of the will well and earnestly investigate God’s Word, and so forth; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV 1, p. 72 f.; cf. Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 511.
Finally, predestination in the Augustinian sense is still taught by some, such as Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 535 sq.; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 393 sq.; Strong, _Syst. Theol._, New York, 1890, p. 171 sq.; Böhl, _Dogm._, 124 f., 527 f., and so forth.
It is noteworthy that in America the Lutheran churches united in the Missouri Synod have approached Calvinism; Dieckhoff, _Der missour. Prädestinatismus u. die Concordienformel_, Rostock, 1884; whereas the Cumberland Presbyterian Church has modified the Westminster Confession in an Arminian spirit; Schaff, _Creeds_ III 771.
At the revision of the Westminster Confession, which was placed on the agenda in Scotland and America some years ago, many also raised objections to the doctrine of election and reprobation, the perdition of children who die young and of pagans, and in general to the confession’s one-sided taking of God’s sovereignty as its point of departure while failing to recognize his universal love.
