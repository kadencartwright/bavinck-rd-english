## 19.

And this glory of election appears still more beautifully when, finally, we attend to its object and end. In earlier dogmatics that object was usually specified. Angels, human beings, and Christ were treated as its object. Concerning human beings there is no difference: whether before or after faith, whether before or after the fall, all assume that human beings are the proper object of predestination and election. This must not be understood in the sense that humanity, peoples, generations, or even the church in general, without further specification and in contrast with individuals and particular persons, would be the object of election, as Schleiermacher, _Gl._ §119; Lipsius §525 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 112–130; and others claim. This representation is a mere abstraction, since humanity, people, generation, and church exist only in particular persons. It is also contradicted by Scripture, for Scripture teaches a personal election: Jacob, Mal. 1:2; Rom. 9:10–12; ὁσοι, Acts 13:48; ὁυς, Rom. 8:29; ἡμας, Eph. 1:4; Paul, Gal. 1:15. The names of the elect stand written in the book of life, Isa. 4:3; Dan. 12:1; Luke 10:20; Phil. 4:3; Rev. 3:5; etc. Yet it is true that those elect are not considered loosely and atomistically in Scripture but as one organism. They are the people of God, the body of Christ, the temple of the Holy Spirit. They are therefore also chosen in Christ, Eph. 1:4, as members of his body. Both Christ and the church are thus included in the decree of predestination. Augustine therefore already said: _sicut ergo praedestinatus est ille unus, ut caput nostrum esset, ita multi praedestinati sumus, ut membra ejus essemus_, _De praed. sanct._, c. 15; cf. _De corr. et gr._, c. 11; _De dono pers._, c. 14. The Synod of Toledo in 675 spoke in the same spirit, in Denzinger, n. 232, and scholasticism dealt extensively with the predestination of Christ, especially in connection with Rom. 1:5, Thomas, _Sent._ I, dist. 40, qu. 11; III, qu. 10; _S. Theol._ III, qu. 24; _S. c. Gent._ IV, c. 9; Petavius, _De incarn. verbi_, lib. XI, cc. 13, 14. The Lutherans denied this, however, because they understood predestination as election from sin to salvation through God’s mercy, Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ III, pp. 18, 43. But the Reformed emphasized all the more that Christ too was ordained by God and, together with the church, was the object of God’s election. There was even still a difference over whether Christ was the object of predestination alone or also of election. Some, such as Calvin, _C. Ref._ XXXVII 714; Gomarus, _Op._ I 430; Marck VII 5; Moor II 55; Kuyper, _Heraut_ 286, 287, said that Christ was destined to be Mediator in order to accomplish salvation for his own; the election of human beings thus logically preceded the foreordination of Christ as Mediator. But others, such as Zanchius, _Op._ II 535 sq.; Polanus, _Synt._ IV 8; _Synopsis_ 24, 24; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Thol._ VII 344–353; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, p. 125 f.; Kuyper, _Uit het Woord_ II 314, also regard Christ as the object of election because he was destined not only to be Mediator but also Head of the church; the election of Christ then logically preceded that of the church. Now it is undoubtedly true that Christ was ordained as Mediator to do all that was necessary for human salvation; and it is equally certain that Christ was not chosen by God’s compassion out of sin and misery to glory and blessedness. Yet Scripture frequently speaks also in connection with the Messiah of God’s election, Isa. 42:1; 43:10; Ps. 89:4, 20; Matt. 12:18; Luke 23:35; 24:26; Acts 2:23; 4:28; 1 Pet. 1:20; 2:4. This election rightly bears that name because the Son was designated by the Father from eternity as Mediator and, above all, because Christ’s human nature was destined from sheer grace and without any merit to union with the Logos and to the office of Mediator. But by this Christ is still only the object of predestination, since predestination, in distinction from election, precisely includes the arrangement of the means to the end. Scripture, however, says on the other hand just as strongly that the church is chosen in and unto Christ in order to bear his image and behold his glory, John 17:22–24; Rom. 8:29. Christ is destined not only to be Mediator but also Head of the church; all things are created through him but also unto him, 1 Cor. 3:23; Eph. 1:22; Col. 1:16 f. This is not as though Christ thereby became the ground and foundation of our election. But the election of the church is the very first benefit to the church; and this benefit too already takes place in fellowship with Christ and has as its end, not its ground, that all other benefits—regeneration, faith, and so forth—be communicated by Christ to the church. In this sense the election of Christ logically precedes ours. But however this logical order was conceived, all the Reformed said that Christ and his church together, that the _Christus mysticus_, was the proper object of election. _Uno et indiviso decreto omnes, Christus et nos, electi sumus_, Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ III 3, 8; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ V 30. They did not, however, stop even here. In agreement with Augustine, _Enchir._ 100, and the scholastics, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 23, art. 1, ad 3, and in contrast with the Lutherans, Quenstedt III, pp. 18, 43, they also included the angels in the decree of predestination. Scripture gave occasion for this, 1 Tim. 5:21; 2 Pet. 2:4; Jude 6; Matt. 25:41, and this example of Christ taught that election does not always presuppose a state of sin and misery. Thus however much ἐκλογη in Scripture is viewed as a separation from the nations, Gen. 12:1; Deut. 7:6; 30:3; Jer. 29:14; 51:45; Ezek. 11:17; Hos. 11:1; Acts 2:40; Phil. 2:15; 1 Pet. 2:9; etc., and the number of the elect is frequently regarded as very small, Matt. 7:14; 22:14; Luke 12:32; 13:23, 24, the world is nevertheless saved in that ἐκκλησια. Not a few human beings from the world, but the world itself is the object of God’s love, John 3:16, 17; 4:14; 6:33; 12:47; 2 Cor. 5:19. In Christ all things in heaven and on earth are reconciled to God; under him they are all gathered together into one, Eph. 1:10; Col. 1:20. The world created by the Son is also destined for the Son as its heir, Col. 1:16; 2 Pet. 3:13; Rev. 11:15. And thus it is not an accidental and arbitrary aggregate but an organic whole, known by God in election and saved by Christ in redemption. _Reconciliatus mundus ex inimico liberabitur mundo. Ecclesia sine macula et ruga ex omnibus gentibus congregata atque in aeternum regnatura cum Christo, ipsa est terra beatorum, terra viventium_, Augustine, _De doctr. chr._ III 34; cf. Perkins, _Werken_ I 770; Twisse, _Vind. gr._ I 312. And precisely because the object of election is a perfect organism, election itself can be conceived only as a fixed and determinate decree of God. In an aggregate the number of parts is entirely indifferent. But all that exists organically rests on measure and number. Christ is chosen by God as Head, the church as his body, and together they must grow up into a mature man in whom every member occupies its own place and fulfills its own task. Election is the divine thought, the eternal plan of that temple that he builds in the course of the ages and of which he himself is the Artist and Architect. Everything is subordinated and serviceable to the building of the temple. Just as all God’s decrees issue in that of election, so all the history of world and humanity cooperates toward the coming of God’s kingdom. Even those who are not citizens in that kingdom, says Calvin, _in salutem nascuntur electorum_, _C. R._ XXXVI 360; Polanus, _Synt._ 252. Creation and fall, preservation and government, sin and grace, Adam and Christ, each contribute in their own way to the realization of this building of God. And this building itself is erected to the honor and glorification of God. Παντα γαρ ὑμων ἐστιν, ὑμεις δε Χριστου, Χριστος δε θεου, 1 Cor. 3:21–23.
