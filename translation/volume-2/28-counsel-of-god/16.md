## 16.

From this it becomes clear in what sense reprobation is to be reckoned to predestination. If one attends only to the fact that God’s counsel encompasses all things, there is every right to speak of a _gemina praedestinatio_. Sin, unbelief, death, and eternal punishment also stand under God’s government. Not only does it in fact accomplish nothing if one prefers here to speak of _praescientia_ and _permissio_ rather than _praedestinatio_, but Scripture also testifies as decisively and emphatically as possible in this matter. It is true that Scripture makes little mention of reprobation as an eternal decree. All the more, however, does it cause it to appear as an act of God in history. He rejects Cain, Gen. 4:5; curses Canaan, Gen. 9:25; drives out Ishmael, Gen. 21:12; Rom. 9:7; Gal. 4:30; hates Esau, Gen. 25:26; Mal. 1:2, 3; Rom. 9:13; Heb. 12:17; and permits the Gentiles to walk in their own ways, Acts 14:16. Even within the circle of revelation there is frequent mention of a rejection by the Lord of his people and of particular persons, Deut. 29:28; 1 Sam. 15:23, 26; 16:1; 2 Kings 17:20; 23:27; Pss. 53:6; 78:67; 89:39; Jer. 6:30; 14:19; 31:37; Hos. 4:6; 9:17. But in this negative rejection a positive action of God also repeatedly appears, consisting in hatred, Mal. 1:2, 3; Rom. 9:13; cursing, Gen. 9:25; hardening and making obstinate, Ex. 7:3; 4:21; 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:4; Deut. 2:30; Josh. 11:20; Ps. 105:25; 1 Sam. 2:25; John 12:40; Rom. 9:18; making foolish, 1 Kings 12:15; 2 Sam. 17:14; Ps. 107:40; Job 12:24; Isa. 44:25; 1 Cor. 1:19; blinding and stupefying, Isa. 6:9; Matt. 13:13; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10; John 12:40; Acts 28:26; Rom. 11:8. God’s government extends over all things, and he also has his hand in the sins of human beings. He sends a lying spirit, 1 Kings 22:23; 2 Chron. 18:22; through Satan incites David, 2 Sam. 24:1; 1 Chron. 21:1, and tests Job, ch. 1; calls Nebuchadnezzar and Cyrus his servants, 2 Chron. 36:22; Ezra 1:1; Isa. 44:28; 45:1; Jer. 27:6; 28:14; etc., and Assyria the rod of his wrath, Isa. 10:5 f. He delivers Christ over to his enemies, Acts 2:23; 4:28; appoints him for a fall and rising, for an aroma of death and life, for a judgment and a stone of stumbling, Luke 2:34; John 3:19; 9:39; 2 Cor. 2:16; 1 Pet. 2:8. He gives human beings over to their sins, Rom. 1:24; sends a powerful delusion, 2 Thess. 2:11; raises up Shimei to curse David, 2 Sam. 16:10; cf. Ps. 39:10; a Pharaoh to demonstrate his power, Rom. 9:17; and the man born blind to reveal his glory, John 9:3. Certainly, in all these works of God the human being’s own sin may not be overlooked. In God’s hardening the human being hardens himself, Ex. 7:13, 22; 8:15; 9:35; 13:15; 2 Chron. 36:13; Job 9:4; Ps. 95:8; Prov. 28:14; Heb. 3:8; 4:7. Jesus speaks in parables not only _in order that_ but also _because_ the unbelievers do not see or hear, Matt. 13:13. God gives human beings over to sin and falsehood because they have made themselves worthy of this, Rom. 1:24; 2 Thess. 2:11. And it is _a posteriori_ that believers see the government and hand of the Lord in the iniquities of their enemies, 2 Sam. 16:10; Ps. 39:10. Nevertheless, God’s will and might are also revealed in all this. In all this he demonstrates his sovereign supremacy. He creates good and evil, light and darkness, Isa. 45:7; Amos 3:6; the wicked for the day of evil, Prov. 16:4; does all that pleases him, Ps. 115:3; deals with the inhabitants of the earth according to his good pleasure, Dan. 4:35; inclines every heart as he wills, Prov. 16:9; 21:1; directs everyone’s course, Prov. 20:24; Jer. 10:23; makes from the same clay vessels for honor and dishonor, Jer. 18; Rom. 9:20; has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills, Rom. 9:18; appoints to disobedience, 1 Pet. 2:8; enrolls for judgment, Jude 4; and has not written the names of many in the book of life, Rev. 13:8; 17:8. These manifold, strong declarations of Scripture are confirmed every day in human history. The defenders of reprobation therefore always appealed to those terrible facts in which history is so rich, Calvin, _C. R._ XXXVII 289 sq. There is so much irrationality in nature, so much undeserved suffering, so many disasters without cause, such unequal, incomprehensible distribution of destinies, such a grievous contrast between joy and pain, that for everyone who reflects there remains only the choice either, with pessimism, to explain this world from the blind will of a wretched God, or, on the ground of Holy Scripture, to rest in faith in the sovereign and free, but nevertheless always—however incomprehensible—wise and holy will of him who will one day cause the full light to arise over these riddles of life. Acceptance or nonacceptance of a decree of reprobation therefore does not have its cause in a smaller or greater measure of love and compassion. The distinction between Augustine and Pelagius, Calvin and Castellio, Gomarus and Arminius does not lie in the latter being so much gentler and kinder, more warmhearted and compassionate human beings. Rather, it lies in the former having accepted Scripture in its entirety, including this its doctrine; in their being theistic and always wishing to be so; in their acknowledging the will and hand of the Lord also in these moving facts of life; in their daring to look reality in all its terror in the face. Pelagianism strews flowers on graves, turns death into an angel, sees in sin a weakness, delivers treatises on the usefulness of adversities, and regards this world as the best possible. Calvinism has no use for such superficial babbling and trifling. It tears the blindfold from its eyes; it does not wish to live in imaginary delusion; it accepts the seriousness of life in its full depth; it stands up for the rights of the Lord of lords; and it bows in humility and worship before the incomprehensible, sovereign will of God almighty. And thereby it proves at bottom much more merciful than Pelagianism. How deeply Calvin felt the gravity of what he said appears from his _decretum horribile_, _Inst._ III 23, 7. Entirely wrongly has this expression been made a reproach against him. It speaks not against Calvin but for him. The _decretum_, as Calvin’s doctrine, is not _horribile_; rather, terrible is the reality that is the revelation of that decree of God, that is taught thus by Scripture and history, that remains entirely the same for every thinking person whether he follows Pelagius or Augustine, and that cannot be nullified in the least by any imagined representations. In the midst of that terrible reality, Calvinism now brings not this solution but indeed this comfort: that in all that happens it acknowledges the will and hand of an almighty God who is at the same time a merciful Father. Calvinism gives no solution but causes the human being to rest in him who dwells in inaccessible light, whose judgments are unsearchable, whose ways are untraceable. In this Calvin rested. _Testis enim mihi erit Dominus, cui conscientia mea subscribet, sic me stupenda haec ipsius judicia quotidie meditari, ut nulla me plus aliquid sciendi curiositas sollicitet, nulla mihi de incomparibili ejus justitia obrepat sinistra suspicio, nulla me obmurmurandi libido prorsus titillet_, _De aet. praed._, _C. R._ XXXVI 316. And in this rest of mind he awaited the day when he would see face to face and receive the solution of these riddles, _Inst._ III 23, 2; _C. R._ XXXVI 366.
