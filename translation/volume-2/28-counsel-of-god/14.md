## 14.

The appeal to Scripture does not resolve this dispute.
For while infralapsarianism is supported by all those passages in which election and reprobation relate to the fallen world and are presented as acts of mercy and justice—Deut. 7:6–8; Matt. 12:25, 26; John 15:19; Rom. 9:15, 16; Eph. 1:4–12; 2 Tim. 1:9—supralapsarianism seeks its strength in all those texts that express God’s absolute sovereignty, especially also in relation to sin—Ps. 115:3; Prov. 16:4; Isa. 10:15; 45:9; Jer. 18:6; Matt. 20:15; Rom. 9:17, 19–21.
The fact that each of the two representations rests on a certain group of texts and in doing so fails to do sufficient justice to another already proves the one-sidedness of both.
Infralapsarianism deserves praise for its modesty; it offers no solution but remains with the historical, causal order; it seems less harsh and takes greater account of the demands of practice.
Yet it does not satisfy, because reprobation can no more be understood as an act of God’s justice than election can.
Faith and good works are not the cause of election, and so sin is not the cause of reprobation either; this lies solely in God’s sovereign good pleasure, and the decree of reprobation therefore always precedes, in a certain sense, the decree to permit sin.
Moreover, if God first made the decree of reprobation after that of the permission of sin, the question always arises again: but why, then, did he permit that sin?
Did that permission consist in a _nuda praescientia_, and was the fall in fact a frustration of God’s plan?
But a Reformed theologian, even if an infralapsarian, can never and may never say that.
He must nevertheless also include the fall in God’s decree in a certain sense and allow it to have been determined beforehand.
But why, then, did God decree the fall _permissione efficaci_?
Infralapsarianism has no answer to this except God’s good pleasure, but it then says the same thing as supralapsarianism.
Reprobation cannot be explained as an act of God’s justice, for the first sin was in any case permitted by God’s sovereignty.
When it works backward, infralapsarianism therefore ends up again at supralapsarianism; if it were unwilling to do this, it would have to take refuge in _praescientia_.
And finally, there is the additional fact that it places the decree of reprobation after the fall—but where, exactly?
Did God decree to reprobate many only after original sin; does only this sin come into consideration in the decree of reprobation, and does God take no account at all in this dreadful decree of the _peccata actualia_?
If reprobation must be traced back to God’s justice, why, then, not place it after—not only the entrance of original sin, but the commission of the _peccata actualia_ by every particular person to be reprobated?
Arminius did indeed teach this, including under it also the sin of foreseen unbelief.
But naturally that was not possible for a Reformed theologian.
Reprobation would then depend on the _nuda praescientia_, that is, on the human being; humanity’s sinful deeds would then become the final and deepest cause of reprobation, and therefore theologians remained, rather arbitrarily, with the decree of reprobation immediately after the fall.
But in fact, with respect to all _peccata actualia_, it therefore taught precisely the same thing as supralapsarianism; reprobation does not precede original sin, but it does precede all other sins.
Infralapsarianism appears to be gentler and more equitable, but on deeper reflection this proves to be little more than an appearance.

Supralapsarianism therefore undoubtedly has this advantage: it refrains from all vain attempts to justify God and, in both reprobation and election, rests in God’s sovereign, incomprehensible, but nevertheless always wise and holy good pleasure.
And yet it is, if not more, then at least just as unsatisfactory as infralapsarianism.
It assumes the appearance of a solution, but in fact supplies one on no point and in no respect.
First of all, the revelation of all God’s attributes is without doubt the final end of all God’s ways, but one may not, with supralapsarianism, immediately include in this final end the manner in which this _gloria Dei_ will hereafter come to revelation in the eternal state of his rational creatures.
For that eternal state, both of blessedness and of perdition, is not the final end itself but a means of bringing all God’s attributes to revelation in a creaturely manner.
After all, it cannot be said that God could not have revealed his honor in the salvation of all if this had pleased him.
Nor is it correct that God reveals _exclusively_ his justice in the eternal state of the lost and _exclusively_ his mercy in that of the elect.
His justice is also revealed in the church purchased by the blood of the Son; and even in the place of perdition there are still sparks of his mercy.
The final end of all God’s works is and must be his glory; but the manner in which this glory will shine is not thereby given of itself; this is determined by his will, and although God has had wise and holy reasons for this as well, we cannot say why he willed precisely this means, why he determined the perdition of many and not the salvation of all.
Moreover, supralapsarianism encounters the difficulty that it gives the decree of election and reprobation only possible human beings as its object and, as Comrie added, a possible Christ.
This latter element was indeed removed from the supralapsarian representation by others; Kuyper, _De vleeschwording des Woords_, p. 202f.
But thereby the principle from which this error arose has not been removed.
If election has only possible human beings as its object, it logically entails the election of a possible Christ, since the election of the church and its Head cannot be separated.
But apart from this, election and reprobation that have only _homines creandi et labiles_ as their object are not yet the decree proper but only a preliminary decree.
Supralapsarianism must subsequently proceed again to the infralapsarian order.
For after possible human beings have first been elected and reprobated, there follows the decree actually to create these possible human beings and allow them to fall, and then there must again follow the decree now partly to elect and partly to reprobate those human beings who are no longer conceived merely as possible in the idea but are now also conceived as actually existing in the decree.
The logical order in supralapsarianism therefore leaves everything to be desired.
Strictly speaking, it differs from infralapsarianism only in that, in the manner of Amyraldism, it places before the infralapsarian series of decrees a decree concerning possibilities.
But what is such a decree concerning possible human beings, of whom it is not yet at all certain whether they will actually exist?
There are infinitely many _homines possibiles_ in the consciousness of God who will never exist.
The decree of election and reprobation therefore has _non-entia_ as its object, and not definite persons known to God by name.
Finally, there is this further difficulty connected with supralapsarianism: it makes eternal punishment an object of God’s will in the same manner and in the same sense as the eternal life of the blessed, and it makes the sin that leads to that eternal punishment a means in the same manner and in the same sense as redemption in Christ is a means to eternal blessedness.
There is no disagreement among the Reformed that sin and punishment are willed and determined by God.
It is also perfectly true that words such as _permissio_ and _praescientia_ in fact contribute nothing to resolving the difficulties.
For the questions nevertheless remain precisely the same: why did God, foreknowing everything, create the human being in such a way that he could fall and then not prevent the fall?
Why did he allow all to fall in one?
Why does he not have the gospel preached to all and grant faith to all?
In a word, if God foresees and permits, he does so _volens_ or _nolens_.
The latter is impossible.
The _permissio_ is thus a _permissio efficax_, an act of his will.
Nor does the concept of _permissio_ have any value or force against the accusation that God is the author of sin, for one who _allows_ someone to sin and perish while he could prevent it is just as guilty as one who incites him to sin.
But on the other hand, there is likewise no disagreement that sin, although not _praeter Dei voluntatem_, nevertheless is and remains _contra ejus voluntatem_; that it is not only a means of arriving at the final end but a disruption of God’s creation; and that Adam’s fall was therefore not progress but most certainly a fall.
And this too is surely established: thought may disapprove of the words _permissio, praescientia, praeteritio, derelictio_, but no one can suggest other, better words.
Even the strictest supralapsarian cannot dispense with those words, either in the pulpit or in the professor’s chair; Frank, _Theol. der Concordienformel_ IV, 148, 272.
For although one may assume that there is a _praedestinatio ad mortem_, no one among the Reformed has dared to speak of a _praedestinatio ad peccatum_.
All without distinction—Zwingli, Calvin, Beza, Zanchius, Gomarus, Comrie, and so forth—have maintained that God is not the author of sin; that the human being was not created for damnation; that the severity of his justice also came to expression in reprobation; that reprobation is not the _causa_, the _principium_, but only the _causa per accidens_, the _remotio principii_ of sin; and that sin is not the _causa efficiens_ but is indeed the _causa sufficiens_ of reprobation, and so forth.
Therefore supralapsarianism is always inconsistent—and fortunately so; it starts with a bold rush but soon recoils and falls back into the infralapsarianism it initially rejected.
This becomes clearly evident among the advocates of supralapsarianism.
Almost all hesitate to place the _reprobatio_ in its entirety and without any restriction before sin.
The Thomists distinguished between _reprobatio negativa_ and _positiva_; the former preceded creation and the fall, while the latter followed them; cf. above, 326.
This distinction also constantly recurs among the Reformed theologians, albeit in a more or less modified form.
Not only is it acknowledged by all that the decree of _reprobatio_ must be distinguished from the _condamnatio_, which is the execution of that decree, occurs in time, and has sin as its cause; Beza, _Tract. theol._ I 176; Polanus, _Syst. theol._, pp. 251, 254.
But within the decree of reprobation itself many distinguish between an antecedent, more general decree of God to reveal his attributes, specifically his mercy and justice, in some _homines creabiles et labiles_, and a subsequent, definite decree actually to create those _homines possibiles_, to allow them to fall and sin, and to punish their sins; Beza I 173, 176, 177; cf. III 403; Piscator in Twisse I 51–52; Perkins, _Werken_ I 763; Twisse I 71; Maccovius, _Loci Comm._, pp. 222, 237; Voetius, _Disp._ V 602 sq.; Mastricht, _Theol. theor. pract._ III 2, 12; _Ex. v. h. ontw. v. Tol._ VII 375f.; Moor II 66, 67.
