## 7.

Among the Reformed another difference soon arose, that between supra- and infralapsarianism. In principle it already has its roots in Augustine’s struggle against the Pelagians. Among the latter, the order of the decrees was as follows: 1º. the decree to create the human being; 2º. on the ground of the foreseen but not foreordained fall, to send Christ, to have him make satisfaction for all and be preached to all, and to grant them all a _gratia sufficiens_; and 3º. on the ground of foreseen faith and perseverance on the one hand and foreseen unbelief on the other, to elect some to eternal life and destine others for eternal punishment. In Augustine the order was entirely different. Sometimes he includes reprobation as well under _praedestinatio_, above, p. 321, but even when he does not, he understands _praescientia_ not negatively and passively but actively. For the will of God is _rerum necessitas_, _De Gen. ad litt._ VI 15; what happens _contra Dei voluntatem_ does not therefore happen _praeter ejus voluntatem_; permission must be conceived positively: _nec utique nolens sinit sed volens_, _Enchir._ 95, 100. The supralapsarian conception that reprobation is an act of God’s sovereignty is already implicit here. Most often, however, Augustine speaks in relation to the fall of God’s _praescientia_ and _permissio_, _De civ._ XIV 11; _De trin._ XIII 12; etc. The decree of creation and of the permission of the fall is then the first, and after it follows the second decree of election and reprobation. These both presuppose the fallen human race, the _massa corrupta_, _Enchir._ c. 98 sq.; _De corr. et gr._ 7 sq. In his presentation Augustine is thus usually infralapsarian; he does not go back behind the fall; he sees in reprobation an act of God’s justice. _Bonus est Deus, justus est Deus. Potest aliquos sine bonis meritis liberare, quia bonus est; non potest quemquam sine meritis damnare, quia justus est_, _C. Jul._ III, c. 18. But Augustine also does not place the decree of election and reprobation any farther back. Only original sin precedes it. This is sufficient for reprobation. Actual sins remain out of account in the decree of reprobation, although they do enter into consideration in determining the measure of punishment. Augustine arrived at this order through what Paul says of Jacob and Esau, Rom. 9:11, and through the lot of unbaptized infants who die early, _Enchir._ c. 92–99; _De civ._ XVI 35; _De dono pers._ c. 8 sq.; _C. Julianum_ IV, c. 8. Yet although original sin is a sufficient reason for reprobation, it is not, even for Augustine, the final and deepest cause. To the question why God has reprobated some and elected others, and also why he has reprobated these particular persons and elected those, there is no answer but the will and good pleasure of God. He has mercy on whom he wills, and he hardens whom he wills, _Enchir._ 95; _De praed. sanct._ 8, 9; _De corr. et gr._ 8. Finally, a third decree follows concerning the means that lead to the end. With reprobation Augustine does not carry the line through. He does indeed teach that God also acts positively and actively in sin: God is _peccatorum ordinator_, _Conf._ I 10; he judged it good that sin should exist, _Enchir._ 27, 96; _De civ._ XXII 1; he punishes sin with sin, _C. Jul._ V 3; _De gr. et lib. arb._ 21. But he usually presents reprobation negatively, as a _praeterire et derelinquere_, and ordinarily does not include it under predestination, but identifies the latter with election and then subsumes both election and reprobation under _providentia_. By contrast, there is indeed a predestination to the means of salvation. For in Augustine predestination or election is always a _praedestinatio ad gloriam_, and this of itself brings with it the _praedestinatio ad gratiam_. Election, therefore, did not occur on account of foreseen faith or foreseen good works; nor did it occur on account of Christ. Rather, it is election to the final end and therefore to the means, that is, Christ, who is himself also predestined, and thus to calling, baptism, faith, and the gift of perseverance; predestination is a preparation of grace. Those who are elected, therefore, infallibly attain heavenly blessedness by the way of grace in Christ, _De corr. et gr._ 7, 9, 13; _De praed. sanct._ 10, 19; _De dono pers._ 18. Many followers of Augustine therefore later arrived at the doctrine of _gemina praedestinatio_, above, p. 323. Coordinated alongside the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_ came a _praedestinatio ad mortem_. This latter, however, could not be conceived in the same sense as the former and was therefore distinguished into _reprobatio negativa_ and _positiva_. The _reprobatio negativa_ then precedes the fall and is an act of sovereignty; it no more occurred on account of merits than did election, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 41, 1; it includes the _voluntas permittendi aliquem cadere in culpam_ and is _causa derelictionis_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 23, art. 3; but cf. Daelman, _Theol._ I 296–303; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae_ II 459 sq. And thus many Thomists—Alvarez, the Salmanticenses, Estius, Sylvius, and others—taught that negative reprobation preceded the fall and was solely an act of God’s sovereignty and good pleasure, Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 217 sq. This supralapsarian reprobation, however, was conceived wholly negatively, as God’s decree not to elect some people, to allow them to fall, and thereafter to destine them for eternal punishment (_reprobatio positiva_). _In substance_, Luther, Zwingli, Calvin, and all supralapsarian Reformed theologians taught nothing different. Roman Catholics do indeed present it differently and charge the Praedestinarians of the fifth century, Gottschalk, Bradwardine, Wycliffe, and especially the Reformers with teaching a _praedestinatio ad peccata_ and making God the author of sin, Bellarminus, _De amiss. gr. et stat. pecc._ II, c. 2 sq.; Petavius, _De Deo_ X, c. 6 sq.; Möhler, _Symbolik_ §12. But this is done only in order to maintain their own semi-Pelagian standpoint and bring it into agreement with Augustine and Thomas. Apart from the doctrine of the assurance of salvation, the modifications the Reformers made in the doctrine of Augustine and Thomas are subordinate and do not touch the essence of the matter. Together with Augustine and Thomas, they taught that election did not occur _ob praevisa merita_ but was the source of faith and good works; that the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_ infallibly brought with it the _praedestinatio ad gratiam_; that _reprobatio negativa_ could not be explained as an act of justice but had to be conceived as an act of sovereignty preceding sin; that this _reprobatio negativa_ entailed the decree to permit sin and to leave some lying in their fall; and that _reprobatio positiva_ took that sin into account. But they frequently added that the concepts of _praescientia_ and _permissio_, though not wrong in themselves, could not and might not be understood as purely passive and, if they were so understood, offered not the slightest solution, and that the distinction between _reprobatio negativa_ and _positiva_ had little value. All three Reformers thereby arrived at the so-called supralapsarian presentation of the doctrine of predestination, according to which the two decrees of election and reprobation are to be regarded as acts of God’s sovereignty preceding the decrees concerning the fall, sin, and redemption in Christ. Calvin in particular often deliberately stops at the proximate causes of salvation and ruin and then reasons in an infralapsarian manner. Let a reprobate seek the cause of his punishment not in God’s decree but in the corruption of his nature, which is his own fault, _Inst._ III 23, 9. Elect and reprobate were both equally guilty, but God is merciful toward the former and just toward the latter, ibid. III 23, 11. By the clay in Rom. 9:21 fallen human beings are to be understood, of whom God elects some and leaves others to their own destruction (_exitio suo, cui natura obnoxii sunt omnes_), comm. on Rom. 9:21; _De aet. praedest._, C. R. XXXVI 287. The fall in Adam is the _propinqua reprobationis causa_, comm. on Rom. 9:11. God hates nothing in us but sin, C. R. ibid. 295, 315. And of this presentation, _quod ex **damnata** Adae sobole Deus quos visum est eligit, quos vult reprobat_, Calvin says: _sicuti ad fidem exercendam longe aptior est, ita maiore fructu tractatur_ . . . _sicuti non solum ad pietatem potius conducit, sed magis videtur mihi theologica, plus convenable à la chrestienté et aussi pour plus édifier_, C. R. XXXVI 317. Yet this does not satisfy Calvin. Sin may be the proximate cause of reprobation, but it is not the final cause, C. R. ibid. For the matter cannot be presented as though God decided to create the human being without a preceding plan, then looked on and waited to see what the human being would do, and only afterward, knowing this in advance, proceeded to election and reprobation. _Praescientia_ and _permissio_ offer no solution, for God, foreknowing the fall, could have prevented it; he therefore voluntarily permitted it because he judged it good, _Inst._ I 18, 1; II 4, 3; III 23, 6–8; C. R. ibid. 359; XXXVII 291, 294. Therefore Adam’s fall, sin in general, and all evil were not only foreseen by God but also in a certain sense willed and determined by him, ibid. There must therefore be a reason, though hidden from us, why God willed the fall; there is yet an _altius Dei consilium_ that precedes the fall, C. R. XXXVI 288. When Pighius therefore objects to Calvin that on his position _discretionem inter electos ac reprobos priorem fuisse hominis lapsu in mente divina_, Calvin does indeed answer that Pighius confuses the _causae proximae et remotae_, that every reprobate must seek the _causa proxima_ in his own sin, and that on the other position equal difficulties remain; but he does not reject Pighius’s inference: there is an _arcanum Dei consilium_ that precedes the fall, C. R. XXXVI 296 sq. Reprobation, like election, has its final and deepest cause in God’s will, _Inst._ I 18, 2; III 22, 11; III 23, 1, 2, 7, 8; C. R. XXXVI 278, 317; etc. Thus in Calvin the supralapsarian presentation alternates with the infralapsarian, Rivetus, _De praed. et elect. nomine et objecto_, _Op._ II; Twisse, _Vind. gr._, 1652, I 105 sq.; Petavius, _De Deo_ X, c. 6 sq.; Kreyher, _Die Erwählungslehre Zw. u. Calv._, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1870, 3tes Heft. Later supralapsarianism was taught by Beza, _Tract. theol._ I 171–205, 360 sq.; III 402–407; Piscator, _De gratia Christi_; Polanus, _Synt._ IV, c. 7–10; Ferrius, _Specimen schol. orthod._ c. 26; Whitaker, _Op._ I 692; Perkins, _Werken_ I 761–806; Twisse, _Op._ I 35 sq.; Gomarus, _Op._, 1664, I 428 sq.; II 24 sq.; Maccovius, _Loci C._ c. 25; Voetius, _Disp._ I 354 sq.; Heydanus, _Corpus theol._ I, loc. 3; Burman, _Synopsis_ I, c. 38, §13 sq.; Wittichius, _Theol. pacif._ §255 sq.; Comrie, _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VI and VII, especially p. 376 f.; Kuyper, _Heraut_ 584.

According to the supralapsarian presentation, God’s knowledge of all possibilities, _scientia simplicis intelligentiae_, precedes all the decrees. According to the rule _quod ultimum est in executione, primum est in intentione_, God established in his first decree the end for which he would create and govern all things, namely, to reveal his virtues, particularly his mercy and his justice, in the eternal blessedness and eternal punishment of certain possible human beings, _creandi et labiles_. In order that these virtues might be revealed, a second decree had to establish that humanity would exist in so pitiable and wretched a condition that it could be the object of that mercy and justice. In order that such human beings might come to exist, a third decree had to determine that a human being would be created, adorned with God’s image and the head of humanity, and that this human being would fall _permissione efficaci_ and drag all his descendants with him in his fall. And finally, in a fourth decree God had to indicate how his mercy would be shown to the elect in the provision of a Mediator, in the gift of faith, and in preservation to the end, and how, on the other hand, his justice would be revealed in the reprobate in the free withholding of God’s saving grace and in handing them over to sins. In this order of the decrees, election and reprobation preceded not only faith and unbelief, renewal and hardening, but also the fall and creation. Here, however, one objection immediately arose. It was established Reformed doctrine that the election of Christ and of the church took place in one decree, in relation to each other, and thus had the _Christus mysticus_ as its object. But in the supralapsarian order the election of the church is detached from that of Christ and separated from it by the decree of creation and the fall. Comrie sought to meet that objection, _Ex. v. ’t Ontw. v. Tol._ VII 346 f., and therefore taught that prior to the decree of creation and the fall the elect were elected to union with Christ. This union is so intimate and indissoluble that when these elect persons were to fall, as was subsequently determined in the decree, Christ, who had been elected as Head, would then also be elected as Mediator of reconciliation. Comrie thus recognized that the election of the church as the body of Christ could not be separated from that of Christ as Head of the church. And so he placed the election of both before the decree of creation and of the fall. Election, therefore, now had a possible Christ as its object alongside possible human beings. The churches, however, had always objected to this supralapsarian presentation. It is not included in any confession. At the Synod of Dort there were indeed a few adherents of this view, especially Gomarus and Maccovius, _Acta syn. Dordr._, sess. 107; van der Tuuk, _Joh. Bogerman_, p. 224 f.; Kist and Royaards, _Archief v. Kerkgesch._ III, 1831, p. 505 f. The delegates from South Holland and Friesland also preferred that no decision be made in this question. But all the judgments of domestic and foreign theologians, even those of Geneva, were infralapsarian. And the Synod defined _electio_ as that decree of God _quo_ . . . _e universo genere humano, ex primaeva integritate in peccatum et exitium sua culpa prolapso_ . . . _elegit_, Canons I 7. It did not condemn supralapsarianism; it let Gomarus’s private opinion stand as such but did not accept it, van der Tuuk, p. 227 f. The Synod at Westminster alone deliberately left this question undecided in chapter 3 of its confession and took the side of neither infralapsarianism nor supralapsarianism. Furthermore, among theologians infralapsarianism was supported by Martyr, _Loci C._ 232 sq., and Zanchius, _Op._ II 485 sq., both of whom are wrongly numbered among the supralapsarians by Vitringa II 41; Bogerman, in van der Tuuk, pp. 226, 349; Polyander, Rivetus, Walaeus, H. Alting, Molinaeus, Wendelinus, Spanheim, Fr. Turretinus, and Heidegger; especially Maresius, who defended infralapsarianism against Voetius in various writings; and others; cf. also Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ II 1024 sq.
