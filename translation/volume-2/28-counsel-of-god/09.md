## 9.

By the counsel of God is understood his eternal decree concerning all that will be or happen in time. Scripture everywhere proceeds from the fact that all that is and happens is the realization of God’s thought and will and finds its exemplar and foundation in God’s eternal counsel, Gen. 1; Job 28:27; Prov. 8:22; Ps. 104:24; Prov. 3:19; Jer. 10:12; 51:15; Heb. 11:3; Ps. 33:11; Isa. 14:24–27; 46:10; Prov. 19:21; Acts 2:23; 4:28; Eph. 1:11; etc. It is already the privilege of a human being to act after deliberation and counsel. With a rational being, thought and intention precede the deed. Much more is this the case with the Lord our God, without whose knowing and willing nothing can come into existence. Among Christians, therefore, there can be no disagreement about the existence of such a counsel of the Lord. Only pantheism, which acknowledges in God no life and consciousness of his own distinct from the world, can raise an objection against it. But it thereby faces the choice either to deny the Logos in the world and derive the world from a blind will, or to acknowledge the Logos in the world but then also to assume in God a consciousness containing the ideas of all things. The Logos in the world presupposes the Logos in God. There could be no thought in creation if it had not been created with understanding and wisdom. Further, this thought of the Lord that is embodied in creation must not be conceived as an uncertain idea whose realization remains doubtful. It is no _nuda praescientia_ that receives its content from creatures; no plan, design, or intention whose execution can be frustrated. Rather, it is both at once, an act of God’s understanding and of his will. Scripture therefore speaks of counsel, of the thoughts of his heart, Ps. 33:11; of the counsel of his will, Eph. 1:11; of foreordination and predetermination, Acts 2:23; 4:28; Rom. 8:29. God speaks and it is; he commands and it stands. God’s counsel is of such a nature that in its time it necessarily brings execution with it. It is efficacious, Isa. 14:27; Pss. 115:3; 135:6; immutable, Isa. 46:10; Ps. 33:11; Heb. 6:17; James 1:17; independent, Matt. 11:26; Eph. 1:9; Rom. 9:11, 20, 21. Yet there is a distinction between the decree and its execution, just as between God’s being and his works outwardly. God’s decree is an _opus ad intra_, immanent in the divine being, eternal and standing outside time. It has indeed been objected that eternity cannot and may not be thought of as a time before time and that God’s counsel and election therefore also may not be understood as a decree made ages beforehand. This observation is correct in itself. Eternity is essentially distinguished from time. God’s counsel, no more than generation, is an act of God in the past; it is an eternal act of God, eternally completed and eternally continuing, outside and above time, Martyr, _Loci Comm._, p. 230b. It is no fruit of all kinds of reasoning and deliberation, so that God would first have been for a time without a decree, undecided, without will, as Scaliger rightly observed, Moor, _Comm. in Marck._ I 900; Vitringa II 13; Heppe, p. 101–102. Nor is it to be conceived as a plan that lies ready somewhere and now awaits execution. Rather, the counsel of God is the eternally active will of God, the willing, decreeing God himself, not something accidental in God but one with his being. God cannot be conceived as not willing, as undecided, Maccovius, _Loci Comm._ 129; Mastricht III 1, 28; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV 1, 7; _Examen v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._, collection 6, pp. 164–166. Yet with this, God’s counsel is nevertheless maintained as an _opus ad intra_, determined by nothing but God himself, distinguished from God’s works in time, Acts 15:18; Eph. 1:4.

The content and object of this counsel of God are all things that will be and happen in time, in a word, the world-idea, the κοσμος νοητος. This world-idea stands in close connection with God’s being but may nevertheless not be identified with God’s being, and thus neither with the Son, the Logos. It stands to God’s being in the same relation as the _Weltbewusstsein_ to God’s self-consciousness. God’s self-knowledge does not exhaust itself in the world, any more than his power or any of his virtues does. But the world is indeed a suitable instrument for bringing all God’s virtues to revelation in a creaturely manner. The world-idea is conceived by God in such a way that it can radiate his glory and bring his perfections into view according to the measure of the creature. It is a mirror in which God displays his image. It is the creaturely reflection of his adorable being, a finite, limited, inadequate, but nevertheless true and faithful imprint of his self-knowledge. In relation to the world itself, this counsel of God is therefore both _causa efficiens_ and _causa exemplaris_. _Causa efficiens_, for all creaturely being can by nature come into existence only through and as a consequence of God’s decree and will. The decree is the womb of all things, Zeph. 2:2. All that is finds its final, deepest ground in God’s good pleasure, in the εὐδοκια του θεου. We cannot ascend higher. Why all things are and are as they are finds for us its final answer in the _Deus voluit_, in his absolute sovereignty. But God’s counsel is also _causa exemplaris_ of all that is and happens. We think things after and as they are. For God, things are after and as they are thought by him. Just as Moses had to make the tabernacle according to the pattern shown him on the mountain, Heb. 8:5, and as every πατρια in heaven and on earth is named from the Father, Eph. 3:15, so all that is temporal is an image of the eternal, all that exists a shadowing of what is thought, and in the deepest ground all that is and happens a reflection of the divine being. Although objection has been made to it because the world-idea does not coincide with God’s being, Thomas’s statement can nevertheless be understood in a good sense: _Deus secundum essentiam suam est similitudo omnium rerum_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 15, art. 1; qu. 44, art. 3; cf. Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 36; Polanus, _Synt._, p. 268; Gomarus, _Theses Theol._ IX §§28–30; Amesius, _Medulla Theol._ I 7, 13 sq.; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ V 12; Turretin, _Th. El._ IV 1, 7; Mastricht III 1, 28; Moor I 903 sq. Therefore this counsel of God must be conceived as nothing other than a single, simple decree. At the Westminster Assembly there was discussion over whether one should speak of decree in the singular or in the plural. The confession uses the word only in the singular. And indeed the world-idea in the divine consciousness is one simple conception. Just as Minerva emerged fully grown from Jupiter’s head and genius suddenly and at once grasps the idea of a work of art, so the world-idea is eternally complete in God’s self-consciousness. But just as an artist can execute his conception only gradually, so God spreads out the one thought of his counsel piece by piece in time before the eyes of his creatures. The world-idea is one, but as it realizes itself it unfolds in all its richness in the forms of space and time. In creatures and in a creaturely manner, that is, outside himself, God can reveal his love and likewise all his virtues only in the forms of length and breadth, depth and height, Eph. 3:18, 19. Only thus do we learn something of God’s manifold wisdom and his unsearchable riches. But so too the one, simple, eternal decree of God unfolds itself in time before our eyes into a multiplicity of things and events, each of which in turn points back to a moment in God’s one decree and causes us, in a human manner, to speak of many decrees of God. This usage is not to be disapproved, provided that the unity of the decree in God and the unbreakable connection of all the particular decrees are maintained and acknowledged.
