## 5.

The Reformation returned to Paul and Augustine and found in the confession of God’s sovereign election the strength to oppose the Pelagianism of the Roman Catholic Church. All the Reformers were unanimous in this. In the earliest period Luther taught and defended predestination just as strongly as Zwingli and Calvin. Nor did he ever retract it, although later, against the Anabaptists, he increasingly placed emphasis upon God’s revelation in Word and sacrament, J. Müller, _Lutheri de praedest. et lib. arb. doctrina_, 1852; Schweizer, _Die protest. Centraldogmen_ I 57–94; Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ I 165 f., 286 f.; II 32 f.; Weber, “Luthers Streitschrift de servo arbitrio,” _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1878, pp. 229–249; Max Staub, _Das Verhältniss der menschl. Willensfreiheit zur Gotteslehre bei Luther u. Zwingli_, Zurich, 1894. Melanchthon first taught entirely the same, _Loci Comm._, 1521, chapter _De hominis viribus adeoque de libero arbitrio_, and also in his commentary on Rom. 9, but from 1527 onward, in the later editions of the _Loci_ from 1535, and in the _Confessio Augustana Variata_ of 1540, he came to an ever greater deviation from predestination and to an open confession of synergism, Schweizer I 381 f. This synergism, like the universalism of Samuel Huber, was decidedly rejected by the genuine Lutherans, such as Flacius, Wigand, Amsdorf, Hesshusius, and others, Frank, _Theol. der Concordienformel_ IV 152; art. “Synergismus” in Herzog². And the Formula of Concord expressed as strongly as possible that the human being by nature is incapable of any spiritual good and that faith is in the absolute sense a gift of God. This should have led to the acceptance of absolute predestination as well. But from the beginning a somewhat different spirit nevertheless dwelt in Luther than in Zwingli and Calvin. For him the confession of predestination rested only on anthropological grounds, on the profound corruption of sin and the impotence of humanity. So long as the human being had no merit and depended solely on grace, enough seemed to have been done. Luther therefore increasingly avoided the speculative doctrine of predestination, the _voluntas beneplaciti_, the hidden God; held to the ministry of Word and sacrament, to which grace is bound; and increasingly placed the universal saving will of God, _voluntas signi_, in the foreground. He silently watched Melanchthon’s change concerning this doctrine and remained with the doctrine of justification by faith. Predestination had no independent theological significance; it was of secondary importance. The synthetic method in this doctrine was opposed; one followed the analytic, from below upward; it was derived not from the idea of God but only from the condition of humanity, Schweizer I 398, 445, 466. Hence Melanchthon’s synergism was still opposed, but gradually predestination itself was also set aside. Against Calvin and Beza, Hesshusius already objected in 1560–61 that their doctrine introduced a _fatum_ and made God the author of sin. In 1561 Marbach opposed Zanchius in Strasbourg. Andreae still taught an election whose cause lay only in God’s grace, but otherwise held to the preaching of the gospel and stopped with the secondary causes, faith and unbelief, Schweizer I 477 f. The Formula of Concord teaches the _servum arbitrium_ as decisively as possible but leaves absolute and particular predestination alone and holds to the universal and earnest will of God in the gospel. It does not deny election, nor does it summon _praescientia_ to its aid; it still agrees with the _ubi et quando visum est Deo_ of the Augsburg Confession; election is unconditional and has its cause only in God’s will. But it identifies election and predestination, makes reprobation dependent upon _praescientia_, and after teaching election immediately adds, from fear of all manner of danger or abuse, that one must not attempt to speculate about that hidden decree but must consider the counsel of God in Christ, in the gospel, whose preaching is universal and earnest, and that the cause of perdition is only human unbelief. God wills that all be saved; he wills no one’s sin and no one’s death. After the Formula of Concord, especially also in the controversy against Huber’s universalism, the conception arose among Lutherans such as Gerlach, Hunnius, and Lyser that with _voluntas antecedens_ God wills the salvation of all, but with _voluntas consequens_ only the salvation of those whose faith and salvation he had foreseen. Toward the end of the century _praescientia_ and _praedestinatio_ are distinguished ever more decisively; predestination is equated with election and is dependent upon Christ’s merit; the cause of reprobation lies in human sin. The Saxon Visitation Articles of 1592, drafted by Hunnius, and the writings of Hunnius, _De providentia Dei et aeterna praedestinatione_, 1597, and _De libero arbitrio_, 1598, teach this clearly. Mediation is sought in the earnestness with which the natural human being can make use of the means of grace, Schweizer I 526–585; Heppe, _Dogm. d. d. Pr._ II 1–79. But the Lutherans could not stop with this doctrine. When the Remonstrants were condemned at the Synod of Dort, they felt increasingly drawn to them. Although the Reformed always distinguished between Lutherans and Remonstrants, the former increasingly judged that their own doctrine had been condemned in the Remonstrants, Schweizer, _Centraldogmen_ II 206 f. The Lutheran theologians of the seventeenth century approached the Remonstrant confession. They first teach a _voluntas Dei antecedens_ that caused Christ to die for all, wills the salvation of all, and then offers the gospel to all; and afterward a _voluntas consequens_ that actually decrees to grant salvation to those _quos in Christum finaliter credituros esse praevidit_, and prepares perdition for those who finally resist grace, Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ III, pp. 1–74; Hollaz, _Examen theol._, pp. 585–649; Gerhard, _Loci theol._, loc. VII; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ 196 f. In 1724 Mosheim declared that the five articles of the Remonstrants contained pure Lutheran doctrine, Schweizer II 210. Pietism, rationalism, supernaturalism, and the entire intellectual tendency of the eighteenth century were unfavorable to the doctrine of predestination. The _voluntas antecedens_ entirely displaced the _voluntas consequens_, and predestination was understood as nothing other than God’s general decree to save human beings through faith in Christ, Wegscheider, _Inst. theol._ §147; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ III 127 f.; Reinhard, _Dogm._ 439 f. Herder regarded it as fortunate that the controversy over grace was buried in the stream of oblivion and exclaimed: _Verdorren müsse die Hand, die ihn je daraus hervorhebt_, _Vom Geist des Christ._ 154. Leibniz still exerted himself to reconcile freedom of the will with predestination, Pichler, _Theol. des Leibniz_ I 357 f. But Kant openly declared that the human being still had a _moralische Anlage_ and thus could do what he ought, _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 50 f.
