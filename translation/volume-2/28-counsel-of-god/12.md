## 12.

Nevertheless, Pelagianism, though already shown in general to be untenable, renews its attack at every particular point in the doctrine of predestination.
First, it assumes a _voluntas_ or _decretum Dei antecedens et conditionatum_ to offer sufficient grace to all fallen human beings.
For this it appeals to various texts of Holy Scripture, Isa. 5:3; Jer. 51:9; Ezek. 18:23, 32; 33:11; Matt. 23:37; John 3:16; Rom. 11:32; and especially 1 Tim. 2:4 and 2 Pet. 3:19.
Pelagians, Semi-Pelagians, Roman Catholics, Lutherans, Remonstrants, among the Reformed the Amyraldians, and all universalists of earlier and later times accept such a first, general decree.
This decree, however, immediately comes into conflict with reality.
Throughout all the centuries only a small portion of humanity has been acquainted with the gospel.
In fact, grace in the history of humanity is not universal but particular.
Various attempts have indeed been made to reconcile this fact with the general decree.
The Pelagians erased the boundary between nature and grace and also found a way to salvation in the _lex naturae_.
Many Roman Catholic theologians judge that grace is or will be granted to all who make good use of the light and power of nature.
The old Lutherans said that grace had been universal in the time of Adam, Noah, and the apostles, Rom. 10:18, but had been restricted again only through human sin.
And more recent theologians have arrived at the doctrine that after death the gospel of grace will still be preached to all _infideles negativi_.
But all these hypotheses go beyond Scripture and cannot nullify the fact of the particularity of grace.
If this fact is established, however, the question arises: why is the gospel preached to one and not to another?
Why is one born in a Christian country and another in a pagan country?
The Pelagians and Semi-Pelagians also attempted to justify this with the consideration that even in this _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_ God had taken account of the human being’s natural merits and the use one had made of one’s natural powers.
But Augustine conclusively refuted this _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam ob praevisa merita ex voluntate naturali_ by pointing to the children who had no such merit and of whom some died baptized and others unbaptized.
The Roman church has therefore always opposed this point in Pelagianism, maintained the _gratia praeveniens_, and called the first grace unmerited; cf. Denzinger, n. 171, 679.
In fact, Pelagianism encounters an insurmountable objection in the case of children.
The _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_, that is, being born in a Christian country or later becoming acquainted with the gospel, is entirely unmerited and unconditional.
Here at the beginning, at the first decree, predestination can only be conceived as absolute and unconditional.
Why one becomes acquainted with the gospel while another remains deprived of it, why the one is thus placed in a position to become eternally blessed and the other is not, cannot be answered from the human being.
Everyone must here, willingly or unwillingly, rest in the will and good pleasure of God.

Second, Pelagianism seeks to maintain itself with respect to the _praedestinatio ad gratiam efficacem_.
Reality teaches that not all who hear the gospel accept it with true faith.
Whence this distinction?
Pelagianism says that the _gratia_ granted to all is in itself _sufficiens_, and that the human will now decides whether or not this _gratia_ will be and remain _efficax_; in Pelagianism, therefore, there is properly speaking no longer any decree after that of the universal offer of grace.
From this point on everything is left to the decision of the human being.
God has done his part; he gave the _posse_; the human being possesses the _velle_.
But no Christian confession has dared to adopt this Pelagian standpoint.
All have taught in a weaker or stronger sense a _gratia efficax_, a _donum fidei_, and thus also distinguished a second decree in predestination.
The only question that then arose was to whom this _gratia efficax_, this _gratia habitualis, infusa_, that is, true faith, was granted.
On this point much confusion prevails.
Nevertheless, in Rome, among the Lutherans, among the Remonstrants, and so forth, the doctrine gradually arose that the grace of faith is granted to those who make good use of the _gratia prima_, that is, the preaching of the gospel, the illumination of the Holy Spirit, and so forth, and who do what lies within their power, _quod in se est_.
This is indeed no _meritum ex condigno_, but it is nevertheless a _meritum ex congruo_.
In distributing the gift of faith, God binds himself to the earnestness of human striving.
The _praedestinatio ad ulteriores gratias_ is a decree not of God’s sovereignty but of his righteousness or equity.
It is equitable that God should grant faith and forgiveness to those who have done their best.
Yet this conception too conflicts both with Scripture and with reality.
The human being is indeed obligated to faith and repentance and is also admonished to them through the preaching of the gospel.
But no conclusion can be drawn from obligation to ability; _du sollst_ by no means entails _du kannst_.
How could the sinful and corrupted human being possess the power to accept or not accept the gospel and to make good use or not make good use of the _gratia prima_?
From where would one derive that power—from oneself, from the operation of the Logos, from the grace of baptism?
The advocates of the _gratia sufficiens_ offered to all are themselves at a loss concerning it.
And Scripture teaches as clearly as possible that the human being is wholly unwilling and unable, that faith is an unmerited gift of grace, and that salvation also subjectively is God’s work.
Nature and grace may stand in connection; between the two there is an essential distinction and no gradual transition; there is no question of a _meritum ex congruo_ either in Scripture or in reality.
Little children precede the wise and understanding, and tax collectors and sinners precede the Pharisees, in the kingdom of heaven.

Third and finally, Pelagianism still seeks to preserve its position with respect to the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_.
Even if someone truly believes and has thereby received forgiveness and the right to life, it is by no means certain that they will preserve that faith and thus obtain salvation.
Therefore a third decree is still necessary in God, namely, to grant salvation to those whose perseverance in faith to the end he has eternally foreseen.
But all the objections named above return here in a strengthened form.
God’s decree becomes entirely conditional and loses the character of a will and decree; it is nothing but a wish whose fulfillment is entirely uncertain; God looks on passively and adopts a waiting posture; the human being decides, arbitrariness and chance sit upon the throne; even among believers the final outcome is still wholly uncertain; the falling away of the saints is possible at every moment.
Further, the separation of the _praedestinatio ad gratiam_ from that _ad gloriam_ is completely in conflict with Scripture; it means that the chain of salvation, Rom. 8:29, can be broken at every point; it dissolves the one work of re-creation into a series of human deeds and actions that stand loosely and without connection alongside one another and lack all continuity.
And finally, with this doctrine the whole work of God in the salvation of sinners is misunderstood and denied; Scripture always and everywhere lays the strongest emphasis upon the faithfulness and immutability of God, upon the eternity of his covenant, upon the firmness of his promises, but none of this is any longer spoken of in Pelagianism.
The Lord does not know those who are his; his covenant and his lovingkindness do indeed waver from moment to moment; sheep are indeed snatched from Jesus’s hand; it is not true that God glorifies all whom he has known, called, and justified.
Pure, consistent Pelagianism is the complete overthrow of all Christianity and religion.
Therefore it has not been accepted by any Christian church.
However greatly the doctrine of predestination has been corrupted by Semi-Pelagian admixtures in the Roman Catholic and Lutheran churches, it is nevertheless confessed by all.
Predestination is in substance a dogma of all Christendom.
