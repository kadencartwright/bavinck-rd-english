## 13.

The word predestination, προορισμος, has, however, been used in Christian theology in very different senses; at one time it had a broader and at another a narrower meaning. From the Pelagian standpoint, it is nothing other than the decree to grant eternal blessedness to those whose faith and perseverance God foresaw and to destine others, whose sins and unbelief he foresaw, to eternal punishment. Creation, fall, Christ, the preaching of the gospel and offer of grace to all, persevering faith and unbelief precede predestination, are not included in it but fall outside it; predestination itself is nothing other than the destination to eternal life or eternal punishment. Here predestination has the narrowest sense, but it also depends entirely upon the _nuda praescientia Dei_, is uncertain, and is unworthy of the name predestination. Not God but the human being makes history and determines its result. This opinion has been sufficiently refuted above and need not be discussed further; cf. also Perkins, _Werken_ I 788 f.; Twisse, _Op._ I 669 sq. But the important difference between infra- and supralapsarianism deserves broader treatment. In fact this consists in nothing other than a narrower or broader description of the concept of predestination. Augustine restricted the word in a double sense. First, he placed the decree of predestination after that of creation and the fall. Second, he usually took the word _in bonam partem_, identified foreordination with election, and preferred to designate the decree of reprobation by the name _praescientia_. Predestination says what God does, namely the good; _praescientia_ looks to what the human being does, namely evil, _De dono pers._, c. 17–19; _De praed. sanct._, c. 10. Scholasticism in general, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 40; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 23; Roman Catholic theology, Denzinger, _Enchir._ 279, 285, 296; Trent VI, can. 6; and Lutheran theology, Müller, _Symb. Bücher_, p. 554, joined this usage. The infralapsarians among the Reformed in the same way placed the decree of creation and fall before that of election and reprobation. But while most had no objection, here after the fall, to including reprobation under _praedestinatio_ also and thus speaking of a _gemina praedestinatio_, Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV 9, 6, others judged it better to restrict the word predestination to election and to treat reprobation separately alongside it under a name of its own, Hyperius, _Methodus Theol._ 183 sq.; Martyr, _Loci C._ 231–233; Zanchius, _Op._ II 479; Keckermann, _Syst. theol._, 1603, p. 296; Pareus on Rom. 8:29, 30, ed. 1609, p. 562. Now if the word _praescientia_ is not understood in the Pelagian sense and reprobation is not withdrawn from God’s will, as among later Roman Catholics and Lutherans, then this is a difference in name but not in the matter itself, Mastricht, _Theol._ III 2, 24; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV 7, 4; Stapfer, _Wederl. Godg._ V 466 f. But characteristic of the infralapsarian opinion is that creation and the fall precede election and reprobation in the decree. By contrast, supralapsarianism extends predestination in such a way that it also includes creation and the fall as means leading to the final end, the eternal state of rational creatures.

Both representations of predestination, the supralapsarian as well as the infralapsarian, have always been acknowledged alongside one another in the Reformed church and theology. The Dutch confessional writings are infralapsarian, but no ecclesiastical assembly, not even that of Dort, ever troubled a supralapsarian. The Lambeth Articles of 1595, included in the Irish Confession of 1615, ch. 3, and the Westminster Confession intentionally leave the question undecided. The Reformed theologians always accorded both representations the right of citizenship, Voetius, _Disp._ V 602–607; Twisse, _Op._ I 50; Spanheim, _Disp. de praedest._ §§8, 9; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VII 296, 383 f. Spanheim used to say that he was a supralapsarian in the professor’s chair and an infralapsarian in teaching the congregation, _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VII 296. And indeed both stand on a Reformed foundation. On the one hand, the supralapsarians teach just as decisively as the infralapsarians that God is not the author of sin, but that sin has its cause in the human will. God as the Almighty may have determined the fall and may also exercise his government in and through sin; he remains holy and righteous, while the human being falls and sins voluntarily, through his own fault. _Cadit homo, providentia Dei sic ordinante, sed suo vitio cadit_, Calvin, _Inst._ III 23, 8. Nor did the supralapsarians arrive at their representation through philosophical thought; they gave it because they considered it more in accord with Holy Scripture. Just as Augustine arrived at his doctrine of predestination through the study of Paul, so the doctrine of Scripture concerning sin led Calvin to his supralapsarianism. According to his own declaration, he offered in it no philosophy but the truth according to God’s Word, _Inst._ I 18; III 21–23. And on the other hand, the infralapsarian Reformed fully acknowledge that God did not foresee the fall and sin and the eternal punishment of many through a _nuda praescientia_ but included and determined them in his decree, Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV 9, 6. There is therefore no difference at all concerning the decrees themselves and their content. Both deny free will, reject faith as cause of election and sin as cause of reprobation, and thus oppose Pelagianism; both finally rest in the sovereign good pleasure of God. There is a difference only about the order of the decrees. The infralapsarian holds to the historical, causal order; the supralapsarian prefers the ideal, teleological order. The former understands the concept of predestination in a narrower sense and places creation, fall, and providence before it; the latter includes all other decrees under predestination. There the emphasis falls upon the multiplicity, here upon the unity, of the decrees; there all decrees have to a certain degree a proper, independent significance, here all the preceding ones are subordinated to the final decree.
