# § 28. The Counsel of God

## 1.

Thus far we have dealt with the divine being as it is and exists in itself. This does not mean that we thought and spoke about God apart from his revelation in nature and Scripture. After all, we can speak of God only on the ground of his revelation. When we ventured to take his names upon our lips, we spoke of him as Christians, instructed by his Word. Yet we did deal with God as, according to his revelation, he exists in himself. And we learned to know him as the eternal being who is at once the highest being and the highest life, as _summum esse_, sheer _essentia_, and at the same time as _ipsissima_ ἐνεργεια, _actus purissimus_, _totus actuosus_; he always works. We now descend to his works. Those works are the creatures, _opera ad extra_. But before we can consider these, the question arises: What is the connection between God as he exists in himself and these works of his? That connection is indicated in the counsel of God, which includes the _opera essentialia ad intra_, that is, the decrees. These are not described to us abstractly in Scripture but are set before our eyes in history itself. God is Lord of the whole earth and demonstrates this from day to day in the creation, preservation, and government of all things. And so it is also with election and rejection. In the Old Testament these are not portrayed as eternal decrees, but confront us on every page as facts in history. From the beginning the human race divides into two: the holy line of Seth, Gen. 4:25, 26; 5:1–32, and the line of Cain that increasingly alienates itself from God, Gen. 4:17–24. When the two generations intermingle and cause iniquity to increase, Noah alone finds grace in the eyes of the Lord, Gen. 6. After the flood the blessing is pronounced over Shem and Japheth, but the curse over Canaan, Gen. 9:25–27. From Shem’s line Abraham is chosen, Gen. 12. Of his sons, not Ishmael but Isaac is the son of promise, Gen. 17:19–21; 21:12, 13. Among Isaac’s sons Esau is hated and Jacob loved, Gen. 25:23; Mal. 1:2; Rom. 9:11, 12. Of Jacob’s sons each receives his own rank and task, and Judah receives the primacy, Gen. 49. While all other nations are temporarily passed by and walk in their own ways, Israel alone is chosen by the Lord to be a people of his own possession. This election, בחר, ידע, Hos. 13:5; Amos 3:2, did not occur because of Israel’s worthiness but only according to God’s compassionate love, Deut. 4:37; 7:6–8; 8:17; 9:4–6; 10:15; Ezek. 16:1 f.; Amos 9:7, and this love is from days of old, Jer. 31:3. It had Israel as people and nation for its object, although thousands broke the covenant and there is a distinction between Israel according to the flesh and according to the promise, Rom. 2:28, 29; Rom. 9–11. And it had as its end that Israel would belong to the Lord, be his people and possession, and walk in holiness before his face, Ex. 19:5; Deut. 7:6; 14:2; 26:18; Ps. 135:4; Mal. 3:17. Within Israel there is then repeatedly mention of a special election to a dignity or service: for example, of Jerusalem and Zion as a dwelling of the Lord, Deut. 12:5; 14:23; 1 Kings 11:30; 2 Kings 21:7; Pss. 78:68, 70; of Moses as mediator of the Old Covenant, Ex. 3; of Levi to the priesthood, Deut. 18:5; 21:5; of Saul and David as king, 1 Sam. 10:24; 2 Sam. 6:21; of the prophets to their office, 1 Sam. 3; Isa. 6; Jer. 1; Ezek. 1–3; Amos 3:7, 8; 7:15; and above all of the Messiah as Redeemer of his people, who is Israel, the servant of the Lord par excellence, Isa. 41:8; 42:1; 44:1; 45:4; etc. Yet although this election in the Old Testament appears mostly as a fact in history and thus coincides with the calling itself, it nevertheless rests upon a foreknowledge and predetermination of God. In general the Old Testament teaches that God creates, preserves, and governs all things through the word and with wisdom, Pss. 33:6; 104:24; Job 38; Prov. 8; etc., so that everything rests on God’s thought. But it is also expressly said that God knows and proclaims the future beforehand, Isa. 41:22, 23; 42:9; 43:9–12; 44:7; 46:10; 48:3 f.; Amos 3:7. In prophecy he makes known beforehand the things that will happen and how they will happen, Gen. 3:14 f.; 6:13; 9:25 f.; 12:2 f.; 15:13 f.; 25:23; 49:8 f.; etc. All the days of a human life are already determined beforehand and written in God’s book before one of them has yet come into existence, Pss. 139:16; 31:16; 39:6; Job 14:5. The righteous are written in the book of life, just as the citizens of a city or kingdom are recorded, and have in this the assurance that they will share in life in fellowship with God in the theocracy of Israel, Ex. 32:32; Ps. 87:6; Ezek. 13:9; Jer. 17:13; Ps. 69:29. In Isa. 4:3 and Dan. 12:1 this is further developed in such a way that those written for life will share in the theocratic salvation in the future. Here the New Testament thought is prepared that the book of life contains the names of the heirs of eternal life. And all things occur according to God’s counsel. With him are wisdom and power, counsel, עֵצָה, and understanding, Job 12:13; Prov. 8:14; Isa. 9:5; 11:2; 28:29; Jer. 32:19. By it he always chooses the best ways to attain his end, needs no one’s counsel, and is awesome, far exalted above the council of the holy ones and of all who surround him, Isa. 40:13; Jer. 23:18, 22; Ps. 89:8. God’s counsel is his determinate thought, his fixed decree concerning all things, Isa. 14:24–27; Dan. 4:24. That counsel is indeed hidden, Job 15:8, but comes to actuality in history itself. For everything occurs according to that counsel; it stands for eternity; no one can resist it, Isa. 14:24–27; 46:10; Ps. 33:11; Prov. 19:21. By contrast, the counsel of the enemies is destroyed, Neh. 4:15; Ps. 33:10; Prov. 21:30; Jer. 19:7.
