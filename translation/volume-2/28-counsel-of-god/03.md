## 3.

Outside the Christian religion too, many controversies have been conducted concerning predestination and freedom of the will. Philosophy has alternately arrived at a pantheistic determinism or a deistic doctrine of freedom. The Jews ascribe freedom of the will to the human being even in the state of sin, Weber, _System_ 223. In Islam, a controversy over predestination and freedom of the will was conducted that is in many respects analogous to that in the Christian church. In Islam God is absolute omnipotence, absolute arbitrariness, over against which the human being is completely passive. In the second century of the Hijra, opposition arose against this from the side of the Muʿtazilites, who defended free will, opposed predestination, and regarded not omnipotence but righteousness as the being of God, Houtsma, _De strijd over het dogma tot op El-Ashʿari_, Leiden, 1875; Kuenen, _Volksgodsd. en wereldgodsd._, 1882, p. 40 f.; cf. also the controversy in the school of Ramanuja in India, which has frequently been compared with that between Gomarists and Arminians, Saussaye, _Rel. gesch._ I 448. In the earliest period, over against pagan fate and Gnostic naturalism, the Christian church placed all emphasis upon the moral nature, freedom, and responsibility of the human being and therefore could not do justice to the doctrine of Scripture concerning God’s counsel. The human being was indeed corrupted by sin to a greater or lesser degree but nevertheless remained free and could accept the offered grace of God. An absolute predestination and an irresistible grace were not taught; God’s counsel consisted in _praescientia_ and the determination of reward or punishment dependent upon it. God gives over to unbelief those of whom he foreknows that they will not believe, and he elects those whose merits he foresaw, Justin, _C. Tryph._ §141; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV 29; Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II 23; etc.; cf. Münscher–von Coelln I 356 f.; Hagenbach §§48 and 57; Calvin, _Inst._ II 2, 4, 9. This has essentially remained the position of the Greek church. The human being became weakened and mortal through sin. Yet he can still will the naturally good and can also accept or reject the offered grace (_gratia praeveniens_). If he accepts it, he is supported by that grace (_gratia cooperans_) and must persevere to the end, for he can still always fall away. Those who accept grace and persevere God has foreknown and destined for salvation. The others, although with _voluntas antecedens_ he wills the salvation of all, he leaves in their fall and destines for destruction, John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 29, 30; _Conf. orthod._, qu. 26–30. In manner of expression Pelagius joined the earlier theologians, but through failure to recognize the doctrine of redemption that the church fathers had always maintained at the same time, he nevertheless taught something essentially new, Harnack, _D. G._ III 183. For him sin was not a disposition or state but only an act that leaves human nature with its free will entirely unimpaired. Grace consists in God’s granting the human being through creation the _posse in natura_ and further offering him an _auxilium divinum_ in the law and in the teaching and example of Christ. This _gratia per Christum_, however, is granted _secundum merita_ to those who make good use of their free will. Finally, predestination was nothing but God’s foresight of the free acts and merits of human beings and the corresponding predetermination of reward and punishment. Properly speaking, therefore, there is no predestination from God’s side, either to grace or to salvation; it depends entirely upon the _praevisio_ of the human being’s good deeds. Pelagius encountered a great difficulty only with infant baptism, for this was granted without any merits, and he could extricate himself from it only through all kinds of evasions and inconsistencies. The doctrine of Pelagius was somewhat softened in what was called in the Middle Ages the Semi-Pelagianism of John Cassian, abbot in Marseilles, pupil of Chrysostom, and well acquainted with the Greek fathers. He did indeed teach that sin had corrupted human nature. But the human being was not dead, only sick. He was like a sick person who cannot heal himself but can nevertheless take the medicine and desire healing; like someone who, having fallen into a well, cannot save himself but can nevertheless grasp the rope thrown to him. The sinful human being therefore cannot merit grace but can nevertheless accept it and, supported by it, persevere. And God grants that grace to those of whom he foresaw that they would accept it and persevere in it, likewise with children and nations. By contrast, he withholds it from those of whom he foresees the opposite. In this consist predestination and reprobation; they depend upon the _praescientia Dei_ concerning the human being’s attitude toward offered grace. _Nostrum est velle, Dei perficere._ Cf. G. J. Vossius, _Historia Pelagianismi_, 1618, better ed. 1655; Wiggers, _Pragm. Darstellung des Aug. u. Pelag._, 1831–33; Wörter, _Der Pelag. nach seinem Ursprunge und seiner Lehre_, Freiburg, 1866; Münscher–von Coelln I 371 f.; Harnack, _D. G._ III 151 f.; Schwane, _Dogm._ II² 512 f.; Möller, art. “Pelagius” in Herzog². Long before the Pelagian controversy, Augustine taught predestination. He arrived at it through his study of the Epistle to the Romans, Reuter, _Augustin. Studien_, 1887, p. 5 f., and intended in it to give nothing other than the doctrine of Scripture, _De dono pers._, c. 19. He already gave it in his _Quaestiones ad Simplicianum_ of the year 397 and then developed it more broadly in his writings _De correptione et gratia_, 427, _De praedestinatione sanctorum_, and _De dono perseverantiae_, 428 or 429. Augustine distinguishes between _praescientia_ and _praedestinatio_. The former is broader than the latter. _Praedestinasse est hoc praescisse, quod_ (Deus) _fuerat ipse facturus_, _De dono persev._ 18; _De praed. sanct._ 10, 19. Predestination is nothing other than _praescientia scilicet et praeparatio beneficiorum Dei, quibus certissime liberantur quicunque liberantur_, _De dono pers._ 14. This predestination occurs not according to merit or worthiness but from sheer grace, not merely unto faith: _non eliguntur quia crediderunt sed eliguntur ut credant_, _De praed. sanct._ 17. All human beings were, after all, alike, one _massa damnata_, _De civ._ XIV 26; _De praed. sanct._ 8; _De nat. et gr._ 4, 8; etc. This comes to expression especially in the predestination of young children, some of whom perish without baptism while others are saved through baptism, _De praed. sanct._ 12; _Enchir._ 98. Predestination has its only cause in God’s sovereign will, in his absolute freedom. He owes no one anything and can justly condemn all, but according to his good pleasure he makes one vessel for honor and another for dishonor, _De praed. sanct._ 8. Alongside predestination, therefore, stands reprobation. Augustine repeatedly reckons this to predestination. He speaks of a _praedestinare ad aeternam mortem_, _De an. et ejus orig._ IV 10; _De civ._ XXII 24; of _praedestinati ad sempiternum interitum_, _In Joh. Ev._, tract. 48; of _mundus damnationi praedestinatus_, ibid., 111; of Judas as _perditioni praedestinatus_, ibid., 107; etc. The text 1 Tim. 2:4 was therefore explained by Augustine in a restricted sense and in various ways, _Enchir._ 103; _De corr. et gr._ 14. Usually, however, predestination is understood as predestination to salvation. With the good, predestination is necessary; with evil, _praescientia_ is sufficient, although this in turn is understood not as merely passive but as active. For God does not ordain to destruction and to the means leading to it, namely sins, in the same way as he ordains to salvation and to the means serving it. Predestination in Augustine is always a _praedestinatio adaequata_, that is, to salvation and therefore also to grace. Among the _praedestinati_ belong also those who do not yet believe or have not even been born. But their number is fixed and immutable. In time they come to Christ, receive baptism, faith, and above all also the _donum perseverantiae_. For this is granted only to the _praedestinati_. Whether someone is predestined he can know only from this: that he perseveres to the end. For God has included in the church some who are not elect and do not persevere, so that the _praedestinati_ would not exalt themselves and seek false security, _De corr. et gr._ 13. Why God saves only some and permits others to perish is a mystery. It is not unjust, for he owes no one anything. Reprobation is an act of righteousness, just as predestination was an act of grace. In both God reveals his virtues, _De civ._ XIV 26; cf. Wiggers I 290 f.; Schwane II² 557 f.; Baltzer, _Des h. Aug. Lehre über Prädest. und Reprob._, Vienna, 1871; Rottmanner, _Der Augustinismus_, Munich, 1892.
