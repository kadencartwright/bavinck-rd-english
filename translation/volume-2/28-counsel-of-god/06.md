## 6.

While the Lutherans increasingly abandoned the original position of Luther and the entire Reformation, the Reformed remained faithful to it; the cause of the division is therefore not to be sought with them. Zwingli taught predestination as decisively as possible, not only on anthropological grounds but above all also on theological grounds derived from the being of God, _Anamnema de Providentia_, _Opera_ IV 79 sq. In the first edition of his _Institutes_ Calvin still spoke very gently and modestly, but through his study of the Epistle to the Romans, April 1538–1541 in Strasbourg, he came to an ever more decisive confession, both with respect to the unfreedom of the human being, II, c. 2, and with respect to election, III, c. 21 sq. Although guarding himself against the paradoxes of Luther and Zwingli and sometimes presupposing rather than teaching predestination—for example in the _Catech. Ecclesiae Genev._ with preface to the assembly of East Frisia, in Niemeyer, _Conf. Ref._ 123 sq.—he nevertheless vigorously championed it wherever it was denied and opposed. He defended it against Albert Pighius of Kampen in his writing _Defensio sanae et orthodoxae doctrinae de servitute et liberatione humani arbitrii_, 1543. Against Bolsec he wrote _De aeterna Dei praedestinatione_, 1552. Against Rome he directed his _Acta Synodi Tridentinae cum antidoto_, 1547. And he did not rest until his doctrine found acceptance throughout Reformation Switzerland, particularly in Zurich, where Bullinger advocated a moderate, infralapsarian conception, Schweizer, _Centraldogmen_ I 255–292. Through Calvin the doctrine of predestination was included in the confession of all Reformed churches. Yet from the beginning there was great difference in the manner of presentation, both in the confessions and among the theologians. Apart from the _Repetitio Anhaltina_ of 1579, in Niemeyer, p. 638; the _Confessio Sigismundi_ of 1613, ibid., 650; and the _Colloquium Lipsiense_ of 1631, ibid., 661 sq., 664 sq., which reproduce the Reformed doctrine only impurely, there is an unmistakable difference among the confessional writings. Calvin’s Genevan Catechism says nothing at all about predestination; the Heidelberg Catechism barely mentions election, questions 52, 54; the Anglican confession speaks only of _praedestinatio ad vitam_ and gives as its object _eos quos_ (Deus) _in Christo elegit_, art. 7; Bullinger’s _Confessio Helvetica II_, art. 10, and _Confessio Scotica I_, art. 8, speak in approximately the same spirit. The _Confessio Gallicana_, art. 12, and _Belgica_, art. 18, and the _Formula consensus Helvetica_, 4–6, are decisive but sober and infralapsarian. The _Consensus Genevensis_, the Canons of Dort, the Lambeth Articles drafted by Dr. Whitaker in 1595, in Schaff, _Creeds of Christendom_ III 523, the Irish Articles of 1615, ibid., 526, and the Westminster Confession, c. 3, express themselves most strictly and most Calvinistically. There is likewise great difference among the theologians. There were always those who, from fear of abuse, treated the doctrine of predestination “_a posteriori_, from below upward,” Trigland, _Kerck. Gesch._ 79, 84, 85 f., 92 f., 99. They preferred the method of ascending from effect to cause, from fruit to root, and of concluding from faith and conversion to election and then applying this to comfort and assurance, rather than deriving predestination and election _a priori_ from the idea of God. Among these belonged especially Bullinger, Ursinus, Olevianus, Boquinus, Hyperius, Sohnius, and others, Heppe, _Dogm. des d. Protest._ II 1–79; Gooszen, in his two works on the Heidelberg Catechism, 1890 and 1893; Dr. van ’t Hooft, _De Theol. van H. Bullinger_, 1888; cf. my article “Calv. en Geref.,” _Vrije Kerk_, February 1893. But this is no difference in kind and principle. All the named theologians repeatedly testified clearly and unequivocally to their agreement with the Calvinistic doctrine of predestination, Trigland, _Kerck. Gesch._ 59–79; Schweizer, _Centraldogmen_ I, passim; cf. II 110. Conversely, the Calvinists too at all times desired sobriety and caution in the treatment of this doctrine. Calvin did not discuss it in the Genevan Catechism. Dort, Canons I 12, 14, and Westminster, Confession c. 4, §8, warn against vain and inquisitive investigations. In the earliest period many theologians treated the doctrine of election not in the locus _De Deo_ but in the locus _De salute_: Calvin himself, _Inst._ III, c. 21–24; Martyr, _Loci Comm._, 1580, p. 229; Sohnius, _Op._ I 256; II 42; Musculus, _Loci Comm._, c. 24; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 54; _Conf. Belg._, art. 16; _Gallicana_, art. 12; etc. Thus they first follow the order of the apostle Paul, who ascends from the doctrine of sin and grace in Rom. 9–11 to election and also in Eph. 1:3 proceeds from the blessings of Christ. But just as Paul, once arrived there, then also derives all saving benefits _a priori_ from election, Rom. 8:29 f.; Eph. 1:4 f., so among the Reformed the analytical method gradually makes way for the synthetic. The life of faith was indeed the condition under which the confession of election arose, but the fact of election was nevertheless the source of all salvation, _fundamentum ac prima causa bonorum omnium_. This was the conviction not only of Calvin but also of Melanchthon, Hemming, Bucer, Olevianus, and others, in Heppe, _Dogm. d. d. Prot._ II 12, 20, 27, 70. Musculus expressly says: we treat election after faith, not as though it first followed upon faith, but so that from there as from the stream we would look up _ad ipsum fontem_, _Loci_, p. 534. Systematic order and theological interest required that predestination be treated in the locus _De Deo_. This had already been done by scholasticism, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 40; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 23. It was still done in part also by the Lutherans, Gerhard, loc. VII. And it became the ordinary order among all the Reformed, Hyperius, _Meth. Theol._, p. 182; Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 171; III 402; Polanus, _Synt._ IV, c. 6–10; Zanchius, _Op._ II 476; Junius, Maccovius, Maresius, Mastricht, and others, whether treated under the doctrine of God’s attributes or in a separate locus after the Trinity. This difference in order is not in itself principial and need not be so. Yet it is not accidental that the _a priori_ order was followed mostly by the Reformed, while the _a posteriori_ order, which treats predestination at the entrance to or in the middle of the locus _De salute_, gradually came into use among the Lutherans, the Remonstrants, the Roman Catholics, and most recent dogmaticians. Even then, however, the difference does not consist in the Reformed deriving predestination in a speculative manner from an _a priori_, philosophical, deterministic conception of God while the others hold to God’s revelation in Christ. For even the strictest Calvinists intend to give in the locus _De Deo_ and within it _De decretis_ nothing other than the doctrine of Scripture, the content of God’s revelation. But among the Reformed predestination has not only an anthropological and soteriological but above all also a theological significance. What is at issue in it is not first the salvation of humanity but the honor of God. A profound _religious_ interest is involved also with the synthetic, _a priori_ order. The claim that this order would presuppose a nominalistic conception of God and offer a dry, lifeless dogma, as some think—for example _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1868, pp. 107–110; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ I 55; Doedes, _Ned. Geloofsbel._ 185, 203 f.; de Cock, _Is de leer der absolute praedest. uitgangspunt of resultaat van de leer der Geref. Kerk?_, 1868—is therefore without any ground. The doctrine of predestination can be treated dryly and abstractly just as well in the middle as at the beginning of dogmatics. True, saving faith is needed not only to confess election but also all the other dogmas, including those concerning God, the Trinity, humanity, and so forth. If this consideration were decisive, all dogmas would have to be moved after the locus _De salute_. Dogmatics, however, describes the truth not as it subjectively comes to the consciousness of believers, but as God has objectively presented it in his Word, cf. Volume I, pp. 49, 50. The synthetic method alone sufficiently maintains the religious interest of God’s honor, cf. Kübel, Herzog² 12, 145 f.
