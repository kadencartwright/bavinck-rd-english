## 15.

Thus neither supralapsarianism nor infralapsarianism has succeeded in providing the solution to this problem and doing justice to the many-sidedness of Scripture.
This can in part be explained from the one-sidedness of which both are guilty.
First, as was already said above, it is not correct to define the _finis supremus_ of all things as the revelation of God’s mercy in the elect and of his righteousness in the lost.
Certainly, the glory of God and the manifestation of his perfections are the final end of everything, but the twofold state of blessedness and wretchedness is not included in that final end and is related to it as a means.
It is by no means possible to prove that this twofold state had to be given with this final end of God’s glory.
If he brings about _opera ad extra_, he can never intend anything other by them than the honor of his name.
But that he seeks this honor in this way and no other depends solely upon his sovereign freedom.
Apart from this, however, it is also not true that God’s righteousness would come to revelation only in the wretched state of the lost and his mercy only in the blessedness of the elect, for his righteousness and holiness also shine in heaven, and even in hell there is still something of his mercy and lovingkindness.
In the second place, it is incorrect to present the wretchedness of the lost as the end of predestination.
Sin certainly cannot be reduced to a _nuda praescientia_ and _permissio Dei_.
Fall and sins and eternal punishment are included in God’s decree and in a certain sense willed by God.
But always only in a certain sense and not in the same manner as grace and blessedness.
In the latter he has good pleasure, but sin and punishment are not his delight, his joy.
When he makes sin serviceable to his honor, he does so through his omnipotence, but it is contrary to sin’s nature.
And when he punishes the wicked, he does not rejoice in their suffering in itself but celebrates in it the triumph of his virtues, Deut. 28:63; Ps. 2:4; Prov. 1:26; Lam. 3:33.
Therefore, if on the one hand, with a view to the comprehensive and immutable character of God’s counsel, there is no objection to speaking of a _gemina praedestinatio_, nevertheless predestination in the one case is not of the same nature as in the other.
_Praedestinatio est constitutio finis et ordinatio mediorum ad finem; cum aeterna damnatio non sit finis hominis, sed tantum extremum, ideo reprobationis genus non proprie praedestinatio constitui potest. Pugnant enim haec inter sese, ordinare ad finem et ordinare ad damnationem. Omnis enim finis sua natura est optimum quid et perfectio rei; damnatio autem est extremum malum et summa imperfectio, ita ut impropria sit locutio, qua dicitur, Deum nonnullos homines praedestinasse ad damnationem_, Keckermann, _Syst. theol._, 1603, p. 296; cf. Twisse I 53 A.
However strongly and frequently Scripture says that sin and punishment are determined by God, it nevertheless uses the words προθεσις, προγνωσις, προορισμος almost exclusively of the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_.
In the third place, it is also less correct for another reason to coordinate the _praedestinatio ad mortem aeternam_ with the _praedestinatio ad vitam aeternam_ and to posit it equally as a final end.
The object of election is not merely a few human beings, as is the case with reprobation; rather, in election the human race under a new Head, namely Christ, is the object, and thus through _gratia_ not merely a few individuals but the human race itself with the whole cosmos is saved.
And in this salvation of the human race and the world, it is not merely a few virtues of God that come to revelation, so that in addition an eternal perdition would still be needed to reveal his righteousness, but in the completed kingdom of God all God’s virtues and perfections come to unfold—his righteousness and his grace, his holiness and his love, his sovereignty and his mercy.
And this _status gloriae_ is therefore the proper, direct end, though subordinate to his honor, that God intends with his creation.
In the fourth place, supralapsarianism and infralapsarianism erred in placing all that preceded the final end in a mutually subordinated relation as means.
Now the means are of themselves subordinate to the final end, but not for that reason to one another.
Creation is not merely a means for the fall, and the fall not merely a means for grace and hardening, and these in turn not merely a means for blessedness and wretchedness.
It must indeed be borne in mind that the decrees are as rich in content as the entire history of the world, for the latter is the unfolding of the former.
Who would be able to summarize this world history in a logical schema of a few concepts?
Creation, fall, sin, Christ, faith, unbelief, and so forth, by no means stand only in a relation of means to one another, so that what precedes can, as it were, fall away when what follows has been attained.
They are not mutually subordinated but also coordinated, as Twisse I 71 already observed.
Creation truly did not take place only so that the fall could occur, but it gave existence to a world that will remain even in the state of glory.
The fall did not occur only so that there might be a _creatura miserabilis_, but as a fact it retains its significance together with all the consequences that have proceeded from it.
Christ did not merely become a Mediator, which would have been sufficient for atonement for sin, but God also ordained him as Head of the church.
The entire history of the world is not a means that falls away when the end has been attained, but continues to work and leaves fruits in eternity.
And election and reprobation themselves do not run here on earth as two straight lines alongside each other, but in unbelievers there is much that does not flow from reprobation, and in believers there is much that is not owed to election.
On the one hand, both presuppose sin and are acts of mercy and righteousness, Rom. 9:15; Eph. 1:4, and yet both are also acts of divine sovereign freedom and sovereignty, Rom. 9:11, 17, 21.
Likewise, Adam even before the fall is already a type of Christ, 1 Cor. 15:47 f., and yet in Scripture the incarnation is always grounded in the fall of the human race, Heb. 2:14 f.
Sometimes Scripture speaks so strongly that reprobation is completely coordinated with election and eternal punishment is intended by God as a goal just as much as eternal blessedness, Luke 2:34; John 3:19-21; 1 Pet. 2:7, 8; Rom. 9:17, 18, 22, and so forth.
But sometimes the _mors aeterna_ falls completely away in the description of the future; the end will be the triumph of the kingdom of God, the new heaven and earth, the new Jerusalem, where God will be all in all, 1 Cor. 15; Rev. 21, 22; everything is subjected to the church as the church is to Christ, 1 Cor. 3 v. 21-23; reprobation is entirely subordinated to election.
Therefore, in the end, neither the supralapsarian nor the infralapsarian conception of predestination is capable of taking up into itself the full, rich truth of Scripture and satisfying our theological thought.
What is true in supralapsarianism is that all the decrees together form a unity; that there is a final end to which everything is subordinate and serviceable; that sin did not enter the world unthought of and unexpected by God but was in a certain sense willed and determined by him; that creation was immediately ordered toward re-creation and that account was already taken of Christ in Adam before the fall.
But what is true in infralapsarianism is that the decrees, though one, are nevertheless also distinguished with a view to their objects; that in those decrees not only a teleological but also a causal order can be observed; that creation and fall are not exhausted in being merely means to the final end; that sin, above all and in the first place, was a disruption of creation and in and for itself can never have been willed by God.
In general, the formulation of the final end of all things, namely that God reveals his righteousness in the reprobate and his mercy in the elect, is indeed far too simple and sparse.
The _status gloriae_ will be so indescribably rich and glorious.
We expect a new heaven, a new earth, a new humanity, a restored creation, an ever-continuing development never again disturbed by any sin.
And creation and fall, Adam and Christ, nature and grace, faith and unbelief, election and reprobation, all work together toward this, each in its own way, not only after but also alongside and with one another.
Indeed, the present world with its history is already in itself a continuing revelation of God’s virtues.
It is not merely a means for the higher and richer revelation that is coming but is also of value in itself.
It therefore continues to work on into the future dispensation and will provide the new humanity with continual material for the worship and glorification of God.
Therefore, among the decrees, as among the facts of world history, there is not only a causal and a teleological but also an organic order.
In our limitation we can place ourselves only at one standpoint or the other, and therefore the advocates of a causal and those of a teleological view of the world and life come into conflict with one another at every moment.
But for God it is entirely different.
He surveys the whole.
All things are eternally present in his consciousness.
His counsel is one simple conception.
And in that counsel all particular decrees stand in the same connection in which the facts of history now partly appear to us _a posteriori_ and will one day fully appear to stand.
That connection is so rich and complex that it cannot be rendered in the single word infralapsarian or supralapsarian.
It is both causal and teleological; what precedes acts upon what follows, but the future also already determines the past and the present.
There is a rich, many-sided _Wechselwirkung_.
Just as in an organism all the members cohere and reciprocally determine one another, so the world is God’s work of art, all of whose parts are organically connected with one another.
And God’s counsel is the eternal idea of that world in both its length and breadth.
