## 2.

The New Testament places this counsel of God in a much clearer light. Not only are all his works known to God from eternity, Acts 15:18 (cf., however, the _variae lectiones_), but everything occurs according to God’s determinate counsel and foreknowledge. The New Testament word βουλη designates the will of God as resting on counsel and deliberation and is therein distinguished from θελημα, the will as will; cf. Eph. 1:11, βουλη του θεληματος. Such a counsel of God precedes all things. It encompasses everything, Eph. 1:11, including the sinful acts of human beings, Acts 2:23; 4:28; cf. Luke 22:22. God has determined beforehand the dwelling place of the nations both as to time and extent, ὁρισας, Acts 17:26. God’s will is also revealed in the perdition of Judas, John 17:12; in the giving over of the Gentiles, Rom. 1:24; in the rejection of Esau, Rom. 9:13; in the hardening of the wicked, Rom. 9:18; in the raising up of Pharaoh, Rom. 9:17; in the enduring of the vessels of wrath prepared for destruction, Rom. 9:22; in Christ’s being appointed (κειμαι) not only for a rising but also εἰς πτωσιν, Luke 2:34; for a κρισις, John 3:19–21; for a λιθος προσκομματος και πετρα σκανδαλου, 1 Pet. 2:7, 8; cf. 1 Thess. 5:9; Jude v. 4. But the βουλη του θεου nevertheless relates above all to the work of redemption, Luke 7:30; Acts 13:36; 20:27; Heb. 6:17. And the New Testament is rich in words that describe this counsel of God more closely. It speaks of εὐδοκια, good pleasure, delight, Matt. 11:26; Luke 2:14; 10:21; Eph. 1:5, 9; Phil. 2:13; 2 Thess. 1:11; προθεσις, purpose, Rom. 8:28; 9:11; Eph. 1:11; 3:11; 2 Tim. 1:9; προγνωσις, foreknowledge, Rom. 8:29; 11:2; 1 Pet. 1:2; ἐκλογη, election, Mark 13:20; Acts 9:15; 13:17; 15:7; Rom. 9:11; 11:5, 28; 1 Cor. 1:27, 28; Eph. 1:4; 1 Thess. 1:4; 2 Pet. 1:10; James 2:5; προορισμος, Rom. 8:29; Cor. 2:7; Eph. 1:5, 11; cf. also Acts 13:48, where it is said that ὁσοι ἠσαν τεταγμενοι εἰς ζωην αἰωνιον believed, that is, not as many as had disposed themselves or were subjectively predisposed, but as many as had been ordained, appointed to eternal life; and also Eph. 2:10, where it is said of the good works of believers that God προητοιμασεν them. The words are thus distinguished: προθεσις indicates that in the work of salvation God proceeds neither arbitrarily nor by chance but according to a fixed plan, an immutable purpose; ἐκλογη modifies this in the sense that this purpose does not encompass everyone but is a προθεσις κατ’ ἐκλογην, Rom. 9:11, an electing purpose, so that not all but many attain salvation; προγνωσις regards the persons who in this electing purpose of God are the object not of his _nuda praescientia_ but of his _dilectio practica_; while finally προορισμος refers more to the means that God disposes in order to bring those whom he has foreknown to the destination determined for them. Προθεσις _certitudinem eventus indicat_, προγνωσις _personarum singularitatem_, προορισμος _mediorum ordinem_. Election is _certa per_ προθεσιν, _determinata per_ προγνωσιν, _ordinata per_ προορισμον, Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV, qu. 7. The eternity of this purpose is not already implied simply by the preposition προ with which the words are compounded, but it is nevertheless clearly expressed in Eph. 3:11; 2 Tim. 1:9; cf. Matt. 25:34; 1 Cor. 2:7; Eph. 1:4. It has indeed been maintained that in Rom. 9 Paul does not deal with the absolute sovereignty and eternal decree of God but only with a _Verhalten Gottes, das in der Zeit, in der Geschichte seine Ursachen wie seine Wirkungen hat_, W. Beyschlag, _Die paulin. Theodicee Röm. IX–XI_, 2nd ed., Halle, 1895; Dr. van Dijk, _De leer der verkiezing volgens het N. T._, _Studiën ond. red. van de la Saussaye, Valeton en Van Dijk_ IV, 3rd part; Buhl, _Der Gedankengang von Röm. 9–11_, _Stud. u. Kr._, 1887, pp. 295–322; Kübel, Herzog² 12, 158. But this claim is refuted by Rom. 9. For the προθεσις κατ’ ἐκλογην clearly precedes the facts of history; history thus serves to make that already existing purpose stand firm, Rom. 9:11. The seed of promise is promised to Sarah long beforehand, v. 9. Before Isaac’s children had been born, God had already said that the elder would serve the younger, v. 11. Verses 15–18 teach that election has its causes not in works but solely and exclusively in the will of the one who calls. Certainly Rom. 9 speaks of an act of God in time, but the cause of that act falls outside time and lies solely in God’s will and good pleasure. Added to this is that elsewhere too the _causa electionis_ is clearly ascribed solely and exclusively to God’s grace, love, and good pleasure, Matt. 11:25; Luke 12:32; Eph. 1:5, 9, 11; 2 Tim. 1:9, 10. It is not even correct in Rom. 9:21 to think either exclusively, with supralapsarianism, of the _massa incorrupta_, or, with Augustine and infralapsarianism, only of the _massa corrupta_. Paul is not thinking here at all of this distinction. What he wishes to say is only this: that God has the absolute right to give his creatures whatever destination seems good to him, cf. Isa. 10:15; Jer. 18; Matt. 20:15. From the standpoint of absolute right, a creature has nothing to say against its Creator. Paul refutes all objections against election not by arguing that it is just and equitable but by appealing to the sovereignty and absolute right of God. Just as the New Testament expresses the eternity of election more clearly than the Old Testament, so it also conceives that election more individually and personally. Whereas in the Old Testament the people of Israel appears as the object of election, here it is particular persons who are chosen in Christ, together form his body, and bear the name εκλεκτοι, Matt. 24:31; Luke 18:7; Acts 13:48; Rom. 8:33; Eph. 1:4; Titus 1:1; 2 Tim. 2:10; 1 Pet. 1:1, 2, 9, etc. This is especially evident also from the book of life, in which are written the names of the heirs of eternal life, Luke 10:20; Heb. 12:23; Phil. 4:3; Rev. 3:5; 13:8; 20:12; 21:27; 22:19. Finally, it is the clear teaching of the New Testament that election has its end not in an earthly life in Canaan, nor even in an eminent place in the kingdom of God, but specifically in heavenly blessedness. Within the church there is indeed again an election to one office or service or another, for example, that of the apostles, Luke 6:13; John 6:70, etc., but election proper has as its end holiness, Eph. 1:4; sonship, Eph. 1:5; salvation, 2 Thess. 2:13; eternal life, Acts 13:48; conformity to Christ, Rom. 8:29; John 17:24; and the glorification of God, Eph. 1:6, 12. In Rom. 9 too Paul does not deal with a higher or lower place in the kingdom of God but makes a distinction within Israel itself between children of the flesh and children of the promise. He speaks specifically of vessels for honor and vessels for dishonor, κατηρτισμενα εἰς ἀπωλειαν. He sets mercy and hardening over against each other, and in vv. 14 and 19 gives voice to objections whose seriousness presupposes precisely the doctrine of such a sovereign election.
