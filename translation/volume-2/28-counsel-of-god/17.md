## 17.

Although reprobation may on the one hand with full right be reckoned to predestination, it is nevertheless not in the same sense and in the same way the content of God’s decree as election is. The advocates of _gemina praedestinatio_ have also acknowledged this at all times. When God’s sovereignty, the positive and unambiguous testimonies of his Word, and the ineradicable facts of history were at issue, they were as unyielding as the apostle Paul and would hear of no concession or mediation. Then they sometimes came to harsh statements that can offend the Pelagian-minded human heart. Thus Augustine once said that God could not be accused even if he had willed to condemn some innocent people. _Si humanum genus, quod creatum primitus constat ex nihilo, non cum debita mortis et peccati origine nasceretur et tamen ex eis creator omnipotens in aeternum nonnullos damnare vellet interitum; quis omnipotenti creatori diceret: quare fecisti sic?_ _De praed. et gratia_ 16. And some theologians, including among the Reformed, spoke in the same spirit. Whoever perceives something of God’s incomparable greatness and the creature’s nothingness, and whoever at the same time considers how often we can look upon the gravest suffering of human and animal with the most indifferent disposition, above all when it serves our own interest, art, or science, loses the courage to attack Augustine or others for such a statement, let alone to call God to account. If it is a matter of _right_, solely and entirely of _right_, what right can we assert over against him who called us forth from nothing and granted everything we have and are? Nevertheless, although one can for a moment speak this way over against someone who thinks he may accuse God of injustice, almost all the Reformed, with Calvin at their head, ultimately rejected such a _dominium absolutum_ decisively and with indignation, above, p. 212 f. The reason why God willed one thing or another, elected this one and rejected that one, may be entirely unknown to us. His will is always wise and holy and good and had its just reasons for everything. His power cannot be separated from his righteousness, Calvin, _C. R._ XXXVI 310, 361. Once the right and honor of God were acknowledged, all the Reformed recommended the most cautious and tender treatment of the doctrine of predestination and warned against vain and inquisitive investigations. _Nos ergo nimis acutos esse non convenit; modo ne interea, quod Scriptura clare docet ac experientia confirmat, vel negemus verum esse vel tanquam Deo minus consentaneum carpere audeamus_, Calvin, ibid., 366; Zwingli, _Op._ VIII 21; Beza, _Tract. theol._ I 197; Martyr, _Loci C._ III, c. 1; Westminster Confession, in Schaff, _Creeds_ III 610; Canons of Dort I 12, 14; etc. Although God knows those who are his and the number of the elect is called small, _bene sperandum est tamen de omnibus, neque temere reprobis quisquam est annumerandus_, _Conf. Helv. II_, in Niemeyer, _Coll. Conf._ 482; Piscator, _Aphorismi_, 1614, p. 223; Zanchius, _Op._ II 497 sq. Further, all maintained that sin, although not _praeter_, is nevertheless very definitely _contra Dei voluntatem_. It could admittedly not be the cause, the _causa efficiens_ and _impulsiva_, of the decree of reprobation, for it itself followed the eternal decree in time and, if it were itself the cause, would have had to cause all human beings to be rejected. Yet it was _causa sufficiens_, and specifically the _causa meritoria_ of eternal punishment. There is, after all, a distinction between the decree of reprobation and reprobation itself. The former has its final and deepest cause only in God’s will; but reprobation itself takes sin into account. The decree of reprobation realizes itself through the human being’s own guilt, Polanus, _Synt._ 251; Twisse, _Vind. gr._ I 273 sq.; Perkins, _Werken_ I 769; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IV 14; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XXIV 50; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VII 445; Heppe, _Dogm._ 132. Therefore this decree is also no _fatum_ that drives the human being forward against his will, no sword of Damocles hanging threateningly over his head. It is nothing other than the divine idea of reality itself. In the decree, causes and effects, condition and fulfillment, the entire connection of things, are linked together precisely as we behold them in reality. Sin, guilt, misery, and punishment have in the decree the same nature and the same mutual relation as in the world of things itself. We see that decree, which is not revealed to us beforehand, gradually unfold in all its fullness in history; with us it is and must be the pure reflection of reality in our consciousness. We think things after they exist. But with God the decree is the eternal idea of reality as it gradually unfolds in time. His thoughts of things precede their being. And now the decree of reprobation says only that this whole sinful reality, this whole world history in the mutual connection of its events, has its ultimate cause not in itself—how could that be?—but outside itself in the thought and will of God. The decree changes nothing in reality; reality is and remains entirely the same whether one follows Augustine or Pelagius. But it causes the believer to confess that even that terrible world, for which the Manichaeans introduce an αντιθεος, pessimism a blind and wretched will, and many a fate or chance, exists according to the will of him who now causes us to walk by faith but one day, on the day of all days, will justify himself before every creature. Entirely incorrect, therefore, is the representation as though God’s counsel in general and the decree of reprobation in particular were a single, naked disposition of will concerning someone’s eternal destiny. It may not be represented as though for a human being only the end had been determined and he were now forcibly driven there whatever he might do. The decree is as immeasurably rich as reality. It is the fountainhead of _all_ that exists. In a single conception it encompasses the end with the ways, the goal with the means. It is no transcendent power that from above arbitrarily intervenes in reality now and then and drives it toward the end. It is the divine, immanent, eternal idea that displays its fullness in the forms of space and time and unfolds successively in length and breadth for our limited view what is one with God. The decree of reprobation therefore also does not lie loosely alongside all other decrees, nor alongside that of election. Sin and grace, punishment and blessing, righteousness and mercy do not lie dualistically alongside each other in reality, as though the reprobate were visited only with sin and punishment and the elect exclusively with grace and blessing. After all, believers still sin daily and stumble in many ways. Are these sins of believers then an efflux of election? No one will claim it. These sins are indeed in turn made serviceable by God to their salvation, and all things cooperate for good for those called, Rom. 8:28. But those sins are not suited for this in themselves and by nature, but only because God as the Almighty can still cause good to proceed from evil. Sins are therefore no means to salvation, as are regeneration and faith. They are no _praeparatio gratiae_ but, considered in themselves, _negatio gratiae_, Becanus, _Theol. schol._ I, tr. 1, c. 14, qu. 3, nn. 12–20. Therefore the law still has significance also for believers; therefore they are admonished to make their election sure with fear and trembling; therefore there is also sometimes mention of temporary hardening and rejection among believers. But conversely, the _reprobati_ share in many blessings that do not as such flow to them from the decree of reprobation but from God’s goodness and grace. They receive many natural gifts—life, health, strength, food, drink, gladness, and so forth, Matt. 5:45; Acts 14:17; 17:27; Rom. 1:19; James 1:17; etc. God does not leave himself without witness among them. He endures them with much patience, Rom. 9:22. He causes the gospel of his grace to be proclaimed to them and has no pleasure in their death, Ezek. 18:23; 33:11; Matt. 23:37; Luke 19:41; 24:47; John 3:16; Acts 17:30; Rom. 11:32; 1 Thess. 5:9; 1 Tim. 2:4; 2 Pet. 3:9. The Pelagians infer from these passages that it is God’s proper, essential will that all human beings, head for head, be saved, and that there is therefore no preceding decree of reprobation. These texts do not teach this. But they do acknowledge as God’s will that all the means of grace labor also for the salvation of the _reprobati_. Now these _media gratiae_ do not as such flow from the decree of reprobation. They can be abused to that end; they can serve to render the human being without excuse, to harden him and aggravate his judgment, just as the sun can warm but also scorch. But in themselves, by nature, they are not _media reprobationis_ but _media gratiae ad salutem_, _Synopsis_ XXIV 54 sq.; Heppe, pp. 134, 135. Election and reprobation may therefore issue in a final and total separation, but here on this earth they cross each other, as it were, time and again. This indicates that neither of them in and of itself is a final end and neither was a _causa finalis_ for God. Both are means for the _gloria Dei_, which is the final end and thus the deepest ground of all things. The beginning and end, reason and goal, of all that exists is therefore something good. Sin and punishment can never have been willed by God in and for themselves. They conflict with his nature. He is far from wickedness and has no pleasure in afflicting. He does not do it from his heart. They can therefore have been willed by God only as means for another, better, greater good. There is even a great difference between election and reprobation. All that God does, he does for his own sake. Election too has its cause and end only in God. But he delights in the work he brings about through election. In it his own virtues shine back upon him. The new creation is a mirror of his perfections. But what he carries out according to the decree of reprobation is not directly and in itself the object of his good pleasure. Sin is not itself a good. It becomes a good only because and insofar as, contrary to its nature and despite itself, it is compelled by divine omnipotence to enhance God’s honor. It is indirectly a good because it is subjected, restrained, overcome, and thus displays God’s greatness, power, and righteousness. For in this his sovereignty ultimately comes most splendidly to expression: that he knows how to lead even evil toward good, Gen. 50:20, and makes it serviceable to the salvation of the church, Rom. 8:28; 1 Cor. 3:21–23; to the glory of Christ, 1 Cor. 15:24 f.; Eph. 1:21, 22; Phil. 2:9; Col. 1:16; and to the glory of his name, Prov. 16:4; Ps. 51:6; Job 1:21; John 9:3; Rom. 9:17, 22, 23; 11:36; 1 Cor. 15:28.
