## 11.

With _providentia generalis_ and _specialis_, however, Pelagianism does not yet rise in its full strength; to a certain extent it acknowledges these. But it appears in opposition especially when the eternal state of rational creatures, the particular decree of predestination, comes under discussion. Now predestination is only a further application of God’s counsel, his _providentia_. Just as no separation can be made between the natural and the moral world, so no boundary can be indicated between what relates to the temporal and what relates to the eternal state of rational creatures. Yet here Pelagianism exchanged predestination for _praescientia_ and described foreordination as that decree of God by which he determined eternal blessedness or punishment for those whose persevering faith or unbelief he had foreseen. However generally this conception may have been adopted in the Christian church—for it is shared by the Greek, Roman Catholic, Lutheran, Remonstrant, Anabaptist, Methodist, and other churches—it is nevertheless decisively contradicted by Scripture, religious experience, and theological thought. First, Scripture teaches plainly and clearly that faith and unbelief, blessedness and perdition are not merely objects of the _nuda praescientia Dei_ but specifically of his will and decree. The προγνωσις in Rom. 8:29; 11:2; 1 Pet. 1:2; cf. Acts 2:23, is no passive foreknowledge, no state of consciousness, but, like the Hebrew ידע, Hos. 13:5; Amos 3:2; etc., a self-determination of God preceding realization in history to enter into a particular relation with the objects of his foreknowledge. It stands in the closest connection with προθεσις, προορισμος, and ἐκλογη, and is an act of his εὐδοκια. Second, it is the doctrine of Scripture that faith cannot proceed from the natural human being, 1 Cor. 2:14; that it is a gift of God, Eph. 2:8; Phil. 1:29; 1 Cor. 4:7; and that it therefore does not precede election but presupposes it and is its fruit and effect, Rom. 8:29; Eph. 1:4, 5; Acts 13:48. Third, it is the unanimous testimony of all religious, Christian experience that salvation, in both objective and subjective senses, is solely and entirely God’s work. In doctrine someone may be Pelagian; in the practice of the Christian life, above all in prayer, every Christian is Augustinian. Then he excludes all boasting and gives honor to God alone. Augustine therefore rightly said that the faith of the ancient church in God’s grace had expressed itself not so much in the _opuscula_ as in the prayers, _De dono pers._ 23; cf. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 16. Fourth, the _praescientia Dei_ is of such a nature that its object is foreknown as indubitably certain, and then it is equivalent to predestination; if, however, the object is totally contingent and arbitrary, _praescientia_ cannot be maintained either. According to the Greek, Roman Catholic, and Lutheran churches, and even according to the Remonstrants in their Remonstrance—all of which seek to evade predestination through _praescientia_—the number of those who believe and will be saved is just as fixed and certain as according to Augustine and the Reformed. Augustine said: _certus est praedestinatorum numerus qui neque augeri potest neque minui_, _De corr. et gr._ 13. And so it is taught by Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 40; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 23, arts. 6, 7; cf. qu. 24, and all Roman Catholic theologians, although they differ in that some derive the certainty of the outcome more from the will, and others, such as Molina and others, more from God’s knowledge, Perrone, _Prael. theol._ II 249; Pesch, _Prael. theol._ II 205; Jansen, _Prael. theol. dogm._ III 143. The Lutheran theologians later indeed made predestination dependent upon _praescientia_ but never called into question the certainty and immutability of the outcome, Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, loc. VII §212 sq.; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. polem._ III, p. 20; Hollaz, _Examen theol._, p. 641. Scripture spoke too clearly and strongly in many places, Dan. 12:1; Matt. 24:24; 25:34; John 10:28; Rom. 8:29–30; 1 Pet. 1:2–4, for this immutability to be denied. Both _formaliter_ and _materialiter_, according to both quantity and quality, the number of those who are saved stands immovably fixed according to the confession of all Christian churches. But if this is acknowledged and thought through, _praescientia_ is one and the same with _providentia_ and _praedestinatio_. God foreknew who would believe, and he knows this eternally and immutably. These will therefore also certainly and infallibly come to faith and salvation in time. For freedom in the sense of chance and arbitrariness, no room remains anywhere from this standpoint. _Praescientia_ includes _praedestinatio_. If one says, like Castellio, in Schweizer, _Centraldogmen_ II 278, that God foreknew the contingent precisely as contingent, then one falls back into Augustine’s line of thought and can thereby reconcile freedom with predestination just as well. The very question is whether the free and contingent as such can be certainly and infallibly known from eternity. If yes, then Augustine is right and the whole doctrine of _praescientia_ is unnecessary. If no, then one must go further and deny _praescientia_ as well; then the outcome of world history must remain absolutely contingent and as such incalculable and unknowable. Cicero already saw this and therefore also denied _praescientia_, cf. above, p. 208 f. Later he was followed by the Socinians; the Remonstrants, Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV, sect. 2, c. 18; Vorstius, in Scholten II 492; and many theologians in more recent times who, in order to maintain creaturely freedom, assumed a self-limitation of God in his knowing, willing, and ability, Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ §509; Martensen §§115, 116; Rothe, _Ethik_ §42; Dorner, _Gl._ I 319 f.; Hofstede de Groot, _Inst. theol. nat._, 3rd ed., p. 183; and others; cf. Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 490. Yet the Christian churches did not dare this consequence. All confess the _providentia_ and _praescientia Dei_. Everything occurs in time as it is eternally known by God. The final result and the ways and means leading to it stand fixed in God’s _praescientia_. Considered in this way, the doctrine of predestination is not a confession of the Reformed church alone, no private opinion of Augustine and Calvin, but a dogma of all Christendom. There is difference in the name by which it is called and in the manner in which it is presented, but there is agreement in substance: all Christian churches and theologians confess that everything is, happens, and turns out as God eternally knows it. In this sense Augustine could rightly say: _praedestinationis hujus fidem, quae contra novos haereticos nova sollicitudine defenditur, nunquam ecclesia Christi non habuit_, _De dono pers._, c. 23; and Prosper: _praedestinationem Dei nullus catholicus negat_, in Perrone II 249. All Christian churches have discussed it in their confession to a greater or lesser degree. Indeed, it may be said that whether one thinks in a Pelagian or Augustinian way, the thing about which one thinks nevertheless remains the same. History does not change. The facts and the connection of the facts in world history exist as they are, independently of the false or true conception we form of them, Schweizer, _Ref. Dogm._ I 73. The distinction lies only in this: that the Reformed, with Scripture in hand and Augustine as their example, did not stop with the secondary causes but dared to ascend to the first cause, that is, the will of God, and could find rest for their thought and life in it alone. The doctrine of predestination has its invincible force and rigor in the facts of world history as they are explained by God’s Word as the execution of his eternal counsel. It is not itself hard and severe; rather, terrifyingly serious are the _facts_ upon which it is built, Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 349. Pelagianism therefore does not satisfy because at every point of life and human history it collides with the tremendous reality. It is a superficial varnish that indeed deceives the human being but does not change reality.
