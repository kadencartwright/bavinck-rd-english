## 18.

Thus predestination ultimately issues in election; in election it reaches its end and comes to its full reality. In its highest form it is God’s decree concerning the revelation of his virtues in the eternal, glorious state of his rational creatures and the arrangement of the means leading to it. Even so, reprobation may not be forgotten. Only over against this dark reverse side does election itself appear in the most glorious light. It is of gripping seriousness that even on this highest terrain, where the eternal weal and eternal woe of rational creatures are at issue, day arises from dark night and light is born from darkness. The law seems to obtain everywhere that many are called and few chosen. There is a profound truth in the proverb that one person’s death is another’s bread. Darwin’s doctrine of “the survival of the fittest” has general validity and is in force throughout the whole creation. Thousands of blossoms fall away so that a few may ripen into fruit. Millions of living beings are born and only a few remain alive. Thousands of human beings labor in the sweat of their brow so that a few can bathe in luxury. Wealth, luxury, art, science, all that is high and noble is built on the foundation of poverty, deprivation, and ignorance. Never and nowhere do things in the world proceed according to the equal distribution of the socialists. There is no equality in any domain. Everywhere there is election alongside and on the foundation of rejection. The world is not arranged according to the Pharisaic law of work and reward; merit and wealth have nothing to do with each other. And on the highest terrain too, it is divine grace alone that makes a distinction. Like all the decrees, election too has its deepest cause in God’s good pleasure. Pelagians of every kind have always wished to understand these decrees as acts of God’s righteousness in which he proceeded according to human merit. God permits himself in his decrees to be determined by the foreseen conduct of the creature. He offers salvation to all. He grants faith to whoever, by his natural power or even by a granted supernatural power, makes good use of this offer. He saves whoever perseveres in faith to the end. Now there is indeed a certain order among the decrees; they include both the end and the means. In his decree God connected the hearing of his children’s prayer to that prayer. If he decreed to give rain in a drought, he determined at the same time that his people would ask him for it and that he would give the rain in answer to their prayer. In his decree he established a connection between sunshine and warmth, sowing and harvest, idleness and poverty, knowledge and power, and so forth, and likewise between sin and punishment, unbelief and perdition, faith and salvation. The harmony between the phenomena and events in the actual world is a perfect imprint of the harmony in the world of God’s thoughts and decrees, Edwards, _Works_ II 514. Scripture frequently stops with these _causae secundae_, and the Reformed too fully acknowledged their significance. But these secondary causes are not therefore yet the final and deepest cause. And one cannot escape the investigation of that cause. Questions press upon us from every side. Why is there such a connection between phenomena and events mutually as we repeatedly observe in the world? An appeal to the nature of things is no conclusive answer, for that nature too is from God and determined by him. Science can establish the _that_, but it presupposes what exists and does not know why it is and must be this way. Why there is causality among creatures mutually; why each creature is what it is; why there is such endless diversity among creatures in kind, nature, genus, species, power, understanding, wealth, honor, and so forth—within the sphere of the created no cause for this can be found. And likewise among rational creatures: why some angels are destined for eternal glory while the fall and perdition of others is foreseen and determined; why precisely that human nature which Christ assumed was deemed worthy of this honor; why one human being is born within and another outside Christianity; why one has so much advantage over another in character, aptitude, disposition, and upbringing; why one child dies early and as a child of the covenant is received into heaven while another outside the covenant enters death without grace; why one comes to faith and another does not—all these are questions that no creature can answer. God’s decrees cannot be understood as acts of a righteousness that deals according to work and merit. Above all, with the angels it appears clearly that the ultimate cause of their election and reprobation must lie in God’s will. For even if one makes use of _praescientia_, saying that God foresaw the perseverance of some and the fall of other angels, this _praescientia_ nevertheless then precedes their creation. Why then did God create those angels whose fall he foresaw? Why did he not give them sufficient grace to stand, as he did the others? Here there is a reprobation that rests solely and entirely upon God’s sovereignty, Bellarmine, _De gr. et lib. arb._ II, c. 17; Twisse, _Vind. gr._ I 76. On the other hand, election in itself is not always an act of mercy or explicable as such. In the election of Christ and the good angels there is no question of sin and thus of mercy. And the election of human beings is indeed an act of mercy but is nevertheless not explicable from mercy alone. For then God would have had to be merciful to all, since all were miserable. Likewise, reprobation was indeed an act of righteousness but cannot be explained from righteousness alone, for then all would have been rejected, Twisse, _Vindic. gr._ IV 111 sq. The decrees may therefore stand in mutual connection, but as acts of God they are themselves not conditional but absolute, acts of God’s absolute sovereignty. God established a causal connection between sin and punishment, and he maintains it in everyone’s conscience; but the decree of reprobation finds its deepest cause not in sin and unbelief but in God’s will, Prov. 16:4; Matt. 11:25, 26; Rom. 9:11–22; 1 Pet. 2:8; Rev. 13:8. Likewise, there is a causal connection between faith and salvation, but the decree of election is not caused by foreseen faith; rather, election is the cause of faith, Acts 13:48; 1 Cor. 4:7; Eph. 1:4, 5; 2:8; Phil. 1:29. Even Christ cannot be regarded as _causa electionis_. This expression is indeed capable of a good explanation. Thomas rightly says that Christ is the cause of our predestination, considered not as act or decree but with a view to its end and goal. _Sic enim Deus praeordinavit nostram salutem, ab aeterno praedestinando, ut per Jesum Christum compleretur_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 24, art. 4. Some Reformed therefore also spoke of Christ as _causa_ or _fundamentum electionis_, or of our election _per_ and _propter Christum_, Hyperius, _Meth. Theol._, p. 193; _Conf. Belg._, art. 16; _Conf. Helv. II_, art. 10; Martinius at the Synod of Dort 1618/19, sessions 65, 67; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ IV §41. Christ is indeed _causa_ or _fundamentum electionis_ insofar as election is realized in and through him. He is also the _causa meritoria_ of the salvation that is the end of election. He is also the Mediator and Head of the elect. The decree of election was also made with a view to the Son, from love for him, Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ V 31. The church and Christ were chosen together, in the same decree, in fellowship with and for one another, Eph. 1:4. But Christ as Mediator is therefore not yet the _causa impulsiva_, _movens_, _meritoria_ of the decree of election. In this sense Christ was indeed called the cause of election by many Roman Catholics; by the Remonstrants, in their _declaratio circa art. 1 de praedest._, in M. Vitringa II 55; by the Lutherans, Formula of Concord, in Müller, pp. 705, 720, 723; Quenstedt III 17, 31 sq.; and by many more recent theologians, Hofmann, _Schriftbew._ I 229; Meyer on Eph. 1:5; Kübel in Herzog² 12, 156; etc. But the Reformed rightly opposed this, Martyr, _Loci C._ 236; Polanus, _Synt._ VI 27; Voetius, _Disp._ II 267; Twisse, _Vindic. gr._ I 139 sq.; Turretin, _El._ IV, qu. 10; Moor II 18 sq. After all, Christ is himself the object of predestination and therefore cannot be its cause. He is a gift of the Father’s love, and this love thus precedes the sending of the Son, John 3:16; Rom. 5:8; 8:29; 2 Tim. 1:9; 1 John 4:9. The Son did not move the Father to love, but electing love arose from the Father himself. And so Scripture everywhere teaches that the cause of all decrees lies not in any creature but only in God himself, in his will and good pleasure, Matt. 11:26; Rom. 9:11 f.; Eph. 1:4 f. Precisely for this reason the doctrine of election is such an inexpressibly rich comfort, both for the unbeliever and for the believer. If it went according to right and merit, all would be lost. But now that it goes according to grace, there is hope even for the most miserable. If work and reward were the standard in the kingdom of heaven, it would be opened for no one. Or if, according to the doctrine of Pelagius, the virtuous person were elected because of his virtue and the Pharisee because of his righteousness, the poor publican would be excluded. Pelagianism is so pitilessly hard. But to confess election is to acknowledge even in the most unworthy of human beings, in the one sunk deepest, a creature of God and an object of his eternal love. Election serves not, as it is so often preached, to repel many, but to invite all to the riches of God’s grace in Christ. No one _may_ believe that he is reprobate, for everyone is earnestly and urgently called and is obligated to believe in Christ unto salvation. No one _can_ believe it, for his life itself and all that he enjoys is proof that God has no pleasure in his death. Nor does anyone actually _believe_ it, for then he would already have hell on earth. But election is a source of comfort and strength, of humility and lowliness, of confidence and decisiveness. Human salvation lies immovably fixed in the gracious and almighty good pleasure of God.
