## 10.

Within the counsel of God, first of all, that decree must be distinguished which formerly was generally designated by the name _providentia_. The word προνοια, _providentia_, providence, originally means nothing other than seeing ahead, caring ahead, deliberating ahead. Thus the word was also first understood in theology. God’s providence was therefore reckoned among the decrees and discussed under God’s will. It was then described as _ratio ordinis rerum in finem_, that is, as that act of God’s understanding and will by which from eternity he ordered all things to an end determined by him. As such a _ratio ordinis_, it must indeed be distinguished from the execution in time, that is, from the _exsecutio ordinis_, which more specifically bore the name _gubernatio_, Boethius, _De consol. phil._ IV, pr. 6; Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 35, n. 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 22, art. 1; _C. Gent._ III 77. Providence is understood as a decree of God in this sense, besides by many Roman Catholic theologians, Petavius, _De Deo_ VIII; _Theol. Wirceb._ III, p. 175 sq.; Perrone, _Prael._ II 233; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 158, also in the earliest period by many Reformed theologians, Ursinus, _Explic. Catech._, qu. 27; Zanchius, _Op._ II 324, 436; Maresius, _Syst. theol._ IV §19; _Conf. Helv. II_, art. 6; cf. later concerning God’s government. In this sense _providentia_ is a _consilium_ or _decretum_ according to which, as to an exemplar, God preserves and governs all things in time. But later the name _providentia_ came to be used mostly for the act of preservation and government itself, as Zwingli, Calvin, Polanus, the _Synopsis_, and others already treat it in this sense after creation. The name matters less, but it is important that God’s decree encompasses all things, not only the determination of the eternal state of rational creatures (_praedestinatio_), but the ordering and arrangement of all things without distinction. And this was formerly designated by the name _providentia_. Predestination thus did not come to stand loosely by itself but was connected with God’s decree concerning all things and was only a particular application of it. As Zwingli expressed it: _est autem providentia praedestinationis veluti parens_; _praedestinatio_ is providence itself insofar as it is related to the eternal destiny of human beings and angels. In closer connection with Scripture, which does not speak of προνοια, the Reformed expressed this by the counsel of God. This encompasses all things and thus relates in the first place to the world as a whole. Everything, even in unconscious nature, is and happens according to God’s counsel. His ordinances underlie all things. Heaven and earth, light and darkness, day and night, summer and winter, sowing and harvest, and so forth, are ordered both in their unity and in their diversity by him who is wonderful in counsel and great in deed, Gen. 1:14, 26, 28; 8:22; Pss. 104:5, 9; 119:90, 91; 148:6; Job 38:10 f.; Isa. 28:29; Jer. 5:24; 31:25 f.; 33:20, 25. Insofar as God’s counsel has irrational nature as its object, it is almost unanimously acknowledged, except by the Manichaeans and others. There is still great agreement also when such a counsel and ordering of God is assumed in a more special sense for rational creatures. The creation of the human race, Gen. 1:26; the distribution of the nations, Gen. 11; the determination of their times and habitation, Acts 17:26; the distinction between nations and human beings in gift, aptitude, rank, station, wealth, status, and so forth, Deut. 32:8; Prov. 22:2; Matt. 25:15; even the inequality within the church in all kinds of gifts, 1 Cor. 4:7; 12:7–11; Rom. 12:4 f., find their deepest cause in God’s will. All being and all diversity of being can be explained only from his good pleasure. The ground of the existence and of the particular existence of all things rests in God alone. That something is and that it is precisely this and not something else; that there is manifold being and life; that there is endless diversity among creatures in species, genus, duration, rank, station, possession, and so forth—this is solely God’s good pleasure. And this good pleasure does not presuppose but creates its object. If the animals could speak and disputed with their Creator because they were not, like us, human beings, everyone would consider this absurd, says Augustine, _De verbis apost._, sermo 11; cf. Calvin, _Inst._ III 22, 1. And indeed nothing befits any creature other than to rest in God’s good pleasure. Properly speaking, there is only a choice between these two: either the creature is its own maker and thus ceases to be a creature, or the creature remains a creature from beginning to end and thus owes its existence and its particular manner of existing to God alone. As soon as this counsel of God was also extended to the moral world, however, opposition arose from every side. Here God’s counsel had reached its end; this was the special domain of humanity; here the human being appeared as the creator of his own destiny. In order to maintain freedom, responsibility, guilt, and so forth, Pelagius made a distinction between the natural and the moral world, between _posse_ and _velle_, and withdrew the latter from God’s counsel and providence. And all Pelagianism is intent on making the moral world, in part or in whole, independent over against God. This attempt, however, already deserves to be rejected in itself and entirely in general. First, it conflicts with Scripture. For Scripture indeed maintains the moral nature of humanity at all times as strongly as possible, but it never seeks to do this by making a dualistic separation between the natural and moral world and withdrawing the latter from God. God’s counsel extends over all things, including the moral, over evil as well as good. Next, such a separation is in fact impossible. The world-idea is one organic whole. _Physis_ and _ethos_ stand in the closest connection and interpenetrate at every moment. There certainly is distinction, but nowhere is there separation. No point can be indicated in creation where God’s counsel and government end and the independent will and act of the creature begin. Above all in this century, the historical and organic view has displaced and condemned this Pelagian separation at every point. Further, this dualism would remove the greatest and most important part of the world from God’s counsel and hand it over to chance and arbitrariness. Indeed, not only from God’s counsel and will, but even from his knowledge, the world is then withdrawn in large part. If God and his creature can be conceived only as competitors, and the one can retain his freedom and independence only at the expense of the other, then God must be increasingly restricted in his knowing and willing. Pelagianism banishes God from the world; it leads to deism and atheism and sets the arbitrariness and foolishness of humanity upon the throne. Therefore the solution of the problem must be sought in another direction, namely, such that because God is God and the world his creature, by his infinitely great knowing and willing he does not destroy but precisely creates and maintains the independence and freedom of creatures.
