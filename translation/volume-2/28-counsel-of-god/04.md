## 4.

Pelagianism was condemned together with Nestorianism at the Council of Ephesus in 431.
The conflict between the followers of Augustine—Prosper, Hilary, the anonymous author of _De vocatione omnium gentium_, Lucidus, Fulgentius, and others—and the Semi-Pelagians—Cassian, Faustus of Riez, the unnamed author of the much-discussed book _Praedestinatus_, which arose in the fifth century and was published by the Jesuit Sirmond in 1643, Gennadius, Vincent of Lérins, and others—first came to a decision at the Synod of Orange in 529.
Here, on the one hand, it was clearly taught that the whole human being is corrupted through the sin of Adam, can. 1, 2; that of oneself one has nothing but _mendacium et peccatum_, can. 22; and that both the _initium_ and the _augmentum fidei_ are owed not to ourselves, to our natural powers, to our free will, but to grace, to the _infusio, operatio, inspiratio, illuminatio_ of the Holy Spirit in us, who bends (_corrigens_) our will from unbelief to faith, can. 3-8.
But, on the other hand, it is said of the free will only that it has been weakened by sin, _infirmatum, attenuatum_, can. 8, 13, 25; that all the baptized, by the grace received in baptism, _Christo auxiliante et cooperante_, can and must fulfill, _si fideliter laborare voluerint_, what belongs to salvation, can. 13, 25; and further, there is complete silence concerning absolute predestination, _gratia irresistibilis_, and the particularity of grace.
This indecisiveness had harmful effects.
It became clearly evident in the Gottschalk controversy.
At that time many already occupied a Semi-Pelagian or Pelagian standpoint—Hincmar, Rabanus, Erigena—and gained the victory at the Synod of Quierzy in 853.
But there were also numerous learned men who could by no means acquiesce in the condemnation of Gottschalk and the declarations of Quierzy.
Among them were Prudentius, Remigius, Ratramnus, Lupus, and others; they defended a _gemina praedestinatio_; the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_ is without any merit, does not occur on account of _praevisa fides, merita_, but is the cause of the _praedestinatio ad gratiam, fidem, merita_, and so forth; the _praedestinatio ad damnationem_, however, does not in the same way include the _praedestinatio ad peccata_, but, at least as _reprobatio positiva_, depends upon the _praescientia_ and _permissio_ of sins.
Hence 1 Tim. 2:4 was still understood in a restricted sense—not of all human beings head for head, but of many or all kinds of human beings—and it was considered absurd that Christ would have made satisfaction for all human beings, thus also for the Gentiles who never hear of him and even for the man of sin, the Antichrist.
The Synod of Valence in 855 still occupied this standpoint as well.
Cf. H. Schrörs, _Der Streit über die Prädest. in IX Jahrh._, Freiburg, 1884; Niemeijer, _De strijd over de leer der praedest. in de 9de eeuw_, Groningen, 1889; Schwane, _D. G._ III 428 f.; Denzinger, n. 283 sq.
And in its main features this is also still the standpoint of scholasticism.
The _praedestinatio ad mortem_ is preferably not called by this name because it then so easily gives the appearance that it entails the _praedestinatio ad peccata_ as a means, but it is usually treated under the name _reprobatio_; sometimes the latter is then distinguished into negative and positive; the first is _ante praevisa merita_ and an act of sovereignty, but the second depends upon and follows the _praescientia_ and the decree to permit sins.
Hence 1 Tim. 2:4 is still understood in a restricted sense, and a _benevolentia Dei universalis_ and a general satisfaction of Christ are not yet taught, Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 40 and 41, with the commentaries of Thomas, Bonaventure, Scotus; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 19 and 23; _C. Gent._ III 163.
But nominalism, the rejection of the Reformation, and Jesuitism have moved the Roman church and theology ever farther away from Augustine and Paul.
Rome did not ignore the call to reformation with impunity and established the following dogma at Trent:
1º Through sin the _liberum arbitrium_ is indeed _viribus attenuatum et inclinatum_, but not _amissum et extinctum_, Trent, sess. 6 cap. 1 and can. 5; before _justificatio_ the human being can still do many natural things that are by no means sin but truly good, ibid., can. 7.
2º For the good in the supernatural sense—for faith, hope, love, for _justificatio_, for acquiring eternal life—the natural human being, deprived through original sin of the _dona superaddita_, is not capable.
For this one needs more than the _vires naturae_ and _legis doctrina_, namely, the _divina gratia_, the _praeveniens Spiritus Sancti inspiratio_, ibid., can. 1-3.
3º This _gratia divina_ is granted to the children of believers in baptism and in adults consists in God’s calling them objectively through the gospel and subjectively touching their heart _per Spiritus Sancti illuminationem_.
This grace is unmerited, _praeveniens, gratia gratis data_, ibid., cap. 5.
4º This grace, however, is not irresistible.
It indeed arouses, assists, and moves the human being and makes one fit to turn toward one’s _justificatio_ and to give free assent to grace, but one can both _recipere_ and _abjicere_ the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, ibid., cap. 5.
If one accepts this grace and, aroused and moved by it, cooperates with God and by the seven _praeparationes_ makes and prepares oneself, _se disponat ac praeparet_, for _justificatio_, then one receives this in baptism from grace and only according to what scholasticism calls a _meritum ex congruo_, ibid., capp. 6 and 8 and can. 4.
5º The _gratia infusa_ of _justificatio_ remains resistible and losable, but when accepted and preserved it enables one to do good works and, according to a _meritum ex condigno_, to merit eternal life, ibid., capp. 9-16.
6º In agreement with this, Rome decisively rejected Augustine’s doctrine of absolute _praedestinatio_.
In doing so, however, it has always avoided the name of Augustine, made a caricature of his doctrine among his followers, and condemned this caricature.
The aforementioned book _Praedestinatus_ represents matters as though a sect of ultra-predestinarians had existed in Gaul in the fifth century and reproduces Augustinianism in paradoxical propositions that no follower of Augustine ever taught, Harnack III 225 f.
And likewise in Gottschalk, Bradwardine, Wycliffe, Hus, Baius, Jansen, and Quesnel, genuine Augustinianism was condemned under a false name, Denzinger, _Enchir._ § 477 sq., 881 sq., 966 sq., 1216 sq.; Harnack III 628-640.
The _Tridentinum_ expresses itself very cautiously concerning predestination.
On the one hand, it appears to teach an election, for it says that no one in this life may establish _se omnino esse in numero praedestinatorum_, since only from special revelation can one know _quos Deus sibi elegerit_, VI cap. 12 and cann. 15, 16.
But on the other hand, it expressly teaches that Christ was sent so that _omnes adoptionem filiorum reciperent_, that he made satisfaction for all, VI capp. 2 and 3, that the human being can accept or reject, preserve or lose grace, and it condemns the doctrine: _justificationis gratiam non nisi praedestinatis ad vitam contingere; reliquos vere omnes qui vocantur vocari quidem sed gratiam non accipere utpote divina potestate praedestinatos ad malum_, ibid., can. 17—as though the latter had been taught by anyone and in this way!
The church leaves these two series of statements unreconciled alongside one another, but theology has always and in all sorts of ways attempted to bring them into agreement.
First, almost all Roman Catholic theologians teach that God _voluntate antecedente_ wills the salvation of all and therefore also had Christ make satisfaction for all; this _voluntas antecedens_ extends even to children who die unbaptized and to the Gentiles.
But according to his _voluntas consequens_, which takes account of the good or bad use human beings make of their freedom and of grace, God does not will the salvation of all.
Here _praedestinatio_ is already made dependent from the beginning upon _praescientia_.
Next, the distinction between _praedestinatio complete_ and _incomplete sumpta_, _adaequata_ and _inadaequata_, gradually arose among them and was generally accepted.
By the former is understood predestination both _ad gratiam et ad gloriam_; by the latter, that which pertains to one of the two.
The intention of this distinction is that the _praedestinatio ad gratiam_ and the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_ are not inseparably connected; one can partake of the _praedestinatio ad gratiam_ and have received the grace of faith and justification, and yet lose it again and thus be deprived of the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_.
This separation was unknown to the medieval theologians Anselm, Lombard, Thomas, and others, and is still contested by some but gains ground ever further.
As for the _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_, this is called unmerited by all over against Pelagianism, Rom. 9:16; John 6:44; it does not occur _ob praevisa merita_; and over against Semi-Pelagianism it also precedes the human will, _gratia praeveniens, praecedens_.
Because the _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_ is the beginning of the _praedestinatio completa_ or _adaequata_, it can be said that the whole predestination is grace and unmerited because it is _gratuita in causa_, while among the Thomists it is _gratuita in se_.
But this does not exclude a certain order being followed in the _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_ and in the predestination to every subsequent grace; according to Molina, God foresaw by the _scientia media_ that some human beings would make good use of the grace offered each time and decreed the distribution of grace accordingly.
He also foresaw that the saints through their prayers or through _merita de congruo_ would acquire grace for others and decreed accordingly to grant it to them.
Someone cannot merit the _praedestinatio ad primam gratiam_ for oneself, but the saints can indeed do so for others, and above all Christ is _causa nostrae praedestinationis_.
According to the Augustinians and Thomists, such as Sylvius, Thoma, Salmanticenses, Gonet, Gotti, Billuart, Alvarez, Lemos, Goudin, and others, and also according to Bellarmine and Suarez, the _praedestinatio ad gloriam_ is indeed absolute; God first decreed to grant blessedness to some prior to and apart from all merits, and afterward decreed so to work upon them through grace that they could merit that blessedness by their works.
But the Molinists—Molina, Valentia, Vasquez, Tanner, Lessius, Becanus, Petavius, Lapide, and others—defend the _praedestinatio ad gloriam post praevisa merita_.
Finally, as for _reprobatio_, the Augustinians and Thomists are divided; some adhere completely to Augustine, are infralapsarian, and make the decree of reprobation follow upon original sin and upon this alone, so that reprobation is a _justa derelictio in eadem massa_ (Gonet, Gotti, Gazzaniga); others distinguish between a negative but absolute decree not to grant blessedness to some and another decree to withhold grace, permit sin, and punish (Alvarez, Estius, Sylvius, Salmanticenses); still others understand by the decree of reprobation nothing but God’s will to permit some to perish through their own fault (Billuart, Goudin); cf. C. Pesch, _Praelect._ II 217 sq.; Jansen, _Prael._ III 171 sq.
But over against them stand the Molinists, who completely reject the so-called _reprobatio negativa_, that is, an absolute, sovereign decree that precedes sin, and teach that God _voluntate antecedente_ wills the salvation of all, and further accept only a _reprobatio positiva_, that is, a decree of God to punish eternally those whose sin and unbelief he has foreseen.
_Reprobatio_ is therefore in every respect _post praevisa merita_, and then, according to some such as Hincmar, is only a predestination of punishment for the wicked, but not of the wicked for punishment.
