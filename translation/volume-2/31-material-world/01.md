# § 31. The Material World

## 1.

Alongside the spiritual world there also exists a material world.
But while the angels in their existence and nature are known only from revelation and are hidden from reason and science, the material world is beheld by all and is discussed in philosophy as well as theology, in religion as well as science.
Here, therefore, difference and collision are possible at every moment.
Philosophy and theology do indeed speak about the material world in different senses.
The former investigates the origin and nature of all things, but the latter proceeds from God and leads everything back to him; it deals with creatures only insofar as they are works of God and reveal something of his attributes; even when it treats creatures, therefore, it is and always remains theology; Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ II c. 2 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ V c. 7.
But although there is thus an important distinction, theology and philosophy nevertheless deal with the same world.
To avoid collision between the two, a division of domains has often been proposed: science would investigate visible things and leave to religion and theology nothing but the religious-ethical world; or, still more strictly, all that exists would belong to science, and religion would be permitted to speak only in judgments of value.
But such a separation is both theoretically and practically impossible.
Just as every scientific system is always ultimately rooted in religious convictions, so there is no religion that does not bring with it a definite view of the created order.
All religions have their cosmogonies, which did not arise from philosophical reasoning but rest upon tradition.
Genesis 1 too is by no means a philosophical worldview but a tradition that in many respects agrees with the cosmogonies of other religions and yet also differs from them in a remarkable manner.
The agreement consists in this: in all cosmogonies the formation of the world is ascribed to God; the first state was undeveloped and chaotic; the formation of the world proceeds through several periods, ascends from lower to higher, and ends in the human being, who is akin to God; cf. above, p. 386.
But the points of difference are much greater still.
The pagan cosmogonies are all at the same time theogonies, all assume a primordial matter, an _Urstof_, and teach a chaos in the proper sense.
The narrative in Genesis, by contrast, is strictly monotheistic, teaches a creation from nothing, and knows no chaos in the proper sense.
It is therefore also incredible that the Jews would have adopted this narrative from the Babylonians during the exile.
First, creation was already known to the Israelites before the exile.
Next, this was also already true of the seven-day week, which is founded upon the days of creation.
Then it is improbable that the Jews would have adopted so important a part of their doctrine from their conquerors.
And finally, the pagan cosmogonies were so thoroughly polytheistic that they had to repel rather than attract the monotheistic people of Israel and could not simply be reworked into so beautiful a monotheistic narrative as Gen. 1.
Rather, everything argues that in Genesis we possess a tradition that comes from the most ancient times, was gradually corrupted among the other peoples, and was preserved in its purity by Israel; Delitzsch, _Neuer Comm. z. Genesis_, pp. 41, 42.
