## 8.

These four attempts to bring Scripture and science into agreement are not opposed to one another in every respect. There is some truth even in the first-mentioned ideal theory. For everyone concedes that Scripture does not speak the language of science but that of daily experience; that also in its account of creation it takes a geo- or anthropocentric standpoint; and that in doing so it does not intend to give a lesson in geology or any other science but remains, also in its account of the origin and becoming of all creatures, the book of religion, revelation, and the knowledge of God. _Non legitur in Evangelio dominum dixisse: mitto vobis paracletum qui vos doceat de cursu solis et lunae; christianos enim facere volebat, non mathematicos_, Augustine, _De actis c. Felice Man._ I 10. _Moses rudi populo condescendens secutus est, quae sensibiliter apparent_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 70, art. 4. _S. Scriptura ex professo non tractat eas res, quas in philosophia cognoscimus_, Alsted, _Praecognita_ 181; cf. Voetius, _Disp._ V 131; Hettinger, _Apol._ III 196. But when Scripture from its own standpoint, precisely as the book of religion, comes into contact with other sciences and sheds its light upon them as well, it does not then suddenly cease to be God’s Word but remains such. Even when it speaks about the becoming of heaven and earth, it gives no saga, myth, or poetic fantasy; according to its clear intention, it gives history then as well, history that deserves faith and trust. Christian theology therefore, with only a few exceptions, maintained the literal, historical understanding of the creation narrative. It is nevertheless remarkable that no confession defined anything about the hexaemeron and that in theology all kinds of interpretations were tolerated alongside one another. Augustine already admonished his readers not to be too quick to regard something in this domain as contrary to Scripture, not to speak about these difficult subjects except after serious study, and not to make themselves ridiculous in the eyes of unbelieving science through ignorance, _De Gen. ad lit._ I 18, 19, 20, 21; cf. also Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 68, art. 1. The theologians did not always take this warning faithfully to heart. And yet geology can render us excellent service in explaining the creation narrative. Just as the Copernican worldview compelled theology toward a different and better explanation of the sun’s standing still in Josh. 10; just as Assyriology and Egyptology provide precious contributions to the interpretation of Scripture; just as history often first enables us to know prophecy in its true significance; so too the geological and paleontological investigations of this century serve toward a better understanding of the creation narrative. It should be carefully borne in mind that the creation and preparation of heaven and earth is a divine work par excellence, a miracle in the absolute sense, full of mysteries and secrets. And yet in Genesis this work is related in so simple and sober a manner that there almost seems to be a disharmony between the fact itself and its description. Behind every feature in the creation narrative lies a world of God’s wonders and mighty deeds, which geology displays before our eyes in an immeasurable series of phenomena. Scripture and theology therefore have nothing to fear from the _facts_ brought to light by geology and paleontology. The world too is a book whose every page has been written by God’s almighty hand. The conflict arises only because the text of the book of Scripture as well as that of nature is often read and understood so poorly. The theologians are not blameless here and have often condemned science in the name not of Scripture but of their own incorrect interpretation. And natural scientists have repeatedly explained the facts and phenomena they discovered in a way, and placed them in the service of a worldview, that was justified by neither Scripture nor science. For the present it would be advisable for geology, which is still a comparatively young science and has indeed already investigated much but still has infinitely much to investigate, to restrict itself to gathering material and to refrain from constructing conclusions and formulating hypotheses. It is as yet wholly unable to do so and must exercise patience for a long time before it is qualified and capable of it.
