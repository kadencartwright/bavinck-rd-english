## 9.

If these preliminary observations are taken to heart, then it is probable, in the first place, that the creation of heaven and earth in Gen. 1:1 preceded the work of the six days in verse 3 f. by a shorter or longer time.
The restitution theory certainly went astray when it placed the fall of the angels and the devastation of the earth in Gen. 1:2.
Not a word is said about this; nor does it say that the earth _became_ waste and void, but that it was so and was created thus; and the waste and void by no means imply that the earth had been devastated, but that, although already earth, it was still unformed, without shape or form.
For the rest, however, it is correct that the creation of heaven and earth and the waste and void state of the earth cannot be placed within the first day; that day first began, and by the nature of the case could first begin, with the light.
The first day does not consist of the original darkness and the light created thereafter; rather, it consists of the first alternation of evening and morning, which occurred after the creation of light.
The darkness of which Gen. 1:2 speaks was not the first evening; rather, only after the light had been created did evening come, and afterward morning.
And with that morning the first day, which had begun with the creation of light, had elapsed; in Genesis the day begins and ends with the morning; cf. Keil, Delitzsch, and others on Genesis.
Augustine, _Conf._ XII 8; Lombard, _Sent._ II dist. 12, 1, 2; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 74 art. 2; Petavius, _De sex dier. opif._ I c. 9 n. 2, and others, therefore also judged rightly that the creation of heaven and earth and the waste state of the earth took place _ante omnem diem_.
Only in this way is proper justice also done to the fact that the creation in Gen. 1:1 is related simply as an event without any further description, while the preparation of the earth, Gen. 1:3 f., is related at length.
Gen. 1:1 says only that God is the Creator of all things but makes no mention that God created them through his Word and Spirit.
Naturally, this is not denied, but it is nevertheless not stated there; nor is it stated how long God took to create heaven and earth or in what manner he did so, or how long the unformed state of the earth lasted.
Only when the six-day work begins is it said that even that unformed earth was maintained and made fruitful by God’s Spirit, Gen. 1:2, and that all things upon and within that earth were brought about by God’s Word, Gen. 1:3f.; in the _distinctio_ and _ornatus_ of the earth during the six days, God’s wisdom comes to expression; Calvin on Gen. 1:3.
But even if, in view of Ex. 20:11; 31:17, one wished to bring what is related in Gen. 1:1 and 2 within the first day, the only result would be that the first day was an entirely unusual one.
It would then have begun at the moment of creation and would first have been dark for some time, Gen. 1:2.
In any case, this exegesis does recognize the truth enclosed in the concordistic theory named above in the second place.
Let it be freely conceded that the interpretation of the creation days as periods arose under the influence of geology.
Yet this by no means condemns it; the only question is whether Scripture permits this interpretation without violence being done to its sense.
Formerly Josh. 10:12 was considered incompatible with the Ptolemaic worldview; today no one rejects it on that ground any longer, for everyone acknowledges that the words of Scripture permit another interpretation than the one formerly held to be the only true one.
In reality, the prophetic expectations prove to extend over much longer periods of time than the words in which they are clothed suggest on a first reading.
The day of YHWH encompasses a much longer period than the word itself implies.
The apostles spoke of a little time, the end, the last time, the last hour, and so forth, and yet eighteen centuries have sped by since their word.
In view of this entirely distinctive measure that Holy Scripture repeatedly applies in temporal determinations, it is not _a priori_ impossible that the days in Gen. 1 may also be understood as periods.
But there are also positive data that make this exegesis not necessary but nevertheless possible.
Too much emphasis is often placed in this connection upon the expressions repeatedly occurring in Scripture: in the days of, at the end of days (after many years), all the day (always), in the day that (when), and so forth; the figurative meaning of the word day in the Old and New Testaments, for example Isa. 2:2, 11; 13:6, and so forth; Matt. 24:22; Luke 10:12; John 9:4; Acts 2:20; 17:31; Rom. 2:16; 1 Cor. 3:13; 1 Thess. 5:2, 4, likewise has insufficient probative force; and the appeal to Ps. 90:4 and 2 Pet. 3:8 is also of little relevance.
But the following is indeed important.
First, as was demonstrated above, it conflicts with Gen. 1 to bring the _creatio prima_ and the earth’s unformed state within the first day.
If one nevertheless does this, that first day becomes in any case an entirely unusual one, beginning with darkness and having an incalculable duration.
Second, the first three days of the _creatio secunda_, according to the intention of Genesis as well, are reckoned by an entirely different measure from the following three.
The essence of day and night does not consist in a shorter or longer duration but in the alternation of light and darkness.
For the first three days this alternation was not effected by the sun, which was first created on the fourth day, but came about in another manner through the light created in verse 3.
If this is the case, however, the length of those first three days is also indeterminable.
Third, it follows from this that the following three days too can be understood in the same manner and in agreement with the first three days as periods.
For the sun, moon, and stars were indeed created on the fourth day, and it is therefore in itself possible that the second triad of days was determined by the rotation of the earth.
But the creation narrative does say that the sun, moon, and stars were then made and received their destination for the earth, yet it does not say in a single word that this destination was immediately and fully attained on that same day.
The creation period itself unfolded under entirely different circumstances from those that commenced after the work of creation was finished.
Only then was everything completed and fixed.
Fourth, it is very difficult to place on the sixth day everything that Gen. 1 and 2 record as occurring on it.
That day must then include the creation of the animals, Gen. 1:24, 25; the formation of Adam, Gen. 1:26; 2:7; the planting of the garden, Gen. 1:8–14; the promulgation of the probationary command, Gen. 2:16, 17; the bringing of the animals to Adam and his naming of them, Gen. 2:18–20; Adam’s sleep and the creation of Eve, Gen. 2:21–23.
This may not be impossible, but it is nevertheless not probable.
Indeed, when Scripture in Gen. 1:26–31 mentions the creation of Adam and Eve in one breath and makes not the slightest mention of all that the second creation narrative inserts between them, and nevertheless places all this on the sixth day, Gen. 1:31, the possibility that the days are periods is thereby sufficiently proved.
Fifth, it is not the intention of Genesis to demonstrate that the creation of all things took place in precisely 6 × 24 hours, not a minute shorter or longer.
Rather, in the creation narrative day designates the time in which God was creatively active.
With each morning he gives existence to a new world; evening commences when he has brought it into being.
The creation days are God’s working days.
Through a six-times-renewed labor he prepared the entire earth and changed the chaos into a cosmos.
This is set before us as an example in the Sabbath commandment.
As with God, so also for the human being rest begins after six days of labor.
For Israel that creation time was also the foundation for the division of the ecclesiastical year.
And for the whole world it remains a type of the aeons of this dispensation, which will one day end in the world-Sabbath, in eternal rest, Heb. 4.
Sixth and finally, much more occurred on every creation day than the sober words of Genesis lead us to suppose.
Just as in the Decalogue one particular sin comprehends many others under it, so in the creation narrative only the principal thing is named for each day, that which is best known and most important to the human being.
Scripture itself proves this, for the first creation narrative contains nothing about paradise, the naming of the animals, Adam’s sleep, and so forth, which are mentioned only in the second creation narrative.
Further, natural research makes known to us all kinds of creatures of which the writer of Genesis did not think for a moment; all kinds of chemical elements, many minerals and plants, and various kinds of animals, insects, infusoria, and bacteria are not named in Genesis.
Yet they must have been created and therefore must find a place in the _hexaemeron_.
On every day the work of creation was certainly much greater and richer than Genesis reports to us in its simple and sublime narrative.
