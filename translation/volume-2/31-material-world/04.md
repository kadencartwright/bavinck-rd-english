## 4.

Christian theology has treated this hexaemeron with particular affection. The literature is astonishingly rich but has been processed almost completely in the important book by Dr. Zöckler, _Geschichte der Beziehungen zwischen Theologie und Naturwissenschaft mit besondrer Rücksicht auf die Schöpfungsgeschichte_, 2 parts, Gütersloh, 1877/79. The oldest Christian interpretation of the hexaemeron has been preserved in the second book of Theophilus’s work _Ad Autolycum_, c. 9–38. It is also discussed at greater or lesser length by Tertullian, _Adv. Hermog._, c. 19 sq.; by Origen in his homily on the hexaemeron, which opens his 17 homilies on Genesis; then especially by Basil, _In Hexaemeron homiliae_ IX; Gregory of Nyssa, _Apolog. in Hexaemeron_; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._, book II; and, in the West, especially by Lactantius, _Inst. div._ II, c. 8–12; Ambrose, _In Hex._, books VI; Augustine, especially _De Genesi ad litt._, books XII; _De civ._ XI 4 sq.; _Conf._ XI–XIII; etc. These works were utilized by Isidore, Bede, Alcuin, and others and then remained the foundation for the treatment of the hexaemeron in scholasticism, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 12–18; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 44–102; Bonaventure, _Sent._ II, dist. 12–18; _Brevil._ II, c. 1–5; etc. Even after the Reformation, the same worldview and the same understanding of the hexaemeron continued to prevail in both Roman Catholic and Protestant theology. On the Roman Catholic side, the principal treatments are those of Cajetan in his commentary on Genesis; Eugubinus in his _Cosmopoeia_, 1535; Catharinus in his elucidations of the first 5 chapters of Genesis; Pererius in his four-volume work on the first book of Moses; Lapide in his well-known commentary; Molina in his treatise _De opere sex dierum_; Suarez in his commentary on the first part of the _Summa_; Petavius in his _Theol. dogm._, tractate _De sex primorum mundi dierum opificio_; Becanus, _Theol. schol._, tr. IV, _De operibus sex dierum_; etc. Among the Lutherans the principal works are Luther’s commentary on Genesis; Melanchthon’s annotations on Gen. 1–6; Chemnitz, _Loci Theol._, 1610, pp. 109–123; Quenstedt, _Theol. did. pol._ I 431 sq.; Hollaz, _Syst. Theol._, pp. 361–373. The literature among the Reformed is richer still. This material is treated not only in commentaries on Genesis by Calvin, Zwingli, Oecolampadius, Musculus, Martyr, Piscator, de Dieu, Cocceius, and others, and it appears not only in dogmatic works such as those of Polanus, Gomarus, Heidegger, Mastricht, Maresius, Moor, and others, but many separate treatises were also devoted to it, such as Capito, _Hexaemeron Dei opus explicatum_, 1539; Danaeus, _Physice christiana, seu christ. de rerum creatarum origine et usu disputatio_, 1575; Zanchius, _De operibus Dei intra spatium sex dierum creatis_, _Op._ III 217–480; Voetius, _Disp._ I 552–881; V 148–241; Rivetus, _Exerc. theol. et schol. in 1 libr. Mosis_, _Op._, 1651, I, p. 1 sq.; Hottinger, _Κτισις ἑξαημερος, i. e. Historiae creationis examen theol.-philol._, Heidelberg, 1659; cf. further Walch, _Bibl. theol. sel._ I 242; Mart. Vitringa II 93.

All these works adopt the standpoint of the Aristotelian-Ptolemaic worldview. The earth rests immovably at the center of the universe; all the stars and the whole heaven move around it. People could not conceive of those stars moving freely in space and therefore imagined each star to be fastened in a sphere. They accordingly had to assume as many celestial spheres as they observed stars with unequal motion and orbital period. And now it was not the stars but the spheres that moved and carried along the stars fastened within them. The firmament thus consists of a system of eight or more concentric spheres nested within one another without empty intervals; the highest, outermost sphere is that of the fixed stars, the “first heaven,” as Aristotle called it. The earth was conceived as a globe or as a disk surrounded by water. Only a few accepted that antipodes could exist and that land also lay on the other side of the ocean; as a rule both were rejected. This Ptolemaic worldview naturally also influenced the exegesis of the hexaemeron. Two tendencies can clearly be distinguished within it. The one rejects the temporal character of the six days, at most attributes visionary significance to them, has everything created at once and simultaneously, and often arrives at all kinds of allegorical explanations. It is represented already by Philo and later in the Christian church by Clement, Origen, Athanasius, Augustine, Erigena, Abelard, Cajetan, Canus, Gonzalez, and others, and also by Moses Maimonides, _More Nebochim_ II, c. 30. The other tendency maintains the literal sense of the creation narrative, particularly also of the six days. It was followed by Tertullian, Basil, Gregory of Nyssa, Ephrem, John of Damascus, and thereafter came to almost exclusive dominance in scholasticism and in Roman Catholic and Protestant theology, although Augustine’s alternative exegesis was always discussed with respect and never condemned as heretical, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 15, 5; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 74, art. 2. Despite this important difference in the exegesis of Gen. 1, there was nevertheless complete agreement in worldview. The Ptolemaic system persisted into modern times, long after Copernicus had advanced his explanation of the motion of the heavenly bodies. It was certainly not the church and orthodoxy as such that resisted the newer worldview, as is so readily represented—for example, by Draper in his _Gesch. van de worsteling tusschen godsd. en wet._, Haarlem, 1887. Rather, it was Aristotelianism, which sought to maintain itself against the modern age in every domain—in science as well as religion, in art as well as the church. Hence the Christian church and theology, although they have now universally exchanged the Ptolemaic for the Copernican hypothesis, have nevertheless continued to exist to the present day and, toward the end of this century, by no means appear doomed to death. This proves that church and theology are not so bound to these worldviews that they stand or fall with them. Indeed, it is impossible to see why the Copernican hypothesis, provided it adequately explains the astronomical phenomena, would have to be rejected by Christian theology as such. For Scripture indeed always speaks geocentrically and also relates the origin of things from the standpoint of the earth, but in doing so it employs the same language of daily experience that we still use, even though we have an entirely different conception of the motion of the heavenly bodies from the one generally prevailing when the biblical books were written. It can even be acknowledged without hesitation that the biblical writers had no other worldview than the one generally accepted at the time; there is, after all, a distinction between _auctoritas historiae_ and _auctoritas normae_, cf. I 371–373. This language of Holy Scripture explains why the miracle narrated in Josh. 10:12, 13; 2 Kings 20:9; Isa. 38:8 is described in terms of the sun’s standing still and moving backward. That does not in any way determine that the miracle itself consisted in an objective standing still and moving backward of the sun. Without being rationalistically explained away, it can be and has been explained in many different ways; cf. Dilloo, _Das Wunder an den Stufen des Achaz_, Amsterdam, 1885. We too would now express the same phenomena in the same way; Scripture relates the miracle as fact but does not say in what manner it came about. More strongly still: even though in an astronomical sense the earth is no longer the center for us, in a religious and ethical sense it most certainly remains so, and it remains so for all people without distinction; science can change nothing about this. In a certain sense the human being is the weakest of all creatures; the strength of many an animal and the power of nature far surpass his. And yet he is king of the earth, the crown of creation; he is weak as a reed, but he is a _roseau pensant_. And though the earth may be thousands of times smaller than many planets, in an ethical sense it is and remains the center of the universe. It is the only planet suited to be a dwelling place for higher beings; here the kingdom of God is established, here the struggle between light and darkness is fought, here God prepares for himself an eternal dwelling in the church. We therefore continue to look upward from this earth, to the place from which both in a physical and an ethical sense rain, sunshine, and growth must come, without thereby determining the place of heaven in an astronomical sense or knowing its location in the universe. It is surely superficial, however, to say that scientific research has deprived God and the angels of their dwelling place. For although Lalande dared to say that he had searched the entire universe and nowhere found God, the universe with its immeasurable spaces is still one riddle to our limited sight; and whoever does not find God in his immediate vicinity—in conscience and heart, in Word and church—will not find him in the universe either, even if he equips his eye with the finest telescope, Ebrard, _Het geloof aan de H. Schrift en de uitkomsten van het onderzoek der natuur_, trans. by Dr. A. v. d. Linde, Amsterdam, 1862; Paul Wigand, _Die Erde der Mittelpunkt der Welt_, issue 144 of _Zeitfr. des chr. Volkslebens_.
