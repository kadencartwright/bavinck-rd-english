## 10.

With this interpretation of the creation narrative, the results of geological research can very well be brought into agreement.
But one must then know with certainty what those results are.
As soon as one undertakes a somewhat careful investigation of them, however, one encounters all kinds of difficulties, and everything becomes more uncertain the more deeply one investigates and the further one penetrates.
Naturally, no one has or can have any objection to the facts that geology brings to light.
Those facts are just as much words of God as the content of Holy Scripture and must therefore be accepted in faith by everyone.
But the exegesis that geologists offer of those facts must be very strictly distinguished from the facts themselves.
The phenomena displayed by the earth are one thing; the combinations, hypotheses, and conclusions that investigators of the earth connect with them are another.
Leaving aside the by no means imaginary possibility that even the observation, ascertainment, and description of geological facts and phenomena sometimes stand very much under the influence of an _a priori_ worldview, geology today unanimously teaches that the earth’s surface is composed of different strata, all of which clearly bear the marks of having been deposited in water; that these strata, wherever and insofar as they are present, always occur in a certain order, so that, for example, a lower formation never lies between higher ones; and finally, that these strata contain a great mass of fossils, which again are not scattered indiscriminately throughout all the strata but are lower in kind the lower the sediments in which they occur.
Those are the facts; and upon them geologists have then constructed all those lengthy geological periods enumerated earlier.
But it is precisely against these geological periods that very serious objections exist.
1º. First, it deserves consideration that geology is still a young science.
It is not yet a hundred years old.
During the first half of its existence, in the hands of men such as von Buch, de Saussure, and others, it was by no means hostile to Scripture; only when Lyell and others placed it in the service of the doctrine of evolution did it become a weapon for combating the biblical creation narrative.
This consideration alone counsels caution; as geological science grows older and richer, it will probably revise itself on this point.
2º. Geology may be called the archaeology of the earth.
It acquaints us with conditions in which the earth formerly existed.
But naturally it tells us virtually nothing about the cause, origin, duration, and so forth of those conditions.
To attempt to construct a history of the earth from the phenomena of the earth seems _a priori_ as hazardous an undertaking as attempting to reconstruct the history of a people from its archaeological remains.
Archaeology may be very useful as an aid, but it cannot replace history.
Geology offers important data, but by its very nature it can never furnish a _Schöpfungsgeschichte_.
Whoever attempts this must resort to conjecture at every moment.
All birth, Schelling said, is from darkness to light.
All origins lie in darkness.
If we are not told who our parents and ancestors were, we do not know.
If there is no creation narrative, the history of the earth is and remains unknown to us.
3º. Geology therefore can never reach as far as the creation narrative; it naturally stands upon the foundation of the created order and does not approach Gen. 1; it can ascertain what it observes but can only conjecture about the origins of what it observes.
The geologist Ritter von Holger says very aptly and beautifully: Wir haben das Unangenehme, dass wir erst ins Theater gekommen sind, nachdem bereits der Vorhang gefallen ist. Wir müssen das Schauspiel das gegeben wurde, aus dem auf der Bühne zurückgebliebenen Dekorationen, Versatzstücken, Waffen u. s. w. (das sind namentlich die paläontologischen Entdeckungen oder die Versteinerungen) zu _erraten_ suchen, daher ist es sehr verzeihlich, wenn wir uns _irren_, in Trissl, _Das bibl. Sechstagewerk_, 1894, S. 73.
4º. Although the strata of the earth, wherever and insofar as they occur, lie in a certain order, it is equally a fact that nowhere do they all occur together as a complete set; some are found here, others elsewhere.
Wir haben nirgends ein vollständiges Exemplar des Buches der Erde, sondern verstreut über dieselbe eine grosse Menge von defecten Exemplaren von dem verschiedensten Formate und auf sehr verschiedenem Materiale, Pfaff, _Schöpf._, 5.
The sequence and order of the strata, and thus also of the geological periods constructed upon them, is therefore not furnished immediately by the facts but rests on a combination of facts that is exposed to all kinds of conjecture and error.
By the geologists’ own admission, much patience and effort are required to establish the correct order of the strata, Geikie, _Geologie_, deutsch von O. Schmidt, Strassburg, 1886, S. 55; Pfaff, _Schöpf._, 5.
5º. Only a very small portion of the earth’s surface has yet been investigated, especially England, Germany, and France.
Little is yet known of the other parts of Europe, and virtually nothing of the greater part of Asia, Africa, New Holland, and so forth.
Even Häckel admits that scarcely one-thousandth of the earth’s surface has been investigated paleontologically, _Nat. Schöpf._, 1874, S. 355.
And this estimate is certainly not too low.
Later investigations may therefore bring all kinds of other facts to light.
The hypotheses and conclusions of geology are thus in any case constructed upon an insufficient number of data.
6º. It is a fact that is gaining ever more recognition from geology that the time required for the formation of the earth’s strata cannot at all be determined from the nature and character of those strata.
Die Beschaffenheit der Schichten, says Pfaff, ibid., 5, giebt durchaus keinen Anhaltspunkt an die Hand, um über die Zeit ihrer Bildung einen Aufschluss zu erhalten.
Under the influence of Darwinism, which sought to explain everything by infinitely small changes over infinitely great spans of time, people have indeed spoken of millions of years.
But these are simply mythological numbers for which every basis is lacking, Schelling, _Werke_, II 1, S. 229.
Geologists do not yet know at all whether the same conditions or different ones prevailed formerly.
And even under the same conditions, everything grows much more rapidly and vigorously in youth than in later years.
Moreover, all the grounds upon which geologists have thus far based their numbers have proved untenable.
Delta formations, the rising and sinking of the mainland, coal formations, and so forth have all once again been abandoned as bases for calculation.
Judicious natural scientists therefore speak quite differently today.
Es fehlet uns jeder exacte Massstab zur Berechnung vorhistorischer Ereignisse, Zittel, _Aus der Urzeit_, 2nd ed., 1875, S. 556.
7º. Nor can the order in which the strata occur serve as a standard for calculating the time and duration of their formation.
Naturally, at a particular place the lowest stratum is older than the one above it, but there is no justification for joining together the different strata from different places and thus forming a sequence of formations and periods.
Wie jetzt in unseren Meeren an einer Stelle sich Kalkniederschläge bilden, während zu derselben Zeit an anderen Orten sich Lagen von Sand oder Lehm übereinander absetzen, so haben sich auch in früheren Zeiten an verschiedenen Orten gleichzeitig verschiedenartige Schichten gebildet, und wieder gleichartige zu verschiedenen Zeiten, Pfaff, ibid.
The strata from so-called different periods are not consistently different, and those regarded as equally old are not always qualitatively alike, Trissl, 61.
At one and the same time, similar formations may have taken place in different parts of the earth, just as still frequently happens today.
8º. Today the time of the formation of the strata and the order of their position are therefore determined almost exclusively by the petrifactions found in them, cf. the article “Geognosie” in Brockhaus.
Geology has become dependent upon paleontology, and today the latter stands almost entirely in the service of the doctrine of evolution.
It is assumed in advance as proven that organic beings developed from the lower to the higher, and upon this the order and duration of the sedimentary formations are then constructed.
Conversely, the order of the sediments was then used again as proof of the theory of evolution, and in this way geology fell into a _circulus vitiosus_.
But the doctrine of evolution is refuted far more than favored by paleontology.
For in the various strata, the different fossils of plants and animals do not occur in merely a few specimens and species.
Rather, at every stratum geology suddenly and all at once confronts an immeasurably rich world of organic life, differentiated into species and not supplemented by any transitional forms.
There are even petrifactions of plants and animals, now extinct, that surpass those of all later formations in size and strength and reveal nature, as it were, in its first creative power and luxuriant fertility, Pfaff, _Schöpf._, 667-709.
9º. Now it is true that fossils are not scattered indiscriminately throughout all strata, but that as a rule particular plants and animals occur in particular strata.
Yet nothing can be inferred with certainty from this either for the doctrine of evolution or for the geological periods.
For the different species of plants and animals were and are distributed over the surface of the earth in accordance with their nature and conditions of life; they lived in different places and zones, and therefore they also had to become fossilized in the different sediments formed in different places.
The petrifactions are therefore not representatives of the time at which organic beings originated but of the higher or lower zones in which they lived.
Suppose, indeed, that the plants and animals now living throughout the whole earth were suddenly buried in strata and became fossilized; then neither from the different kinds of fossils nor from the different strata in which they occurred could any conclusion be drawn concerning the time of their origin.
And to this must be added all kinds of other circumstances that make the division and calculation of the geological periods almost impossible, such as, for example, that in the earliest time the different species of plants and animals were not distributed over the whole earth as they were later; that no petrifactions of numerous plants and animals have been preserved in the different strata; that many kinds of causes may have brought some plants and animals into places and zones in which they did not naturally live; that the same strata do generally, but by no means always, contain the same kinds of fossils; and that strata qualitatively alike, which at first had been assigned to the same time, were later placed elsewhere in the sequence because new and different petrifactions were found in them, and so forth.
10º. Geologists themselves frequently acknowledge that the geological periods cannot be strictly separated.
This is especially evident in the Tertiary and Quaternary periods.
Here almost everything is uncertain.
The boundary, beginning, end, and duration of these two periods are uncertain.
The cause, extent, and duration of the so-called ice age are uncertain; there is disagreement about whether one or more ice ages must be assumed; and even the very existence of an ice age is still subject to serious doubt.
The cause, time, and manner in which the great prehistoric animals perished are uncertain, while their fossils have sometimes been preserved entirely intact.
The appearance of the human being—before or after the ice age, in the Tertiary or Quaternary period, together with or after the mammoth, mastodon, and rhinoceros—is uncertain.
The cause of the diluvial formations and of their extension over the whole earth is uncertain.
The cause and time of the formation of mountains and glaciers are uncertain; here the fact alone that the movement of the glaciers from the north toward central Europe would require the Scandinavian mountains to have a height of 44000 meters presents an almost insurmountable objection, and so forth.
11º. Finally, there is in addition the fact that Scripture and the unanimous tradition of almost all peoples tell of an immense flood that brought about a tremendous transformation in the entire condition of the earth.
According to Scripture, after the flood an entirely different condition sets in for humanity and the earth.
Humanity before the flood was distinguished by great intelligence, vigorous enterprise, titanic courage, long life, strong physical development, and dreadful godlessness.
And without doubt nature, the plant kingdom, and the animal kingdom were in harmony with this.
But in the flood almost all human beings perish, many species of plants and animals become extinct, nature is placed under restraint, and a milder dispensation begins, the dispensation in which we live.
These testimonies of Scripture are confirmed by geology on every side.
The human being from the Tertiary period has not yet been found, and it is not probable that such a human being will ever be found; humanity at that time, before the flood, was probably not yet dispersed over the earth; the flood explains why no fossils of human beings from before that time remain; and the skulls and bones of human beings that have been found here and there all came from the Quaternary period and did not differ from ours.
Further, geology clearly teaches that the human being lived at the same time as the mammoth—the Heb. behemoth, Job 40:10—and that the mammoth therefore still belongs within historical time.
The universality of the diluvial formations proves that the flood must have extended over the whole earth.
The mountains arose for a large part within historical time.
The causes of the ice age, if it ever existed, are completely unknown and may therefore very well have lain in the flood and in the decline in temperature that subsequently set in everywhere.
Only through and after the Diluvium did the earth receive its present form.
There is really only one serious objection to identifying the Diluvium with the flood, and that is time.
Geology commonly places the ice age and the Diluvium several thousand years before Christ.
But on the one hand it must be observed in reply that the chronology of Scripture is by no means settled either.
One need not go so far as de Sacy, who said, _il n’y a pas de chronologie biblique_, in order nevertheless to agree with Voetius when he declared, _ex S. Scriptura exacta supputatio haberi non potest_, _Disp._ V 153.
It is not impossible that generations were sometimes skipped and that personal names were intended as names of peoples.
And on the other hand the geological calculations are, as said above, far too uncertain for an objection against the foregoing interpretation to be derived from them.
12º. When we gather all this together and take it into account, Scripture offers, from the moment of creation in Gen. 1:1 until the flood, more than sufficient time in which to place all those facts and phenomena that geology and paleontology have brought to light in this century.
It is impossible to see why they could not all find a place within that time.
At present theology need do no more than this; it need not involve itself with the causes of those phenomena; let the explanation of the facts remain with geology!
But perhaps Scripture can nevertheless render better service in this matter than natural science commonly supposes.
For it points out to us that creation is a divine work par excellence.
At the origin and formation of things forces were at work, until the flood conditions existed, and in that flood a catastrophe occurred—phenomena such as have not occurred again since that time.
Origination is always governed by laws different from those governing development.
The laws of the creature are not the rule of creation, and much less are they the rule of the Creator.
Further, theology should adhere only to the indisputable facts made known by geology but guard against the hypotheses and conclusions that geologists add to them.
It therefore refrains from every attempt to identify the so-called geological periods with the six days of creation.
For it is nothing but an unprovable opinion that those periods followed one another successively and in that order.
This does not deny that, for example, the Azoic formations began already from the moment of creation.
Rather, everything indicates that under the operation of all kinds of mechanical and chemical forces they began at that time already.
But geology knows nothing that rules out their having taken place later as well, contemporaneously with the Paleozoic formations and so forth, and it conjectures about both the causes and the manner of their origin.
And so it is with all the other periods.
It is very probable that the so-called Tertiary period reaches all the way to the flood and that the Diluvium and the ice age coincide with this catastrophe.
Next, the simultaneous occurrence of plant and animal fossils in the so-called Paleozoic period decides nothing about the order of their origin.
After all, geology knows nothing of the origin of those organic beings; it finds them but does not penetrate to their origin.
And geology too must assume that the plant kingdom originated before the animal kingdom, for the simple reason that animals live on plants.
Insofar as geology can say anything about the order in which things originated, it agrees completely with Scripture.
First the inorganic creation; then the organic, beginning with the plant kingdom; afterward the animal kingdom, and this again in the same order: first the aquatic animals, then the land animals, and among these especially the mammals, Pfaff, _Schöpf._, 742.
Thus, as Christians and theologians, we await the _certain_ results of natural science with some confidence.
Theology need not fear thorough and comprehensive investigation.
It must only guard against attaching too much value to an investigation that is still entirely new, inexact, and incomplete and is therefore continually supplemented by guesses and conjectures.
It should be on its guard against making premature concessions to, and seeking agreement with, so-called scientific results that can be overthrown any day and be shown to be untenable by deeper investigation.
And as the science of divine and eternal things, it should have patience until the science that opposes it has conducted deeper and broader research and, as happens and has happened in most cases, corrects itself.
In this way theology better upholds its dignity and honor than by ceaselessly accommodating itself to the opinions of the day.

Standp. der Naturw._, 2nd ed., Regensburg, 1894; Id., _Sündfluth oder Gletscher_, 1894; Hahn, _Die Entstehung der Weltkörper_, Regensburg, 1895.
