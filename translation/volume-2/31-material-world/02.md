## 2.

In the account of Gen. 1, the first verse must be understood as the description of a distinct fact.
In verse 2 the earth already exists, though in a waste and empty condition.
And verse 1 relates the origin of that earth; it was immediately created by God as earth.
After heaven is only briefly mentioned alongside earth in verse 1, verse 2 immediately passes over to the earth; cosmogony becomes geogony.
And that earth is earth from the first moment; no ὑλη in the Aristotelian sense, no _materia prima_, nor chaos in the sense of the pagan cosmogonies.
_Ein geschaffenes Chaos ist ein Unding_ (Dillmann).
The earth is now indeed described to us as תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ, as a תְהוֹם over which darkness extended.
But this expresses something entirely different from what is usually understood by chaos.
The word תֹהוּ occurs repeatedly, especially in Isaiah, and everywhere suggests a space that is empty, a place where there is no way and everything is trackless and unformed.
The word בֹהוּ is found again in Isa. 34:10 and Jer. 4:23, both times in connection with תֹהוּ, and expresses the same thought.
The condition of the earth in Gen. 1:2 is not that of a positive devastation but that of a being not yet formed.
There is no light, no life, no organic being, no form and shape of things.
It is further explained by the fact that the earth was a תְהוֹם, a surging mass of water shrouded in darkness.
The earth is ἐξ ὑδατος και δι’ ὑδατος, 2 Pet. 3:5; Pss. 104:5, 6.
According to the intention of Genesis, this unformed, undeveloped condition certainly lasted some time, however brief.
What is described is not a merely logical supposition but an actual condition.
The only question that then arises is how long this condition lasted.
This in turn depends entirely upon whether the creation of heaven and earth of which Gen. 1:1 speaks falls before or within the first day.
Genesis gives no other impression than that the creation of heaven and earth in verse 1 and the unformed condition of the earth in verse 2 precede the first day.
After all, in verse 2 there is still darkness and no light; the day is not and does not begin with darkness but with light; only through the creation of light in verse 3 does the day become possible; God therefore called not the darkness but the light day, and the darkness he called night, verse 5; the alternation of light and darkness, of day and night, could begin only with the creation of light; only after there had been light could it become evening and thereafter morning again, and with this morning the first day ended, for Gen. 1 reckons the day from morning to morning.
The work of the first day therefore consisted not in the creation of heaven and earth, nor in allowing the unformed condition to continue, but in the creation of light and the separation of light and darkness.
There would be no objection whatever to this exegesis if it were not stated elsewhere that God created heaven and earth in six days, Ex. 20:11; 31:17.
This, however, can only be understood of the _creatio secunda_.
For in these two texts the emphasis falls not upon God’s having brought forth everything from nothing but upon his having been active for six days in forming heaven and earth, and this is set before us as an example.
There is clearly a distinction between what God does “in the beginning,” Gen. 1:1; cf. John 1:1, and what he does by speaking in the six days, Gen. 1:3 f.; the unformed condition of Gen. 1:2 separates the two from each other.
The _creatio prima_ is immediate, _immediata_; it is a bringing forth of heaven and earth from nothing; it presupposes absolutely no preexistent matter; it took place _cum tempore_.
But the _creatio secunda_, which begins with verse 3, is not direct and immediate; it presupposes the matter created in verse 1 and connects with it; and it occurs specifically _in tempore_, indeed in six days.
Hence this _creatio secunda_ already anticipates the works of preservation and government; it is already in part preservation and no longer sheer creation.
Indeed, at the same moment that heaven and earth are created by God in verse 1, they are also preserved by him.
_Creatio_ passes immediately and directly into _conservatio_ and _gubernatio_.
Nevertheless, the work of the six days, Gen. 1:3 f., must still be reckoned to creation.
For all the creatures brought forth in those six days—light, firmament, sun, moon, stars, plant, animal, human being—did not, according to Genesis, arise from the matter present by way of evolution through immanent forces according to fixed laws.
That matter was incapable of bringing forth all this by the natural way alone, through immanent development.
It did not possess within itself the fitness, the _potentia_, for this; it had only a _potentia obedientialis_.
Speaking and creating, God brought forth the whole cosmos from the primordial matter, the _Urstoff_, of Gen. 1:1.
At every new formation he indeed connected with what already existed, but the higher nevertheless did not proceed from the lower through immanent power alone.
Each time a creative word of God’s omnipotence was necessary.
