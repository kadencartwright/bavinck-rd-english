## 3.

The work of creation has been divided by Herder and others into two triads, in such a way that the works of the second triad correspond to those of the first.
There is indeed a correspondence between the work of the first and fourth days; but the second and fifth, and likewise the third and sixth, do not stand in such parallelism.
For on the fifth day not only the birds in the firmament but also the fish and aquatic animals are created, which corresponds much more closely to the work of the third day.
There is, however, clearly discernible in the works of creation a progression from the lower to the higher, from the general presuppositions of organic life to that organic life itself in its different forms.
Better, therefore, is the old division of the whole work of creation into three parts: _creatio_, Gen. 1:1, 2; _distinctio_ on the first three days, between light and darkness, heaven and earth, land and sea; and _ornatus_ from the fourth through the sixth day, the population of the prepared earth with all kinds of living beings; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 74.
Yet this division too is not intended as a strict separation, for the plants created on the third day also serve as adornment, and so forth.
The _distinctio_ and _ornatus_ bring the earth’s _tohoe wabohoe_ to an end.
The unformed and undeveloped state of the earth of which verse 2 speaks, however, may not for a moment be conceived as passive.
However long or short it may have existed, forces and operations lay within it.
For we read that God’s Spirit hovered over the waters.
The verb רחף means to hover over something with the wings, Deut. 32:11; and the use of this word proves that רוּחַ אֱלֹהִים must be understood not as the wind but specifically as the Spirit of God, to whom elsewhere also the work of creation is ascribed, Ps. 33:6; 104:30.
The Spirit of God, as the principle of creaturely being and life, works formatively and life-givingly upon the earth’s mass of water and thus answers the creative Word of God, which during the six days, in connection with what already exists, calls the different formations of creatures into existence.
The work of the first day consists, then, in the creation of light, in the separation of light and darkness, in the alternation of day and night, and therefore also in motion, change, and becoming.
For according to Huygens’s currently most widely accepted hypothesis, light is not a substance but an immensely rapid undulation or vibration of the ether atoms, and thus nothing but motion.
It is therefore to be distinguished from the light-bearers—the sun, moon, and stars—and according to Genesis precedes them.
Light is also the most general presupposition of all life and development.
Whereas the alternation of day and night is needed only for animals and human beings, light is already a necessity for the plant world; moreover, it gives form, shape, and color to all things.
On the second day a distinction is made between the firmament, the heaven of air and clouds, which according to optical appearance is frequently called a curtain, Ps. 104:2; a veil, Isa. 40:22; a sapphire, Ex. 24:10; Ezek. 1:22; a mirror, Job 37:18; a roof and vault over the earth, Gen. 7:11; Deut. 11:17; 28:12; Ps. 78:23, and so forth; and the earth with its waters, Ps. 24:2; 136:6.
The work of separation and distinction begun on the first day is continued on the second; the distinction of light and darkness, of day and night, is now made serviceable to the separation of heaven and earth, of air and clouds above, of earth and water below.
At the end of the second day the words “God saw that it was good” are absent.
From this some have inferred that the number two was an ominous number or even that hell was created on that day; but the reason is surely this: the work of the second day is most closely connected with that of the third and is completed only in the separation of the waters; the divine approval therefore follows after that.
For on the third day the separation between earth and water, land and sea, is accomplished; thereby the earth has become a cosmos, with continents and seas, mountains and valleys, countries and rivers.
Without doubt, all these formations took place only through the most tremendous operations of the mechanical and chemical forces that lie within nature.
These were awakened by God’s word of power and by the animation of the Spirit and gave the earth its cosmic form.
From now on other forces, namely organic ones, also appear.
The earth is still naked and bare.
Therefore this day does not end before vegetation in general has also been created, which then divides chiefly into two kinds, namely herbs and trees, each of which has its own seed and thus reproduces itself.
This plant world could not do without light, but it could do without the sun.
But this is not so with the animal and human world; before these are created, therefore, the sun, moon, and stars must first be prepared on the fourth day.
This does not imply that the mass of matter for these planets was first created then, but only that all those thousands of planets first became on this day what they would henceforth be for the earth: that together they take the place of the light and are for the earth signs of wind and weather, of events and judgments; for the regulation of fixed times for agriculture, navigation, feasts, and the life of human beings and animals; and finally for the reckoning of days and months and years.
The fourth day thus recounts the appearance of the starry heavens for the earth; day and night, and so forth, are henceforth regulated by the sun; the earth becomes a member, a part of the universe; it is set in harmony with all the other planets.
Now the earth is ready as a dwelling place for animate, living beings, for animals and human beings.
On the fifth day the waters themselves, through God’s word of power, bring forth all aquatic animals, and the air is filled with all kinds of birds.
Of both kinds of animals a great multitude is created, of all kinds and number.
Then on the sixth day follows the creation of the land animals, which at God’s command come forth from the earth, specifically in three kinds—wild animals, cattle, and creeping animals—and finally also the creation of the human being, who according to a definite counsel of God is formed from the earth in body and is created directly by God in soul.
Thus the whole creation was completed.
God saw all that he had made, and behold, it was very good.
He took pleasure in his own work.
Therefore he rested on the seventh day.
His rest is a consequence of his satisfaction and pleasure in his works, which are now complete as works of creation, but at the same time it positively consists in blessing and sanctifying the seventh day, so that creation, continuing to exist on that seventh day, blessed by God with all kinds of powers, sanctified by God to his service and honor, would henceforth itself develop under the providence of the Lord and fulfill its destiny.
