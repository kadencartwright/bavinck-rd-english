## 5.

The Copernican worldview therefore encounters no objection from Christian theology.
The case is entirely different, however, with the hypotheses accepted by present-day science for the formation of our planetary system and of the earth.
With respect to the former, Kant and Laplace proposed the hypothesis that our planetary system, and indeed properly the whole universe, had originally been one gaseous chaos of very high temperature, rotating from west to east around its own axis.
This rotation resulted in pieces flying off that, because they continued moving in the same direction, gradually assumed the form of spheres, Haeckel, _Natürl. Schöpfungsgesch._, 5th ed., 1874, S. 285 f.; Fr. Pfaff, _Schöpfungsgesch. mit bes. Berücksichtigung des bibl. Schöpfungsberichts_, 3rd ed., 1881, 8. 190 f.; Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_, 16th ed., 1888, S. 130 f.
Now it deserves notice first of all that, however deistically conceived, this hypothesis was by no means proposed by Kant in order to set God aside.
Rather, he judged that this chaotic condition of all matter was the simplest that could follow upon nothing, and that those materials themselves had all been so formed by God as the first cause that through immanent forces and according to fixed laws they could produce the present world-system without any miraculous intervention by God.
This hypothesis, however, is further inadequate to explain the origin of the universe, motion, and organic beings.
In general it must be observed that, however primitive and chaotic that first condition of all matter may be conceived and however many millions of years it may be moved back, it nevertheless provides no rest for thought.
One will then either have to acknowledge with Kant that this very first condition of the creature in its entirety depends immediately upon God and follows upon nothing, or one will have to see in that chaotic condition not only the beginning of the present world-system but also the end and destruction of a preceding world, and so _in infinitum_, and thus will have to eternalize matter and motion, Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._ II 522; Strauss, _Der alte u. d. neue Glaube_, 225; Büchner, ibid., 133; Haeckel, ibid., 288.
But further, this hypothesis is burdened by many objections and does not explain the phenomena.
They do not all need to be discussed here, for example, the fact that there are also celestial bodies that have a retrograde motion and rotate not from west to east but from east to west.
They are nevertheless so weighty that they are acknowledged even by Haeckel.
It need only be recalled that, given the gaseous nebular mass and also given mechanical motion, this is by no means sufficient to explain this world-system.
For motion and matter are not enough.
There must also be direction in that motion, and besides matter something else must also have existed to explain the world of spiritual phenomena.
Why did this world-system arise from that nebular mass, a system that everywhere displays order and harmony and would collapse at the slightest deviation?
How could the universe come into being through an unconscious, purposeless motion of atoms?
The chance of such a world-whole arising from such a chaotic condition is highly improbable and properly speaking entirely impossible.
And to this must be added that even if this hypothesis explained the phenomena, it would still remain a hypothesis.
For what inference can be drawn from possibility to actuality?
_A posse ad esse non valet consequentia._
What proof can be adduced that the world-system not merely could have arisen in this way but actually did arise in this way?
There is a great difference between a logical supposition and an actual condition that is said once to have existed.
When natural science investigates phenomena, it attempts to reduce them to their simplest form.
It therefore ultimately assumes very primitive and most simple data—atoms, ether, chaos, and so forth.
But these are logical suppositions at which it arrives.
That such atoms once existed purely as atoms in a most primitive condition, in a condition that followed upon nothing, is by no means proven thereby.
Like the original elements of things (atoms, dynamids, monads), the primitive conditions that are posited prior to formation are also nothing but auxiliary conceptions, not reality.
The case is the same as with that religionless condition now assumed in research into the origin of religion, cf. I 208, or with Rousseau’s state of nature, from which the state arose through the _contrat social_.
Perhaps all such hypotheses can render some service as auxiliary conceptions in thought, like auxiliary lines in _mathesis_, but they are not therefore real explanations, actual _principia_ of what exists.
Finally, what no science can teach, revelation teaches, confirmed in this by the tradition of all peoples: namely, that it pleased God in forming the world to proceed from the imperfect to the perfect, from the simple to the composite, from the lower to the higher.
There is a truth in the doctrine of evolution that is also acknowledged by Scripture.
Gen. 1:2 expresses this clearly.
But there the condition of creation is a real condition: no chaos in the proper sense, no ὑλη in the Aristotelian sense, no _prima materia_ without _forma_, no inconceivable mass of pure atoms, but a condition of formlessness of earth and heaven that existed for a time, in which the Spirit of God was active, hovering and brooding.
It is therefore not permissible, with many Christian apologists, to adopt the Kant-Laplace hypothesis without any form of criticism and then be grateful that one has been able to insert it so well into Gen. 1:2.
Rather, Scripture relates to us a real condition, while science speaks of suppositions that are scientifically untenable, Pfaff, _Schöpfungsgesch._, S. 731 f.; Ulrici, _Gott und die Natur_, 2nd ed., 1866, S. 334-353; Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_, 4th ed., 1876, S. 179 f.; T. Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_, 2nd ed., 1892, II 327-352; Braun, _Die Kant-Laplace’sche Weltbildungstheorie_, in _Neue Kirchl. Zeitschr._ III, 9th issue; Steudel, _Christenthum und Naturwissenschaft_, Gütersloh, 1895, S. 142 f.; Schanz, _Ueber neue Versuche der Apol._, Regensburg, 1897, S. 211 f.
