## 6.

The same difference that appears in the formation of our world-system also presents itself in the developmental history of the earth.
On the basis of the earth’s strata and the fossils of plants, animals, and human beings found in them, geology has constructed a hypothesis concerning the earth’s periods of development.
According to this, the oldest period is the azoic, or that of the _Urformatie_, in which chiefly the eruptive kinds of rock were formed and no trace of organic life is yet found.
This was followed by the Paleozoic period, or that of the primary formation, in which, besides all kinds of rock, coal in particular was formed, and plants of the lowest kind and all classes of animals except birds and mammals are already encountered.
In the third, Mesozoic period, or that of the secondary formation, falls, among other things, the chalk formation, and all kinds of plants and animals, including the first oviparous animals and mammals, are found.
The following tertiary or Cenozoic formation extends from the chalk formation to the ice age and, besides plants and land and freshwater animals, brings forth especially the beasts of prey and many of the extinct mammals.
According to an occasional scholar, for example Burmeister, _Gesch. der Schöpfung_, 7th ed., 1872, p. 612, the human being already lived contemporaneously with these in the tertiary period; but according to most, the human being first appeared at the end of this era, after the ice age, in the quaternary period; Pfaff, _Schöpfungsgesch._, p. 485 f.; Ulrici, _Gott u. die Natur_, p. 353 f.; Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_, 184 f.; Zittel, _Aus der Urzeit_, 2d ed., 1875, p. 537.
This doctrine of the geological periods undoubtedly stands on much firmer ground than Kant’s hypothesis; it rests upon data supplied by the investigation of the earth’s strata.
Here the conflict between revelation and science therefore also bears a much more serious character.
On many points there is difference and contradiction: first in the time, and second in the order, in which the different creatures arose.
As regards the time, the difference is very great.
The chronology of the LXX differs considerably from that of the Hebrew text.
The church fathers frequently adhered to the Greek translation and then calculated the time from the creation of the world to the capture of Rome by the Goths at 5611 years; Eusebius; Augustine, _De civ._ XII 10.
In later times, especially since the Reformation, preference was generally given to the chronology of the Hebrew text, and it was then calculated that the creation of the world took place in 3950 B.C. (Scaliger), or 3984 (Kepler, Petavius), 3943 (Bengel), 4004 (Ussher); the Jews now count the year 5658; cf. Zöckler, _Die Lehre vom Urstand des Menschen_, 1879, p. 289 f.; Schanz, _Das Alter des Menschengeschlechts_, Freiburg, 1896, p. 1 f.
But still more precise calculation was attempted.
There was serious controversy over whether creation had taken place in the spring or in the autumn; the former was the opinion of Cyril, Basil, Bede, Cajetanus, Molina, Lapide, Luther, Melanchthon, Gerhard, Alsted, Polanus, G. J. Vossius, and so forth; the latter was defended by Petavius, Calvisius, Calovius, Danaeus, Zanchius, Voetius, Maresius, Heidegger, Turretin, and others.
Sometimes the date was determined still more precisely, as March 25 or October 26; Voetius, _Disp._ I 587; Hagenbach, _D. G._, 630 note.
Over against these, the geologists and natural scientists of the present time place their calculations, which are built upon the rotation of the earth in connection with its flattening at the poles, the earth’s heat steadily diminishing toward the surface, the formation of the deltas of the Nile and Mississippi, the formation of the earth’s strata and of the different kinds of rock, especially coal, and so forth.
Fabulous numbers, as among some pagan peoples, are thus assumed for the age of the earth.
Cotta speaks of an unlimited span of time, Lyell of 560 million, Klein of 2000 million, Helmholtz of 80 million, and even Pfaff of at least 20 million years; Pfaff, _Schöpf._, 640–666; Id., “Das Alter der Erde,” in _Zeitfr. des chr. Volkslebens_ VII; Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, 5th ed., pp. 42–52; Haeckel, _Nat. Schöpf._, p. 340 f.
But in the second place, there is also a very great difference between the creation narrative in Genesis and the opinions of many scholars concerning the order in which created beings arose.
To mention only a few points: according to Scripture, light was indeed already created on the first day, but our solar system was formed only on the fourth day, after the earth had already been prepared on the second and third days and covered with luxuriant plant growth; according to the geologists, the order is precisely the reverse.
According to Genesis, the plant kingdom was indeed created on the third day, but animals were first created on the fifth day; geology, however, teaches that animals of lower kinds and fish already occur in the primary or Paleozoic period.
Genesis relates that all aquatic animals and all birds were created on the fifth day, and all land animals together with the human being on the sixth day, but according to geology some mammals already belong to the secondary or Mesozoic period.
Thus it appears that differences exist on many important points.
