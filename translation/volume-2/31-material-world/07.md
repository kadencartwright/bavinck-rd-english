## 7.

Naturally, various attempts at reconciliation have been made.
First, the ideal theory should be mentioned, so called because it adheres only to the idea and not to the letter of the creation narrative.
It sees in Gen. 1 not a historical narrative but a poetic description of God’s creative act.
The six days are not chronologically ordered periods of longer or shorter duration but only different viewpoints from which the one created world is considered in turn, in order thus to give the limited vision of the human being a better overview of the whole.
It is therefore left entirely to paleontology to determine the time, manner, and order of the origin of the different periods.
It can be said that this theory was prepared by the allegorical exegesis that from ancient times was customary in the Christian church with respect to Gen. 1.
Following Philo and appealing to Sir. 18:1, _Deus omnia **simul** creavit_, Origen, Augustine, and many others taught that God had created all things at once and simultaneously.
The six days are not actually successive periods but indicate only the causal connection, the logical order, of the creatures and describe how the angels successively gained knowledge of the whole of creation.
Even among those who maintained the literal sense of the creation narrative, allegory nevertheless continued to play a large role; the chaos, the light, the term _one day_ instead of _the first day_, the absence of divine approval at the end of the second day, paradise, the creation of Eve, and so forth, gave occasion for ingenious spiritualizations.
Such allegorizing, mythologizing, and rationalizing explanations of the creation narrative came into favor especially after the awakening of natural science and were applied by Hobbes, Spinoza, Beverland, Burnet, Bekker, Tindal, Edelmann, J. L. Schmidt, Reimarus, and others.
Herder, _Aelteste Urkunde des Menschengeschlechts_, saw in Gen. 1 a beautiful poem of earliest humanity that, taking its point of departure from the dawning day, celebrated the seven-day week.
Modern philosophy and theology have advanced still farther, have rejected even the concept of creation along with the creation narrative, and see in Gen. 1 a myth that contains at most a religious kernel.
Christian theologians have not gone this far but, often in order to reconcile religion and science, have nevertheless returned to Augustine’s allegorical conception and surrendered the literal and historical interpretation of Genesis I.
To these belong very many Roman Catholic theologians: Michelis, _Entw. der beiden ersten Kapitel der Genesis_, 1845, and various articles in his journal _Natur u. Offenbarung_, 1855 f.; Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_, p. 251 f.; Schanz, _Apol. des Christ._ I 293 f.; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 105 f.; H. Lüken, _Die Stiftungsurkunde des Menschengeschl._, 1876; Güttler, _Naturforschung u. Bibel_, 1877; Hummelauer, B. Schäfer, and others; cf. Hettinger, _Apol._ III⁷, p. 206; etc.
But they also include numerous Protestant theologians, such as Zollmann, _Bibel u. Natur in der Harmonie ihrer Offenbarungen_, 1869, p. 52 f.; Dillmann, _Comm._ on Genesis; Riehm, _Christ. und Naturwiss._, Leipzig: Hinrich, 1896; Steudel, _Christ. u. Naturw._, Gütersloh, 1895; Vuilleumier, “La première page de la Bible,” _Revue de théol. et de philos._, Juillet 1896, pp. 362–377; Sept., pp. 393–418.

A second attempt at reconciliation is the restitution theory.
It seeks agreement between revelation and science by separating Gen. 1:2 and 3, attributing a long duration of existence to the chaos, and placing within it all those phenomena that geology supplies.
The _hexaemeron_, which begins with verse 3, relates only the restoration and preparation of the earth for the human being.
Such a fairly long interval between Gen. 1:1 and 2 was already accepted by Episcopius, _Instit. theol._ IV, sect. 3, c. 3; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II, c. 19–21; and others, in order thus to make room for the fall of the angels.
In the previous century it was adopted by the rationalists J. G. Rosenmüller, J. D. Michaelis, and Reinhard; especially in this century it found acceptance among the theosophists Oetinger, Hahn, St. Martin, Baader, Schelling, Fr. von Meyer, Steffens, Schubert, and Keerl, who placed within it the fall of the angels and the devastation of creation caused thereby; and it was also taught by Chalmers; Cardinal Wiseman, _Zusammenhang zw. Wiss. u. Offenb._, deutsch von Haneberg, 3te Aufl., 1866, S. 263 f.; Vigouroux, _Les livres saints_ III 442; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 474; etc.

A third theory, the concordist theory, seeks to attain harmony between Scripture and science by understanding the creation days as periods of longer duration.
The exegesis of the six days already presented difficulty at an early date.
Sun, moon, and stars were first created on the fourth day; the three preceding days therefore had in any case to be of a different nature than the final three.
Basil explained this by saying that God produced the first three days by an emission and contraction of the light created on the first day, Strauss I 621.
But this explanation did not satisfy everyone, for example not Augustine, who sometimes departs from his own _Simultan-theorie_, _De Gen. ad lit._ I 16.
Moreover, there was still a difference over whether the work of creation on each day was completed in one moment or instead came about successively only in the course of each day.
Descartes had said, namely, that the _res pure naturales_ could have proceeded from the chaos without any creative act of God.
The idea of development was thereby suggested.
Some Cartesian theologians, such as Wittichius, Allinga, and Braun, therefore taught that each work of creation occupied a day; cf. M. Vitringa II 95; Moor II 212.
And Whiston had already said that the days had to be understood as years, and he was followed in this by others.
But the father of the concordist theory was Abbot Jerusalem.
It was adopted by natural scientists such as de Luc, Cuvier, Hugh Miller, Pfaff, and others; by theologians such as Lange, Delitzsch, Rougemont, Godet, Ebrard, _Der Glaube an die H. Schrift und die Ergebnisse der Naturforschung_, 1861; Luthardt, _Apol. Vorträge_, 8 Aufl., 1878; Zöckler, among other places in Herzog² 13, 647; Brandt, in _Bew. d. Gl._, 1867, p. 339 f.; Hengstenberg, cf. _Bew. d. Gl._, 1867, p. 400 f.; and also by Roman Catholics such as Heinrich, Palmieri, Simar, _Dogm._ 249; Pesch, _Prael._ III 40; etc.
Many have frequently combined this theory with the restitution theory and then contented themselves with agreement in the main points.
Hugh Miller, for example, caused the azoic period to coincide with Gen. 1:3, the Paleozoic with Gen. 1:6–13, the Mesozoic with Gen. 1:14–23, and the Cenozoic with Gen. 1:24, Zöckler, _Gesch._ II 544.

Finally, the fourth theory, which has been called the antigeological theory, maintains the literal and historical interpretation of Genesis I and seeks to place the results of geology partly within the six creation days and partly afterward in the time from Adam to Noah, especially also in the flood.
From ancient times the flood was already regarded as highly significant.
People reasoned about whether it was total or partial, which has always remained under discussion, about the construction of the ark, about the height of the flood, and so forth, Zöckler II 122 f.
But the flood first acquired geological significance after Newton; in 1682 Thomas Burnet published his _Theoria sacra telluris_ and assumed in it a very great difference between the time before and after the flood; with him the latter becomes the demise of an old world and the birth of an entirely new one.
It was a tremendous catastrophe that changed the whole surface of the earth, brought oceans and mountains into existence, put an end to the mild spring climate, luxuriant fertility, and extraordinary longevity of human beings before that time, and above all caused the earth’s axis, which previously stood parallel to the axis of the sun, to stand obliquely to the earth’s orbit.
This entirely new theory was vigorously contested, among others by Spanheim and Leydecker, but was nevertheless also developed further by Whiston, Clüver, and many others, Zöckler II 143–192.
Toward the end of the previous century this diluvialist theory was increasingly abandoned, but it nevertheless remained in honor among many orthodox Roman Catholic and Protestant theologians; cf. Keil on Genesis, and further others in Zöckler II 470–482, 788 f.
The proponents of this attempt at reconciliation continue to identify the biblical flood with the _diluvium_ or ice age of geology and judge in connection with this that the flood was universal and extended over the whole earth.
Most geologists and theologians, such as Sedgwick, Greenough, Buckland, Hitchcock, Hugh Miller, Barry, Dawson, Diestel, Dillmann, Pfaff, Kurtz, Michelis, Reusch, Güttler, and also Kuyper, _Heraut_ 929, 930, 962, have recently come to the opinion that the biblical flood was entirely different from the _diluvium_ of geology and therefore must also be understood as partial; it can be called universal only insofar as the entire human race perished through it, although this last point too is still denied by some, such as Cuvier.
But the debate over this important and difficult question is far from decided in favor of this latter opinion; the most recent study of the flood, by Martin Gander, is a forceful plea for the diluvialist conception.
