## 2.

This divine origin of the human being has never been disputed in the Christian church and theology. Outside revelation, however, all kinds of conjectures have been ventured about the origin of humanity. Many pagan legends still ascribe the creation of the human being to gods or demigods, as, for example, in Hesiod, _Op. et Dier._, book I 23–25; Ovid, _Metam._ I 82 sq., 363 sq. Philosophy too, especially that of Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, usually recognized in the human being formed from the dust of the earth a higher, rational principle originating from the gods. But in both religion and philosophy entirely different ideas about the origin of the human being have repeatedly been entertained. At one time the human being emerges autochthonously from the earth; at another he has developed from one animal or another; or again he is regarded as the fruit of some tree, and so forth; cf. Andrew Lang, _Onderzoek naar de ontwikkeling van godsdienst, kultus en mythologie_, trans. from the English by Dr. L. Knappert, I, 1893, pp. 143, 275. With the materialism of the previous century in France, this view of the origin of the human being came into favor again; the human being, including the psychical side, was explained wholly and entirely from matter. Since that time the idea of evolution gained more and more ground. Linnaeus, 1778, and Blumenbach, 1840, still maintained the specific difference between human being and animal. But Kant already regarded the human being’s transition from an earlier animal to a later human condition as something self-evident, _Kritik der Urtheilskraft_ § 77–80; _Ueber die verschiedenen Racen der Menschen_, 1775. Lamarck, 1744–1829; Saint-Hilaire, 1772–1844; Oken, 1779–1851; von Baer, 1836; H. Spencer, 1852; Schaaffhausen, 1853; Huxley, 1859; Nägeli, 1859—all were already advocates of the human being’s descent from animals. Philosophy taught it long before natural science. Charles Darwin, 1809–1882, was therefore far from the first to advance the doctrine connected with his name. His distinction consisted only in making a very large number of observations that concerned the life of human beings and animals and brought their kinship to light; in knowing how to combine these observations in a distinctive way and place them in the service of the already prevailing hypothesis; and in indicating a path by which, as it seemed, this descent of the human being from the animal was made possible: _The Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection_, 1859, Dutch trans. _Het ontstaan der soorten_, by Dr. T. C. Winkler, 2nd ed., 1883; _The Descent of Man_, 1871, Dutch trans. _De afstamming van den mensch en de seksueele teeltkeus_, ed. by Dr. H. Hartogh Heys van Zouteveen, 3rd ed., 1884. A whole host of scholars—Lyell, Owen, Lubbock, Tylor, Hooker, Tyndall, Huxley, Moleschott, Haeckel, Hellwald, Büchner, Vogt, and others—thereby regarded the earlier hypothesis as virtually proven and presented it as an unshakable result of natural science. By Darwinism is now to be understood the doctrine that the species into which organic beings were formerly divided do not bear constant properties but are mutable; that the higher organic beings arose from the lower and, in particular, that the human being gradually developed in the course of the ages from an extinct ape lineage; that the organic in turn arose from the inorganic; and that evolution is thus the path along which, under the dominion of purely mechanical and chemical laws, everything that exists has come into being. That is the thesis—or rather, the hypothesis. Darwin attempts to explain this evolution and render it possible through the following considerations: first, nature everywhere acquaints us with a _struggle for life_, in which every being participates and by which it is compelled to develop and perfect itself or else perish; second, out of numerous plants, animals, and human beings, nature selects for continued life and propagation those that are most favorably organized (_natural selection_), and this natural selection is supported by sexual selection, through which each female prefers the best-organized male; third, the favorable traits thus acquired through struggle and selection pass from parents to children or even to grandchildren (atavism) and increasingly perfect the organisms. Naturally these are not proofs but only suppositions and explanations of how, in Darwin’s opinion, evolution might be possible. Evidence for the hypothesis is actually derived only from the affinity observable among organic beings and existing also in physical and psychical respects between animal and human being; from the alteration and inheritance of traits that we repeatedly observe in the human and animal worlds; from the rudimentary organs that remain to the human being from his earlier animal condition; and from embryology, according to which the higher organisms as embryos pass through the developmental stages of the lower, Romanes, _De bewijzen voor de theorie van Darwin_, trans. from the English by Spaink, Amsterdam, 1884.
