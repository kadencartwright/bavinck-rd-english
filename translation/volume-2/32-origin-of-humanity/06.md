## 6.

Finally, there is also disagreement about the human being’s original dwelling place. Genesis relates that after God had created Adam, he planted a garden in Eden. Eden, עֵדֶן, joy, land of joy, is therefore not the same as paradise but is a region in which the garden, גַן, LXX παραδεισος—according to Spiegel, from the Zend _pairidaêze_, enclosure—was planted. This paradise is then further called the garden of Eden, Gen. 2:15; 3:23; the garden of God, Ezek. 31:8, 9; the garden of YHWH, Isa. 51:3; and is sometimes identified with Eden, Isa. 51:3; Ezek. 28:13; 31:9. Further, God planted that garden in Eden מִקֶּדֶם, from the east, eastward, toward the east, namely, from the writer’s standpoint. A river flowed from the region of Eden to water the garden; and from there—that is, from the garden, when it flowed out of the garden—it divided into four heads or branches bearing the names Pishon, Gihon, Hiddekel, and Phrath. The last two rivers are the Tigris and Euphrates, but there has always been disagreement about the first two. Like Josephus, the church fathers usually understood the Pishon to be the Ganges and the Gihon the Nile. But they did not undertake a precise investigation of the location of paradise. For them the earthly paradise often blended with the heavenly and was then interpreted allegorically. Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ VIII, 1, says that there were three opinions about paradise. Some understood it as an earthly paradise, others as a heavenly paradise, and still others combined both opinions. Those who regarded it as an earthly paradise thought that it had been situated very high, midway between heaven and earth; that it even reached to the moon; that the whole earth had once been paradise; or that it had lain on the far side of the ocean. According to some, paradise was completely destroyed after the fall, especially by the flood; according to others, it still existed but had become inaccessible through mountains and seas; and still others thought that it had been taken up into heaven. The first to attempt to identify the location of paradise geographically was Augustine Steuchus of Gubbio—hence Eugubinus—† 1550. In his work _Kosmopoiia_, published in Lyon in 1535, he developed the so-called Pasitigris hypothesis, according to which the four rivers are the mouths of one great stream, the united Tigris-Euphrates, and paradise had thus been located near the present city of Korna. This hypothesis found much favor among Roman Catholics such as Pererius, Jansen, Lapide, Petavius, Mersenna; among Reformed theologians such as Calvin on Gen. 2:10 and Marck, _Histor. Paradisi_, 1705, book I; among Lutherans and others; and was adopted in modified form by Pressel, article “Paradies” in Herzog¹. Around the middle of the 17th century there arose alongside it the so-called Armenia hypothesis, which had already been prepared by Rupert of Deutz, Pellicanus, and Fournier and was then developed especially by Reland, professor at Utrecht, † 1706. This view identifies the Pishon as the Phasis, the Gihon as the Araxes, Havilah as Colchis, and Cush as the land of the Κοσσαιοι between Media and Susiana, and therefore sought paradise much farther north, namely high in Armenia, approximately between Erzerum and Tiflis. It gained even more currency than the Pasitigris hypothesis and was defended in the present day by von Raumer, Kurtz, Baumgarten, Keil, Lange, Delitzsch, Rougemont, and others. Friedrich Delitzsch, by contrast, in his work _Wo lag das Paradies_, Leipzig, 1881, again sought the location of paradise farther south, namely in the region near Babylon that on account of its beauty was called the “garden of the god Dunias” by the Babylonians and Assyrians. The river from Eden was thus the Euphrates in its upper course, while Pishon and Gihon were two canal-rivers. Others, however, have gone much further and see in the paradise narrative a saga that gradually migrated from east to west and in which Pishon and Gihon originally designate the Indus and Oxus, J. D. Michaelis, Knobel, Bunsen, Ewald, and others. Others see in it a myth in which Havilah represents the legendary land of gold and the Gihon is the Ganges or the Nile, Paulus, Eichhorn, Gesenius, Tuch, Bertheau, Schrader, and others. Most anthropologists and linguists no longer reckon with Gen. 2 at all and name entirely different countries as the human being’s original dwelling place. But they are far from unanimous and have bestowed this honor upon virtually every country. Romanes, Klaproth, de Gobineau, and George Browne named America; Spiller thought of Greenland because the polar regions were the first to become habitable after the cooling of the earth; Wagner regarded Europe as the land where the ape first developed into a human being. Unger specifically named Styria; L. Geiger, Germany; Cuno and Spiegel, southern Russia; Poesche, the region between the Dnieper and Niemen; Benfey and Whitney, central Europe; Warren, the North Pole. Others, such as Darwin, Huxley, Peschel, and others, preferred Africa because the gorilla and chimpanzee, the human being’s nearest relatives, were found there. And Link, Haeckel, Hellwald, and Schmidt invented a certain land called Lemuria, where apes first became human beings and which had lain between Africa and Australia but happened to disappear into the depths of the sea at the end of the Tertiary period. Many also assume not merely one original dwelling place for humanity but believe that evolution from animal to human being took place in various parts of the earth and thus combine Darwinism with polygenism, as, for example, Haeckel, Vogt, Schaaffhausen, Caspari, Fr. Müller, and others. This great disagreement among anthropologists already demonstrates that science has thus far been able to establish nothing here with any certainty. It loses itself in conjectures but knows nothing about the origin and dwelling place of the first human being. There is accordingly not a single fact that compels us to abandon Holy Scripture’s identification of Eden. Rather, ethnology, linguistics, history, and natural science furnish us with data that render Asia probable as the human being’s original dwelling place. Neither Africa nor Europe nor America, and much less a land such as Lemuria, can make as strong a claim to this as Asia. Here we find the oldest peoples, the oldest civilization, the oldest languages; all of ancient history points us toward this continent. From this part of the earth Europe and Africa, Australia and also America were populated. Certainly, many questions arise here to which no answer can yet be given. It is especially uncertain how and when America was populated, Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._ I 542 f.; Peschel, _Völkerkunde_ 402 f.; Vigouroux, _Les livres saints_ IV 98 s.; Dr. E. Schmidt, _Die aeltesten Spuren des Menschen in N. Amerika_, nos. 38 and 39 of _Deutsche Zeit- und Streitfragen_, _Wetenschappelijke Bladen_, 1895. But these difficulties in no way overthrow the teaching of Scripture that Asia is the cradle of humanity. There may be disagreement about the location of paradise and Eden, so that it has been placed by turns in the center, east, and south of Asia; it may even be impossible any longer to establish its geography. Scripture and science both testify that the human being’s original dwelling place is to be sought in Asia, Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, pp. 35–41; Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._, passim, especially I 128 f., 170 f., 395 f., 654 f.; II 779 f.; idem, _Lehre vom Urstand_ 216 f.; Herzog, article “Paradies” by Pressel; Herzog², article “Eden” by Rütschi; Reusch, _Bibel und Natur_, 498 f.; Zöckler, _Bibl. u. Kirchenhist. Studien_, Munich, 1893, V 1–38; Delitzsch, _Neuer Comm. zu Genesis_, p. 81 f.
