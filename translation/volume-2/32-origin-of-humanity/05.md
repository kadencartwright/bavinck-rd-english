## 5.

The unity of the human race is firmly established for Holy Scripture, Gen. 1:26; 6:3; 7:21; 10:32; Matt. 19:4; Acts 17:26; Rom. 5:12f.; 1 Cor. 15:21f., 45f., but it was almost never acknowledged by the peoples who lived outside revelation.
The Greeks regarded themselves as autochthons and looked down upon the barbarians from on high.
And this contrast is found among almost all peoples.
In India, a sharp separation was even gradually made between the four classes of the people, and a distinct origin was assumed for each.
Only the Stoa declared that all human beings formed one συστημα πολιτικον, one body of which each was a member, and therefore also preached universal justice and love of humanity; Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ IV 287 f.
After the Renaissance, the idea of a different origin of the human race arose again here and there.
This idea appeared at one time in the form of polygenism proper in Caesalpinus, Blount, and other deists; in part as co-Adamism, that is, the descent of the different races from different progenitors, in Paracelsus and others; and in part as pre-Adamism, that is, the descent of wild and dark-skinned peoples from a progenitor before Adam, while Adam alone was then the progenitor of the Jews or also of white humanity, in Zanini and especially Isaac de la Peyrère.
In 1655 the latter published, without the name of author, printer, or place, a small work entitled _Praeadamitae_, followed by a _Systema theologiae ex praeadamitarum hypothesi_.
In it he asserted that human beings had existed long before Adam, appealing to Gen. 4:14, 16, 17; 6:2–4.
These human beings descended from that first pair whose creation is related in Gen. 1.
In Gen. 2, however, the creation is related of Adam and Eve, who were the progenitors of the Jews.
They transgressed the law given to them in paradise and fell into still greater sins than the peoples descended from the first human being, for the latter did not sin, as Paul expresses it in Rom. 5:12–14, in the likeness of Adam’s sin; they transgressed no positive law; they committed _peccata naturalia_ but no _peccata contra legem_.
For a time this theory enjoyed great popularity and called forth opponents on every side; Spanheim, _Op._ III 1249 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ V qu. 8; Marck, _Historia Paradisi_ II 2 § 3 sq.; Moor, _Comm._ II 1001–5; M. Vitringa II 127; cf. _Studien en Bijdragen_ of De Moll and Scheffer IV 238 f.; Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._ I 545 f.; II 768 f.; _Urstand_, 231 f.
But it nevertheless soon fell into oblivion.
Only a few, such as Bayle, Arnold, and Swedenborg, considered it not entirely objectionable.
Especially when knowledge of the peoples expanded in the previous century and the great differences in color, hair, stature, custom, and so forth among the peoples were recognized, many again arrived at the idea of different progenitors: Sullivan, 1795; Crüger, 1784; Ballenstedt, 1818; Stanhope Smith, 1790; Cordonnière, 1814; Gobineau, 1853–55; and others.
This was made serviceable by some to the defense of slavery, as by Dobbs in Ireland against Wilberforce, by Morton, Nott, Gliddon, Knox, Agassiz, and others.
Another kind of polygenism was taught by Schelling, _Werke_ II 1, pp. 500–515.
He too assumed many races of human beings before Adam, but they had so raised and developed themselves from a low, animal level that they finally produced _him_, in whom the human first came to revelation and who could therefore rightly bear the name “the human,” _haadam_.
And likewise a certain pre-Adamism was taught by Oken, Carus, Baumgärtner, Perty, Bunsen; cf. also Bilderdijk, _Opstellen v. godg. en zedek. inh._ II 75; Strauss, _Dogm._ I 680, and so forth.
To this was now added after 1860 Darwinism, which because of its doctrine of variability could very well be monogenistic but nevertheless became polygenistic among many of its adherents.
The development from animal to human being took place at different times and places and gave rise to different races; Haeckel, Schaaffhausen, Caspari, Vogt, Büchner, and so forth.
From the Darwinian standpoint, however, the question of the antiquity of the human being cannot be answered.
The transition from animal to human being took place so slowly that there was in fact no first human being.
Over against this polygenism, monogenism was still defended by von Humboldt, Blumenbach, St. Hilaire, von Baer, von Meyer, Wagner, Quatrefages, Darwin, Peschel, Ranke; Virchow too regards it as not impossible, Hettinger, _Apol._ III 224.

Now the existence of peoples and races within humanity is certainly an important problem whose solution has by no means yet been found.
The differences in color, hair, skull, language, conceptions, religion, morals, customs, and so forth are so great, and the spread of the one humanity over the entire earth—for example, to the South Sea islands, to America, and so forth—is so unknown that the thought of a different origin of the peoples can hardly occasion surprise.
Scripture therefore also derives the origin of languages and peoples from an act of God by which he intervened in the development of humanity, Gen. 11; cf. Schelling, _Werke_ II 1, pp. 94–118; Lüken, _Die Tradit. des Mensch._, 278 f.; Auberlen, _De goddel. openbaring_ I; Kaulen, _Die Sprachenverwirrung zu Babel_, Mainz, 1861; Strodl, _Die Entstehung der Völker_, Schaffhausen, 1868.
The origin of the peoples has a profound religious-ethical significance and testifies to spiritual decline.
The wilder and coarser humanity becomes, the more languages, conceptions, and so forth diverge.
The more people live in isolation, the more linguistic difference increases.
Confusion of speech is the consequence of confusion in thought, consciousness, and life.
And yet unity is still preserved in that division and rupture.
Linguistics has discovered kinship and unity of origin where formerly these were not remotely suspected.
The existence of races and peoples is a fact, and yet the determination of their boundaries is so difficult that the greatest disagreement exists about it.
Kant assumed 4 different races, Blumenbach 5, Buffon 6, Peschel 7, Agassiz 8, Haeckel 12, and Morton as many as 22; Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, 316 f.
Among all races there are again transitions that seem to mock every classification.
Genesis 10 therefore maintains the unity of humanity amid all diversity, and Joh. von Müller said, not without reason: _von diesem Kapitel muss alle Historie anfangen_.
Darwinism can in fact raise no objection against this unity.
The difference between human being and animal is, after all, always much greater than that among human beings themselves.
If the human being could develop from an animal, it is impossible to see why the common origin of humanity would in itself encounter an objection.
Darwinism in fact provides the means to make all kinds of changes within the same species under all kinds of influences of climate, manner of life, and so forth, possible and intelligible.
To that extent it renders excellent service to the defense of the truth.
For however great the differences among the races may be, deeper investigation brings the unity and kinship of all human beings all the more strongly to the fore.
This appears in the fact that parents from different races can mate and produce fertile children; that every class of human beings can inhabit and live in every zone of the earth; that peoples who have never come into contact with one another nevertheless have various traits and customs in common, such as gestures, the decimal system, skin painting, tattooing, circumcision, _couvade_, and so forth; that numerous physiological phenomena are the same among all races, such as upright posture, the shape of the skull, the average weight of the brain, the number and length of the teeth, the duration of pregnancy, the average pulse rate, the internal structure of the organism, of hand, foot, and so forth, the average lifespan, body temperature, the periodicity of the catamenia, susceptibility to diseases, and so forth; and finally, that in intellectual, religious, moral, social, and political respects they have all kinds of things in common—language, understanding, reason, memory, knowledge of God, conscience, consciousness of sin, repentance, sacrifices, fasting, prayer, traditions about a golden age, about the flood, and so forth.
The unity of the human race taught by Scripture is most strongly confirmed by all this.
In the end it is not a matter of indifference, as has sometimes been asserted.
On the contrary, it is of the highest importance; it is the presupposition of religion and morality.
The solidarity of the human race, original sin, reconciliation in Christ, the universality of the kingdom of God, the catholicity of the church, and love of neighbor are built upon it.
