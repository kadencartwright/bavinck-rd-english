## 3.

However much authority Darwinism immediately claimed, it nevertheless encountered serious opposition from the beginning, not only among theologians and philosophers but also among all kinds of natural scientists, such as Agassiz, _Essay on the Classification_, 1865; Dawson, _Die Natur und die Bibel_, aus dem Engl., Gütersloh, 1877; Nägeli, _Entstehung u. Begriff der naturhist. Art_, 2nd ed., 1865; _Mech. phys. Theorie der Abstammungslehre_, 1883; Wigand, _Der Darwinismus u. s. w._, 3 vols., 1874–77; and further Dana, Flourens, de Beaumont, Jordan, Blanchard, Quatrefages, Oswald Heer, Oscar Fraas, Ad. Bastian, R. Wagner, Pfaff, Kölliker, and others.
Almost every year at the assembly of natural scientists Virchow repeats his protest against those who present Darwinism as a dogma.
Du Bois-Reymond spoke of _sieben Welträthsel_ that could not be solved by natural science and wrote not long before his death that perhaps we would have to return to supernaturalism.
The vital force, formerly rejected with mockery, is once again defended by Gustave Bunge; in Max Verworn, Wilhelm Ostwald, and Ed. Rindfleisch a tendency toward pantheism and mysticism can be discerned; Haeckel himself commended monism as the unity of religion and science.
Romanes, who was a decided Darwinist, died in 1895 reconciled with the faith of the Anglican Church.
Undoubtedly a reaction against materialism and Darwinism is under way.
To this must be added the criticism to which Darwinism has been subjected by theologians and philosophers.
They too have the right to speak in this question and have raised weighty objections; cf. Ulrici, _Gott u. die Natur_, 1866; _Gott u. der Mensch_ I, 1874; Zöckler, _Gesch. der Bez._ II 579 f.; idem, _Die Lehre v. Urstand des Menschen_, 1879, S. 113 f.; Hartmann, _Wahrheit u. Irrthum im Darw._, 1875; Carneri, _Sittlichkeit u. Darw._, Vienna, 1877; Weygoldt, _Darw. Relig. Sittl._, Leiden, 1878; Hamann, _Entwicklungslehre u. Darwinismus_, Jena, 1892; Dutoit-Haller, _Schöpfung u. Entw. nach Bibel u. Naturw._, Basel, 1892; Joh. Ranke, _Der Mensch_, Leipzig, 2nd ed., 1894; Ebrard, _Apol._ I 352 f.; Steudel, _Christ. u. Naturwiss._, 1895, S. 148 f.; Bettex, _Naturstudium u. Christ._, 2nd ed., 1896; Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_ II 147, 171 f.; Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_, 330 f.; Gutberlet, _Der Mensch, sein Ursprung u. seine Entw._, Paderborn, 1896; Hettinger, _Apol._ I 167 f.; Kuenen, _Theol. Tijdschr._ X 408 f.; Lamers, _Nieuwe Bijdr._ III 185 f.; Serrurier, _Gids_, May 1895; _Tijdspiegel_, April 1896, p. 455; _Wet. Bladen_, May 1896; Salisbury, in his opening address to _The British Association of Science_ in Oxford, August 1894; etc.

In view of this change of direction, theology has often been far too hasty and has accommodated itself too quickly to the doctrine of evolution, for example, Powell, _Evidences of Christianity_, 1860; Temple, _Christ. en Natuurwetenschap_, trans. by Dr. J. W. Gunning, Haarlem, 1887; Drummond, _De opkomst van den mensch_, 1895; Carrière, _De zedel. wereldorde_, p. 365 f.; etc.
There are, after all, objections to Darwinism that are far too serious and weighty for it to be accepted as a scientific explanation of the phenomena.
First, it is entirely powerless to render the origin of life even somewhat intelligible.
At first recourse was taken to _generatio aequivoca_, that is, to the origin of organic beings through the accidental combination of inorganic substances.
When Pasteur’s investigations proved this untenable, the supposition was adopted that protoplasms or germs of life had been brought to earth by meteorites from other celestial bodies (Helmholtz, Thomson).
When this hypothesis too proved to be little more than a passing thought, the doctrine was proclaimed that cells and germs of life had always existed alongside the inorganic and thus, like matter, force, motion, and so forth, were eternal.
But thereby the inadequacy of the theory of evolution was acknowledged by its advocates themselves; whoever makes matter, motion, and life eternal does not solve the riddle but despairs of its solution.
In the second place, Darwinism is also unable to explain the further development of organic beings.
On the one hand, Scripture acknowledges the truth contained in evolution when it causes plants and animals to come forth from the earth at God’s command, Gen. 1:11, 20, 24; but on the other hand, it says that the earth could bring forth these organic beings only through a word of God’s omnipotence and that those organic beings existed alongside one another from the beginning as specifically distinct; they all have their own nature, Gen. 1, v. 11, 21.
This does not exclude the possibility that all kinds of changes can take place within the species, nor does it curtail the right of science to define the boundaries of species more precisely.
Indeed, it is by no means necessary to see original creations in all the species now enumerated by botany and zoology; the concept of species is still far from sharply and clearly defined.
But it is equally certain that the essential diversity and inequality of creatures rests upon God’s creative omnipotence.
It is he who makes a distinction between light and darkness, day and night, heaven and earth, plant and animal, angel and human being, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 47.
And this diversity and inequality of creatures, particularly of organic beings, remains a riddle in Darwinism.
If the human being had descended from the animal, the very great difference that exists between them and appears throughout the entire organism would remain an insoluble riddle.
It is now generally acknowledged that the many species of plants and animals cannot be derived from one or even from four or five original organisms; these species diverge far too greatly morphologically and physiologically.
_Natural selection_ and _sexual selection_ are inadequate to make such changes of species possible, and they were already significantly restricted and modified by Darwin himself.
Moreover, such transitions from one species to another have never been observed, either in the past or in the present.
The same species of plants and animals that we now know also existed thousands of years ago and appeared all at once in great numbers.
The transitional forms that would bring the species now existing closer to one another have been found nowhere.
Paleontology proves no slow and linear ascent of organic beings from the lower to the higher but shows that all kinds of species existed alongside one another from the beginning.
Such transitional forms, however, would have to be found in great numbers because the morphological changes took place so slowly, only over thousands of years, and were each time of such slight significance.
It is inconceivable that all of these would accidentally have perished through catastrophes, all the less because all the lower organisms, despite their imperfection and unfitness for the struggle for life, have continued to exist alongside the higher to the present day.
To this must be added that from various sides, especially by August Weismann, the thesis has been defended on good grounds that acquired traits are precisely not inherited, Koster, _Wet. Bladen_, March 1897, pp. 321–344.
In complete contradiction to Darwin’s theory, morphological traits are the least variable and physiological traits the most variable.
If those morphological changes also proceeded so slowly and were each time of such slight significance, they could be of no benefit in the _struggle for life_; during the time of transition they were a defect rather than a perfection.
As long as breathing through gills was passing over into breathing through lungs, it was a hindrance rather than an advantage in the struggle for existence.
For all these reasons, the natural scientist, whose science must rest upon facts, would do better to abstain from judgment in this matter.
Both historically and logically, materialism and Darwinism are not results of experimental science but of philosophy.
Indeed, Darwin himself says that many of the views he advanced are speculative in a high degree, _Afst. des menschen_ II 365.
According to Haeckel, he discovered no new facts but did combine and utilize them in a distinctive manner, _Nat. Schöpf._, 1874, S. 25.
The great affinity between human being and animal has always been acknowledged and expressed in _animal rationale_.
But in earlier times this was not yet combined with the monistic philosophy that a pure potency which is nothing—such as atoms, chaos, cells, and so forth—can nevertheless become everything.
That was first discovered by pantheism, materialism, and Darwinism!
In the third place, the origin of the human being remains an insoluble problem for Darwinism.
There is, properly speaking, no positive evidence for the animal descent of the human being.
Haeckel’s ontogeny can no longer count as evidence after the refutation by Bischoff and others.
The arguments derived from all kinds of human bones and human skulls found in caves, most recently in the Dutch East Indies, cf. Prof. Hubrecht in _Gids_, June 1896, have one after another been abandoned in turn.
The investigation, on the one hand, of the anthropoid ape species and, on the other, of all kinds of bones, skulls, abnormal human beings, microcephalics, dwarfs, and so forth, has ended by establishing that the distinction between animal and human being is essential and has always existed, Pfaff, _Schöpf._ 721.
It is therefore also universally acknowledged that no ape species, whether now existing or formerly existing, can be the stock from which the human race descended.
The strongest defenders of Darwinism admit that a transitional species must be assumed, of which, however, not the slightest trace has been found thus far.
Virchow said at the assembly of natural scientists in 1894: _Bis jetzt is noch kein Affe entdeckt worden, der als der eigentliche Urvater des Menschen betrachtet werden konnte, auch kein Halbaffe. Diese Frage steht nicht mehr im Vordergrund der Forschung_, in Hettinger, _Apol._ III 297.
In the fourth place, however, Darwinism above all still fails to explain the human being in his psychical aspect.
Darwin began with the attempt to derive all the spiritual phenomena in the human being—consciousness, language, religion, morality, and so forth—from phenomena that occurred in animals, _Afst. des menschen_, chs. 3 and 4; _The Expression of Emotions in Man and Animals_, 1872, and many others followed him in this.
But these attempts too have so far not succeeded.
Just like the nature of force and matter, the origin of motion, the emergence of life, and teleology, consciousness, language, freedom of the will, religion, and morality still belong to the _Welträthsel_ that await solution.
Thought, which is purely spiritual, stands in an entirely different relation to the brain than bile does to the liver and urine to the kidneys.
Language is and remains, in Max Müller’s words, the Rubicon between us and the animal.
The psychological explanation of religion is untenable, Volume I, p. 208.
And the derivation of morality from the social instincts fails to do justice to the authority of the moral law, the categorical nature of the moral imperative, the “_Sollen_” of the good, conscience, responsibility, consciousness of guilt, remorse, pangs of conscience, and punishment.
Indeed, although Darwinism is not in itself identical with materialism, it nevertheless moves along that line and finds its principal support there, but thus also prepares the way for the corruption of religion and morality and kills what is human in the human being.
It makes no difference if one says that it is better to be a highly developed animal than a fallen human being; the doctrine of the animal descent of the human being attacks the image of God in the human being and degrades him to the image of the orangutan and chimpanzee.
And that image of God cannot be maintained from the standpoint of evolution.
It compels us to return to creation as Scripture teaches it to us.
