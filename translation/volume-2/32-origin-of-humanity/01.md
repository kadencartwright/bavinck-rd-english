# § 32. The Origin of the Human Being

## 1.

Creation culminates in the human being. In the human being the spiritual and material worlds are united. According to the first creation narrative, the human being—man and woman together—was created on the sixth day, Gen. 1:26 f., after the creation of the land animals had taken place. In this Scripture also teaches a close kinship between human being and animal. Both were created on the same day, and both were also formed from the dust of the earth. But along with that kinship there is also a great difference. At God’s command the animals are brought forth by the earth, Gen. 1:24; the human being, however, is created after a deliberation of God, in his image, as lord over all things. These brief indications are elucidated and expanded in the second creation narrative. The first creation narrative gives a general history of creation that finds its goal and end in the human being; the second deals particularly with the creation of the human being and the relation in which the other creatures stand to him. In the first account the human being is the end of nature; in the second, the beginning of history. The first narrative shows how all the other creatures prepare for the human being; the second introduces the history of the human being’s temptation and fall and for that purpose describes especially his original state. In the first account, therefore, the creation of all other things—heaven, earth, firmament, and so forth—is related at length and in regular order, while the creation of the human being is not discussed extensively. The second presupposes the creation of heaven and earth, gives not a chronological but a topical order, and does not say when plants and animals were created but describes only the relation in which, according to their nature, they stand to the human being. Gen. 2:4b–9 does not imply that the plants were formed after the human being, but only that the garden in Eden was; without doubt the writer conceives the creation of the plants as occurring between verses 6 and 7. Likewise, in Gen. 2:18 f. the creation of the animals is indeed placed only after that of the human being, not in order thereby to describe the objective course of creation but only to indicate that the helper for the man is to be found not among the animals but solely in a being like him. Finally, the description of the creation of the woman is in no respect at odds with that in Gen. 1 but is only a further explanation of it. Cf.
