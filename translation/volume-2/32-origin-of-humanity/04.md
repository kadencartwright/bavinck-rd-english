## 4.

In connection with this doctrine concerning the origin of the human being, evolution also comes into conflict with Scripture regarding humanity’s age, unity, and original dwelling place. A great age was ascribed to the human race by many peoples, such as the Japanese, Indians, Babylonians, Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans, who spoke of various world ages and of 10 and 100 thousands of years. Modern anthropology has repeatedly returned to these fabulous figures, but it is no more consistent than pagan mythology; it varies between 10 and 250 thousand years and more. There is great difference of opinion about the existence of human beings in the Tertiary period; it is denied by most scholars, including many who are otherwise Darwinists, such as Virchow, Mor, Wagner, Oskar Schmidt, Haeckel, Zittel, and others. The question is also so difficult to answer because the boundaries between the Tertiary and Quaternary periods cannot be identified. But even if the human being lived in the Tertiary period and was a contemporary of the mammoth, this would prove not that the human being is so old but that this period is much younger than was originally thought. Far more uncertain are the calculations based upon the pile dwellings found in Switzerland and elsewhere; the bones and skulls discovered in caves near Liège, Amiens, Düsseldorf, and elsewhere; the formation of the deltas of the Nile and Mississippi; and the Stone, Bronze, and Iron periods, which according to some followed one another chronologically. All the calculations based upon these have therefore repeatedly been contradicted and refuted by sober natural scientists such as Fraas, Quenstedt, Pfaff, Dawson, Tylor, Virchow, Schaaffhausen, Kjerulf, and even Hellwald, _Culturgesch._ I 10. Here, even more than with the age of the earth, it holds true that one can indeed name figures but has no material for a history extending over so long a time. Of far more value for determining the age of the human race are the chronological data supplied by the history and monuments of various peoples. The history of India and China supplies no fixed basis for chronology and reaches back only a few centuries before Christ. But the situation is somewhat different with the history of Egypt and Babylonia. Here there is undoubtedly an ancient civilization; it exists as far back as we can go in history. Scripture itself also teaches this clearly. Yet the chronology is still so uncertain that nothing can be built upon it. This is conclusively evident from the fact that the reign of the Egyptian king Menes begins, according to Boeckh, in 5702; according to Brugsch, in 4455; according to Lepsius, in 3893; according to Bunsen, in 3623; according to Wilkinson, in 2691; and so forth; and that Bunsen has the historical period for Babylon begin in 3784, von Gutschmid in 2447, Brandis in 2458, Oppert in 3540, and so forth, Hettinger, _Apol._ III 258 f. Every student of ancient history has his own chronology; it is a labyrinth for which the thread is lacking. Only among the people of Israel can one properly speak of a history and a chronology. Fritz Hommel therefore rightly says that the chronology for the first millennium before Christ is fairly well established, sometimes even down to details; that in the second millennium before Christ only a few fixed points seem to be given; and that in the third millennium, that is, before 2000 BC, everything is uncertain, _Gesch. des alten Morgenlandes_, Stuttgart, 1895, p. 38. Besides, for other reasons as well the human race cannot have existed for many thousands of years before Christ. The population of the earth would then have been much larger at the time of Christ and much more widely dispersed. And yet a thousand years before Christ the greater part of the earth was still uninhabited: northern Asia, central and northern Europe, Africa south of the Sahara, Australia, the South Sea islands, America. Even at the time of Christ, apart from Asia, humanity still lived principally around the Mediterranean Sea. If humanity were so old, many more ruins of cities and remains of human beings would also have been found, whereas these are now few in number and confined to one part of the earth. The most reliable figures therefore go back no further than five to seven thousand years before Christ, Pfaff, _Schöpf._ 710–728; Gander, _Die Sündflut_, 1896, pp. 78–90. In an article on _Die Zeitdauer der geolog. Epochen_ in the journal _Gaea_ XXX, 1894, p. 621 f., Upham cites various scholars who place the Ice Age approximately seven or eight thousand years before Christ. If we further bear in mind that there is anything but agreement about biblical chronology, then on this point too there is no important difference between Scripture and science. But even if, according to the customary reckoning, the flood took place in 2348 BC, there was, until the call of Abraham in 1900, a period of 450 years, which is sufficient for fairly powerful kingdoms to arise on the Euphrates and the Nile. Noah and his three sons, if each marriage is reckoned at six children, could in fourteen generations of 33 years—that is, in 462 years—have had more than twelve million descendants. Schanz, _Das Alter des Menschengeschlechts_, Freiburg, 1896; Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_ 518 f.; Hettinger, _Apol. der Christ._ I⁷ 281–310.
