## 8.

But when the theory of inspiration, as among the Jews and Mohammedans, had thus been drawn to its most extreme consequence, opposition arose from every side. Criticism of Scripture was not lacking in earlier times either. Jehoiakim burned Baruch’s scroll, Jer. 36. Apion gathered together all the accusations advanced by the pagans against the Jews concerning circumcision, the prohibition of pork, the exodus from Egypt, the sojourn in the wilderness, and so forth. Josephus, _Contra Apionem_; J. G. Müller, _Des Flavius Josephus’ Schrift gegen den Apion_, 1877.

The Gnostics, Manichaeans, and the sects related to them in the Middle Ages tore the New Testament loose from the Old and ascribed the latter to a lower god, the demiurge. Marcion especially in his _Antitheses_, and his disciples Apelles and Tatian, proceeding from the Pauline opposition between righteousness and grace, law and gospel, works and faith, flesh and spirit, directed their attack against the anthropomorphisms, contradictions, and immorality of the Old Testament. They said that a God who is angry, repents, takes vengeance, is jealous, commands theft and lying, descends, gives a strict law, and so forth, cannot be the true God. They also pointed with special fondness to the great difference between Christ, the true Messiah, and the Messiah as the prophets expected him. Of the New Testament Marcion rejected all writings except those of Luke and Paul, and even corrupted these by abridgment and interpolation. Tertullian, _Adv. Marcionem_; Epiphanius, _Haer._ 42; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ passim; Harnack, _Dogmengesch._ I 226 f.

Celsus continued this conflict in an acute manner and supplied a sharp critique of the opening chapters of Genesis, the days of creation, the creation of man, the temptation, the fall, the flood, the ark, the building of the tower of Babel, the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah; and further of Jonah, Daniel, the supernatural birth of Jesus, baptism, resurrection, and miracles. For lack of a better explanation, he accused Jesus and the apostles of deception.

Porphyry made a beginning with historical criticism of the biblical books. He opposed the allegorical exegesis of the Old Testament, ascribed the Pentateuch to Ezra, regarded Daniel as a product from the time of Antiochus, and also subjected many narratives in the Gospels to sharp criticism. Julian later renewed all these attacks against Scripture once again in his Λογοι κατα χριστιανων. But with this the criticism of that period reached its end. Scripture came to universal and undisputed dominion; criticism was forgotten.

It revived in the Renaissance but was then restrained for a time by the Reformation and the Roman Catholic Counter-Reformation. Soon it rose anew in Rationalism, Deism, and French philosophy. First, in the rational eighteenth century, it directed itself more against the content of Scripture; afterward, in the historically minded nineteenth century, more against the genuineness of the writings. Porphyry replaces Celsus, Renan follows Voltaire, Paulus of Heidelberg gives way to Strauss and Baur. But the result always remains the same: Scripture is a book full of error and falsehood.
