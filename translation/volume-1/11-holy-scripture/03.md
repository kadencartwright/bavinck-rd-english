## 3.

Revelation, taken as a whole, reaches its end and goal only in the parousia of Christ. But it falls into two great periods, two distinct dispensations (see above, p. 270). The first dispensation served to incorporate the full revelation of God into the history of humanity and make it a part of that history. This whole economy can be regarded as a coming of God to his people, as a seeking of a tabernacle for Christ. It is therefore predominantly a revelation of God in Christ. It bears an objective character. It is characterized by extraordinary acts; theophany, prophecy, and miracle are the ways along which God comes to his people. Christ is its subject. He is the Logos who shines in the darkness, comes to his own, and becomes flesh in Jesus. The Holy Spirit was not yet, because Christ had not yet been glorified. In this dispensation, the committal to writing keeps pace with revelation. Both grow from century to century. As revelation advances, Scripture increases in scope. When the full revelation of God is given in Christ, when theophany, prophecy, and miracle have reached their culmination in him and the grace of God in Christ has appeared to all people, then Scripture too is completed. Christ has fully revealed the Father to us in his person and work; therefore that revelation is fully described for us in Scripture. Revelation has in a certain sense reached its end. The dispensation of the Son gives way to the dispensation of the Spirit. Objective revelation passes over into subjective appropriation. In Christ God has created an organic center within history; from there circles are now drawn ever more widely within which the light of revelation shines. When the sun rises, its rays cover only a small surface of the earth; standing at the zenith, it shines over the whole earth. Israel was only the instrument of revelation; it completed its service and falls away when it has brought forth the Christ according to the flesh; now the grace of God appears to all people. Revelation therefore does continue, but in another way and in other forms. The Holy Spirit takes everything from Christ; he adds nothing new to revelation. Revelation is complete and therefore incapable of increase. Christ is the Logos, full of grace and truth; his work is finished; the Father himself rests in his work. His work cannot be supplemented or increased by the good works of the saints, his word by tradition, or his person by the pope. In Christ God has fully revealed and fully given himself. Therefore Scripture too is complete; it is the perfect word of God. And yet, though in another way, revelation continues, for it has its final goal not in Christ, who is mediator, but in the new humanity, in God’s dwelling with his people. It continues in all three of its forms: theophany, prophecy, and miracle. God comes to and dwells in the congregation of Christ; where two or three are gathered in his name, he is in their midst. He continues to perform miracles; he renews the congregation through regeneration, sanctification, and glorification; the spiritual miracles do not cease. God is always at work. But this is not enough. Not only the realm of being but also that of consciousness must be renewed. In the Logos was the life but also the light of men; Christ is full of grace but also of truth; revelation consisted in miracle but also in prophecy. Word and deed went together in the first dispensation; they also accompany each other in the economy of the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit regenerates but also illuminates. But just as the spiritual miracles add no new element to the objective facts of revelation and are only effects of the miracle of God’s grace wrought in Christ, so too prophecy in the congregation, the illumination of the Holy Spirit, is not a revelation of mysteries but an application of the treasures of wisdom and knowledge that are contained in Christ and displayed in his word. And these two activities of the Holy Spirit go hand in hand in this dispensation. Prophecy and miracle, word and deed, illumination and regeneration, Scripture and church accompany one another. Even now revelation is not doctrine alone, which enlightens the understanding, but also a life, which renews the heart. It is both together in indissoluble unity. The one-sided tendencies of intellectualism and mysticism must both be avoided, for both misconceive the riches of revelation. Because head and heart, the whole man in his being and consciousness, must be renewed, revelation continues in this dispensation in Scripture and church together. And in this they stand in the closest possible relation to one another. Scripture is the light of the church; the church is the life of Scripture. Outside the church Scripture is a riddle, an offense. Without regeneration no one can know it. Whoever does not partake of its life cannot understand its sense and meaning. Conversely, the life of the church is a mystery if Scripture does not let its light shine upon it. Scripture explains the church; the church understands Scripture. In the church Scripture confirms and seals its revelation, and in Scripture the Christian learns to understand himself and the church learns to understand herself—in relation to God and the world, in past, present, and future.

Therefore Scripture does not stand by itself. It may not be conceived deistically. It is rooted in a history of centuries and is the fruit of revelation among Israel and in Christ. Yet it is not a book from long-vanished times that connects us only with persons and events of the past. Holy Scripture is no dry account and no ancient chronicle, but the ever-living, eternally youthful word that God now, in this time, and continually sends forth to his people. It is God’s ever-continuing address to us. It serves not only to inform us historically of what happened in the past. It does not even intend to provide us with a historical narrative according to the standard of exactness required in other sciences. Holy Scripture is a _Tendenz_-book, a book with a purpose: whatever was written before was written for our instruction, so that through the patience and comfort of the Scriptures we might have hope. Scripture was written by the Holy Spirit so that it might serve him in his guidance of the church, in the perfecting of the saints, and in the building up of the body of Christ. In it God comes daily to his people. In it he speaks to his children, not from afar but from near at hand. In it he reveals himself day by day to believers in the fullness of his grace and truth. Through it he works his miracles of mercy and faithfulness. Scripture is the enduring bond between heaven and earth, between Christ and his congregation, between God and his children. It not only binds us to the past; it binds us to the living Lord in heaven. It is the _viva vox Dei, epistola Dei omnipotentis ad suam creaturam_. By the word God once created the world, and by the word he sustains it; but by the word he also re-creates it and prepares it as his dwelling. Theopneusty is therefore also an abiding property of Holy Scripture. Its theopneusty did not occur only at the moment it was committed to writing; Scripture remains theopneustic. _Divinitus inspirata est scriptura, non solum dum scripta est Deo spirante per scriptores; sed etiam dum legitur Deo spirante per scripturam et scriptura ipsum spirante_, Bengel on 2 Tim. 3:16. Having arisen from revelation, Scripture is kept alive and made _efficax_ through theopneusty. It is the Holy Spirit who sustains both prophecy and miracle, Scripture and church, and relates them to one another, and who thus prepares the parousia. For when being and consciousness have both been wholly renewed, revelation has reached its end. Scripture is then no longer necessary. Theopneusty then belongs to all the children of God. They will all be taught by the Lord and serve him in his temple. Prophecy and miracle have become nature, for God dwells among his people.
