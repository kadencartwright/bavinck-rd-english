Now this separation has proved practically impossible. In Christianity, as in every other religion, detaching the idea from history amounts to nothing less than the loss of the idea itself. Lessing’s proposition in his essay _Ueber den Beweis des Geistes und der Kraft_—_Zufällige Geschichtswahrheiten können der Beweis von nothwendigen Vernunftwahrheiten nie werden_—once gained great currency. But this can be explained only from the unhistorical sense and deification of reason in the eighteenth century. The present century has unlearned its enthusiasm for necessary truths of reason and its contempt for history. It has learned to understand history in its deeper sense and eternal significance.

If the facts of history were contingent, history itself would become an aggregate of self-contained occurrences, without order, connection, or plan. There would then no longer be history, and its study would be an empty, useless occupation. It would then follow of itself that the history of revelation too loses all its value. But history is precisely the realization of thoughts of God, the working out of a counsel of God concerning his creatures; there is unity, progression, order, _logos_ in it. And such a conception of history first became possible through Christianity. It is not found among the Greeks and Romans; there were only peoples, there was no humanity. Scripture, however, acquaints us with the unity of the human race; it gives us the grand conception of a world history. And in this history Scripture itself occupies the first and all-governing place.

The _Geschichtswahrheiten_ are therefore not contingent, least of all the history of revelation among them. It is so necessary that without it all history and all humanity fall apart. It is the bearer of the thoughts of God; the apocalypse of God’s purpose that repeatedly filled the apostle Paul with admiration and worship; the _revelatio mysterii_ without which man gropes about in darkness. On the other hand, the _Vernunftwahrheiten_ of which Lessing spoke are anything but necessary. Kant’s critique has demonstrated otherwise. Precisely with respect to those _nothwendige Vernunftwahrheiten_, a disquieting skepticism generally prevails today. The relation is therefore exactly reversed. The historical has been understood in its eternal significance, and the rational has come to light in its mutability.

It is also a fact that we inherit everything from preceding generations. We bring nothing into the world, 1 Tim. 6:7. Physically and psychically, intellectually and ethically, we are dependent on the world around us. Religiously it is no different. Revelation, which exists in history, can come to us only by way of tradition in the broadest sense. The question why that revelation is not granted to every human being—Rousseau in his _profession de foi_, and Strauss, _Glaub._ I 268 f.—is not really appropriate. It presupposes that revelation contains nothing but doctrine and forgets that it is, and must be, history.

The center of revelation is the person of Christ. And Christ is a historical person; his incarnation, suffering and death, resurrection and ascension are not susceptible of repetition. Indeed, it belongs precisely to the ἐνσαρκωσις that he enter into history and live in the form of time. He would not have been like us in all things if he had not subjected himself to time and space, to the law of becoming. Revelation, not as doctrine but as incarnation, can by its nature be nothing other than history—that is, occur at a definite time and be bound to a definite place. The incarnation is the unity of being, ἐγω εἰμι, John 8:58, and becoming, σαρξ ἐγενετο, John 1:14.

Moreover, in revelation too God follows the basic lines he has drawn for human society. Humanity is not an aggregate of individuals but an organic whole in which all live from one another. Revelation follows this law; re-creation attaches itself to creation. Just as in every sphere we partake of the goods of humanity by means of tradition, so too in religion. This also belongs to the idea of incarnation. The incarnation is itself an _actus transiens_, but through tradition becomes the possession and blessing of all human beings.

That this revelation is nevertheless still restricted to such a small portion of humanity presents a difficult problem that must later be investigated more closely. But this moving fact can never be a reason for those who know revelation to reject it. Many peoples live in a state of raw barbarism. This too happens according to the will and good pleasure of God. Yet it occurs to no one on that account to despise the blessings of civilization. Rousseau rhapsodized about natural man, but quietly remained in France.
