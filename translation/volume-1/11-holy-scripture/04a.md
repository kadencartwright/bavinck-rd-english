### B. The Doctrine of Inspiration

## 4.

The authority of Holy Scripture has been acknowledged by all Christian churches. There is no dogma concerning which greater unity exists than that of Holy Scripture. The genesis of this faith in Holy Scripture can no longer be traced. It exists as far back as we can go. In the Old Testament the authority of YHWH’s commandments and ordinances—that is, of the Torah—and likewise of the prophets is already established. Moses and the prophets have always been men of divine authority among Israel; their writings were immediately acknowledged as authoritative.

The Jews built upon this a doctrine of inspiration as strict and exclusive as possible. The Torah stands at the head of the Old Testament Scripture; in its content it is identical with divine Wisdom, the image of God, the daughter of God, the self-sufficient revelation of salvation intended for all peoples, the highest good, the way to life. If Israel had not sinned, it would have been sufficient. But now the writings of the prophets were later added to it by way of explanation. All those writings are divine, holy, the rule of doctrine and life, and of infinite content. Nothing in them is superfluous; everything has significance—every letter, every sign, down to the figure and form of a letter—for everything comes from God.

According to Philo, _De migr. Abrahae_, and Josephus, _Ant._ 4, 6, 5; _C. Ap._ 1,7, the prophets during inspiration found themselves in a state of rapture and unconsciousness which they compared with pagan manticism and sometimes extended to persons other than the prophets. Yet the divine authority of Holy Scripture stands unshakably firm for them too. Only in practice was that authority again impaired by tradition. Scripture, namely, was insufficient in itself. According to the conception of the Jews there was also an oral tradition which originated from God and had been handed down through Moses, Aaron, the elders, the prophets, and the men of the Great Synagogue to the scribes. It was finally deposited in the Mishnah and Gemara, which were now added as _norma normata_ to the _norma normans_ and brought into agreement with Scripture especially with the aid of thirteen hermeneutical rules. Zunz, _Die gottesd. Vorträge der Juden_, 1832, S. 37 f.; Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._, 1880, S. 14 f., 78 f.; Schürer, _Neutest. Zeitgeschichte_, Leipzig, 1874, S. 437 f.
