### D. The Concept of Inspiration

## 13.

Holy Scripture nowhere offers us a clearly formulated dogma concerning inspiration, but it gives us the matter, the fact of theopneusty, and all the moments necessary for constructing the dogma. It teaches the theopneusty of Scripture in the same sense and manner, just as plainly and clearly but just as little formulated in abstract concepts, as the dogma of the Trinity, the incarnation, satisfaction, and so forth.

This has repeatedly been denied. Almost every sectarian and heretical tendency begins with an appeal to Scripture against the confession and attempts to present its deviation as required by Scripture. But in most cases deeper investigation leads to the acknowledgment that orthodoxy has the testimony of Scripture on its side. The modern theologians now generally admit freely that Jesus and the apostles accepted the Old Testament Scripture as God’s Word, Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 185, S. 141; Strauss I 79; Pfleiderer, _Der Paulinismus_, 2e Aufl., Leipzig, 1890, S. 87 f. Rothe, _Zur Dogm._ 178 f., acknowledges this too with respect to the apostles, but thinks that ecclesiastical dogmatics cannot appeal to Jesus for its doctrine of inspiration.

This opinion, however, stands virtually alone and is shared by few. Jesus’s positive statements concerning the Old Testament Scripture, Matt. 5:8; Luke 16:17; John 10:35; his citation and use of it, Matt. 19:4, 5; 22:43, etc., speak too powerfully for this, and are no freer than those of the apostles. But the opposition that Rothe makes between the doctrine of Jesus and that of the apostles does not elevate but in fact undermines the authority of Jesus himself. For we know nothing of Jesus except through the apostles. Whoever therefore discredits the apostles and presents them as unreliable witnesses of the truth immediately contradicts Jesus himself, who appointed his apostles as perfectly reliable witnesses and would lead them through his Spirit into all the truth. And to this certainly belongs the truth concerning Holy Scripture as well. The slogan “Back to Christ” is deceptive and false when it stands in opposition to the testimony of the apostles.

Another contrast, made to escape the self-testimony of Scripture, is also very common. Scripture, it is then said, may here and there teach inspiration; but in order to construct the doctrine of Scripture concerning Scripture, account must also be taken of the facts that Scripture makes known to us in its origin, development, history, composition, and content. Only such a theory of inspiration, therefore, is true and good as is compatible with the _phenomena_ of Scripture and is derived from these themselves.

Very often it is then made to appear that the opposing party imposes its own _a priori_ opinion upon Scripture and presses it into the corset of scholasticism. And appeal is made to the claim that, over against all those theories and systems, one wishes precisely to let Scripture itself speak and testify only of itself. What orthodoxy lacks, it is said, is precisely reverence for Scripture. It does violence to the text, the facts of Holy Scripture, Dr. G. Wildeboer, _Letterk. des O. V._, p. V.

This representation sounds beautiful and plausible on first hearing, but on closer consideration nevertheless proves untenable. In the first place, the opposition is not between one theory of inspiration or another and the self-testimony of Scripture. Inspiration is a fact taught by Holy Scripture itself. Jesus and the apostles gave a testimony concerning Scripture. Scripture contains a doctrine concerning itself as well. Apart from every dogmatic or scholastic development of this doctrine, the question is simply whether Scripture deserves belief in this self-testimony or not. There can be disagreement over the question whether Scripture teaches such a theopneusty of itself. But if it does, then it must also be believed in this, just as in its statements concerning God, Christ, salvation, and so forth.

The so-called _phenomena_ of Scripture cannot overthrow this self-testimony of Scripture and may not even be summoned as a party against it. For whoever makes his doctrine of Scripture dependent upon historical investigation into its development and structure already begins by rejecting the self-testimony of Holy Scripture and thus no longer stands in faith in that Scripture. He thinks he can construct the doctrine of Scripture better from his own investigation than derive it in faith from Scripture; he places his own thoughts in the place of and above those of Scripture.

Further, the self-testimony of Scripture is plain, clear, and even acknowledged as such by opponents. But the view concerning the _phenomena_ of Scripture is the result of lengthy historical-critical investigation and changes into all manner of forms according to the different standpoint of the critics. The theologian who wishes to arrive at a doctrine concerning Scripture from such investigations in fact sets his scientific insight over against Scripture’s doctrine concerning itself. Along this road, however, one never arrives at a doctrine concerning Scripture. Historical-critical investigation can provide a clear insight into the origin, history, and structure of Scripture, but never leads to a doctrine, to a _dogma de S. Scriptura_. By its nature this can be built only upon a testimony of Scripture concerning itself. No one thinks to call a history concerning the origin and components of the _Iliad_ a doctrine.

Under this method, then, not only one theory of inspiration or another falls away, but inspiration itself as a fact and testimony of Scripture. Inspiration, if the word is still retained, then becomes nothing but the brief summary of what the Bible _is_, or rather of what one _thinks_ the Bible is, and can then stand in direct conflict with what the Bible itself claims to be and as which it presents and announces itself.

The method followed is in essence no different from one in which the doctrine of creation, man, sin, and so forth, is built not from Scripture’s testimony concerning them but from the independent study of those _facta_. In both cases it is a correction of the Bible’s doctrine by one’s own scientific investigation, a making of Scripture’s testimony dependent upon human judgment. The facts and phenomena of Scripture, the results of scientific investigation, may serve to explain and elucidate Scripture’s doctrine concerning itself, and so forth, but can never nullify the fact of inspiration to which Scripture testifies. While on the other side it is therefore asserted that only such an inspiration is acceptable as agrees with the _phenomena_ of Scripture, on this side the principle is that the phenomena of Scripture—not as criticism sees them but as they are in themselves—are compatible with its self-testimony.
