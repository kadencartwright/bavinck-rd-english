## 14.

What Scripture teaches concerning itself is usually comprehended in the word theopneusty or inspiration. The word θεοπνευστος at 2 Tim. 3:16 does not occur earlier and may therefore have been used first by Paul. Etymologically it can have both an active and a passive meaning and thus be translated both “breathing God” and “breathed by God.” But the passive meaning deserves preference, since it is supported most by the passages where the word occurs outside the New Testament and is recommended by the New Testament’s doctrine of Scripture, 2 Pet. 1:21. In the Vulgate it is rendered _divinitus inspirata_.

The word inspiration originally had a much broader sense. The Greeks and Romans ascribed an _afflatus_ or _instinctus divinus_ to all who accomplished something great and good. _Nemo vir magnus sine aliquo afflatu divino unquam fuit_, Cicero, _De Nat. D._ 2, 66. _Est deus in nobis, agitante calescimus illo_, Ovid, _Fasti_ 6, 5. The inspiration of poets, artists, _vates_, and others can indeed also serve to elucidate the inspiration of which Holy Scripture speaks.

Almost all great men have declared that their finest thoughts arose suddenly and unconsciously in their souls and were a surprise to themselves. One testimony may suffice. Goethe once wrote to Eckermann, cited by Hoekstra, _Godg. Bĳdr._, 1864, pp. 27, 28: _Jede Produktivität höchster Art, jedes bedeutendes Aperçue, jede Erfindung, jeder grosse Gedanke der Früchte bringt und Folgen hat, steht in Niemandes Gewalt und ist über alle irdische Macht erhaben. Dergleichen hat der Mensch als unverhoffte Geschenke von oben, als reine Kinder Gottes zu betrachten, die er mit freudigem Danke zu empfangen und zu verehren hat. In solchen Fällen ist der Mensch als das Werkzeug einer höhern Weltregierung zu betrachten, als ein würdig befundenes Gefäss zur Aufnahme eines göttlichen Einflusses._ Carlyle, _On heroes, hero-worship and the heroic in history_, 4th ed., London, 1854, therefore pointed to heroes or geniuses as the core of the history of humanity. In turn, these geniuses in every sphere have inspired the masses. Luther, Bacon, Napoleon, and Hegel transformed the thoughts of millions and changed consciousness.

This fact already teaches us that an operation of one spirit upon another is possible. Its manner differs when one human being speaks to another, when an orator inspires the crowd through his word, when the hypnotist transplants his thought into the magnetized person, and so forth; but there is always suggestion of thoughts, inspiration in the broader sense.

Now Scripture teaches us that the world is not independent and does not exist and live from itself, but that the Spirit of God is immanent in everything created. The immanence of God is the basis for all inspiration and theopneusty, Ps. 104:30; 139:7; Job 33:4. Being and life are inspired into every creature from moment to moment by the Spirit. More specifically, that Spirit of the Lord is the principle of all understanding and wisdom, Job 32:8; Isa. 11:2, so that all knowledge and art, all talent and genius, proceed from him. In the church he is the Spirit of regeneration and renewal, Ps. 51:13; Ezek. 36:26, 27; John 3:3; the distributor of gifts, 1 Cor. 12:4–6. In the prophets he is the Spirit of prediction, Num. 11:25; 24:2, 3; Isa. 11:2; 42:1; Mic. 3:8, etc. And so in the composition of Scripture he is also the Spirit of inspiration.

This last activity of the Holy Spirit therefore does not stand by itself; it is connected with his whole immanent activity in the world and in the church. It is the crown and pinnacle of all. The inspiration of the writers in the production of the biblical books is built upon all those other activities of the Holy Spirit. It presupposes a work of the Father by which the organs of revelation were prepared for the task to which they would later be specially called, long beforehand from birth, indeed even before birth in their ancestry, environment, education, development, and so forth, Ex. 3–4; Jer. 1:5; Acts 7:22; Gal. 1:15, etc.

Inspiration may therefore not, as the modern theologians do, be identified with heroic, poetic, or religious inspiration. It is not a work of the _providentia Dei generalis_, not an operation of God’s Spirit in the same measure and manner as in heroes and artists, even though this operation of God’s Spirit is repeatedly presupposed among the prophets and biblical writers. The Spirit in creation prepares the way for the Spirit in re-creation.

Further, inspiration proper also presupposes an antecedent work of the Son. The gift of theopneusty is granted only within the circle of revelation. Theophany, prophecy, and miracle precede inspiration proper. Revelation and inspiration are distinct: the former is a work of the Son, of the Logos, the latter of the Holy Spirit. There is therefore truth in Schleiermacher’s thought that the holy writers stood under the influence of the holy circle in which they lived. Revelation and inspiration must be distinguished. But inspiration is not identical with revelation (cf. above, p. 300). It is rooted in revelation but rises above it.

Finally, inspiration generally, though not always, also presupposes a work of the Holy Spirit himself in regeneration, faith, and conversion. The prophets and apostles were generally holy men, children of God. This thought of Ethical theology therefore also contains elements of truth. Yet inspiration is not identical with regeneration. Regeneration embraces the whole man; inspiration is an operation in consciousness. The former sanctifies and renews; the latter illumines and instructs. The former does not of itself bring inspiration with it, and inspiration is possible without regeneration, Num. 23:5; John 11:51; cf. Num. 22:28; 1 Sam. 19:24; Heb. 6:4. Regeneration is a _habitus permanens_; inspiration an _actus transiens_.

Inspiration thus stands in the closest connection with all these activities of God. It may not be isolated from them. It is incorporated into all those operations of God in everything created. Yet here too the theory of evolution must be opposed, as though the higher could proceed solely by immanent development from the lower. The operation of God’s Spirit in nature, in humanity, in the church, in the prophets, and in the biblical writers is related and analogous, but not identical. There is harmony, not sameness.
