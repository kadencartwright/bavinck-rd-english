## 19.

From this, finally, the relation in which Scripture stands to the other sciences becomes clear. Much misuse has been made of the saying of Baronius: Scripture does not tell us how heaven goes, but how we go to heaven. Precisely as the book of the knowledge of God, Scripture has much to say also to the other sciences. It is a light on the path and a lamp for the foot, also of science and art. It claims authority in every sphere of life. Christ has all power in heaven and on earth.

Objectively, the restriction of inspiration to the religious-ethical portion of Scripture is untenable; and subjectively, the separation between the religious and the rest of human life cannot be maintained. Inspiration extends to all parts of Scripture, and religion is a matter of the whole man. Much of what is mentioned in Scripture is also of principial significance for the other sciences. The creation and fall of man, the unity of the human race, the flood, the origin of peoples and languages, and so forth, are facts of the highest importance for the other sciences as well. At every moment science and art come into contact with Scripture; the _principia_ for all of life are given in Scripture. Nothing may be taken away from this.

On the other hand, however, a great truth also lies in Cardinal Baronius’s saying. All those facts too are communicated in Scripture not in and for themselves, but with a theological purpose, so that we might know God unto salvation. Scripture never deliberately concerns itself with science as such. Christ himself, though free from all error and sin, nevertheless never moved in the narrower sense in the sphere of science and art, commerce and industry, jurisprudence and politics. His was another greatness: the glory of the Only Begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth. But precisely thereby he was a blessing also for science and art, society and state.

Jesus is Savior—that alone, but that also entirely. He did not come merely to restore man’s religious-ethical life and leave everything else untouched, as if it had not been corrupted by sin and did not need restoration. No: as far as sin extends, so far also extends the grace of Christ. It is the same with Scripture. It too is thoroughly religious, the Word of God unto salvation, but precisely therefore also a word for family and society, for science and art.

Scripture is a book for all humanity, in all its ranks and stations, in all its generations and peoples. But for this very reason it is not a scientific book in the narrower sense. Wisdom, not scholarship, speaks in it. It does not speak the exact language of science and the school, but that of intuition and daily life. It judges and describes things not according to the results of scientific investigation, but according to intuition, according to the first, living impression that phenomena make upon man. It therefore speaks of the land drawing near, of the rising and standing still of the sun, of blood as the soul of the animal, of the kidneys as the seat of affections, of the heart as the source of thoughts, and so forth, and in this concerns itself not at all with the scientifically precise language of astronomy, physiology, psychology, and so forth.

It speaks of the earth as the center of God’s creation and takes no side between the Ptolemaic and Copernican worldviews. It does not decide between Neptunism and Plutonism, nor between allopathy and homeopathy. The _scriptores_ of Holy Scripture probably knew no more in all these sciences—geology, zoology, physiology, medicine, and so forth—than all their contemporaries. Nor was this necessary. For Holy Scripture uses the language of daily experience, which is always true and always remains so.

If Scripture had instead used the language of the school and spoken with scientific exactness, it would have stood in the way of its own authority. If it had decided for the Ptolemaic worldview, it would be incredible in an age that embraced the Copernican system. Nor could it have been a book for life, for humanity. But now it speaks in universally human language, intelligible to the simplest, clear to learned and unlearned alike. It uses the language of intuition, which will always continue to exist alongside that of science and the school. It can therefore also endure to the end of the ages. It is therefore old without ever becoming obsolete. It is always young and fresh; it is the speech of life. _Verbum Dei manet in aeternum._
