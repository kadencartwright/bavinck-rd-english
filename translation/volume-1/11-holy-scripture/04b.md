The Christian community, with Jesus and the apostles, rejected the whole Jewish tradition but nevertheless from the beginning acknowledged the divine authority of the Old Testament Scripture, Harnack, _Dogm. gesch._ I 39 f., 145 f., 244 f. The church has never been without the Bible. It received the Old Testament from the hands of the apostles immediately with divine authority. From the beginning Christian faith included faith in the divine authority of the Old Testament.

Clement of Rome teaches the inspiration of the Old Testament as clearly as possible. He calls the Old Testament writings τα λογια του θεου, _1 Cor._ 53, and τας γραφας, τας ἀληθεις τας δια πνευματος του ἁγιου, ibid. 45; cites passages from the Old Testament with the formula “the Holy Spirit says,” ibid. 13; and says of the prophets: οἱ λειτουργοι της χαριτος του θεου δια πνευματος ἁγιου ἐλαλησαν, ibid. 8. He extends inspiration to the apostles too and says that they went out to preach μετα πληροφοριας πνευματος ἁγιου, ibid. 42, and that Paul wrote to the Corinthians πνευματικως, ibid. 47.

For the rest, the apostolic fathers supply little material for the dogma of Scripture. Inspiration itself stands firm, but there is still disagreement concerning the scope and limits of inspiration; little is said of the New Testament writings, and apocryphal writings are sometimes cited as canonical. The apologists of the second century, Justin, _Coh. ad Graecos_ c. 8, and Athenagoras, _Leg. pro Christo_ cap. 7, compare the writers to a cithara, lyre, or flute, which the divine πληκτρον used as an organ. The doctrine of the apostles stands on one line with that of the prophets. Like Abraham, we believe τῃ φωνῃ του θεου, τῃ δια τε των ἀποστολων του χριστου λαληθεισῃ παλιν και τῃ δια των προφητων κηρυχθεισῃ ἡμιν, Justin, _Dial._ 119. The Gospels share in the same inspiration as the prophets, δια το τους παντας πνευματοφορους ἑνι πνευματι θεου λελαληκεναι, Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ 3, n. 12.

In Irenaeus there is already full acknowledgment of the inspiration of both Testaments: _Scripturae perfectae sunt, quippe a Deo et Spiritu ejus dictae_, _Adv. haer._ 2, 28. They have one author and one goal, 4, 9. Further, the Holy Scriptures are cited by the church fathers as θεια γραφη, κυριακαι γραφαι, θεοπνευστοι γραφαι, _coelestes literae, divinae voces, bibliotheca sancta, chirographum Dei_, and so forth. The writers are called λειτουργοι της χαριτος του θεου, ὀργανα θειας φωνης, στομα θεου, πνευματοφοροι, χριστοφοροι, ἐμπνευσθεντες, θεοφορουμενοι, _Spiritu divino inundati, pleni_, and so forth.

The act of inspiration is represented as an impelling, leading, and so forth, but especially often as a dictation of the Holy Spirit, Irenaeus, loc. cit.; Augustine, _De cons. Evang._ 1, 54. The writers were the hands of the Holy Spirit, Augustine, ibid.; they were not the _auctores_ but only _scriptores, scribae_. God alone is the _auctor_ of Holy Scripture, Isidore of Seville, lib. 1, _De offic._ c. 12, in Dausch, _Die Schriftinspiration_, 1891, S. 87. Scripture is an _epistola omnipotentis Dei ad suam creaturam_, Augustine, _In Ps._ 20, Serm. 2, 1; Gregory the Great, _Epist._ l. 4, ep. 31.

There is nothing indifferent and nothing superfluous in it, but everything is full of divine wisdom: _nihil enim vacuum, neque sine signo apud Deum_, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ 4, 21, 3. Origen especially pressed this strongly and said that no tittle or iota was in vain, that nothing was in Scripture _quod non a plenitudine divinae majestatis descendat_, _Homil._ 2, 21, 39 _in Jerem._ Jerome likewise said: _singuli sermones, syllabae, apices, puncta in divinis scripturis plena sunt sensibus et spirant caelestia sacramenta_. Holy Scripture was therefore without any defect or error, even in chronological and historical matters, Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ 23; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ 3, 5.

What the apostles wrote must likewise be accepted as if Christ himself had written it, for they were, as it were, his hands, Augustine, _De cons. Evang._ 1, 54. In his letter to Jerome he says that he firmly believes that none of the canonical writers _scribendo aliquid errasse_. If there is an error, therefore, _non licet dicere: auctor hujus libri non tenuit veritatem sed: aut codex mendosus est, aut interpres erravit, aut tu non intelligis_, _C. Faust._ 11, 5.

At the same time, over against Montanism the self-consciousness of the writers during inspiration was emphasized as strongly as possible. Preliminary investigation, difference in development, use of sources and memory, difference in language and style were fully acknowledged by Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Augustine, Jerome, and others. Some even assumed a difference in the manner of inspiration between the Old and New Testaments, or also a difference in degree of inspiration according to the moral condition of the writers, Novatian, _De trin._ 4; Origen, _C. Cels._ 7, 4. But none of this detracted from faith in the divine origin and divine authority of Holy Scripture. These were universally established. The practical use of Scripture in preaching, argumentation, exegetical treatment, and so forth, proves this still more and more powerfully than isolated statements.

In this first period the church had more to do with establishing the canon than with the concept of inspiration, but by the canonical it understood precisely _divinae scripturae_, Council of Carthage, 397, can. 47, and ascribed authority to these alone, Council of Toledo, 447; cf.
