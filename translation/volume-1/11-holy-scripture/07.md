## 7.

The Reformers accepted Scripture and its theopneusty as these had been handed down to them by the church. From his soteriological standpoint Luther now and then rendered an unfavorable judgment concerning certain books—Esther, Ezra, Nehemiah, James, Jude, Revelation—and admitted minor inaccuracies, but on the other hand nevertheless maintained inspiration in the strictest sense and extended it even to the letters. Bretschneider, _Luther an unsere Zeit_, 1817; Schenkel, _Das Wesen der Protest._ II 56 f.; Köstlin, _Luthers Theologie_ II 246 f.; W. Rohnert, _Was lehrt L. von der Insp. der H. Schrift_, Leipzig, 1890; Fr. Pieper, “Luther’s doctrine of inspiration,” _Presbyt. and Ref. Rev._, April 1893, pp. 249–266.

The Lutheran symbols have no separate article on Scripture but everywhere presuppose its divine origin and authority, _Conf. Aug._ praef. 8, art. 7; _Art. Smalc._ II, art. 2, 15; _Form. Conc._, Pars I, Epit. _de comp. regula atque norma_ 1. The Lutheran dogmaticians—Melanchthon in the _praefatio_ to his _Loci_; Chemnitz, _Examen Conc. Trid._, loc. 1; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, loc. 1, and others—all have the same conception. Not first Quenstedt and Calovius, but already Gerhard calls the writers _Dei amanuenses, Christi manus et Spiritus Sancti tabelliones sive notarios_, ibid., loc. 1, cap. 2, § 18. Later authors merely developed and applied the principle further. Heppe, _Dogmatik des deutschen Prot._ I 207–257; Hase, _Hutt. Rediv._ § 38 f.; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, cap. 4; Rohnert, _Die Inspiration der H. Schrift_, 169 f.; Koelling, _Die Lehre von der Theopneustie_, 1891, S. 212 f.

Among the Reformed we encounter the same doctrine concerning Scripture. Zwingli frequently subordinates the external word to the internal, admits historical and chronological inaccuracies, and sometimes extends inspiration to pagan writers, Zeller, _Das theol. System Zwingli’s_, 137 f.; Chr. Sigwart, _Ulr. Zwingli_, 45 f. But Calvin regards Scripture in the full and literal sense as God’s Word, _Instit._ I, capp. 7–8; commentary on 2 Tim. 3:16 and 2 Pet. 1:20. He indeed does not acknowledge the epistle to the Hebrews as Pauline but nevertheless regards it as canonical, and assumes an error at Matt. 22:9; 23:25, but not in the _autographa_. Cramer, _De Schriftbeschouwing van Calvĳn_, _Heraut_, no. 26 f.; Moore, “Calvin’s doctrine of holy Scripture,” _Presb. and Ref. Rev._, Jan. 1893, p. 49 etc.

The Reformed confessions generally have an article on Holy Scripture and clearly express its divine authority, First Helvetic 1–3; Second Helvetic 1, 2, 13, 18; Gallican 5; Belgic 3; Anglican 6; Scottish 18, etc. And all Reformed theologians without distinction adopt this same standpoint: Ursinus, _Tract. theol._, 1584, pp. 1–33; Zanchi, _Op._ VIII, col. 319–451; Junius, _Theses Theol._, cap. 2; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ I 15; _Synopsis_, disp. 2; Voetius, _Disp. Sel._ I 30 sq., etc.; Cramer, _De roomsch-kath. en oudprot. Schriftbeschouwing_, _Heraut_, no. 26 f.; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, S. 9 f.

On a single occasion a faint attempt at a more organic conception can be discerned. Inspiration did not always consist in revelation, but when known matters were concerned, in _assistentia_ and _directio_. The writers were not always passive but also active, so that they used their own understanding, memory, judgment, and style, yet in such a way that they were nevertheless led by the Holy Spirit and preserved from error, _Synopsis_ 3:7; Rivet, _Isag. seu introd. generalis ad Script. V. et N. T._, cap. 2; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._, loc. 2, §§ 33, 34. Yet not the least detriment was thereby done to the divinity and infallibility of Scripture.

The writers were not _auctores_ but _scriptores, amanuenses, notarii, manus, calami Dei_. Inspiration was not negative but always positive, an _impulsus ad scribendum_ and a _suggestio rerum et verborum_. It communicated not only unknown matters and words but also those already known, for the writers nevertheless had to know them precisely now and precisely so, not only _materialiter_ but also _formaliter_, not only _humane_ but also _divine_, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ 23, 24; Voetius, _Disp._ I 30.

Inspiration extended to all chronological, historical, and geographical matters, to the words, even to vowels and signs. J. Buxtorf, _Tract. de punctorum origine, autiquitate et auctoritate_, 1648; _Anticritica_, 1653; Alsted, _Praecognita Theol._, p. 276; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ I, p. 75; Voetius, _Disp._ I 34; _Cons. Helv._ art. 2. Barbarisms and solecisms were not admitted in Holy Scripture. Difference of style was explained from the will of the Holy Spirit, who wished to write differently now in this place and then in another, Quenstedt and Hollaz in Rohnert, _Die Inspir. der H. Schrift_, 205, 208; Winer, _Grammatik des N. T. Sprachidioms_, 6e Aufl., 11 f.; Voetius, ibid.; Gomarus, _Op._ 601.

_Materialiter_, as regards letters, syllables, and words, Scripture is _e sensu creaturarum_; but _formaliter_, as regards the _sensus θεοπνευστον_, _male creaturis accensetur, cum sit mens, consilium, sapientia Dei_, Hollaz, _Exam._, ed. Teller, p. 992, in Dausch, 112. In 1714 Nitzsche in Gotha wrote, according to Tholuck, _Vermischte Schriften_ II 86, a dissertation on the question whether Holy Scripture itself was God.
