## 11.

For the inspiration of the New Testament we find the following data in the writings of the apostles.

a) Throughout the New Testament Jesus’s testimony is regarded as divine, true, and infallible. He is the Logos who declares the Father, John 1:18; 17:6; ὁ μαρτυς ὁ πιστος και ἀληθινος, Rev. 1:5; 3:14; cf. Isa. 55:4; the Amen, in whom all the promises of God are yes and amen, Rev. 3:14; 2 Cor. 1:20. No deceit, δολος, was found in his mouth, 1 Pet. 2:22. He is the Apostle and High Priest of our confession, Heb. 3:1; 1 Tim. 6:13. He does not speak ἐκ των ἰδιων as does Satan, who is a liar, John 8:44. Rather, God speaks through him, Heb. 5:1. Jesus was sent by God, John 8:42, and speaks nothing except what he has seen and heard, John 3:32. He speaks the words of God, John 3:34; 17:8, and bears witness only to the truth, 5:33; 18:37. His testimony is therefore true, John 8:14; 14:6, confirmed by the testimony of God himself, 5:32, 37; 8:18.

Jesus is not only holy and without sin in the ethical sense, John 8:46, but is also intellectually without error, lie, or deception. It is entirely true that Jesus, in the narrower sense, did not move in the sphere of science. He came to earth to declare the Father to us and accomplish his work. But the inspiration of Scripture concerning which Jesus speaks is not a scientific problem but a religious truth. If he erred here, he was mistaken on a point most closely connected with religious life and can no longer be acknowledged in religion and theology as our highest prophet. The doctrine of the divine authority of Holy Scripture forms an important element in the words of God that Jesus proclaimed.

This infallibility in Jesus was not, however, an extraordinary supernatural gift, no _donum gratiae_ and no _actus transiens_, but _habitus_, nature. If Jesus had written anything, he would not have needed any special assistance of the Holy Spirit in doing so. He did not need inspiration as an extraordinary gift, because he received the Spirit without measure, John 3:34; was the Logos, John 1:1; and the fullness of God dwelt bodily in him, Col. 1:19; 2:9.

b) Jesus, however, left us nothing in writing, and he himself departed. He therefore had to take care that his true testimony was handed down uncorrupted and pure to humanity. To this end he chooses the apostles. The apostolate is an extraordinary office and an altogether special service in Jesus’s church. The apostles were specifically given to him by the Father, John 17:6; chosen by him, John 6:70; 13:18; 15:16, 19; and prepared and equipped by him in all manner of ways for their future task.

That task consisted in this: after Jesus’s departure they were to appear as _witnesses_, Luke 24:48; John 15:27. They had been ear- and eyewitnesses of Jesus’s words and works; they had beheld the Word of life with their eyes and touched it with their hands, 1 John 1:1, and now had to bring this testimony concerning Jesus to Israel and the whole world, Matt. 28:19; John 15:27; 17:20; Acts 1:8.

But every man is a liar; God alone is true, Rom. 3:4. The apostles too were incapable of this task of witnessing. They were therefore not the witnesses proper. Jesus merely employs them as instruments. The witness proper, who is faithful and true as he himself is, is the Holy Spirit. He is the Spirit of truth and will testify of Jesus, John 15:26; and the apostles can appear as witnesses only after and through him, John 15:27. That Spirit is accordingly promised and granted to the apostles in a special sense, Matt. 10:20; John 14:26; 15:26; 16:7; 20:22.

John 14:26 especially teaches this clearly. The Holy Spirit ὑπομνησει ὑμας παντα ἁ εἰπον ὑμιν. He will take the disciples, with their persons and gifts, their memory and judgment, and so forth, into his service. He will add materially nothing new to revelation that is not already contained in Christ’s person, word, and work, for he takes everything from Christ and to that extent merely brings it to the apostles’ remembrance and in this way leads them into all the truth, John 14:26; 16:13, 14. And this testimony of the Holy Spirit through the mouth of the apostles is the glorification of Jesus, John 16:14, just as Jesus’s testimony was a glorification of the Father, 17:4.

c) Equipped with that Spirit in a special sense, John 20:22; Acts 1:8; Eph. 3:5, the apostles also appear openly as witnesses after the day of Pentecost, Acts 1:8, 21, 22; 2:14, 32; 3:15; 4:8, 20, 33; 5:32; 10:39, 51; 13:31. The significance of the apostolate lies in bearing witness to what they have seen and heard. For this they are called and equipped. From this they derive their authority. To this they appeal over against opposition and resistance. And God in turn attaches his seal to their testimony by signs and wonders and spiritual blessing, Matt. 10:1, 9; Mark 16:15 f.; Acts 2:43; 3:2; 5:12–16; 6:8; 8:6 f.; 10:44; 11:21; 14:3; 15:8, etc.

From the outset, _jure suo_, the apostles are the leaders of the Jerusalem church. They have oversight of the believers in Samaria, Acts 8:14; visit the churches, Acts 9:32; 11:22; make decisions in the Holy Spirit, Acts 15:22, 28; and enjoy universally acknowledged authority. They speak and act by virtue of Christ’s commission.

And although Jesus nowhere gave an express command also to record his words and deeds—only in Revelation is a command to write repeatedly mentioned, 1:11, 19, etc.—the apostles speak with the same authority in their writing. Writing is a special form of witnessing. In writing too they are witnesses of Christ, Luke 1:2; John 1:14; 19:35; 20:31; 21:24; 1 John 1:1–4; 1 Pet. 1:12; 5:1; 2 Pet. 1:16; Heb. 2:3; Rev. 1:3; 22:18, 19. Their testimony is faithful and true, John 19:35; 3 John 12.
