## 16.

This organic conception, however, has repeatedly been used precisely to detract from the first element, the authorship of the Holy Spirit. The incarnation of Christ requires that we trace it into the depth of his humiliation, in all his weakness and reproach. The inscripturation of the word, of revelation, invites us to acknowledge in Scripture too what is weak and lowly, that form of a servant. But just as the human in Christ, however weak and lowly, nevertheless remained free from sin, so too Scripture is _sine labe concepta_. Wholly human and in all its parts, but equally _θεια παντα_. Yet this divine character of Scripture has been diminished in many ways. The history of inspiration teaches us that at first, down to the seventeenth century, inspiration was extended ever further, even to the vowels and points (_inspiratio punctualis_), and subsequently that it was gradually contracted and restricted more and more: from the points to the words (_insp. verbalis_); from the _singula verba_ to the word, the thought (_Wort_ rather than _Wörterinspiration_ in Philippi, _Kirchl. Glaub._ I³, 252); from the word as thought to the matters (_insp. realis_); from the matters to the religious-ethical content, to what is revealed in the proper sense, to the word of God _sensu stricto_, to the special object of saving faith (_insp. fundamentalis, religiosa_); from these matters in turn to the persons (_insp. personalis_); and from these finally to the denial of all inspiration as a supernatural gift.

Now it is gratifying that even the most negative tendency still wishes to secure a place for Scripture and ascribe some value to it in the religious life and thought of Christendom. The doctrine of Holy Scripture is not an opinion of this or that school, no dogma of a particular church or sect, but an _articulus fundamentalis_, an article of faith of the one holy catholic Christian church. Its significance for all Christianity is being understood better and better; its unbreakable connection with Christian faith and life is being acknowledged ever more fully. Relegated for a time in dogmatics to the _media gratiae_, it has honorably regained its place in the introduction to dogmatics, Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._ 212. The entire Roman and Greek Church still stands undiminished in the confession of the divine inspiration of Holy Scripture. Many Protestant churches and tendencies have until now resisted every effort to drive them from this foundation. Through preaching and instruction, through reading and study, the congregation continues to live indirectly or directly from Scripture and to nourish itself with Scripture. Even one who denies inspiration in theory often speaks and acts in the practice of life as though he fully accepted it. The dualism of believing and knowing in which, according to the representation of many, orthodoxy finds itself in the doctrine of Scripture does not compare with the equivocal position of the _Vermittelungstheologie_, which denies inspiration in the professor’s chair and in fact confesses it in the pulpit. Radicalism comes increasingly to acknowledge that the inspiration of Scripture is taught by Scripture itself and must be accepted or rejected with it. All this shows that life is stronger than doctrine and that Scripture itself reacts again and again against every naturalistic explanation. It itself claims to have proceeded from the Spirit of God and maintains this claim against all criticism. Every attempt to strip it of the mysterious character of its origin, content, and power has thus far ended by giving up the attempt and leaving Scripture as Scripture. Inspiration is therefore no explanation of Scripture and thus, properly speaking, no theory either; rather, it is and ought to be a believing confession of what Scripture testifies concerning itself, despite the appearance that stands against it. Inspiration is a dogma like the Trinity, the incarnation, and so forth, which the Christian accepts not because he perceives its truth but because God so testifies. It is not a scientific proposition but a confession of faith. With inspiration, as with every other dogma, the first question is not, “How much can and may I confess without coming into conflict with science?” but, “What is the testimony of God, and what accordingly is the declaration of Christian faith?” And then only one answer is possible: Scripture presents itself as the word of God, and the church of God in all ages has acknowledged it as such. Inspiration rests on the authority of Scripture and has received the testimony of the church of all ages.

The _inspiratio personalis_ and _fundamentalis_ conflict with this dogmatic and religious character of the doctrine of inspiration. There is indeed a good thought in these conceptions too. For it was certainly the persons who, with all their gifts and powers, were taken into service by the Holy Spirit in inspiration; and these persons were holy men, men of God, suitably equipped for this work. There is also without doubt a distinction in Scripture between more and less important parts; not all books of the Bible have equal value. But both conceptions of inspiration weaken the testimony that Scripture gives concerning itself and are born not of the plerophory of faith but of a compromise with science. Moreover, the _inspiratio personalis_ also founders on these objections: it erases the distinction between inspiration and illumination (regeneration), between the intellectual and the ethical life, between the φερεσθαι of the prophets, 2 Pet. 1:21, and the ἀγεσθαι of the children of God, Rom. 8:14, and between Holy Scripture and devotional literature. Furthermore, with Rome it reverses the relation of Scripture and church, deprives the congregation of the stability it needs, and makes it dependent on science, which must determine what in Scripture is or is not God’s word. Many advocates of this theory of inspiration do indeed try to escape these objections by appealing to the person of Christ as the source and authority of dogmatics, but this is of no avail because there is disagreement precisely over the question who Christ is and what he taught and did. If the apostolic testimony concerning Christ is not reliable, no knowledge of Christ is possible. Added to this is that if Christ is the authority, he is so also in the doctrine concerning Scripture; inspiration must then be accepted precisely on his authority. The theory mentioned above conflicts with the authority of Christ himself. The _inspiratio fundamentalis_ differs from the _inspiratio personalis_ in that it still assumes a special activity of the Spirit in the writing, but only in some portions of Scripture. This conception, however, is so deistic and dualistic that for this reason alone it is unacceptable. Moreover, word and fact, the religious and the historical, what is spoken by God and by human beings, are so interwoven and intertwined in Scripture that separation is impossible. The history in Scripture too is a revelation of God. And finally, these two theories still do not meet the objections brought against Scripture and its inspiration from the side of science. For these by no means concern a few subordinate points at the periphery of revelation but touch its very heart and center. The _inspiratio personalis_ and _fundamentalis_ are in no respect more scientific and rational than the strictest _inspiratio verbalis_.

The other theories of inspiration—_inspiratio punctualis, verbalis, realis_, and also Philippi’s _Wortinspiration_—differ little from one another. The activity of the Holy Spirit in the writing consisted in this: after preparing the human consciousness of the _scriptores_ in all kinds of ways—through birth, upbringing, natural gifts, investigation, memory, reflection, life experience, revelation, and so forth—he then, in and under and with the writing itself, caused to arise in that consciousness those thoughts and words, that language and style, which could interpret the divine thought in the best way for people of every rank, station, nation, and age. The words are comprehended in the thoughts and the vowels in the words. But it does not follow from this that the vowel signs in our Hebrew manuscripts originated with the writers themselves. Nor does it follow that everything is full of divine wisdom, that every jot and every tittle has an infinite content. Everything most certainly has its sense and significance, but in the place and the context in which it occurs. Scripture may not be viewed atomistically, as though every word and every letter, detached and isolated by itself, had as such been inspired by God with an intention of its own, with a content of its own and thus divine and infinite. That leads to the foolish hermeneutical rules of the Jewish scribes and does not honor but dishonors Holy Scripture. Inspiration must instead be conceived organically, so that even the least thing has its place and significance and yet at the same time lies at a much greater distance from the center than other parts. In the human organism nothing is accidental—neither length, nor breadth, nor color, nor shade—but it does not follow that everything stands in the same close connection with the center of life. Head and heart occupy a much more important place in the body than hand and foot, and these in turn stand far above nails and hair in value. In Scripture too not everything is grouped equally close around the center; there is a periphery that moves in a wide circle around the midpoint, but it too belongs to the circle of God’s thoughts. There are therefore no kinds and degrees in inspiration itself. The hair of the head shares in the same life as heart and hand. It is one _anima, quae tota est in toto corpore et in omnibus partibus_. It is one Spirit from whom all Scripture has proceeded through the consciousness of the writers. There is, however, a difference in the manner in which the same life is immanent and active in the different parts of the body. There is diversity of gifts, also in Scripture, but it is the same Spirit.
