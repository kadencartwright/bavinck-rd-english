As a result of this criticism, many modified the doctrine of inspiration. At first inspiration was still maintained as a supernatural operation of the Holy Spirit in writing, but was restricted to the religious-ethical sphere. In chronological, historical, and other matters it was weakened or denied, so that greater or smaller errors could occur there. The Word of God was to be distinguished from Holy Scripture.

The Socinians already taught this. The writers of the Old and New Testaments indeed wrote _divino spiritu impulsi eoque dictante_, but the Old Testament has only historical value; only doctrine is immediately inspired, while in the rest a _leviter errare_ is possible, Fock, _Der Socin._ 326 f. The Remonstrants adopted the same standpoint. They acknowledge inspiration, _Conf._ art. 1, but admit that the writers sometimes expressed themselves _minus exacte et praecise_, Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ I, c. 4, § 10; or sometimes erred in the _circumstantiae fidei_, Episcopius, _Instit. Theol._ IV 1, cap. 4; or, more strongly still, needed and received no inspiration in the historical books, H. Grotius, _Votum pro pace ecclesiae_; Clericus, in Dr. Cramer, _De geschied. van het leerstuk der inspiratie in de laatste twee eeuwen_, 1887, p. 24. We then find the same doctrine of inspiration in S. J. Baumgarten, J. G. Töllner, Semler, Michaelis, Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 19; Vinke, _Theol. Christ. Dogm. Comp._, 1853, pp. 53–57; Egeling, _Weg der Zaligheid_, 3e dr., II 612, etc.

But this theory encountered many objections. The separation between what is necessary for salvation and incidental history is impossible, since doctrine and history are wholly interwoven in Scripture. It fails to do justice to the consciousness of the writers, who by no means restrict their authority to the religious-ethical but extend it to the entire content of their writings. It conflicts with the use of Scripture by Jesus, the apostles, and the whole Christian church.

This dualistic conception therefore gave way to another, Schleiermacher’s dynamic view, _Christ. Gl._ §§ 128–132. It consists in transferring theopneusty from the intellectual to the ethical sphere. Inspiration is not in the first place a property of Scripture but of the writers. These were regenerated, holy men; they lived in proximity to Jesus, underwent his influence, moved within the holy circle of revelation, and were thus renewed also in their thinking and speaking. Inspiration is the habitual property of the writers. Their writings also share in this; they too bear a new, holy character.

But the inspiration of the writers is therefore not essentially but only gradually distinct from that of all believers, for all believers are led by the Holy Spirit. Nor may it be conceived mechanically, as if it were the writers’ portion only now and then and with respect to certain subjects. God’s Word is not mechanically contained in Scripture as a painting in its frame, but penetrates and animates all parts of Scripture as the soul does all members of the body. Yet not all parts of Scripture partake of this inspiration, this Word of God, in equal measure. The nearer something lies to the center of revelation, the more it breathes the Spirit of God.

Scripture is therefore at once a divine and a human book: on the one hand containing the highest truth and yet at the same time weak, fallible, imperfect; not revelation itself but the record of revelation; not the Word of God itself but a description of that Word; defective in many respects but nevertheless a sufficient instrument for us to attain an infallible knowledge of revelation. Finally, not Scripture but the person of Christ, or revelation in general, is the _principium_ of theology.

Naturally this theory of inspiration permits very many modifications. Theopneusty can be placed in a more or less intimate relation to revelation; the operation of the Holy Spirit can be conceived more or less positively; the possibility of error can be admitted to a greater or lesser extent. But the fundamental thoughts nevertheless remain the same. Inspiration is first a property of the writers and afterward of their writings. It is not a momentary act or a special gift of the Holy Spirit but a habitual property. It works so dynamically that the possibility of error is not excluded in every part.

This theory has almost entirely replaced the old doctrine of inspiration. There are now only a few theologians who have not adopted it in its main features. But this theory satisfies neither the church nor so-called science. The critical objections against Scripture concern not the periphery but the center of revelation itself. Others have therefore gone still further and denied all inspiration as a supernatural operation of God’s Spirit. The Bible is an accidental collection of human writings, even if written by men of deep religious disposition and arising among a people that may preeminently be called the people of religion. Revelation and inspiration can then be spoken of only in a figurative sense. At most a special guidance of God’s general providence can be discerned in the origin and collection of those writings. Inspiration differs only in degree from the religious inspiration in which all the pious share. Spinoza, _Tract. theol. polit._, cap. 12; Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ § 13; Strauss, _Glaub._ I 136 f.; Schweizer, _Glaub._ I 43 f., 179 f.; Biedermann, _Dogm._ §§ 179–208; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 39 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 179 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 78 f.

It is nevertheless remarkable that all these men continue to acknowledge to some extent the religious value of Scripture. They see in it not only a source for knowledge of Israel and earliest Christianity, but also seek to maintain it as a means of grace for cultivating religious-ethical life; cf. Bruining, _Theol. Tĳdschr._, Nov. 1894, pp. 587 f. To this extent they distinguish themselves favorably from all radicals who have settled accounts with Scripture entirely, shaken off all reverence for it, and have nothing but mockery and contempt for it. In the first centuries Celsus and Lucian were the spokesmen of this attitude. Toward the end of the Middle Ages the blasphemy of the _tres impostores_ gained currency. In the eighteenth century this hatred against Christianity was expressed by Voltaire, who from 1760 onward had no other name for Christianity than _l’infâme_ and from 1764 usually signed his letters with _écrasez l’infâme_. And in this century this enmity against Christ and his Word has still increased and expanded.
