## 6.

The Council of Trent declared in sess. 4 that the truth is contained in the written books and unwritten traditions which were received by the apostles from the mouth of Christ or handed down by the same apostles, _Spirito Sancto dictante_, as if from hand to hand, and have come to us.
Following the example of the fathers, it therefore accepts and venerates all the books of the Old and New Testaments, _cum utriusque unus Deus sit auctor_, and likewise the traditions _... tanquam vel oretenus a Christo, vel a Spiritu Sancto dictatas ..._.
Here inspiration was indeed extended to tradition, but it was nevertheless also clearly affirmed of Holy Scripture.
Among Roman Catholic theologians, however, great disagreement soon arose concerning the nature and scope of inspiration.
Both _auctor utriusque testamenti_ and _dictare_ were interpreted differently.
The theologians of the 16th century generally still adhered to the stricter tendency of the church fathers and scholastics.
Most were adherents of Augustine in the doctrine of grace—Jansenists, Augustinians, and Dominicans.
The principal representatives of this tendency are Melchior Canus, _Loci theol._ 1563; Bannez, _Comment. in primam partem D. Thomae_, Lugd., 1788; Baius and Jansenius; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae_ tom. 2, Wirceb., 1758; Rabaudy, _Exerc. de Scriptura sacra_; and in this century Fernandez, _diss. crit. theol. de verbali S. Bibl. inspiratione_; but also some Jesuits, such as Tostatus, Costerus, Turrianus, Salmeron, and Gregory of Valencia, _De rebus fidei h. t. controv._, Lugd., 1591, etc.
All of them teach a positive operation of God’s Spirit upon the writers that extended even to the _singula verba_.
But a laxer tendency soon arose, particularly among the Jesuits.
In 1586 Lessius and Hamelius opened their lectures at the Jesuit college in Leuven and defended there, among others, the propositions: 1° _ut aliquis sit S. Scriptura, non est necessarium, singula ejus verba inspirata esse a Sp. S°._; 2° _non est necessarium, ut singulae veritates et sententiae sint immediate a Sp. S°. ipsi scriptori inspiratae._; 3° _liber aliquis (qualis forte est secundus Machabaeorum) humana industria sine assistentia Sp. Sⁱ. scriptus, si Sp. Sᵘˢ postea testetur, ibi nihil esse falsum, efficitur Scriptura sacra._
Here verbal inspiration is rejected; the immediate inspiration of many matters—for example, historical matters that the writers knew—is deemed unnecessary; and for some books even what Bonfrerius later called _inspiratio subsequens_, or an _a posteriori_ approval by the Holy Spirit, is held to be sufficient.
The faculties of Leuven and Douai condemned the propositions, but others in turn rejected this censure, and the pope made no decision.
The first two propositions gained wide acceptance, but the third went too far and was adopted by only a few, including Bonfrerius, Frassenius, Richard Simon, _Histoire critique du N. T._, 1689, ch. 23, and, in this century, the bishop of Speyer, Haneberg, _Gesch. der bibl. Offenbarung_, 3e Aufl., 1863.
Another tendency, represented by Mariana, _Tract. varii_ VIII; Ed. du Pin, _Dissert. préliminaire_ tom. I, Paris, 1701; and J. Jahn, _Introductio in libros Vet. Test._, 1814, understands inspiration as a merely negative assistance of the Holy Spirit by which the writers were preserved from error.
Both these tendencies held fast to the infallibility of Holy Scripture but identified this factual outcome with the divine origin of Scripture.
By contrast, infallibility in matters that were not religious-ethical in the narrower sense was abandoned, and inspiration was restricted to what was properly dogmatic-ethical, by Erasmus, on Matt. 2 and Acts 10, and in _Apologia adv. monachos quosdam Hispanos_; Abbé Le Noir; Lenormant, _Les origines de l’histoire d’après la Bible_, Paris, 1880; de Broglie; Langen in Bonn; and Rohling, _Natur und Offenbarung_, 1872.
Related to this is the view of Holden, a doctor of the Sorbonne, in his _Divinae fidei analysis_, 1770, and of Chrismann, _Regula fidei catholicae_; they assumed inspiration proper for truths of faith and morals, but for the remaining content of Scripture taught only such assistance as all believers enjoy.
The view of the Roman Catholic Tübingen school has a similar tendency; under the influence of Schleiermacher, its representatives—Drey, _Apologetik_, 1838, S. 204 f.; Kuhn, _Einl. in die Dogmatik_, 1859, S. 9 f.; Schanz, _Apol. des Christ._ II 318 f., etc.—connected inspiration with the whole organism of revelation and allowed it to extend in differing degrees to the different parts of Scripture.
Most Roman Catholic theologians after the Reformation follow a middle path.
On the one hand, they reject the lax inspiration that would consist only in negative assistance or subsequent approval, for then every decree of the councils and everything true could be called inspired.
On the other hand, they also deny the strict _inspiratio verbalis_, according to which not only all matters but even all _singula verba_ were dictated and inspired, for many matters and words were known to the writers and therefore did not need to be inspired; the difference in language and style, the use of sources, etc., also prove the incorrectness of verbal inspiration.
An _inspiratio realis_ is therefore sufficient, sometimes taking the form of revelation proper and sometimes of assistance.
We find this theory in Bellarmine, _de Verbo Dei._ I c. 14; cf. _de Conc._ II c. 12; XII c. 14; C. a Lapide on 2 Tim. 3:16; _de Theologia Wirceburgensis_, disp. 1 cap. 1; Marchini, _de divinitate et canonicitate sacr. Bibl._ Pars 1 art. 7; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._ ed. 8, 1857, I p. 385 sq.; Perrone, _Praelect. Theol._ IX, 1843, p. 66 sq.; Heinrich, _Dogm._ I 382 f.; Franzelin, _Tractatus de div. Script._; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ I 50; H. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ II 108 f.; F. Schmid, _de inspirationis Bibl. vi et ratione_; Jansen, _Praelect. theol._ I 767 sq.
For more on these different theories of inspiration, see Perrone, ib. IX 58 sq.; Jansen, ib. 762 sq.; P. Dausch, _Die Schriftinspiration_, 1891, S. 145 f.
The _Vaticanum_ did not present a definite theory, but it did decisively condemn the theories of _inspiratio subsequens_ and _mera assistentia_ and, after repeating the Tridentine decree, declared that the church recognizes these books as holy and canonical not because _sola industria humana concinnati, sua_ (that is, the church’s) _deinde autoritate sint approbati_, nor because _revelationem sine errore contineant_, but because _Spiritu Sancto inspirante conscripti Deum habent auctorem, atque ut tales ipsi ecclesiae traditi sunt_.
In can. 2, 4 the council once more calls the books _divinitus inspiratos_.
In cap. 3 _de fide_ it says that _fide divina et catholica ea omnia credenda sunt, quae in verbo Dei scripto vel tradito continentur_.
This decree leaves nothing to be desired in clarity.
The Roman Catholic Church understands inspiration as a positive operation of God’s Spirit and holds fast to the infallibility of Scripture.
And Leo XIII’s encyclical _de studiis Scripturae sacrae_ of 18 Nov. 1893 was written in this same faith.
