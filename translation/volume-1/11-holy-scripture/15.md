## 15.

In what, then, does inspiration itself consist? Scripture sheds light on this when it repeatedly says that the Lord speaks through the prophets or through the mouth of his prophets. Of God the preposition ὑπο is used; he is the one speaking, the proper subject. But the prophets in speaking or writing are his organs; of them the preposition δια with the genitive is always used and never ὑπο, Matt. 1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 3:3; 4:14, etc.; Luke 1:70; Acts 1:16; 3:18; 4:25; 28:25. God, or the Holy Spirit, is the speaker proper, the one whose statement it is, the _auctor primarius_; and the writers are the organs through whom God speaks, the _auctores secundarii_, the _scriptores_ or _scribae_.

Further elucidation is supplied by 2 Pet. 1:19–21, where the origin of prophecy is sought not in the will of man but in the propulsion of God’s Spirit. The φερεσθαι—cf. Acts 27:15, 17, where the ship is driven by the wind—differs essentially from the ἀγεσθαι of the children of God, Rom. 8:14. The prophets were borne along, impelled by the Holy Spirit, and as a consequence spoke. The proclamation of the apostles is likewise called a speaking (ἐν) πνευματι ἁγιῳ, Matt. 10:20; John 14:26; 15:26; 16:7; 1 Cor. 2:10–13, 16; 7:40; 2 Cor. 2:17; 5:20; 13:3. Prophets and apostles are therefore θεοφορουμενοι; it is God who speaks in and through them.

Scripture itself, however, leads us to conceive this speaking of God through the mouth of the prophets as organically as possible. Here there is a distinction between the prophets and the apostles, and again among each of them mutually. Moses stands at the head of the prophets; God spoke with him as a friend with his friend. In Isaiah the propulsion of the Spirit bears a different character than in Ezekiel; Jeremiah’s prophecies differ by their simplicity and naturalness from those of Zechariah and Daniel. Among all the prophets of the Old Testament the propulsion of the Spirit is more or less transcendent; it comes to them from above and without, falls upon them, and works momentarily. Among the apostles, by contrast, the Holy Spirit dwells immanently in their hearts, leads and impels, illumines and instructs them. There is therefore also a very great difference in the organic character of inspiration.

Nevertheless, the whole of Scripture commands us to conceive inspiration not mechanically but organically. This is not because mechanical inspiration would in itself be impossible and impermissible or contrary to human dignity. If it is not unworthy of a child to believe its parents and teachers on authority and simply learn from them what it does not know; if it is not unworthy of a servant to receive commands from his master which he does not understand and merely has to carry out—what would be unworthy in man’s standing in such a relation to the Lord his God?

But God did not take this path. In revelation and inspiration he descended to man and accommodated himself to the peculiarities and even to the weaknesses of human nature. This too was a grace of the ἐνσαρκωσις. Just as the Logos did not seize upon a human being and unite him with himself, but entered into human nature and himself prepared and formed it through the Spirit by whom it was conceived, so the Spirit of the Lord also acted in inspiration. He entered into the prophets and apostles themselves and thus took and worked them into his service, so that they themselves investigated and thought, spoke and wrote. It is he who speaks through them; but at the same time it is they themselves who speak and write. They were borne along by the Spirit, but they themselves nevertheless spoke, ἐλαλησαν, 2 Pet. 1:20.

The Old Testament Scripture is frequently cited in the New Testament by the _auctor primarius_, Luke 1:70; Acts 1:16; 3:18; 4:25; 28:25, and always at Heb. 1:5 f.; 4:3; 5, etc.; but just as often by the _auctores secundarii_, Moses, David, Isaiah, and others, Matt. 13:14; 22:43; John 1:23, 46; 5:46; 12:38.

The moments of inspiration may not each be considered separately and in isolation, but stand in connection with everything preceding. The prophets and apostles were prepared and equipped for their task from youth. Their character, nature, inclination, understanding, development, and so forth, are not suppressed, but, as themselves already formed by the Spirit of the Lord, are now also taken into service and used by that same Spirit. Their entire person with all its gifts and powers is made serviceable to the calling to which they are called. Investigation, Luke 1:1; reflection and memory, John 14:26; use of sources, and so forth, are therefore not excluded by inspiration but incorporated into it.

Almost all books of the Old and New Testaments are therefore in a certain sense occasional writings. A direct command to write is mentioned in only a few texts; they by no means cover the entire content of Scripture. But those occasions that impelled writing also belong to the guidance of the Spirit; precisely through these he impelled them to write. The call to be prophet and apostle naturally and of itself included the call to speak and bear witness, Ex. 3; Ezek. 3; Amos 3:8; Acts 1:8, etc., but not the call to write. For many prophets and apostles did not write. A special command to write cannot be derived from Matt. 28:19. Writing is not mentioned among the _charismata_ in 1 Cor. 12, Bellarmine, _De verbo Dei_ IV, capp. 3–4.

But the Holy Spirit so guided the history of the church under Israel and in the New Testament that the deed had to pass over into the word and the word into writing. From this guidance the call to write, the _impulsus ad scribendum_, was born in prophets and apostles. And that writing is the highest, most powerful, most universal testimony, which does not vanish on the breath of the wind but _manet in aeternum_. Precisely because the writings of the prophets and apostles arose not outside but from and within history, there is a science in theology that investigates and makes known all those occasions and circumstances under which the biblical books arose.

When the prophets and apostles thus bear witness in writing, they also retain their own character, their own language and style. At all times this difference among the books of the Bible has been acknowledged, though not always satisfactorily explained. It cannot be explained by saying that the Holy Spirit, by sheer arbitrariness, wished to write now in this and then in another manner. Rather, in entering into the writers he also entered into their style and language, their character and distinctiveness, which he himself had already prepared and formed.

To this belongs also the fact that in the Old Testament he chooses Hebrew, and in the New Testament Hellenistic Greek, as the vehicle of divine thoughts. Here too there was no arbitrariness. Purism defended a precious truth in a clumsy manner. Measured by the Greek of Plato and Demosthenes, the New Testament is full of barbarisms and solecisms. But the marriage contracted in Hellenistic Greek between pure Hebrew and pure Attic, between the Eastern and Western spirit, was in the sphere of language the realization of the divine thought that salvation is from the Jews but is intended for all humanity. The language of the New Testament is not the most beautiful, considered grammatically and linguistically, but it is the most suitable for communicating the thoughts of God. In this respect too the Word became truly and universally human.

Finally, when the prophets and apostles wrote, their own experience and history repeatedly supplied the material for their writing. In the Psalms it is the pious singer who by turns laments and rejoices, sits bowed down in sorrow or exults with joy. In Rom. 7 Paul portrays for us his own life experience. Throughout Scripture it is repeatedly the persons of the writers themselves whose life and experience, hope and fear, faith and trust, complaint and misery are described and portrayed. That rich life, that deep experience of, for example, a David, was so formed and guided by the Spirit of the Lord that, taken up into Scripture, it would be for instruction to subsequent generations, that through patience and the consolation of the Scriptures we might have hope, Rom. 15:4.

Organic inspiration alone does justice to Scripture. In the doctrine of Scripture it is the elaboration and application of the central fact of revelation, the incarnation of the Word. The Λογος became σαρξ, and the Word became Scripture. These are two facts that not only run parallel but are also most intimately connected. Christ became flesh, a servant, without form or glory, the most despised among men. He descended into the lowest parts of the earth and became obedient unto the death of the cross. In the same way the Word, the revelation of God, entered into the creaturely, into the life and history of human beings and peoples, into all human forms of dream and vision, investigation and reflection, even into what is humanly weak, despised, and ignoble. The Word became Scripture and as Scripture subjected itself to the fate of all writing.

All this occurred so that the surpassing greatness of the power, including the power of Scripture, might be God’s and not from us. Just as every human thought and action is wholly the fruit of the action of God, in whom we live and have our being, and at the same time wholly the fruit of human activity, so Scripture too is entirely the product of the Spirit of God who speaks through prophets and apostles, and at the same time entirely the product of the activity of the writers.
