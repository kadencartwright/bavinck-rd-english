## 18.

Furthermore, the organic conception of inspiration furnishes many means for meeting the objections raised against it.
For it entails that, in causing the word of God to be written, the Holy Spirit disdained nothing human that could serve as an organ of the divine.
God’s revelation is not abstractly supernatural but has entered into the human—in persons and circumstances, in forms and customs, in history and life.
It does not remain hovering high above us but has descended into our condition; it has become flesh and blood and has been made like us in all things except sin.
It now forms an ineradicable element of this cosmos in which we live and continues its renewing and restoring work within it.
The human has become an organ of the divine, the natural a revelation of the supernatural, the visible a sign and seal of the invisible.
In inspiration use was made of all the gifts and powers that lie within human nature.
This makes entirely understandable, first, the differences in language and style, in character and individuality, that may be observed in the biblical books.
Formerly this difference was explained from the will of the Holy Spirit, and a deeper account was lacking.
But on the organic view of inspiration this difference is entirely natural.
The organic conception does not exclude but incorporates the use of sources, the writers’ familiarity with earlier writings, their own investigation, memory, reflection, and life experience.
The Holy Spirit himself prepared his _scriptores_ in this way; he did not suddenly descend upon them from above but employed their entire personality as his instrument.
Here too the dictum applies: _gratia non tollit sed perficit naturam_.
The personality of the writers was not effaced but maintained and sanctified.
Inspiration therefore in no way requires that, in literary or aesthetic terms, we place the style of Amos on a par with that of Isaiah, or that we deny all barbarisms and solecisms in the language of the New Testament.
In the second place, the organic conception of revelation and inspiration entails that ordinary human and natural life is not excluded but is itself made serviceable to the thoughts of God.
Scripture is the word of God; it does not merely contain it but is it.
The formal and material elements in this expression, however, may not be separated from each other.
Inspiration alone and by itself would not yet make a writing the word of God in the scriptural sense.
Even if a book on geography, for example, were entirely inspired and dictated word for word in the most literal sense, it would not thereby be theopneustic in the sense of 2 Tim. 3:16.
Scripture is the word of God because in it the Holy Spirit testifies to Christ, because it has the Λογος ἐνσαρκος as its subject matter and content.
Form and content interpenetrate each other and cannot be separated.
But in order to paint this full-length portrait of Christ before our eyes, human sin and the satanic lie must also be depicted in all their horror.
In a painting shadow is necessary to make the light stand out all the more brightly.
Sin in biblical saints too must be called sin, and error may not be excused even in them.
And while the revelation of God in Christ thus takes unrighteousness into itself as the antithesis, it does not disdain what is humanly weak and natural either.
Christ regarded nothing human as alien to himself, and Scripture does not forget even the smallest concerns of daily life, 2 Tim. 4:13.
The Christian does not stand in antithesis to the human; it is the latter’s restoration and renewal.
In the third place, Scripture’s intention and purpose are most intimately connected with its content.
Whatever was written beforehand was written for our instruction.
It serves for teaching, reproof, correction, and instruction in righteousness, so that the man of God may be complete, fully equipped for every good work.
It serves to make us wise unto salvation.
Holy Scripture has a thoroughly religious-ethical purpose.
It does not wish to be a textbook for the various sciences.
It is the _principium_ of theology alone and demands that we read and study it _theologically_.
In all the disciplines that cluster around Scripture, our concern must be the saving knowledge of God.
For this Scripture offers us all the data.
In that sense it is wholly sufficient and perfect.
Whoever, however, wishes to derive from Scripture a history of Israel, a biography of Jesus, a history of Israelite or early Christian literature, and so forth, repeatedly finds himself disappointed.
There are then gaps that can be filled only by conjecture.
Historical criticism has completely forgotten this purpose of Scripture.
It seeks to furnish a history of Israel’s people, religion, and literature, and approaches Scripture _a priori_ with demands that it cannot satisfy.
It encounters contradictions that cannot be resolved, divides sources and books endlessly, and arranges and orders them quite differently, with no result except the emergence of hopeless confusion.
No life of Jesus can be constructed from the four Gospels, and no history of Israel from the Old Testament.
The Holy Spirit did not intend that.
Inspiration did not consist in notarial recording.
The harmonization of the Gospel narratives has failed; cf. Dieckhoff, _Die Inspiration und Irrthumslosigkeit der H. Schrift_, Leipzig 1891. Id. _Noch einmal über die Insp. u. Irrth. der H. S._, Rostock 1893. Herzog² art. “Evangelienharmonien” and “Synopse.”
Scripture does not meet the demands of exact knowledge such as we require in _mathesis_, astronomy, chemistry, and so forth.
Such a standard may not be applied to it.
That is why the _autographa_ too have been lost.
That is why the text, however slightly, is corrupt; that is why the church—and indeed not the laity alone—possesses Scripture only in an imperfect and fallible translation.
These are facts that cannot be denied.
And they teach us that Scripture has its own standard, calls for its own manner of interpretation, and has its own purpose.
That purpose is none other than to make us wise unto salvation.
The Old Testament is not a source for a history of Israel’s people and religion but is indeed a source for a _historia revelationis_.
The Gospels are not a source for a life of Jesus but are indeed a source for a dogmatic knowledge of his person and work.
Scripture is the book for the Christian religion and for Christian theology.
To this end it was given.
For this it is suited.
And therefore it is the word of God, granted to us through the Holy Spirit.
