## 10.

This canon of the Old Testament possessed divine authority for Jesus and the apostles, just as it did for their contemporaries. This is clear from the following data: a) The formula by which the Old Testament is cited in the New varies, but it always proves that for the writers of the New Testament the Old Testament is of divine origin and bears divine authority. Jesus sometimes cites a passage from the Old Testament with the name of the writer—for example, Moses, Matt. 8:4; 19:8; Mark 7:10; John 5:45; 7:22; Isaiah, Matt. 15:7; Mark 13:14; David, Matt. 22:43; Daniel, Matt. 24:15—but he also frequently cites with the formula “it is written,” Matt. 4:4f.; 11:10; Luke 10:26; John 6:45; 8:47, or “Scripture says,” Matt. 21:42; Luke 4:21; John 7:38; 10:35, or also according to the _auctor primarius_, that is, God or the Holy Spirit, Matt. 15:4; 22:43, 45; 24:15; Mark 12:26. The Evangelists frequently use the expression “what was spoken through the prophet,” Matt. 1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 3:3; etc., or through the Lord or the Holy Spirit, Matt. 1:22; 2:15; Luke 1:70; Acts 1:16; 3:18; 4:25; 28:25. John usually cites according to the _auctor secundarius_, ch. 1:23, 46; 12:38. Paul always speaks of Scripture, Rom. 4:3; 9:17; 10:11; 11:2; Gal. 4:30; 1 Tim. 5:18; etc., which is sometimes even represented as wholly personal, Gal. 3:8, 22; 4:30; Rom. 9:17. The Epistle to the Hebrews usually names God or the Holy Spirit as _auctor primarius_, 1:5f.; 3:7; 4:3, 5; 5:6; 7:21; 8:5, 8; 10:16, 30; 12:26; 13:5. This manner of citation teaches clearly and plainly that for Jesus and the apostles the Scripture of the Old Testament was indeed composed of different parts and came from different writers, but nevertheless formed one organic whole that had God himself as its author. b) This divine authority of Old Testament Scripture is also repeatedly stated and taught decisively by Jesus and the apostles, Matt. 5:17; Luke 16:17, 29; John 10:35; Rom. 15:4; 1 Pet. 1:10-12; 2 Pet. 1:19, 21; 2 Tim. 3:16. Scripture is a unity that can be broken and destroyed neither in its entirety nor in its parts. In the last-cited text, the translation “every God-breathed Scripture is also profitable” is burdened by the objection that the predicate ἐστιν could then not have been omitted after ὠφελιμος, Hofmann, _Weiss. u. Erf._ I 43. The translation “every scripture, quite in general, is God-breathed and profitable” is excluded by the nature of the case. There remains, therefore, only a choice between the two translations: “all Scripture,” or “every Scripture”—namely, that comprehended in τα ἱερα γραμματα, v. 15—“is God-breathed.” In substance this makes no difference, and in view of passages such as Matt. 2:3; Acts 2:36; 2 Cor. 12:12; Eph. 1:8; 2:21; 3:15; Col. 4:12; 1 Pet. 1:15; Jas. 1:2, πας without the article nevertheless also appears capable of meaning “whole.” c) Jesus and the apostles never take a critical stance toward the content of the Old Testament but accept it wholly and without reservation. In all its parts—not only in its religious-ethical statements or in those passages in which God himself speaks, but also in its historical components—Old Testament Scripture is unconditionally acknowledged by them as true and divine. Jesus regards, for example, Isa. 54 as coming from Isaiah, Matt. 13:14; Ps. 110 from David, Matt. 22:43; the prophecy cited in Matt. 24:15 from Daniel; and he ascribes the law to Moses, John 5:46. The historical narratives of the Old Testament are repeatedly cited and unconditionally believed—for example, the creation of the human being, Matt. 19:4, 5; Abel’s murder, Matt. 22:35; the flood, Matt. 24:37-39; the history of the patriarchs, Matt. 22:32; John 8:56; the destruction of Sodom, Matt. 11:23; Luke 17:28-33; the burning bush, Luke 20:37; the serpent in the wilderness, John 3:14; the manna, John 6:32; the history of Elijah, Luke 4:25, 26; Naaman, ib.; Jonah, Matt. 12:39-41; etc. d) Dogmatically, for Jesus and the apostles the Old Testament is _sedes doctrinae, fons solutionum, πασης ἀντιλογιας περας_. The Old Testament is fulfilled in the New. It is repeatedly represented as though everything happened for the purpose of fulfilling Holy Scripture, _ἱνα πληρωθῃ το ρηθεν_, Matt. 1:22 and _passim_; Mark 14:49; 15:28; Luke 4:2; 24:44; John 13:18; 17:12; 19:24, 36; Acts 1:16; Jas. 2:23; etc. This fulfillment is noted down to small details, Matt. 21:16; Luke 4:21; 22:37; John 15:25; 17:12; 19:28; etc.; everything that happened to Jesus was described beforehand in the Old Testament, Luke 18:31-33. Jesus and the apostles repeatedly justify their conduct and prove their doctrine by an appeal to the Old Testament, Matt. 12:3; 22:32; John 10:34; Rom. 4; Gal. 3; 1 Cor. 15; etc. And for them this divine authority of Scripture extends so far that even a single word, indeed a tittle and a jot, is covered by it, Matt. 5:17; 22:45; Luke 16:17; John 10:35; Gal. 3:16. e) Nevertheless, the Old Testament is generally cited in the New Testament according to the Greek translation of the LXX. The writers of the New Testament, writing in Greek and for Greek readers, generally used the translation familiar and accessible to them. The citations can be divided into three groups according to their relation to the Hebrew text and to the Greek translation. In some texts there is divergence from the LXX and agreement with the Hebrew text—for example, Matt. 2:15, 18; 8:17; 12:18-21; 27:46; John 19:37; Rom. 10:15, 16; 11:9; 1 Cor. 3:19; 15:54. In others there is, conversely, agreement with the LXX and divergence from the Hebrew—for example, Matt. 15:8, 9; Acts 7:14; 15:16, 17; Eph. 4:8; Heb. 10:5; 11:21; 12:6. In a third group of citations there is a more or less significant divergence from both the LXX and the Hebrew text—for example, Matt. 2:6; 3:3; 26:31; John 12:15; 13:18; Rom. 10:6-9; 1 Cor. 2:9. It also deserves notice that some books of the Old Testament—namely, Ezra, Nehemiah, Obadiah, Nahum, Zephaniah, Esther, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Songs—are never cited in the New Testament; that no apocryphal books are indeed cited, yet in 2 Tim. 3:8; Heb. 11:34f.; Jude 9f., 14f., names and facts are mentioned that do not occur in the Old Testament; and that on a few occasions Greek classics are also cited, Acts 17:18; 1 Cor. 15:33; Titus 1:12. f) Finally, as regards the material use of the Old Testament in the New Testament, here too there is great diversity. Sometimes the citations serve as proof and confirmation of some truth—for example, Matt. 4:4, 7, 10; 9:13; 19:5; 22:32; John 10:34; Acts 15:16; 23:5; Rom. 1:17; 3:10f.; 4:3, 7; 9:7, 12, 13, 15, 17; 10:5; Gal. 3:10; 4:30; 1 Cor. 9:9; 10:26; 2 Cor. 6:17. Very frequently the Old Testament is cited to prove that it _had_ to be fulfilled in the New Testament and has been fulfilled, whether in the literal sense, Matt. 1:23; 3:3; 4:15, 16; 8:17; 12:18; 13:14, 15; 21:42; 27:46; Mark 15:28; Luke 4:17f.; John 12:38; Acts 2:17; 3:22; 7:37; 8:32; etc., or in the typical sense, Matt. 11:14; 12:39f.; 17:11; Luke 1:17; John 3:14; 19:36; 1 Cor. 5:7; 10:4; 2 Cor. 6:16; Gal. 3:13; 4:21; Heb. 2:6-8; 7:1-10; etc. Often the citations from the Old Testament serve simply for clarification, explanation, admonition, consolation, and so forth—for example, Luke 2:23; John 7:38; Acts 7:3, 42; Rom. 8:36; 1 Cor. 2:16; 10:7; 2 Cor. 4:13; 8:15; 13:1; Heb. 12:5; 13:15; 1 Pet. 1:16, 24, 25; 2:9. In that use we are often surprised by the sense that the New Testament writers find in the text of the Old Testament, especially in Matt. 2:15, 18, 23; 21:5; 22:32; 26:31; 27:9, 10, 35; John 19:37; Acts 1:20; 2:31; 1 Cor. 9:9; Gal. 3:16; 4:22f.; Eph. 4:8f.; Heb. 2:6-8; 10:5. This exegesis of the Old Testament in the New Testament presupposes in Jesus and the apostles the thought that a word or sentence can have a much deeper meaning and a much wider scope than its writer suspects or has placed within it. This is also frequently the case with classical writers. No one will suppose that, when Goethe wrote down his classical poetry, he had in mind everything now found in it. Hamerling clearly expressed this in his _Epilog an die Kritiker_, _Poet. Werke_, Tiel, Campagne I 142f. In Scripture this is the case in a much stronger measure, since, according to the conviction of Jesus and his apostles, it has the Holy Spirit as _auctor primarius_ and bears a teleological character; cf. also Valeton, _Theol. Stud._ 1887, issue 6. Theremin, _Die Beredsamkeit eine Tugend_ S. 236. Not only in those few passages mentioned above, but in its entire conception and interpretation of the Old Testament, the New Testament is governed by the thought that the Israelite has its fulfillment in the Christian. The entire economy of the Old Covenant, with all its institutions and ordinances and in its entire history, points forward to the dispensation of the New Covenant. Not Talmudism but Christianity is the rightful heir of the treasures of salvation promised to Abraham and his seed.
