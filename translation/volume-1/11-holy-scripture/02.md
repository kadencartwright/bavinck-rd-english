## 2.

The bearer of humanity’s ideal goods is language, and the σαρξ of language is writing. God attaches himself to this as well in revelation. In order fully to enter into humanity and become entirely its possession, revelation assumes the μορφη, the σχημα, of Scripture. Scripture is the servant form of revelation. Indeed, the central fact of revelation—namely, the incarnation—leads toward Scripture. In prophecy and miracle revelation descends so low and so deeply that it does not disdain as a means even the lowest forms of human life, specifically of religious life. The Logos himself becomes not merely ἀνθρωπος but δουλος, σαρξ. In the same way the word of revelation assumes the imperfect, defective form of writing.

But only in this way does revelation become the good of humanity. The goal of revelation is not Christ; Christ is center and means. The goal is that God may dwell once again in his creatures and reveal his glory in the cosmos: Θεος τα παντα ἐν πασιν. This too is in a certain sense an ἐνανθρωπησις του θεου, an incarnation of God. And in order to attain that goal, the word of revelation passes over into writing. Scripture too is therefore means and instrument, not goal. It flows from the incarnation of God in Christ, is in a certain sense its continuation, the way along which Christ makes his dwelling in his church: the _praeparatio viae ad plenam inhabitationem Dei_. But in this indwelling of God it also has its τελος, its end and goal, 1 Cor. 15:28. Like the whole of revelation, it too is an _actus transiens_.

This makes clear the relation in which Scripture stands to revelation. Earlier theology caused revelation to be almost entirely absorbed into theopneusty, into the gift of Scripture. It mentioned revelation only in passing and conceived it far too narrowly. It seemed as though nothing lay behind that Scripture. Scripture came to stand entirely detached and isolated. It was not rooted in history. It was almost as if it had suddenly fallen from heaven. The mighty conception of revelation as a history that begins at the fall and ends only in the parousia was almost entirely foreign to it.

This view is untenable. For in the great majority of cases revelation precedes theopneusty and is often separated from it by a long period of time. God’s revelation to the patriarchs, in the history of Israel, and in the person of Christ was sometimes described only centuries and years later. Prophets and apostles too often committed their revelations to writing only a considerable time after receiving them—for example, Jer. 25:13; 30:1; 36:2 f. Further, many persons such as Elijah, Elisha, Thomas, Nathanael, and others were organs of revelation who nevertheless never wrote a book included in the canon. Others, by contrast, received no revelations and performed no miracles, yet did record them in writing, as did, for example, the writers of many historical books.

Further, revelation took place in various forms—dream, vision, and so forth—and intended to make known something that was hidden. Theopneusty was always an operation of God’s Spirit in consciousness and had as its goal the guarantee of the content of Scripture. Modern theology therefore rightly distinguished between revelation and Scripture. But it often fell into another extreme. It detached Scripture so completely from revelation that Scripture became nothing more than an incidental appendage, an arbitrary addition, a human record of revelation that might perhaps still be useful but in any case was not necessary.

This theme has been sung in all manner of variations. Not the letter but the Spirit; not Scripture but the person of Christ; not the word but the fact is the _principium_ of theology. And Lessing came to the well-known plea: “O Luther! You great and holy man, you delivered us from the yoke of the pope—but who will deliver us from the yoke of the letter, from the paper pope?” This view is no less wrong and still more dangerous than the other. For revelation and theopneusty in many cases entirely coincide. By no means everything described in Scripture was revealed beforehand; much arose in consciousness during the act of writing itself—for example, in the Psalms, the epistles, and so forth. Whoever denies theopneusty and despises Scripture also loses a very large portion of revelation; he retains nothing but human writings.

Further, even where revelation in fact or word preceded its description, it is known to us solely and exclusively through Holy Scripture. We know literally nothing of God’s revelations among Israel and in Christ except from Holy Scripture alone. There is no other _principium_. With Holy Scripture, therefore, the whole of revelation falls, and the person of Christ as well. Precisely because revelation is history, there is no other way to know anything about it than the ordinary way for all history—that is, testimony. Testimony decides for our consciousness concerning the reality of a fact. There is no fellowship with Christ except through fellowship with the word of the apostles, John 17:20, 21; 1 John 1:3. For us, for the church of all ages, revelation exists only in the form of Holy Scripture.

Finally, as will appear later, theopneusty is a property of the writings, a distinct and separate act of God in the production of Scripture, and thus to that extent is itself to be acknowledged and honored as an act of revelation. Contempt for or rejection of Scripture is therefore not an innocent action with respect to human testimonies concerning revelation, but denial of a special act of revelation by God, and thus in principle denial of all revelation.

Both tendencies are therefore one-sided: both the one that misconceives revelation for the benefit of Scripture and the one that misconceives Scripture for the benefit of revelation. There φανερωσις fails to receive its due; here θεοπνευστια. There one has Scripture without writings; here writings without Scripture. There history is neglected; here the word is despised. The first tendency lapses into orthodox intellectualism; the second runs the danger of Anabaptist spiritualism.

The correct view is that Scripture is neither identified with revelation nor detached from it and placed outside it. Theopneusty is an element _in_ revelation, a final act in which God’s revelation in Christ is concluded for this dispensation; thus, to that extent, the end, crown, perpetuation, and publication of revelation: _medium, quo revelatio immediata mediata facta inque libros relata est_, Baumgarten in Twesten, _Vorles. über die Dogm._ I 402.
