g) The prophets do not derive their revelation from the law. Although the extent of the Torah cannot be determined from their writings, prophecy nevertheless presupposes a Torah. All the prophets stand on the foundation of a law; they place themselves with their opponents on a common basis. They all presuppose a covenant made by God with Israel and a gracious election of Israel, Hos. 1:1-3; 6:7; 8:3; Jer. 11:6 f.; 14:21; 22:9; 31:31 f.; Ezek. 16:8 f.; Isa. 54:10; 56:4, 6; 59:21. The prophets were not the creators of a new religion, an ethical monotheism, Kuenen, _Prof._ II 335 f. The relation of YHWH to Israel was never like that of Chemosh to Moab, Kuenen, _Godsd. van Israel_ I 222. The prophets never speak of such an opposition between their religion and that of the people. They acknowledge that through almost every century the people made themselves guilty of idolatry, but they always and unanimously regard this as unfaithfulness and apostasy; they proceed from the assumption that the people knew better. Together with the people they are rooted in the same revelation and the same history. They speak from the conviction that they and the people share the same service of God, that YHWH has chosen and called them to his service. In this they find their strength, and therefore they measure the people by the relation that rightfully exists between them and YHWH, Hos. 12:14; Mic. 6:4, 8; Isa. 63:11; Jer. 7:25, etc.; König, _Die Hauptprobleme der altisr. Religionsgeschichte_ 1884, S. 15 f., 38 f. Torah designates not only instruction from God in general but is also repeatedly the name for the already existing, objective revelation of YHWH, Isa. 2:3; Mic. 4:2; Amos 2:4; Hos. 8:1; 4:6; Jer. 18:18; Ezek. 7:26; Zeph. 3:4. God’s covenant with Israel, on whose foundation the prophets stand together with the whole people, naturally also includes all kinds of statutes and judgments, and the prophets therefore repeatedly speak of commandments, Isa. 48:18; Jer. 8:13; statutes, Isa. 24:5; Jer. 44:10, 23; Ezek. 5:6, 7; 11:12, 20; 18:9, 17; 20:11 f.; 36:27; 37:24; Amos 2:4; Zech. 1:6; Mal. 3:7; 4:4; and judgments, Ezek. 5:7; 11:12, etc. This Torah must have contained the doctrine of the unity of YHWH, of his creation and government of all things, the prohibition of idolatry, and other religious and moral commandments, as well as all kinds of ceremonial elements (Sabbath, sacrifice, purity, and so forth) and historical elements (creation, the exodus from Egypt, the making of the covenant, and so forth). Though there may be disagreement concerning the extent of the Torah before prophetism, the relation of law and prophets cannot be reversed without coming into conflict with the whole history of Israel and the nature of prophetism. The prophet among Israel was, as it were, _die lebendige Stimme des Gesetzes und der Vermittler seiner Erfüllung_ (Staudenmaier). The most negative criticism finds itself compelled still to accept as historical the person of Moses and his monotheism, the sojourn in Egypt, the exodus, the conquest of Canaan, and so forth, although its criticism of the Pentateuch leaves it no basis for doing so; cf., e.g., Smend, _Lehrb. der altt. Rel._, S. 13 f.

h) It is _a priori_ probable that among a people long acquainted with the art of writing (Herzog² 13, 689 f.), the law too would long have existed in written form. Hos. 8:12 also appears to state this, Bredenkamp, _Gesetz und Proph._ 21 f.; König, _Der Offenbarungsbegriff_ II 333 f. This Torah possessed authority among Israel from the beginning. Nothing is known of doubt or opposition. Moses occupied an entirely unique place among all the prophets, Exod. 33:11; Num. 12:6-8; Deut. 18:18; Ps. 103:7; 106:23; Isa. 63:11; Jer. 15:1, etc. He stood in a special relation to YHWH; the Lord spoke with him as a friend with his friend. He was the Mediator of the Old Testament. Everywhere the law ascribes to itself a divine origin. It is YHWH who gave Israel the Torah through Moses. Not only the Ten Words, Exod. 20, and the Book of the Covenant, Exod. 21-23, but all the other laws as well are derived from God’s speaking to Moses. At every turn the formula occurs in the laws of the Pentateuch: the Lord said or spoke to Moses. Almost every chapter begins with it, Exod. 25:1; 30:11, 17, 22; 31:1; 32:9, etc.; Lev. 1:1; 4:1; 6:1, etc.; Num. 1:1; 2:1; 3:44; 4:1, etc. And Deuteronomy intends to give nothing except what Moses spoke to the children of Israel, Deut. 1:6; 2:1, 2, 17; 3:2; 5:2; 6:1, etc.

i) The historical books of the Old Testament were all written by prophets and in a prophetic spirit, 1 Chron. 29:29; 2 Chron. 9:29; 20:34, etc. In their addresses and writings the prophets not only repeatedly refer to Israel’s history, but they are also the ones who preserved, edited, and transmitted it to us. Yet in doing so they by no means intend to furnish us with a faithful and continuous account of the fortunes of the Israelite people, as other historians aim to do. In the historical books of the Old Testament too, the prophets place themselves on the foundation of the Torah and view and describe the history of Israel from its standpoint, Judg. 2:6-3:6; 2 Kings 17:7-23, 34-41. The historical books are the commentary in facts on God’s covenant with Israel. They are not history in our sense but prophecy and wish to be judged by a different standard from the historical books of other peoples. Their purpose is not that we should obtain exact knowledge of Israel’s history but that in the history of Israel we should understand God’s revelation, his thought and counsel. The prophets are always proclaimers of the Word of YHWH, both when they look backward into history and when they look ahead into the future.

j) Finally, as for the poetic books in the narrower sense that were included in the canon, all of them, like the other writings of the Old Testament, bear a religious-ethical character. They presuppose the revelation of God as their objective foundation and show the effect and application of that revelation in the various conditions and relations of human life. Ecclesiastes depicts the vanity of the world without and over against the fear of the Lord. Job concerns itself with the problem of God’s justice and the suffering of the devout. Proverbs portrays true wisdom for us in its application to the rich diversity of human life. The Song of Songs celebrates the intimacy and power of love. And the Psalms show us God’s manifold grace in the mirror of the experiences of the devout. Lyrical and didactic poetry among Israel enters the service of God’s revelation. According to 2 Sam. 23:1-3, David, the sweet psalmist of Israel, spoke by the Spirit of YHWH, and YHWH’s word was on his tongue.

k) As the various writings of the Old Testament arose and became known, they were also acknowledged as authoritative. The laws of YHWH were deposited in the sanctuary, Exod. 25:22; 38:21; 40:20; Deut. 31:9, 26; Josh. 24:25 f.; 1 Sam. 10:25. The poetic productions were preserved, Deut. 31:19; Josh. 10:13; 2 Sam. 1:18; the Psalms were collected early for the sake of the _cultus_, Ps. 72:20; and the men of Hezekiah already made a second collection of Proverbs, Prov. 25:1. The prophecies were read frequently; Ezekiel knows Isaiah and Jeremiah, and later prophets appeal to those who preceded them. Daniel, cap. 9:2, already knows a collection of prophetic writings to which Jeremiah also belonged. In the postexilic community the authority of the law and the prophecies is established, as is clearly evident from Ezra, Haggai, and Zechariah. Jesus ben Sira holds the law and the prophets in very high esteem, cap. 15:1-8; 24:23; 39:1 f.; cap. 44-49. In the preface his grandson mentions three parts into which Scripture is divided. The LXX contains various apocryphal writings, but these themselves testify to the authority of the canonical books, 1 Macc. 2:50; 2 Macc. 6:23; Wis. 11:1; 18:4; Bar. 2:28; Tob. 1:6; 14:7; Sir. 1:5; 17:12; 24:23; 39:1; 46:15; 48:25, etc. Philo cites only canonical books. The fourth book of Ezra, cap. 14:18-47, knows the division into 24 books. Josephus, _C. Ap._ 1, 8, counts 22 books in three parts. _Omnium consensu_, the Old Testament canon of Philo and Josephus was identical with ours, G. Wildeboer, _Het ontstaan van den Kanon des O. V._ 1889, p. 126 f., 134; Strack in Herzog² 7, 429.
