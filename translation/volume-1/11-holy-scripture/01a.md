# § 11. Holy Scripture

### A. Revelation and Scripture

## 1.

The history of religions indicates not only an intimate connection between religion and revelation but also, further, between revelation and writing. Magical formulas, liturgical texts, ritual treatises, ceremonial laws, priestly documents, historical and mythological literature, and so forth, occur in the religious sphere among all civilized peoples. But in a still narrower sense the religions also have sacred scriptures. Many peoples possess a book or collection of books that has divine authority and serves as a rule for doctrine and life. Among these are the Shu-king of the Chinese, the Veda of the Indians, the Tripitaka of the Buddhists, the Avesta of the Persians, the Quran of the Muslims, the Old Testament of the Jews, and the Bible of the Christians. Together these seven were designated “book religions” by Max Müller, _Vorlesungen über den Ursprung der Religion_ 1880 S. 149. This phenomenon by itself already indicates that revelation and writing cannot stand in an accidental, arbitrary relation. Even the history of the doctrine of the Quran offers remarkable parallels with that of the dogma of Scripture in the Christian church, M. Th. Houtsma, _De strĳd over het dogma in den Islâm tot op El-Ash’ari_, Leiden 1875 p. 96f. See further on the sacred books Ch. de la Saussaye, _Lehrb. der Rel. Gesch._ I 137f. Lamers, _Wetenschap v. d. godsd._ II 249f. Max Müller, _Theosophy or psychological religion_ 1893. Lecture 2: _The value of the sacred books._

Now thought and word, thinking and speaking, are first of all most intimately related to one another. They are not, as Max Müller, _Vorlesungen über die Wissenschaft der Sprache_ I, 3e Aufl. 1875 S. 459, _Das Denken im Lichte der Sprache_, Leipzig 1888 S. 70-115, believes, identical; for there is also a thinking, a consciousness, an awareness, however unclear, without a word. _Homo enim nihil potest dicere, quod non etiam sentire possit; potest etiam aliquid sentire, quod dicere non possit_, Augustine, _Serm._ 117, c. 5. Yet only the word is the fully formed thought, the thought that has attained independence and full maturity and is therefore also bright and clear; it is an indispensable aid to conscious thinking. Language is the soul of the nation, the guardian of the goods and treasures of humanity, the bond between persons, peoples, and generations, the one great tradition that makes the human world, one in _physis_, also one in consciousness. But just as thought is embodied in the word, so in turn the word is embodied in writing. Language too is nothing but an organism of signs, but of audible signs. And the audible sign naturally seeks stability in the visible sign, in writing. Writing is properly the art of signs and occurs in this broad sense among all peoples, but it gradually developed from sign-writing and pictography, through word-writing or ideography, to alphabetic writing. However refined and increased in precision it may be, it is imperfect. Our thought, says Augustine, _de trin._ 7, 4, _de doctr. christ._ 1, 6, falls short of the thing, and our speech falls short of our thought; and so too there is a great distance between word and writing. Sounds are always reproduced in visible signs only approximately. Thinking is richer than speaking, and speaking richer than writing. Yet writing is of great value and significance. Writing is the perpetuated, universalized, and eternalized word. It makes thought the possession of those who are far from us and who come after us, the common property of humanity. It portrays the word and speaks to the eyes. It gives body and color to thought. Writing is the ἐνσαρκωσις of the word.

This holds in general. The Traditionalists—de Bonald, Lamennais, and Bautain—certainly went too far when they said that language came directly from God, that all treasures of truth were preserved in language, and that the human being now came to partake of all truth from and through language, tradition, Stöckl, _Lehrb. der neueren Philos._ I 406f. Yet there is a good thought in this. And especially in the religious sphere, word and writing acquire a higher significance. Revelation in Christianity is a history. It consists in acts, events, that pass away and soon belong to the past. It is an _actus transiens_, temporal, even momentary, and shares this temporal form, this transience, with all earthly things. Yet it contains eternal thoughts that had significance not only for the moment in which revelation took place and for the persons to whom it came, but that are of value for all times and for all people. How can these seemingly contradictory things be reconciled? For the strangeness of this has almost always been felt. Deism in England called attention to it. Herbert of Cherbury declared that credence could be given only to a revelation that came to us ourselves immediately; revelations received by others are for us only history, tradition, and in history we can never advance beyond probability, _de veritate_ 1656 p. 288, Lechler, _Gesch. des engl. Deismus_ 49. Hobbes likewise said that a revelation received by others could not be proved to us, _Leviathan_ ch. 32. Locke made the same distinction between _original and traditional revelation_, _Essay concerning human understanding_ IV ch. 18. Deism inferred from this that _religio naturalis_ was sufficient. It separated fact and idea, the temporal and the eternal, _zufällige Geschichtswahrheiten_ and _nothwendige Vernunftwahrheiten_. And Hegel’s speculative rationalism followed the same path: the idea does not pour itself out into a single individual.
