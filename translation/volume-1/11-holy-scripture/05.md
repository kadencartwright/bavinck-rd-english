## 5.

The theology of the Middle Ages remained with the church fathers and did not develop the doctrine of inspiration any further. John of Damascus, _de fide orthod._ 4, 17, touches only briefly on Scripture and says that the law and the prophets, the evangelists and apostles, the pastors and teachers spoke through the Holy Spirit; Scripture is therefore theopneustic. Erigena, _de div. nat._ I 66 sq., says that in all matters the authority of Holy Scripture must be followed, for _vera auctoritas rectae rationi non obsistit_, but also _omnis auctoritas, quae vera ratione non approbatur, infirma videtur esse_. Thomas treats the doctrine of Holy Scripture no more than Lombard does, but nevertheless gives his thought on inspiration in his doctrine of prophecy, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 171 sq. Prophecy is specifically a gift of the understanding and consists first in _inspiratio_, that is, in an _elevatio mentis ad percipienda divina_, which occurs _Spiritu Sancto movente_, and second in _revelatio_, by which divine things are known, darkness and ignorance are removed, and prophecy itself is completed, qu. 171 art. 1. More particularly, prophecy consists in the gift of the _lumen propheticum_, by which divine things become visible just as natural things do through the natural light of reason, ib. art. 2. But this revelation varies: sometimes it occurs through the mediation of the senses, sometimes by means of the imagination, and sometimes also in a purely spiritual manner, as with Solomon and the apostles, ib. qu. 173 art. 2. Prophecy _per intellectualem visionem_ generally stands higher than prophecy _per imaginariam visionem_; but if the _lumen intellectuale_ does not reveal supernatural things but only causes things naturally knowable to be known and judged in a divine manner, then such a _prophetia intellectualis_ stands below the _visio imaginaria_ that reveals supernatural truth. The writers of the _hagiographa_ often wrote about matters that are knowable by nature, and they then spoke _non quasi ex persona Dei, sed ex persona propria, cum adjutorio tamen divini luminis_. Thomas therefore acknowledges different modes and degrees of inspiration. He also says, qu. 176 art. 1, that the apostles received the gift of languages so that they could preach the gospel to all nations, _sed quantum ad quaedam quae superadduntur humana arte ad ornatum et elegantiam locutionis, apostolus instructus erat in propria lingua, non autem in aliena_; and likewise the apostles were sufficiently equipped with knowledge for their office but did not know everything there is to know—for example, arithmetic and so forth. But error or untruth cannot occur in Scripture, _S. Theol._ I qu. 32 art. 4. II 2 qu. 110 art. 4 ad 3. Scripture is treated most extensively by Bonaventure in the prooemium to his _Breviloquium_, ed. Freiburg 1881 p. 1-32: Holy Scripture has its origin not in human investigation but in the revelation of the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit. No one can know it except through faith, for Christ is its content. It is _cor Dei, os Dei_ (of the Father), _lingua Dei_ (of the Son), _calamus Dei_ (of the Holy Spirit). Four things about Scripture especially come into consideration: its _latitudo_: it contains many parts, the Old and New Testaments, different kinds of books—legal, historical, prophetic, and so forth; its _longitudo_: it describes all times from creation to the day of judgment in the three periods of _lex naturae, lex scripta_, and _lex gratiae_, or in seven _aetates_; its _sublimitas_: it describes the different hierarchies, _ecclesiastica, angelica, divina_; its _profunditas_: it has a _multiplicitas mysticarum intelligentiarum_. However much Holy Scripture may use different modes of speech, it is always genuine; there is nothing false in it. For the Holy Spirit, _ejus auctor perfectissimus nihil potuit dicere falsum, nihil superfluum, nihil diminutum_. Reading and investigating Holy Scripture are therefore so urgently necessary; and for this purpose Bonaventure wrote his precious _Breviloquium_. Duns Scotus, in the _Prologus_ to his _Sententiae_, does indeed offer various grounds on which faith in Holy Scripture rests, such as prophecy, internal harmony, authenticity, miracles, and so forth, but he does not treat the doctrine of Holy Scripture. And elsewhere too we find little material for the dogma of Scripture in Scholasticism. No need was felt for a separate treatment of the _locus de S. Scr._, since its authority stood firm and no one disputed it. In the Middle Ages it had, at least formally, an uncontested dominion. It was represented symbolically as the water of life, glorified in encomiums, venerated and worshiped like the image of Christ, copied out, illustrated, bound, and displayed in the most costly manner. It had a place of honor at the councils, was preserved as a relic, worn around the neck as an amulet, buried with the dead, and used as the basis for taking oaths. And it was also read, studied, expounded, and translated far more than the Protestants later believed, Vigouroux, _Les livres saints et la critique rationaliste_, 3e ed. I 226 s. Janssen, _Gesch. des deutschen Volkes_ I 48f. Herzog² 3, 545f. There was no opposition to Scripture. Abelard too, _Sic et Non_, ed. Henke et Lindenkohl, Marb. 1851 p. 10.11, does not say that the prophets and apostles erred in writing, but only that they sometimes erred as persons, appealing to Gregory, who had acknowledged this of Peter as well; the _gratia prophetiae_ was sometimes taken from them so that they would remain humble and would acknowledge that they received and possessed only as a gift that Spirit of God _qui mentiri vel falli nescit_. Nor is Agobard of Lyon an opponent of inspiration; against Fredegis of Tours he merely advocates a more organic conception that acknowledges differences of language and style, grammatical deviations, and so forth, Münscher-v. Coelln, _Dogm. Gesch._ II 1 S. 105. Ecclesiastically, the inspiration and authority of Holy Scripture were repeatedly declared and acknowledged, Denzinger, _Enchir._ n. 296. 386. 367. 600.
