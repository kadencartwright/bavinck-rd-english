## 12.

The result of this investigation into Scripture’s teaching concerning itself may be summarized in this: it regards and presents itself as the word of God.
The expression “word of God” or “word of the Lord” has various meanings in Scripture.
It frequently designates the power of God by which he creates and sustains all things, Gen. 1:3; Ps. 33:6, 147:17, 18, 148:18; Rom. 4:17; Heb. 1:3, 11:3.
Next, the special revelation by which God makes something known to the prophets is so called.
In the Old Testament the expression occurs in this sense on almost every page; again and again it says there, “The word of the Lord came.”
In the New Testament we find it in this sense only in John 10:35; the word now no longer comes to pass or descends upon the prophets from above and from without on isolated occasions: it has come to pass in Christ and abides.
Further, “word of God” means the content of revelation; then Scripture speaks of the word or words of God alongside judgments, laws, commandments, and statutes that were given to Israel, Exod. 9:20, 21; Judg. 3:20; Ps. 33:4, 119:9, 16, 17, etc.; Isa. 40:8; Rom. 3:2, etc.
In the New Testament the gospel that was revealed by God in Christ and proclaimed by the apostles is so called, Luke 5:1; John 3:34, 5:24, 6:63, 17:8, 14, 17; Acts 8:25, 13:7; 1 Thess. 2:13, etc.
It is not improbable, furthermore, that on a single occasion in Scripture the term “word of God” is used to designate the written law, and thus a part of Scripture, Ps. 119:11, 105.
Schultz, _Grundriss der ev. Dogm._ 4 f.
No such passage can be identified in the New Testament.
Nor is “word of God” in Heb. 4:12 equivalent to Scripture.
Yet in fact the New Testament regards the books of the Old Testament as nothing other than the word of God.
God, or the Holy Spirit, is the _auctor primarius_, who spoke through (δια c. gen.) the prophets in Scripture, Acts 1:16, 28:25.
Scripture is so called, then, both because of its origin and because of its content.
The formal and material senses of the expression are most intimately connected in Scripture.
Finally, the name “word of God” is used for Christ himself.
He is the Logos in an utterly unique sense, _revelator_ and _revelatio_ at once.
All revelations of God, all words of God, in nature and history, in creation and re-creation, under the Old and New Testaments, have their ground, unity, and center in him.
He is the sun; the other words of God are his rays.
The word of God in nature, among Israel, in the New Testament, and in Scripture may not for a moment be detached from him or conceived apart from him.
There is revelation of God only because he is the Logos.
He is the _principium cognoscendi_, in a general sense, of all science; and in a special sense, as Λογος ἐνσαρκος, of all knowledge of God, of religion and theology, Matt. 11:27.
