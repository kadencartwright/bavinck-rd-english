### E. Objections to Inspiration

## 17.

Many very serious objections are brought against this inspiration of Scripture. They are drawn from historical criticism, which contests the authenticity and credibility of many biblical books; from the internal contradictions that repeatedly occur in Scripture; from the manner in which the Old Testament is quoted and explained in the New; from secular history, with which the narratives of Scripture often cannot be reconciled; from nature, which in both its origin and its present existence is said to contradict Scripture’s account of creation and miracles; from religion and morality, which often pass an unfavorable judgment on the faith and life of biblical persons; from Scripture’s present form, since textual criticism shows that its _autographa_ are lost, its _apographa_ corrupt, and its translations defective; and so forth. It is a vain attempt to explain these objections away and act as though they did not exist. Yet in the first place attention must be drawn to the ethical significance of the struggle waged against Scripture through all the centuries. If Scripture is the Word of God, that struggle is not accidental but necessary and also entirely explicable. Because Scripture is the description of God’s revelation in Christ, it must awaken the same opposition as Christ himself. He came into the world for a κρισις and was appointed for the fall and rising of many. He brings division between light and darkness and reveals the thoughts of many hearts. Scripture likewise is a living and powerful word, a judge of the thoughts and deliberations of the heart. It was not only inspired; it remains theopneustic. Just as the act of inspiration is preceded by much—the whole activity of the Holy Spirit in nature, history, revelation, and regeneration—so it is also followed by much. Inspiration does not stand by itself. After the act of inspiration the Holy Spirit does not withdraw from Holy Scripture or abandon it to its fate, but sustains and animates it and brings its content in all manner of forms to humanity, to its heart and conscience. Through Scripture as the Word of God, the Holy Spirit engages in a continual struggle against the thoughts and deliberations of the ψυχικος ἀνθρωπος. It need therefore cause not the least surprise that Scripture has at all times encountered contradiction and opposition. Christ bore a cross, and a servant is not greater than his lord. Scripture is the handmaid of Christ. It shares in his reproach. It awakens the hostility of sinful man.

Not all opposition to Scripture can, of course, be explained from this. Yet the attacks to which Scripture is exposed in this century may not be viewed in isolation. They are undoubtedly connected with the whole spiritual tendency of this century. It is not ours to judge persons and intentions, but it would be superficial to maintain that the struggle against Scripture in this century stood entirely by itself, was governed by altogether different and much purer motives than in earlier centuries, and that now only the head had a voice while the heart remained wholly uninvolved. Every believer experiences that in the best moments of his life he also stands strongest in faith in Scripture; his confidence in Scripture increases with his faith in Christ, and conversely _ignoratio Scripturarum_ is naturally and in the same measure an _ignoratio Christi_ (Jerome). The connection between sin and error often lies deep beneath the surface of conscious life. In others it can almost never be demonstrated, but sometimes it is disclosed to one’s own soul’s eye. The struggle against Scripture is in the first place a manifestation of the hostility of the human heart. But that hostility can express itself in different ways. It appears by no means only, and perhaps not even most strongly, in the criticism to which Scripture is subjected in our time. Scripture as the Word of God encounters resistance and unbelief in every natural man. In the days of dead orthodoxy, unbelief in Scripture was in principle just as powerful as it is in our historical and critical age. The forms change, but the essence remains one. Whether hostility to Scripture expresses itself in a criticism such as that of Celsus and Porphyry or reveals itself in a dead faith, the hostility is in principle the same. For not the hearers but the doers of the Word are pronounced blessed. The servant who knew his lord’s will and neither prepared himself nor acted according to that will shall be beaten with many blows.

It therefore remains the duty of every person first of all to put off this hostility toward the Word of God and take every thought captive to the obedience of Christ. Scripture itself appears everywhere with this demand. Only the pure in heart will see God. Regeneration enables one to see the kingdom of God. Self-denial is the condition for being Jesus’ disciple. The wisdom of the world is foolishness with God. Scripture occupies so exalted a place over against every person that, instead of submitting to his criticism, it rather judges him in all his thoughts and desires. And this has at all times been the standpoint of the Christian church toward Scripture. According to Chrysostom, humility was the foundation of philosophy. Augustine said: _quemadmodum rhetor ille rogatus, quid primum esset in eloquentiae praeceptis, respondit: pronuntiationem; quid secundum, pronuntiationem; quid tertium, pronuntiationem; ita si me interroges de religionis Christianae, primo, secundo et tertio semper respondere liberet: humilitatem._ Calvin, _Inst._ II 2, 11, cites this with approval. And Pascal, _Pensées_, Art. 8, cries out to man: _humiliez-vous, raison impuissante, taisez-vous, nature imbécile..... écoutez Dieu!_ Thus the church has stood toward Scripture in every age. And the Christian dogmatician may take no other position. For a dogma does not rest on the results of any historical-critical investigation but solely on God’s testimony, on Holy Scripture’s self-testimony. A Christian believes not because everything discloses God’s love but despite everything that awakens doubt. In Scripture too much remains that awakens doubt. All believers can speak of this from experience. The practitioners of biblical criticism often represent matters as though the ordinary congregation knew nothing of the objections brought against Scripture and felt nothing of the difficulty of continuing to believe Scripture. But that is an inaccurate representation. To be sure, ordinary Christians do not know the obstacles that scholarship places in the way of faith in Scripture. Yet to a greater or lesser degree they know the struggle waged in both head and heart against Scripture. There is not a single believer who has not learned in his own way to know the opposition between the σοφια του κοσμου and the μωρια του θεου. It is one and the same, ever-continuing struggle that all Christians, learned or unlearned, must wage in order to keep their thoughts captive under the obedience of Christ. No one rises above that struggle here on earth. Throughout the whole domain of faith there remain _cruces_ that must be overcome. There is no faith without struggle. To believe is to struggle, to struggle against the appearance of things. As long as anyone still believes anything, his faith is contested from every side. The modern believer is not delivered from this either. Concessions weaken but do not liberate. Thus enough objections remain even for one who submits to Scripture with childlike faith. These need not be concealed. There are _cruces_ in Scripture that cannot be explained away and will probably never be resolved. But these difficulties that Holy Scripture itself presents to us over against its inspiration were for the most part not newly discovered in this century; they have been noticed at all times, and nevertheless Jesus and the apostles, Athanasius and Augustine, Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventure, Luther and Calvin, and all Christians of all churches through all the centuries have confessed and acknowledged Scripture as the Word of God. Whoever wishes to wait to believe Scripture until all objections have been removed and all contradictions reconciled will never come to faith. What a person sees, why would he still hope for it? Jesus pronounces blessed those who have not seen and yet have believed. Moreover, there are objections and difficulties in every science. Whoever will not begin with faith will never come to knowledge. _Erkenntnisstheorie_ is the principle of philosophy, but it is a mystery from beginning to end. Whoever refuses to undertake scientific investigation until he sees the road prepared along which we come to knowledge will never begin. Whoever refuses to eat before he understands the whole process by which food comes to him will starve to death; and whoever refuses to believe the Word of God before he sees all difficulties resolved will perish from spiritual want. “Understanding will not do it; grasp what remains uncomprehended” (Beets). Nature, history, and every science offer as many _cruces_ as Holy Scripture. Nature contains so many riddles that it can often make us doubt the existence of a wise and righteous God. There are ἐναντιοφανη in abundance on every page of the book of nature. There is an inexplicable remainder (Schelling) that mocks every explanation. Who therefore gives up faith in the providence of God that extends over all things? Islam and the life and destiny of uncivilized peoples constitute a _crux_ in the history of humanity just as great and difficult as the composition of the Pentateuch and the Synoptics. Who therefore doubts that God also writes that book of nature and history with his almighty hand? Of course, here, as also with Scripture, one can throw oneself into the arms of agnosticism and pessimism. But despair is a _salto mortale_ in the scientific domain as well. With unbelief the mysteries of being do not decrease but increase. And the unrest of the heart becomes greater.
