d) Among the apostles Paul again occupies a position of his own. He sees himself called to defend his apostolate against the Judaizers, Gal. 1–2; 1 Cor. 1:10–4:21; 2 Cor. 10–13. Over against that opposition he maintains that he was set apart from his mother’s womb, Gal. 1:14; was called as an apostle by Jesus himself, Gal. 1:1; personally saw Jesus himself, 1 Cor. 9:1; 15:8; was favored with revelations and visions, 2 Cor. 12; Acts 26:16; received his gospel from Jesus himself, Gal. 1:12; 1 Tim. 1:12; Eph. 3:2–8; and is therefore, just as much as the other apostles, an independent and reliable witness, especially among the pagans, Acts 26:16. His apostolate too was confirmed with miracles and signs, 1 Cor. 12:10, 28; Rom. 12:4–8; 15:18, 19; 2 Cor. 11:23 f.; Gal. 3:5; Heb. 2:4, and with spiritual blessing, 1 Cor. 15:10; 2 Cor. 11:5, etc.

He is therefore conscious that there is no other gospel than his, Gal. 1:7; that he is faithful, 1 Cor. 7:25; has the Spirit of God, 1 Cor. 7:40; that Christ speaks through him, 2 Cor. 13:3; 1 Cor. 2:10, 16; 2 Cor. 2:17; 5:23; that he proclaims God’s Word, 2 Cor. 2:17; 1 Thess. 2:13, even down to the expressions and words, 1 Cor. 2:4, 10–13; and not only when he speaks but also when he writes, 1 Thess. 5:27; Col. 4:16; 2 Thess. 2:15; 3:14. Like the other apostles Paul repeatedly appears with apostolic commission, 1 Cor. 5; 2 Cor. 2:9, and gives binding commands, 1 Cor. 7:40; 1 Thess. 4:2, 11; 2 Thess. 3:6–14.

He indeed appeals on one occasion to the judgment of the church, 1 Cor. 10:15, but not to subject his statement to its approval or disapproval. On the contrary, he does so to be vindicated by the conscience and judgment of the church, which itself also has the Spirit of God and the anointing of the Holy One, 1 John 2:20. So little does Paul thereby make himself dependent on the judgment of the church that at 1 Cor. 14:37 he says that if anyone thinks himself a prophet and to have the Spirit, this will be evident precisely in his acknowledgment that what Paul writes are the Lord’s commandments.

e) These writings of the apostles possessed authority from the outset in the churches where they were known. They were quickly circulated and thereby acquired ever more extensive authority, Acts 15:22 f.; Col. 4:16. The Synoptic Gospels display such great affinity that one must have been wholly or partly known to another. Jude is known to Peter, and 2 Pet. 3:16 already knows many letters of Paul and places them on one line with the other Scriptures.

Gradually New Testament writings came to be read aloud in the church, Justin Martyr, _Apol._ 1:67. These must already have existed in the first half of the second century, Papias in Eusebius, _H. E._ 3:39; Justin Martyr, _Apol._ 1:66, 67. A dogmatic use is already made of them by Athenagoras, _De resurr._ c. 16, who there proves his argument with 1 Cor. 15:33; 2 Cor. 5:10. And Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ 3:4, cites texts from Paul with the formula διδασκει, κελευει ὁ θειος λογος.

Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ 3, 11; Tertullian, _Ad Prax._ 15, and others; the Peshitta; and the Muratorian Fragment establish beyond all doubt that in the second half of the second century most writings of the New Testament possessed canonical authority and enjoyed equal dignity with the books of the Old Testament. Disagreement remained concerning some books—James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John—Eusebius, _H. E._ 3:25. But the objections against these _antilegomena_ steadily diminished in the third century. The Synod of Laodicea in 360, of Hippo Regius in 393, and of Carthage in 397 could therefore include these _antilegomena_ too and close the canon.

These decisions of the church were no arbitrary and authoritarian act, but only codification and registration of the right that had long existed concerning these writings in the churches. The canon was not formed by a decision of councils. _Canon non uno quod dicunt actu ab hominibus, sed paulatim a Deo animorum temporumque rectore, productus est_, Loescher in Herzog² 7, 424.

In the important dispute between Harnack and Zahn over the history of the New Testament canon, Harnack undoubtedly places too one-sided an emphasis upon the concepts of divinity, infallibility, inspiration, canon, and upon the formal establishment of the dogma of the New Testament Scripture. Long before this occurred in the second half of the second century, the New Testament writings had attained universally acknowledged authority through the authority of the apostles, public reading in the church, and so forth. Zahn very rightly calls attention to this internal process. Compare, on this dispute, Köppel, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1891, 1es Heft, and Barth, _Neue Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1893, 1es Heft.

f) It cannot be determined with certainty what principles guided the church, under both the Old and New Testaments, in this acknowledgment of the canonicity of the Old and New Testament writings. Apostolic origin cannot have been decisive, for Mark, Luke, and Hebrews were also included. Nor is acknowledgment of canonicity grounded in the fact that no other writings concerning Christ existed, for Luke 1:1 mentions many others, and according to Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ 1:20, there was an ἀμυθητον πληθος ἀποκρυφων και νοθων γραφων. The principle of canon formation cannot lie in size and importance, for 2 and 3 John are very small; nor in the acquaintance of the writers, Mark and Luke, with the apostles, for the letters of Clement and Barnabas were not included; nor in originality, for Matthew, Mark, and Luke; Ephesians and Colossians; Jude and 2 Peter are dependent on one another.

Nothing else can be said than that the acknowledgment of these writings took place of itself in all the churches, without any agreement. With a few exceptions, the Old and New Testament writings were accepted immediately, from the time of their origin, in their entirety, without a word of doubt or protest, as holy, divine Scriptures. The place and time where authority was first accorded to them cannot be indicated. The canonicity of the biblical books is rooted in their existence. They possess authority of themselves, _jure suo_, because they exist. It is the Spirit of the Lord who guided in their writing and brought them to acknowledgment in the church. Harnack, _D. G._ I 304 f., 318 f.; Wildeboer, _Het ontstaan v. d. Kanon des O. V._ 107 f.; Reuss, _Gesch. des N. T._ § 298 f.; Herzog², art. “Kanon”; W. Lee, _The Inspiration of holy Scripture_, 3e ed., Dublin, 1864, p. 43.
