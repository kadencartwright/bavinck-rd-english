# § 17. Faith

## 1.

From the preceding investigation it has become clear to us that the _principium internum_ of Christian religion and theology cannot lie in the understanding, reason, or heart of the psychical man. Neither proofs nor reasonings nor agreement with man’s needs are capable of convincing us of the divinity of religious truth. In every domain, and therefore also in religion, the _principium internum_ must answer to the _principium externum_; it must be of the same nature. Just as light can be perceived only by the eye, so divine truth can be acknowledged only by one who is akin to God and bears his image. All the tendencies named above have sensed this to a greater or lesser degree. Even those who wished to prove the truth of the Christian religion intellectually nevertheless judged that a certain moral disposition was necessary on man’s part in order to feel the force of those proofs. In the first part of his apology Pascal attempted to bring man to a knowledge of his misery and to awaken in him the need for redemption and peace. Through and after Schleiermacher most theologians came to the insight that religion has a nature of its own and must therefore also be known through its own organ in man. The attempt to find that organ in reason, conscience, heart, or feeling was already a step on the right road and deserved to be preferred by far over the rationalistic effort to demonstrate religious truth to the sober understanding. But even this did not yet go far enough. If there were no sin, man would possess within himself the capacity to distinguish truth from falsehood as clearly as the eye distinguishes light from darkness. But no one can maintain that this is the case. Sin has darkened man in his understanding and corrupted him in his will. The impure of heart cannot see God. It is therefore necessary that objectively the truth meet us in its purity and that subjectively our eye be opened to that truth. Scripture proceeds from this. It does not submit itself to the judgment of the natural man, for only the spiritual man, the regenerate person, can discern the things of the Spirit of God and of the kingdom of heaven. Scripture therefore reserves to itself the right and power to bring man to acknowledgment of its truth. It does this in a spiritual manner, not by proofs and reasonings but along the path of a spiritual experience, through the instruction of the Holy Spirit. There is therefore not only a revelation of God in Christ outside us but also an illumination of the Holy Spirit within us; after God has spoken to us through the Son, the Holy Spirit has come to lead us into the truth. God gave not only a Scripture that perfectly contains objective revelation but also founded a church that understands the Word of God and confesses it as truth. For this reason it also conflicts with the essence of the Christian religion to make its truth depend on the investigation and judgment of sinful man. The dogmatician must take his standpoint not before or outside faith but in the midst of it. The methodological doubt of Bacon and Descartes has no place in either philosophy or theology. Doubt may at most provide the occasion to seek the truth; faith alone causes it to be found. Faith is always the deepest ground of scientific labor, Snellen, _Bijblad v. d. Herv._, Feb. 26, 1895, p. 5. Voetius therefore rightly took the field against Descartes’s _theologia dubitans_, _Disp. Sel._ III 825–870; A. C. Duker, _Schoolgezag en eigen onderzoek_, 1861, p. 192 f. It is a mistaken method when the philosopher divests himself of his natural certainty, and the dogmatician of his faith, before beginning scientific work. Such a method is not only impractical but also creates the appearance that the scientist’s conviction and faith actually rest on those grounds which his thought later causes him to find. Descartes found a fixed point of departure in the _cogito ergo sum_. But no one will think that by this he named the final and deepest ground of belief in his own existence. Even if the incorrectness of this thesis were demonstrated to him, he would continue to believe in his own existence just as firmly as before. Just as little as philosophy may set natural certainty aside can the dogmatician take his position outside the faith in which he has often been raised from childhood. Science must not create its object but explain it. Christian theology therefore has a subjective starting point. It takes its standpoint in the congregation that believes and confesses the truth of God. There is no other standpoint. Rome indeed seeks one but cannot find it. The Reformation also abandoned the search for one; it proceeded from the believing subject. And when the theology of rationalism and supernaturalism had surrendered this standpoint, modern theology through and after Schleiermacher returned to this Reformation principle in almost all its tendencies. The whole of theology became ethical, in the sense that it took seriously the proposition that only the regenerate person sees the kingdom of God and that only one who wills to do God’s will can recognize whether Jesus’s teaching is from God. Almost all theologians now take their standpoint in believing consciousness, in regeneration, in religious experience. In all this there is a remarkable agreement. The charge of subjectivism against this standpoint is entirely misplaced. Nowhere, in any science, is there another point of departure. Light presupposes the eye; sound is perceptible only to the ear. Everything objective exists for us only through the mediation of subjective consciousness. Without consciousness the entire world is dead to me. To desire an objectivity that is not subjectively mediated is an impossible demand. Perception always stands between the being of things and our own being. Theology therefore shares its subjective starting point with all science, indeed with all relations that exist between man and world. The charge of subjectivism becomes legitimate only when the subjective organ, which is indispensable for perceiving what exists objectively, is elevated to the _principium_ of knowledge. The eye may be an indispensable organ for the perception of light, but it is nevertheless not the source of light. It is precisely the error of idealistic rationalism that it identifies the organ with the source of knowledge, above, p. 149. Thought is not the source of what is thought, representation is not the cause of the thing, the ego is not the creator of the non-ego; and so neither can faith, regeneration, or experience be the source of our religious knowledge or the principle of our theology. However right modern theology is to take its standpoint in the subject, it errs when, under the influence of idealistic philosophy, it therefore changes theology into anthropology, ecclesiology, or the science of religion. To that extent Ritschl was right to recall the theology of consciousness, which had arisen generally after Schleiermacher, to the objective, historical revelation in Christ.
