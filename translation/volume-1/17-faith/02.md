## 2.

Most often in Scripture, at least in the New Testament, this _principium internum_ is designated by the name faith. Regeneration, purity of heart, love for God’s will, and the Spirit of God are also presented in Scripture as the _principium internum_, Matt. 5:8; John 3:3, 5; 7:17; 1 Cor. 2:12; etc. Nevertheless, the name faith is preferable for designating this principle. This is so not only because faith comes most prominently to the fore as such in Scripture, but especially because the concept of faith places us in the domain of consciousness and at the same time preserves the connection with the way in which we arrive at knowledge in other domains. We saw earlier that human reason is never and nowhere the source of knowledge. There are no innate concepts. The human being is physically, and likewise intellectually, entirely dependent on the world outside him. All knowledge comes from without. But from the human side all that knowledge is mediated by consciousness. Not feeling or the heart, but the head—consciousness in its entire compass (sensation, awareness, perception, understanding, reason, conscience)—is the subjective organ of truth. When Scripture calls faith the _principium internum_, it thereby endorses the same conception and acknowledges that even God’s revelation can come to our knowledge only through consciousness. This is not to say that regeneration, love, and purity of heart are not of the greatest significance in this connection; the contrary will become evident later. But the proper organ through which God’s revelation is known is an act of consciousness—namely, faith. God’s revelation is a system of God’s words and deeds that exist outside and independently of us. How could these ever come to our knowledge except through consciousness? God’s revelation is gospel, is promise—the promise of forgiveness and salvation; but on our part nothing other than faith can answer to a promise. Only through faith does a promise become our possession. Faith is therefore the _principium internum cognoscendi_ of revelation, and thus also of religion and theology, Hartmann, _Religionsphilos._ II 65 f.

There is still another reason why it is preferable to designate the _principium internum_ by the name faith. For in this way the connection of religious knowing with all other human knowing is preserved. There is certainly a difference, but attention must nevertheless be given first of all to the agreement. We arrive at knowledge in religion and theology in no other way than in the other sciences. Faith is not a new organ implanted in the human being, not a sixth sense, not a _donum superadditum_. However much it may conflict with the “natural” human being, it is nevertheless perfectly natural, normal, human. Revelation connects with nature objectively and subjectively, re-creation with creation. Believing in general is a very ordinary way of arriving at knowledge and certainty. In every domain we begin by believing. Our natural inclination is to believe. Acquired knowledge and experience alone teach us incredulity, Hoekstra, _Bronnen en grondslagen van het godsd. geloof_, 1864, p. 383. Faith is the basis of society and the foundation of science. All certainty ultimately rests in faith. The word “believe” is indeed also used in a weaker sense, when we do not know something with certainty but nevertheless regard it as probable on grounds sufficient for us. Believing then stands in contrast to knowing and is akin to opining. It occurs in this sense in the expression, “I do not know it, but I believe it.” But the use of the word is by no means exhausted by this meaning. Etymology already indicates that another, deeper meaning lies hidden in faith. The Dutch word _geloof_ derives from the same root as _loven_ (“praise”), _verloven_ (“betroth”), _beloven_ (“promise”), _veroorloven_ (“permit”), _oorlof_ (“leave”), _lof_ (“praise”), and _gelofte_ (“vow”); it is related to _lieven_ (“love”) and expresses trusting in someone with one’s whole person, a loving surrender and dedication to someone, Kahnis, _Luth. Dogm._, 2e Ausg., I 106; Hoekstra, _Wijsg. Godsd._ I 82 f.; cf. _Woordenboek der Ned. Taal_, s.v. In other languages too the word faith has this original meaning of dedication, trust, and certainty, as in Hebrew האמין‎, ‏אמונה; Greek πειθω, πιστις; Latin _fido, fides_; French _foi_; and English _faith_ alongside _belief_, etc. The verb “to believe” is accordingly often used for all such certain knowledge as rests not on proofs but on immediate, direct insight. Plato distinguished ἐπιστημη, διανοια, πιστις, and εἰκασια and understood by πιστις certainty concerning the sensible world on the ground of perception, _Rep._ VI p. 511, VII p. 534; cf. Zeller, _Philos. der Griechen_, 4e Aufl., II 591, 593; Siebeck, _Gesch. der Psych._ S. 209. He therefore says, _Tim._ p. 29 c.: ὁ τι γαρ προς γενεσιν οὐσια, τουτο προς πιστιν ἀληθεια. Aristotle distinguished between knowledge obtained by way of demonstration and knowledge of the _principia_ derived from the νους itself. He does not call the latter by the name faith, but he nevertheless says, _Eth. Nicom._ VI 3: ὁταν γαρ πως πιστευῃ και γνωριμοι αὐτῳ ὠσιν αἱ ἀρχαι, ἐπισταται. When afterward in Christianity the word faith acquired such a profound religious significance, many church fathers took particular pleasure in pointing to the important place believing occupies in life and science. Clement of Alexandria frequently understands by πιστις all immediate knowledge and certainty, and then says that there is no science without faith, that the first _principia_, including, for example, the existence of God, are not proved but believed, _Strom._ II n. 4, 6; cf. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ II 470; Strauss, _Glaub._ I 301 f. Augustine especially brought to light this significance of faith for society and science. One who does not believe never comes to know: _nisi credideritis, non intelligetis_, _De trin._ 15, 2, and passim. Faith is the foundation and bond of all human society. If the proposition _quod non video, credere non debeo_ were to prevail, all bonds of blood, friendship, and love would be broken. _Si ergo non credentibus nobis quae videre non possumus, ipsa humana societas, concordia pereunte, non stabit; quanto magis fides, quamvis quae non videntur, rebus est adhibenda divinis?_ _De fide rerum invisibilium_, cap. 3; cf. _De util. cred._ c. 10 sq.; _Conf._ 6, 5; cf. Ritter, _Gesch. der christl. Philos._ II 252 f.; Gangauf, _Metaph. Psych. des Aug._, 1852, S. 52 f. Since then these same thoughts have repeatedly returned among Christian theologians, for example, in more recent times in Dorner, _Glaub._ I 3; Lange, _Dogm._ I 342 f.; Oosterzee, _voor Kerk en Theol._ I 94; Kuyper, _Encycl._ II 71 f.; etc. The name faith has thus been given to the immediate knowledge of the _principia_; to trust in ourselves, in our perception and our thinking; to the acknowledgment of the objective existence of the external world; to the mutual trust on which all human society is built; to everything known and done by intuition. In such faith Schiller saw the guarantee of the existence of that New World which Columbus sought: _Wär’ sie noch nicht, sie stieg’ jetzt aus den Fluthen empor._

No one who thinks matters through will deny this profound significance of πιστις for life, art, and science. Over against those who think that nothing may be held as true which cannot be perceived by the senses or mathematically proved, it stands beyond all doubt that by far the greater and more important part of what we know rests not on proofs but on immediate certainty. The domain of the latter is much greater than that of demonstrative certainty. And demonstrative certainty is always built in turn upon immediate certainty, and stands or falls with it. The way of arriving at knowledge and certainty in a manner other than by mathematical and logical proofs is therefore nothing foreign to human nature. Faith in the general, broad sense is an indispensable element in society and a normal path to science. It did not first become necessary through sin; apart from sin, intuitive knowledge and immediate certainty would have occupied an even broader place in human life. Even now, a person’s originality is greater to the extent that he lives not by reflection but by intuition. All the examples mentioned above can therefore render excellent service as analogies of religious faith. They share this with religious faith: knowledge is obtained immediately, not by reflection, and in certainty it is in no way inferior to knowledge that rests on proofs. They moreover preserve the connection that exists between the ordinary way in which we human beings arrive at knowledge and the way of faith that leads to certainty in the religious domain. They show that believing in itself is so little in conflict with human nature and the demand of science that without it there can be no question of a normal human being or a normal science.
