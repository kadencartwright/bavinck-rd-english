## 6.

Science, and philosophy in particular, has generally paid little attention to this certainty of faith. Kant was the first to adopt and acknowledge it in a modified sense. Kant accepted, in essence, three kinds of certainty. The first is empirical, problematic certainty, which rests on one’s own or another’s perception and consists in opining, in a theoretical or practical believing. Next there is logical, scientific, apodictic certainty, which is itself twofold and bears either an intuitive character, as in mathematics, or a discursive character, as in philosophy. These are the same kinds of certainty that had already been accepted in Greek philosophy. But alongside these Kant now also makes room for moral, assertoric certainty. According to Kant, the supersensible and supernatural are unknowable. God deliberately withheld knowledge of them from us so that we would place man’s destiny not in knowing but in acting, in the fulfillment of his moral calling. In view of this ethical destiny, man accepts the truth of certain propositions without which he cannot fulfill this ethical task. Thus on practical, psychological grounds he believes in the existence of God, the soul, and immortality. This is moral faith. The certainty man obtains through this faith is not theoretical in nature but practical, moral. It causes him to say not “I am certain,” nor “it is morally certain,” but “I am morally certain.” There are thus three kinds of certainty—empirical, logical, and moral—expressed and represented by the words opining, knowing, and believing, _Kritik der reinen Vernunft_, _Methodenlehre_ II 3, ed. Kirchmann, p. 632 f. Philosophy after Kant did indeed adopt this doctrine of certainty in part but added nothing essentially new to it. Cf. Dr. Franz Grung, _Das Problem der Gewissheit_, Heidelberg, 1886.

Kant’s doctrine of moral certainty, by contrast, exercised great influence on theology. When the authority of Scripture and church had been undermined, people sought in it the foundation of religion and theology. The familiar text John 7:17, already used by Kant himself, _Rel. innerhalb der Gr._, etc., ed. Rosenkranz, p. 135, became the point of departure for this tendency. There is indeed a profound truth in this moral certainty, and it may gratefully be acknowledged that Kant made room for it in his philosophy. We have an entirely different certainty concerning invisible things than concerning those we can perceive with our senses or prove with our thought. Faith in things hoped for and unseen also does not bypass the will, the moral disposition, and spiritual experience. Nevertheless, it is not advisable to exchange the certainty of faith of Holy Scripture, the congregation, and Christian theology for Kant’s moral certainty. First, it is not superfluous to observe that certainty is always a state of consciousness. With respect to a question or proposition the human spirit can exist in different states; it can stand over against it in a state of uncertainty, doubt, supposition, opinion, and so forth, but also in a state of absolute certainty. Certainty is the repose of human consciousness in truth found and acknowledged. The understanding strives for knowledge, for truth. This is the character and nature of the understanding; truth is its good, its wealth, the fulfillment of its need. When it finds that truth it is therefore satisfied; it rests in it and feels safe and certain. Certainty is rest, peace, joy, blessedness—_in veritate requies_. Certainty is the normal state of the human spirit, as health is that of the body. Doubt and uncertainty, by contrast, are unrest, discord, misery. Strictly speaking, then, certainty itself is not moral; it is called moral only because the grounds on which the truth rests in it are moral in nature. Next, the grounds on which scientific and moral truth rest cannot be separated from each other and set over against each other as theoretical and practical. The dualism that Kant accepts in his twofold certainty does not exist in reality. Subjectively, head and heart, and objectively, visible and invisible things, cannot be divided in two in this manner. The heart also exerts influence in scientific inquiry. πιστις in the broad sense occupies a large place there too. Much of what passes for fixed and certain in science rests on moral or immoral grounds. And conversely, even in moral certainty it is nevertheless consciousness that weighs and judges the moral grounds upon which one proposition or another is acknowledged and accepted as true. The will may move the understanding to accept one truth or another, but that acceptance itself is nevertheless an act of the understanding; and the understanding can do this only because it itself recognizes and perceives the truth to a greater or lesser degree. The certainty of faith comes about neither through theoretical proofs nor through a decision of the will. Finally, various objections have already been brought against Kant’s theory of postulates. They can be increased by these questions: is it in agreement with the nature of true morality, which consists also in meekness, humility, and so forth, to postulate the existence of God and of immortality on the ground of our moral nature and destiny? Does moral certainty retain its power even when the moral person falls into sins, undergoes temptation and assault, and is tossed back and forth by doubt? Are moral grounds sufficient, over against the opposition of science, for building on them faith in God’s existence, the consciousness of forgiveness, and the hope of salvation? But even if moral certainty were sufficient for the philosopher, it is unusable for the Christian. For even if Kant’s _Vernunftreligion_ could rest on it as on a firm foundation, it is incapable of bearing the truth of the Christian religion.

For all these reasons Kant’s moral certainty cannot replace Christian certainty of faith. This is proved once more, superfluously, by the distinction Kant makes between opining, believing, and knowing. According to him, opining was holding something to be true on insufficient grounds, believing on subjectively sufficient grounds, and knowing on objectively sufficient grounds, _Kritik der reinen Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, p. 632 f. Now this distinction is correct with respect to believing in daily life and in relation to things that can be known. In that case believing is indeed a lesser and weaker degree of knowing. But in religion faith is certainty itself. Augustine already defined the distinction more correctly. He says: _tria sunt item velut finitima sibimet in animis hominum distinctione dignissima: intelligere, credere, opinari. Quae si per se ipsa considerentur, primum semper sine vitio est, secundum aliquando cum vitio, tertium nunquam sine vitio_, _De util. cred._, c. 11. _Inter credere autem atque opinari hoc distat, quod aliquando ille, qui credit, sentit se ignorare quod credit, quamvis de re, quam se ignorare novit, omnino non dubitet, sic enim firme credit; qui autem opinatur, putat se scire, quod nescit_, _De mendac._, c. 3. Thomas defines the distinction as follows: _de ratione opinionis est quod accipiatur unum cum formidine alterius oppositi; unde non habet firmam inhaesionem. De ratione vero scientiae est quod habeat firmam inhaesionem cum visione intellectiva; habet enim certitudinem procedentem ex intellectu principiorum. Fides autem medio modo se habet; excedit enim opinionem in hoc quod habet firmam inhaesionem; deficit vero a scientia in eo quod non habet visionem_, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 67, art. 3. In a similar sense Zanchius gave this definition: _opinio_ is a _cognitio neque certa neque evidens_, _fides_ a _cognitio certa sed non evidens_, _scientia_ a _cognitio aeque certa ac evidens_, _Op._ II 196. Believing and knowing, therefore, do not differ in certainty. Faith brings with it a certainty just as strong as knowing. Indeed, the certainty of faith is the more intense of the two; it is almost unshakable and ineradicable. For faith a person will hazard his life and everything. Galileo abjured three times his belief in the Copernican system. Against his conviction, Kepler occupied himself with astrology at Graz in order to make his living; the needy mother (astronomy) had to live from the foolish daughter (astrology). Who surrenders his life for a scientific thesis, for example that the earth revolves around the sun? But religion cultivates martyrs. In intensive power the certainty of faith far surpasses scientific certainty. Bonaventure, however, rightly distinguishes in certainty between _certitudo adhaesionis_ and _certitudo speculationis_, _Sent._ III, dist. 23, art. 1, qu. 4; cf. Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II 883. The former is greater in the certainty of faith than in scientific certainty, for often no arguments, flatteries, or tortures are capable of causing someone to waver in his faith. With his whole soul he clings to the object of faith. But _certitudo speculationis_, though it need not always be, can sometimes be stronger in science than in faith. This is the same thought Augustine expressed: knowing is always _sine vitio_, but believing occurs _aliquando cum vitio_. Believing itself is no proof for the truth of what is believed. There is a great difference between subjective assurance and objective truth. Everything in faith depends on the grounds on which it rests.
