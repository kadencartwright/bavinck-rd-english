## 3.

But because of this agreement, the difference that exists between faith as immediate certainty and faith in the religious sense may not be overlooked.
In religion, specifically in the Christian religion, _πιστις_ receives a distinctive value.
The Greeks too used the word in a religious sense, for belief in the gods, Cremer, _Wörterbuch der neut. Gräcität_, s.v.; but even then the word itself still has no religious meaning, any more than when we speak of belief in God, in the soul and its immortality, and so forth.
Here believing is still the ordinary faith we exercise daily, but applied to religious representations.
In the New Testament, however, _πιστις_ is determined wholly in a religious manner, in its object, ground, and origin; it itself denotes a religious relation of the human being to God.
In Heb. 11:1 the _ἐλπιζομενα, πραγματα οὐ βλεπομενα_ are called the general object of Christian faith.
By this alone, faith in the religious sense is distinguished from the knowledge we possess through immediate certainty.
Belief in the external world, in the senses, in the laws of thought, and so forth rests upon one’s own internal perception.
We have an immediate awareness of all these things.
But the object of Christian faith is invisible and not susceptible to perception.
If something is perceived immediately by ourselves, faith is superfluous; faith stands over against sight, Rom. 8:24; 2 Cor. 5:7.
This does not conflict with the fact that revelation nevertheless took place in space and time and that the person of Christ could be seen and touched.
For as the object of faith, this whole revelation is nevertheless imperceptible.
Many saw Jesus and yet did not believe in him; only his disciples beheld in him a _δοξα ὡς μονογενους παρα πατρος_, John 1:14.
The objects of faith are words and deeds only _sub ratione Dei_.
But in Scripture _πιστις_ as _fides salvifica_ receives a still more pregnant meaning; its object is not all sorts of words and deeds of God in themselves, but the grace of God in Christ, the promises of the gospel, Mark 1:15; John 3:16, 17:3; Rom. 3:22; Gal. 2:20, 3:26, and so forth.
For faith, this special object falls under a category other than that of true over against false.
And the general nature of faith is so modified by it that faith does not consist entirely in a firm and certain knowledge, in an objective acceptance as true, but also includes a heartfelt trust in, a complete surrender to, and a personal appropriation of the promises of God bestowed in the gospel.

Furthermore, faith in the religious sense is distinguished from immediate certainty in that the latter rests on one’s own insight but the former on the insight of others.
It is already noteworthy that the word faith is not generally used for all those cases in which knowledge rests upon immediate perception.
Belief in the external world, in the senses, in the laws of thought, and in the _prima principia_ is usually designated immediate knowledge.
And indeed this immediate knowledge, like demonstrative knowledge, rests upon one’s own perception and insight.
To that extent it can also be called knowledge in distinction from believing.
But with the _fides salvifica_ the case is different.
It most certainly has the grace of God in Christ as its object.
But we would have no knowledge whatsoever of that grace of God if it had not come to us through the testimony of others, if it were not assured to us in Holy Scripture.
Thus the testimony of the apostles comes to stand between the person of Christ and our faith.
The Word of God is the _medium gratiae_.
For faith, this Scripture comes into consideration only as God’s Word, 1 Thess. 2:13.
For religious faith can rest only on a testimony of God, John 3:33; Rom. 10:14 f.; 1 John 5:9-11.
Yet faith is bound to that Scripture.
It has the grace of God as its object, but as that grace is attested in Holy Scripture; or, as Calvin expresses it, _Inst._ III, 2, 6, its object is _Christum evangelio suo vestitum_.
Therefore faith extends in one act both to the person of Christ and to Scripture.
It embraces Christ as Savior and Scripture as God’s Word.
And therefore both are also true: through Christ to Scripture and through Scripture to Christ.
Scripture leads to Christ and directs a person’s thoughts and affections toward him who now lives in heaven.
Even those who remove all _notitia_ and _assensus_ from the _fides salvifica_ and wish to make it consist entirely in _fiducia_ cannot avoid having this _fiducia_ arise from an impression made upon the soul by the image of Christ.
Pietism too is bound to external means.
It may restrict those means and want nothing to do with a catechism or dogmatics; it nevertheless attempts to lead children to conversion by speaking to them of dear Jesus, Pierson, _Eene levensbeschouwing_, p. 13.
There is also no other way to the human heart than through the head and consciousness.
God’s Spirit alone can work immediately in the heart; we are bound to the means.
Scripture is and remains the _medium gratiae_.
Conversely, faith in Christ in turn reacts upon faith in Scripture; it binds and fastens us to that Scripture and causes us to trust it in distress and death.
Though our soul may be bound indissolubly to Christ, the living Lord in heaven, through the _unio mystica_ of the Holy Spirit, for our consciousness Christ and the whole world of the _ἐλπιζομενα_ exist only through the testimony of God in his Word.
And therefore the _fides salvifica_ always includes knowing as well.
This knowledge of saving faith is indeed essentially distinguished from the knowledge of the _fides historica_.
Even if the latter precedes, it is nevertheless later grafted anew onto the _fides salvifica_ and changes in character.
It is not a bare acceptance as true but a firm and certain knowledge in the sense of Holy Scripture.
The biblical idea of knowing is altogether different and much deeper than that of ordinary usage.
Nevertheless, the knowledge the believer possesses is not immediate and is not acquired through personal insight; it is bound to Holy Scripture and rests upon the testimony of apostles and prophets as upon the Word of God.

Finally, Christian faith is further distinguished from immediate certainty in that it does not arise spontaneously from human nature.
Every human being believes spontaneously, without command and on the ground of personal insight, in the external world, in the _principia_, in perception, in the laws of thought, and so forth.
But it is not so with Christian faith.
Faith is not possessed by all, 2 Thess. 3:2.
Although it is completely human—no _donum superadditum_ but the restoration of the human being—the psychical human being nevertheless stands hostile to believing.
For faith in the Christian sense presupposes self-denial, the crucifixion of one’s own thoughts and will, distrust in ourselves, and, over against these, trust in God’s grace in Christ.
Therefore, just as the _fides salvifica_ has God himself as its object and rests upon his testimony, so it also has him as its author.
He himself is the one who through the Holy Spirit moves the human being to faith and leads all his thoughts captive to the obedience of Christ, Matt. 16:17; John 6:44; 1 Cor. 12:3; 2 Cor. 10:5; 1 Thess. 2:13; 2 Thess. 3:2; Eph. 1:15, 16; Col. 1:13; Phil. 1:29.
By this Christian faith is determined wholly in a religious manner.
Its object, ground, and origin lie wholly and solely in God.
Through this religious character the _fides salvifica_ is essentially distinguished from the immediate certainty that is sometimes designated by the name faith, and also from the _πιστις_ of which the Greeks sometimes spoke in a religious sense.
Christian faith is religion pure and simple, _religio subjectiva_.
The human being who believes in this way is truly religious; he is God’s image, child, and heir.
