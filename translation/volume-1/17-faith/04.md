## 4.

In the Christian church, however, the conception that saw faith as the assent of the understanding to revealed truth soon became dominant. Very common is the definition of faith as ἑκουσιος της ψυχης συγκαταθεσις, as ὑπερ τας λογικας μεθοδους την ψυχην εἰς συγκαταθεσιν ἑλκουσα; cf. Suicerus, _Thes. Eccl._, s.v.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ II 467 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_, 2e Aufl., IV 246 f. Augustine gives the same definition: _credere est cum assensione cogitare_, _De praed. sanct._, c. 2; _quid est credere, nisi consentire, verum esse quod dicitur_, _De spir. et litt._, c. 31; _fides est virtus, qua creduntur, quae non videntur_, _Enchir._, c. 8; _Tract._ 40 _in Joh._, n. 9; _De trin._ 13, 1. Scholastic theology usually began its investigation into the nature of faith with the definition in Heb. 11:1 and the last-named definition of Augustine, Lombard, _Sent._ 3, 23, and others. Thomas treats faith in his _Summa Theol._ II 2, qu. 1 sq., and says that the object of faith is God and other things _nisi secundum quod habent aliquem ordinem ad Deum_, qu. 1, art. 1. The ground of faith lies solely in the fact that _aliquid est a Deo revelatum_, ibid., and its author is God alone, ibid., qu. 6, artt. 1 and 2. The _Vaticanum_ defined faith as a _virtus supernaturalis, qua Dei aspirante et adjuvante gratia, ab eo revelata vera esse credimus, non propter intrinsecam rerum veritatem naturali rationis lumine perspectam sed propter auctoritatem ipsius Dei revelantis, qui nec falli nec fallere potest_, sess. 3, _Const. dogm. de fide_, cap. 3. At Rome faith is an _assensus firmus ac certus_ to the truths of revelation on the ground of the authority of God in Scripture and church, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 2, art. 1; qu. 4, art. 2; Bellarmine, _De justific._ I, capp. 5, 6; Becanus, _Theol. schol._, tom. II, pars 2, tract. 1, cap. 1 sq.; Perrone, _Praelect. theol._ V, 1840, p. 251 sq.; Jansen, _Praelect. theol._ I 684; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ IV 205 f.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der rel. Erk._ II 426 f.

In practice this conception of faith had very harmful consequences. First, faith became in fact nothing other than an intellectual assent to a mysterious doctrine far surpassing reason, whether _explicite_ to all its various dogmas or _implicite_ to certain necessary ones. The distinction between this faith and the _fides historica_ accepted by Protestants is impossible on this conception and was therefore decisively rejected by Roman theology. Rome has nothing other than a _fides historica_. From this it further followed that this faith, if it was nothing other than an intellectual assent, could not possibly be sufficient for salvation. It had to be supplemented by another virtue in the will, namely love, and then becomes _fides formata_. Faith thereby loses its central place in the Christian life; it is lowered to one of the seven _praeparationes_ for the _gratia infusa_ of _justificatio_, and the center of gravity comes to lie entirely in love, that is, in good works. Finally, it was difficult to maintain that faith in the sense described above was a fruit of the _gratia interna_ as Augustine understood it. The confession that faith was a gift of God was weakened. The assistance and inspiration of which the _Vaticanum_ speaks are often restricted in Roman theology to the gift of a general grace or even to the gift of natural powers. Believing was the more meritorious the more it was man’s own free act and consisted in accepting incomprehensible _mysteria fidei_, in a _sacrificium intellectus_.

The Reformation altered this Roman conception of faith in every respect. It restored the religious nature of πιστις. First, it made a principled distinction between _fides historica_ and _fides salvifica_. Historical faith might in some cases precede saving faith and be of great value in itself, but it was and remained essentially different from saving faith. All the Reformers were unanimous in judging that saving faith consisted, if not solely then certainly also, in knowledge. None of them allowed faith to be absorbed into an unconscious emotion or state of mind. But the knowledge that was an element of saving faith was nevertheless entirely different from that of historical faith. The latter might later benefit _fides salvifica_, but in doing so it itself changed in character and began to live from a new principle. Among the Reformers _fides_ therefore again received a spiritual and religious nature of its own, distinguished not gradually but essentially from every other faith in life and science, indeed even from historical faith. Naturally such a faith could not arise from the same principle from which every other kind of human faith arises. The Reformation was unanimous in the confession that _fides salvifica_ was a gift of God. It was a fruit neither of man’s natural powers nor of general grace. It was a fruit of the special grace of the Holy Spirit, an activity of the new, regenerate person, and therefore also perfectly sufficient for salvation. At Rome faith occupies only a preparatory place—and rightly so, for fundamentally it is nothing other than historical faith. But in the Reformation it once again received the central place it occupies in the New Testament; it does not need to be supplemented by love, but is sufficient for participation in all the goods of salvation. One who believes in this way stands not in the vestibule but in the sanctuary of Christian truth; he is incorporated into Christ, partakes of all his benefits, and is an heir of eternal salvation. With this profound conception of _fides salvifica_, however, it was difficult to define its nature correctly and express it in clear words. The theology of the Reformation has wrestled with this at all times. With respect to the question in what the proper act of faith consists, the answers diverge very widely. It has been defined in terms of knowing, assenting, trusting, taking refuge, and so forth, or by one of these or by all together. All this can be investigated more closely only later, in the doctrine of faith. But this much is certain: in the theology of the Reformation faith was not knowledge of certain doctrinal truths but a bond of the soul to the person of Christ according to the Scriptures and to Scripture as the Word of Christ. _Fides salvifica_ was once again religious through and through. God’s grace in Christ was its object, God’s testimony in his Word was its ground, and the Holy Spirit was its author. It was religiously determined in every respect.
