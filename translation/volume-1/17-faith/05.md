## 5.

According to Scripture, this faith brings with it a certainty of its own. It is a ὑποστασις and ἐλεγχος of things hoped for and unseen, Heb. 11:1—not because it is so firm and steadfast in itself, but because it rests on God’s testimony and promise, as the continuation of Heb. 11 plainly teaches. It makes the invisible goods of salvation certain for us, indeed far more certain than personal insight or scientific proof could ever make them. Scripture therefore speaks of the παρρησια, Heb. 4:16; πεποιθησις, Eph. 3:12; πληροφορια, Heb. 6:11, 12; 10:22, of faith; and attributes to it θαρσος, Matt. 9:2; καυχησις, Rom. 5:12; χαρα, 1 Pet. 1:8; and so forth. It stands over against doubt, anxiety, fear, and distrust, Matt. 6:31; 8:26; 10:31; 14:31; 21:21; Mark 4:40; Luke 8:25; John 14:1; Rom. 4:20; James 1:16. Throughout Scripture certainty is a mark of faith. Even amid the severest trials, when everything is against him, hoping against hope, the believer stands firm as seeing the Invisible One, Job 19:25; Pss. 23; 32; 51; Rom. 4:20, 21; 5:1; 8:38; Heb. 11; and so forth. The believer will surrender everything before he will deny his faith. Nothing is too precious to him for his faith—not his money or possessions, not his honor or life. Faith is ἡ νικη ἡ νικησασα τον κοσμον, 1 John 5:4.

This certainty of faith was unknown to science. It made its entrance into the world with Christianity, Janet and Séailles, _Histoire de la philosophie_, Paris, 1887, p. 668. Greek philosophy acknowledged two kinds of certainty, one arising from sensory perception and another obtained through thought. The first was generally placed far below the second; sensory perception furnished only δοξα, but thought led to ἐπιστημη. Within the latter Aristotle again distinguished between the certainty that rested on proofs and that which rested on evidence. In science there were therefore three ways of arriving at certainty: perception, argumentation, and evidence. This threefold certainty gained citizenship in philosophy, with the qualification that empiricists sought certainty chiefly in perception and rationalists in thought. Alongside this scientific certainty Christianity placed the certainty of faith. Concretely and practically this certainty was displayed to the skeptical world in the believing congregation, especially in its martyrs and witnesses unto blood. And theoretically it was expressed and developed in Christian theology. In the doctrine of the certainty of faith there is an important difference between Rome and the Reformation concerning the question whether _certitudo fidei_ also includes _certitudo salutis_, the absolute assurance of one’s own salvation. From Augustine onward this _certitudo salutis_ was denied and opposed by the Roman Church and theology. Rome maintains that absolute assurance of salvation is the possession only of certain believers who have received it through a special revelation, but that it by no means flows from the nature of faith. With respect to their salvation ordinary believers have only a _certitudo moralis, conjecturalis_, but no _certitudo fidei_. There is no place for this certainty in the Roman system, since it can exist only with the confession of God’s electing love and would make the laity independent of church and priest. Cf. Augustine, _De bono persev._, capp. 8, 13, 22; _De civ. Dei_, c. 12; _Epist._ 107; etc.; G. J. Vossius, _Historia pelagiana_, 1655, p. 578 sq.; _Comment. op Sent._ I, dist. 17; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 112, art. 5; Bellarmine, _De Justif._ I, c. 10; III, c. 2 sq.; _Conc. Trid._, sess. 6, cap. 9, can. 13, 14, and later in the doctrine of faith. Roman Catholic theologians do, however, acknowledge _certitudo fidei_ with respect to the objective truths of revelation. _Facilius_, said Augustine, _Conf._ 7, 10, _dubitarem vivere me, quam non esse veritatem, quae per ea, quae facta sunt, intellecta conspicitur_. Albert the Great distinguished between faith in philosophy and in theology. There faith is nothing other than _credulitas_ and no path to knowledge; but _in theologicis fides lumen est, certissimam faciens adhaesionem et assensum . . . et ideo est via et medium ad scientiam veritatis divinorum_, in Stöckl, _Philos. der M. A._ II 365; cf. further Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 4, art. 8; Bellarmine, _De Justif._ III, c. 2; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae_ VIII, p. 86 sq.; Dens, _Theol._ II 241 sq.; Daelman, _Theol._ I, p. 12 sq. Protestant theologians generally agree with this. No one expressed this certainty of faith more starkly and strongly than Calvin. For him faith is _certa, firma, plena et fixa certitudo_, more _certitudo_ than _apprehensio_, _cordis fiducia et securitas_, and so forth, _Inst._ I, 7, 5; II, 2, 8; III, 2, 14 f.; 14, 8; 24, 4; etc. Among both Lutheran and Reformed theologians faith is a _firmus assensus_, a _certa cognitio_, which excludes all doubt and uncertainty, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, p. 299 f.; Hase, _Hutt. Rediv._ §108; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 21; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, p. 384 f.
