## 7.

This _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ was applied too one-sidedly by Calvin and the Reformed theologians to the authority of Holy Scripture. It seemed to have no other content than the subjective assurance of Scripture as the Word of God. As a result, this _testimonium_ came to stand by itself; it was detached from the life of faith and seemed to designate an extraordinary revelation which Michaelis was honest enough to acknowledge that he had never experienced. Scripture, however, teaches something altogether different. In general, the Holy Spirit is promised by Jesus as the Comforter, as the Spirit of truth, who leads first the apostles, but then through their word all believers as well, into the truth, bears witness to Christ among them, and glorifies him, John 14:17; 15:26; 16:14. To that end he convicts of sin, John 16:8–11, regenerates, John 3:3, and brings one to the confession of Christ as Lord, 1 Cor. 12:3. Further, he assures believers of their status as children and of the heavenly inheritance, Rom. 8:14 f.; 2 Cor. 1:22; 5:5; Eph. 1:13; 4:30; makes known all the things granted to believers by God, 1 Cor. 2:12; 1 John 2:20; 3:24; 4:6–13; and is in the congregation the author of all Christian virtues and all spiritual gifts, Gal. 5:22; 1 Cor. 12:8–11. It is clear from all these passages that the testimony of the Holy Spirit is religious-ethical in nature and is most intimately connected with one’s own life of faith. It does not bypass faith; it is no voice from heaven, no dream or vision. It is a testimony that the Holy Spirit bears in, with, and through our own spirit in faith. It is not granted to unbelievers but is the portion only of the children of God. Episcopius, _Instit. Theol._ IV, sect. 1, c. 5, § 2, therefore raised the objection that the _testimonium Sp. S._ cannot be the ground of faith, since it follows faith, John 7:38; 14:17; Acts 5:32; Gal. 3:2; 4:6. But faith is itself a work of the Holy Spirit from its very beginning, 1 Cor. 12:3, and receives its seal and confirmation in the Spirit of υἱοθεσια. Believing itself is a witnessing of the Holy Spirit in our hearts and through our spirit. In this testimony there is a distinction between the Spirit of God and our spirit only insofar as our spirit repeatedly still resists it and must continually be led to obedience. The testimony of the Holy Spirit therefore gives no assurance of the objective truths of salvation apart from the state of the religious subject. It assures those truths because they stand in an inseparable connection with the regeneration and conversion, forgiveness and sonship, of the believer. The _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ is first of all an assurance that we are children of God. That is the central truth, the core and center of this testimony. But in connection with this it also seals the objective truths of salvation, the transcendent and transeunt truths, as Frank called them. This must, however, be qualified: the _testimonium Sp. S._ reveals none of these truths to us, nor does it enable us to derive them by reflection from the nature of our spiritual life. The illumination of the Holy Spirit is not a source of knowledge of Christian truth; it makes known to us no material truths hidden from the natural man. It only causes us to understand and apprehend the same things differently, more deeply, spiritually. Paul says expressly that the Spirit causes us to know the things objectively granted to us by God in Christ, 1 Cor. 2; cf. Hoekstra, _Grondslag, wezen en openbaring van het godsd. geloof_, 1861, pp. 165 f., 184. The Spirit whom believers receive is the Spirit of Christ, who takes everything from Christ and is received through the preaching of the gospel, John 14:17; Gal. 3:2; 4:6; 1 John 2:20, 24, 27. But the truths themselves are known to us from another source—from Scripture; they are only subjectively sealed by the testimony of the Holy Spirit.

It follows from this that the proper object to which the Holy Spirit bears witness in the hearts of believers is nothing other than the _divinitas_ of the truth granted to us in Christ. Historical, chronological, and geographical data are never as such, in themselves, the object of the testimony of the Holy Spirit. Even the facts of salvation are not, as _nuda facta_, the content of that testimony. No believer is assured in a scientific sense by the testimony of the Holy Spirit of the supernatural conception and resurrection of Christ. The sole thing to which the testimony of the Holy Spirit relates is the _divinitas_. But then it is also the _divinitas_ of all those truths revealed in Scripture and granted to us by God in Christ. It is incorrect to restrict the _testimonium Sp. S._ to that which alone is religious-ethical in the narrower sense. The _divinitas_ is indeed the only direct object of that testimony, but this _divinitas_ is not only a property of certain religious and moral statements but equally of facts and deeds. Christ himself is a historical person, redemption was accomplished through historical deeds, and the _testimonium Sp. S._ also impresses the mark of _divinitas_ upon this history. Calvin therefore rightly connected this _testimonium Sp. S._ with Scripture as the Word of God. For not only does this Scripture _omnium consensu_ possess a rich religious-ethical significance for the Christian life, but it also contains a doctrine concerning itself. The Holy Spirit reveals no single truth to the believer, neither concerning the person of Christ nor concerning Holy Scripture. The believer can confess only what God has granted him in Christ. But Scripture contains a doctrine concerning itself just as much as it does concerning Christ. And the _testimonium Sp. S._ with respect to Holy Scripture consists not in the believer’s receiving an immediate, heavenly intuition of the divinity of Scripture; nor in his inferring its divinity mediately from the _notae_ and _criteria_; nor in his ascending to its divinity from experience of the power that proceeds from it. Rather, it consists in his freely and spontaneously acknowledging the authority with which Holy Scripture everywhere comes forward and which it repeatedly and expressly claims for itself. Not the authenticity, nor the canonicity, nor even the inspiration, but the _divinitas_ of Scripture—its divine authority—is here the proper object of the testimony of the Holy Spirit. It causes the believer to submit to Scripture and binds him to it in the same measure and with the same force as to the person of Christ himself. It assures him that in need and death, in life and dying, he can rely on that Word of God and with it can even appear without fear before the Judge of heaven and earth. Historical criticism of Scripture therefore encounters resistance in the congregation only insofar as it detracts from this _divinitas_ of Holy Scripture and thereby undermines the testimony of being a child of God, the hope of glory, and the certainty of salvation. In a certain sense a circular argument lies hidden in this _testimonium Sp. S._ The _divinitas_ of Scripture is proved from this testimony, and the _divinitas_ of this testimony in turn from Scripture. The _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ merely occurs here in two respects. The believer first feels himself bound in his soul to the Word of God and then later learns from Holy Scripture to understand that this faith in Scripture was worked in his heart by the Holy Spirit. Strictly speaking, the testimony of the Holy Spirit is not the ultimate ground of his faith either, for Scripture is αὐτοπιστος; it is, however, the means by which he acknowledges the divinity of Scripture. Scripture and the testimony of the Holy Spirit are related as objective truth and subjective assurance, as the _prima principia_ and their evidence, as the light and the eye. Once Scripture has been acknowledged in its divinity, it stands immovably firm before the faith of the congregation as the Word of God, so that it is the _principium_ and _norma_ of faith and life.
