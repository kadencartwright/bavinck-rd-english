## 6.

Against this representation, however, the objection is raised from various sides that believing thereby becomes wholly arbitrary.
Instead of giving reasons why Scripture is believed as the Word of God, one answers that God grants one to believe it as such.
With that argument the Muslim can prove his belief in the Quran, and every superstitious person his superstition.
The _sic volo, sic jubeo_ takes the place of reason and proof.
Cf., for example, Scholten against Saussaye, _Leer der Herv. Kerk_, 3rd ed., preface.
Against this it should first be observed that the believer indeed can indicate no deeper ground for revelation than its divine authority, which he acknowledges by faith.
But that does not mean that he has nothing to advance against the opponent of that revelation.
It is true that he has no conclusive proofs and cannot move his opponent to faith, but in defense he has at least as much to say as his opponent does in attack.
Unbelief too rests ultimately not on proofs and arguments but is rooted in the heart.
Believers and unbelievers stand completely equal in this respect; the conviction of both is bound up with their entire personality and is only afterward, _a posteriori_, supported by proof and argument.
And when the two now contend against each other with these _a posteriori_ proofs and arguments, believers are in no less favorable a position than those who do not believe.
God is knowable enough for those who seek him and, conversely, hidden enough for those who flee from him.
_Il y a assez de lumière pour ceux, qui ne désirent que de voir, et assez d’obscurité pour ceux, qui ont une disposition contraire. Il y a assez de clarté pour éclairer les élus, et assez d’obscurité pour les humilier. Il y a assez d’obscurité pour aveugler les réprouvés, et assez de clarté, pour les condamner et les rendre inexcusables_, Pascal, _Oeuvres_, Paris, Hachette, 1869, I 345.
The situation with religion, Theism, revelation, and Scripture is not yet so hopeless as science has wished us to believe for years.
Theodor von Lerber recently wrote of science, not entirely without reason: _ich habe als Dilettant zu oft neben ihr gesessen und ihr in die Karten und auf die Finger geschaut, um noch übermässigen Respekt vor der Dame zu haben. Sie wird mir auf meinen Grabstein schreiben müssen: er dachte klein von mir und starb_, in A. Zahn, _Socialdemokratie und Theologie_, Gütersloh, 1895, S. 34.
Historical and rational proofs will convert no one, but in defense of faith they are as powerful as the opposing party’s arguments for justifying its unbelief.
Further, the testimony believers give to Christian revelation, to Scripture, is indeed not universally human but is nevertheless universally Christian.
The whole of Christendom is unanimous in this confession.
The _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ is not the testimony of a _spiritus privatus_ but of the one and the same Spirit who dwells in all believers.
In discussing this testimony, Calvin declared that he was describing nothing other than the experience of all believers, _Inst._ I 7, 6.
It is a mighty testimony that the church of all ages gives to Scripture as the Word of God.
There is no dogma concerning which such great unanimity prevails.
It is a fact that may not be placed on the same level as hallucination or arbitrariness and that in any event requires explanation.
Next, the believer certainly can indicate no deeper ground for his faith than the divine authority of Holy Scripture.
But he can give a further explanation concerning the manner in which he came to this faith.
It is true that a person is ordinarily born and raised in that faith; later he himself discovers that he is bound to Scripture with his whole soul and then tries to reflect upon this and give an account of this mystical bond.
But it repeatedly happens that a person comes to conversion and to faith in Scripture later in life.
And those who have lived from that faith since their youth are also frequently shaken and tossed to and fro by criticism; only gradually do they attain the firm assurance of faith.
What, then, is the experience through which faith in revelation is first awakened or later restored and strengthened?
It naturally differs among the various believers, but it is nevertheless always religious-ethical and spiritual in character.
What truly causes us to believe is neither the insight of our understanding nor a decision of our will, but a power that stands above us, bends our will, illuminates our understanding, and without compulsion yet efficaciously leads our thoughts and deliberations captive to the obedience of Christ.
Augustine confessed this when he ascribed faith to _gratia interna_.
Thomas acknowledged it when he said that the _assensus fidei_ was _a Deo, interius movente per gratiam_.
The Vatican Council expressed it when it testified that faith did not come about _absque illuminatione et inspiratione Spiritus Sancti_.
And this was the conviction of the entire Reformation: faith is a gift of God, a work of the Holy Spirit.
Believing is an act of the understanding, an immediate adherence of consciousness to revelation that is not mediated by proofs.
But this faith presupposes a change in the relation of the whole human being to God; it presupposes regeneration, the transformation of the will.
_Nemo credit nisi volens._
Knowing compels; no one can deny a mathematical proposition.
But believing is free; it is the act of the highest freedom because it is the act of the deepest self-denial.
If God binds salvation not to knowing but to believing, that is proof that he does not compel and does not wish to compel.
The Epistle to Diognetus, cap. 7, says so beautifully: βια γαρ οὐ προσεστι τῳ θεῳ.
Precisely because faith is not the fruit of scientific proofs, it does not come about apart from the human heart and will.
This is the truth contained in the doctrine of Kant and the Neo-Kantians concerning _moralischen Glauben_.
Faith is not the conclusion of a syllogism.
On the other hand, however, it is not a decision of the will or a postulate either.
The prodigal son does not postulate when he returns to his father’s house.
Faith is also not an _imperium voluntatis_.
One cannot believe at will.
The will cannot command consciousness to accept something as truth when consciousness itself sees no truth in it whatsoever.
Believing is not arbitrary, but neither is it blind.
It presupposes a transformation of the will, _operari sequitur esse_, but it is a free, spontaneous acknowledgment by the understanding of the Word of God.
Just as the eye, seeing the sun, is immediately convinced of its reality, so the regenerate person beholds the truth of God’s revelation.
For the regenerate person, faith in revelation is as natural as acknowledgment of the moral law is for the moral person.
It is implanted in the nature of spiritual life; it is rooted in the mysterious depths of the regenerate heart.
The believer can surrender this faith no more than he can surrender himself.
Indeed, he can deny himself and sacrifice his life, but he cannot relinquish his faith.
For the Christian, faith in revelation is one with faith in himself, Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ I 222; Kuyper, _Encycl._ II 77.
The Christian would have to surrender faith in himself, in his sonship, in the forgiveness of his sins, and in the truthfulness and faithfulness of God in order not to see the Word of God in revelation.
Faith in revelation is one with the best that is in him; in his best moments he stands most firmly in that faith.
Whatever may arise against it, he can do no other and may do no other.
Finally, much does arise against this faith of his.
This comes not only from without but much more still from within.
However much the will has been bent and the understanding illumined, much still remains in the believer that rises up against this obedience of faith.
Faith, because it is the evidence of things not seen, is a continual struggle.
Sins of the heart and errors of the head resist faith, and they frequently have appearances in their favor.
A dualism remains in the believer as long as he is on earth—a dualism not of head and heart, but of σαρξ and πνευμα, of the παλαιος and the καινος ἀνθρωπος.
Faith retains a more or less supernatural character insofar as it transcends the nature of the psychical man.
It is not yet fully natural; as soon as it becomes so, it itself ceases and passes over into sight.
Faith is faith precisely because it sees something the psychical man does not perceive.
But this dualism, however painful, serves on the other hand to confirm faith.
For if faith does not arise from the natural dispositions of the human being and is neither the conclusion of a syllogism nor a decision of the will, then its presence is at the same time evidence of its truth.
Our own spirit does not naturally impel us to call God our Father and reckon ourselves among his children.
There is an essential and also readily recognizable difference between the testimony of the Holy Spirit when he says to our soul, “I am your salvation,” and the seduction of Satan when he says, “Peace, peace, and no danger.”
_Potestne quis a diabolo impelli, ut Deum in fide Abba, Patrem vocet?_ Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._, loc. 24, § 78.
Christian faith points back to the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_.
“Though Theology sneer and Philosophy mock, God himself is the ultimate ground of my faith in God” (Beets).
