## 2.

In fact, Rome thereby adopts the same subjective standpoint as the churches of the Reformation. The motives, however strong, cannot actually move someone to faith. It is God’s Spirit alone who can inwardly convince someone firmly and certainly of the truth of divine revelation. For Rome too, the deepest ground of faith is not Scripture or the church but the _lumen interius_. Various theologians have also acknowledged this. For the sake of clarity, the act of faith may for a moment be analyzed as a syllogism. The major then runs: God is truthful; if he reveals himself, his revelation must be accepted in faith. The minor runs: these facts—for example, the church and Scripture—are revelations of God. The conclusion follows: therefore these too must be believed. Concerning the major there is no difference. It stands firm by virtue of the idea of God and is acknowledged by all. No one denies that, _if_ God reveals himself, he also deserves to be believed as the truthful One. All the more, therefore, everything depends on the minor. The difference concerns precisely whether and where God has revealed himself. The believing Christian says: in Scripture, or also in the church. But how and why are these acknowledged as God’s revelation? If on the ground of proofs, of the _motiva credibilitatis_, then that ground is human and fallible, and faith is not purely religious and certain. God’s revelation can be believed in a religious sense only on the ground of God’s authority. But this authority of God can be heard only either outside me, in Scripture or the church, whose ultimate ground of faith I am precisely investigating, or within me, in the grace that moves me to faith, in the _lumen interius_, the _testimonium Sp. S._ Whoever wishes to maintain the authority of God as the ultimate ground of faith, and therefore faith in its religious nature, must adopt one of these two standpoints. Now Canus said without hesitation that the ultimate ground lay in the grace that inwardly moved him to faith. I believe, he said, that God is triune because God has revealed it; _huic autem: Deus revelavit, immediate credo, a Deo motus per instinctum specialem_, _Loc. Theol._, l. 2, c. 8, ad 4. For Canus, therefore, the ultimate ground of his faith lay objectively in the testimony of God, but the fact that he acknowledged this testimony as divine was due to the grace that moved his will and understanding to believe. This opinion of Canus was indeed adopted by some other Roman Catholic theologians, such as Arragon, Gonet, and others; Prof. Hayd, _Philos. Jahrbuch von Gutberlet_ III, 1890, p. 27, even spoke of an immediate testimony of God within us that assures us of the divinity of Jesus’s person and teaching. Many nevertheless raised the objection that Canus does not really answer the question on what ground he accepts the testimony of God in Scripture and the church as divine. He says only that God by his grace inwardly grants him to believe it in this way but gives no further account of it. Moreover, this opinion of Canus also failed to commend itself because, as Suarez already observed, it bore so much resemblance to, and could so easily lead to, Calvin’s doctrine of the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_. Roman Catholic theologians were therefore intent on seeking the ultimate ground of faith somewhere other than the _lumen interius_. Suarez was of the opinion that not only the object but also the ground of faith itself is again a matter of faith. We believe not only that Scripture is true because God has revealed himself in it; we also believe the fact that God has revealed himself in it because God himself testifies to this in Scripture. Revelation is at once the _quo_ and the _quod creditur_. _Unus enim et idem actus credit Deum et Deo._ Just as the eye perceives colors and at the same time the light by which those colors become visible, and just as reason knows derived truths and at the same time the _principia_ by which they are knowable, so faith too knows both the revealed truths and the testimony on which they rest as divine. Very many theologians, including in more recent times, expressed agreement with this opinion of Suarez, but it did not satisfy everyone. The circular reasoning was evident: Scripture is believed because it is revealed, and that it is revealed is in turn believed because Scripture testifies to it. It also leads to a _progressus in infinitum_: I believe one revelation because another revelation testifies to it, and so forth. Suarez does answer this objection by saying that revelation must be believed for its own sake, that when God reveals himself he also reveals at the same time that it is _he_ who reveals himself. But the question still always remains open: why do we believe the testimony in which God declares that it is he who has revealed himself? Lugo therefore taught that acceptance of the fact that God revealed himself in Scripture was indeed supernatural, but that it nevertheless could not be called faith in the proper sense. For believing is always accepting something as true on the ground of a testimony. If accepting the fact that God revealed himself were believing in the proper sense, then it would again presuppose a divine testimony, and so _in infinitum_. Lugo therefore assumed that acknowledgment of the fact of revelation rested not on God’s testimony but on the believer’s immediately and directly perceiving revelation itself, with its miracles, prophecies, and so forth, as revelation, just as reason immediately acknowledges the truth of the _principia_. Revelation announces itself as divine through its content, just as, according to Thomas’s image, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 43, art. 1, an ambassador authenticates himself through the content of his message—for example, through secrets that only the one who commissioned him can know. But this conception again encounters the objection that this acknowledgment of revelation is either truly immediate, in which case it becomes a new revelation in the subject, an intuition of the divine that we do not possess here on earth as those who walk by faith; or it is in fact mediated, in which case it also flows from the _indicia_ and _criteria_ of revelation and so comes once again to rest on the proofs for revelation, on the _motiva credibilitatis_. This solution too is therefore unsatisfactory. Others have therefore again said: the testimony of God is the ultimate ground of faith. To the question “Why do you believe?” the Christian answers: “Because God has spoken. _Deus dixit._” He can indicate no other, deeper ground in this matter. If he is further asked, “But why do you believe that God has spoken, for example in Scripture?” he can only answer that God so works inwardly within him that he acknowledges that Scripture as God’s Word. But with this he has also said everything. God’s testimony is the ground, but grace, the will, is the cause of faith. The proofs may be _motiva credibilitatis_; the will is finally the _motivum credendi_. This standpoint too is not above criticism. For the question can be, and actually has been, asked: what ground does the _understanding_ have for accepting Scripture as God’s Word? The answer that the will or grace moves the understanding to do so is insufficient. The will cannot move the understanding to accept something as true without a ground whose credibility it itself perceives. The understanding itself must acknowledge that something is divine and therefore deserves faith. Otherwise faith is irrational and the believer disposes of the difficulty with a _sic volo, sic jubeo, stat pro ratione voluntas_. The Roman Catholic theologians too have therefore been unable to give an answer satisfactory to all concerning the deepest ground of faith. Very many simply refrain from a decision and leave the choice between the various opinions named above free. This is enough to show that Rome, with its infallible church and infallible pope, has no advantage over the churches of the Reformation. The deepest ground of faith lies in the subject at Rome too, just as in Protestantism. Cf. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der rel. Erk._ II 486–500; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_, 2e Aufl., IV 473–532; von Schäzler, _Neue Untersuchungen über das Dogma von der Gnade und das Wesen des Glaubens_, Mainz, 1867, pp. 513–537; Al. Schmid, _Untersuchungen über den letzten Gewissheitsgrund des Offenbarungsglaubens_, Munich, 1879; Jansen, _Praelect. Theol._ I 711–724.
