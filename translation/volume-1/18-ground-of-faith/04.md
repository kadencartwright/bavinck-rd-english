## 4.

Both Roman Catholic and Protestant theology, in their inquiry into the deepest ground of faith, have ended at the religious subject and must take their position in the faith of the congregation. Every other path taken to prove religious truth has shown itself to be an impasse. Apparently this is a disappointment, and so it is also felt by everyone who misconstrues the proper essence of revelation and religion and changes them into an intellectually demonstrable doctrine. But in fact this result is a gain for theology. For it proves that theology has arrived at an understanding of religion’s distinctive nature and that it is in the same condition as all other sciences. The subjective starting point is by no means peculiar to theology alone. Everything objective can be approached only from the subject; the _Ding an sich_ is unknowable and does not exist for us. The world of sounds has reality only for one who hears, the world of thoughts only for the thinking spirit. It is wasted effort to try to prove the objective existence of colors to the blind. All life and knowledge rest on a harmony of subject and object. And man is therefore so rich because he is connected with the objective world by the most diverse and manifold relations. He is akin to the whole world; physically, vegetatively, sensitively, intellectually, ethically, and religiously he stands in harmony with that world; he is a microcosm. Now Scripture leads us to conceive all these relations of man to the world religiously and to explain them theistically. Man did not place himself in this relation to the cosmos. From the beginning he is adapted to that world, and it in turn to him. Because he is God’s image, he is also lord of the earth. And not only is it God who once established these bonds between man and world; it is also he who continually, from moment to moment, maintains them and causes them to operate. It is the same Logos through whom all things within and outside man were made. He is before all things, and through him they still hold together, John 1:3; Col. 1:15. More particularly, Scripture makes known to us the Spirit of God as the principle and author of all life in man and world, Gen. 1:2; Pss. 33:6; 104:30; 139:7; Job 26:13; 33:4, and specifically also of intellectual, ethical, and religious life, Job 32:8; Isa. 11:2. Naturally, the operation of that Spirit differs according to the relations in which man stands to the world. Physically it consists as yet only in the impulse that causes man and animal to seek their food from God, Ps. 104:20–30. It is then still like the instinct that unconsciously guides action. But this operation of God’s Spirit assumes a higher form in man’s intellectual, ethical, and religious life. It then becomes _ratio, conscientia, sensus divinitatis_, which are not dormant faculties but capacities that pass into action through the operation of kindred phenomena from the external world. And this action may now bear the name of testimony, given by the human spirit to the corresponding phenomena outside him. Our spirit does nothing other than continually bear witness to the truth that comes to us from without. It does not bring forth that truth from itself by thinking and reasoning; it does not produce and create it, but only reproduces and thinks it after. Truth exists prior to and independently of the human spirit; it rests in itself, in the Logos in whom all things consist. On man’s part it is necessary only that he perceive the truth and receive it into himself, that he bear witness to it and seal it through thought and knowledge. Thus Jesus testified to what he had seen and heard, John 3:32; he bore witness to the truth, John 18:37; and thus the apostles were witnesses of the Word of life whom they had beheld and touched in Christ, John 15:27; 1 John 1:3. For the human spirit, bearing such testimony to the truth is rest, joy, blessedness. The distance between us and the truth has then fallen away; it has found us, and we have found it. There is immediate contact. By itself it makes us its witnesses. Every truth makes the one who knows it a witness, a herald, a prophet. Entering our spirit, it brings its own testimony with it; it itself produces that testimony within us. Religiously conceived, it is the Logos himself who through our spirit bears witness to the Logos in the world. It is one and the same Spirit who spreads out the truth objectively before us and raises it subjectively to certainty in our spirit. It is his testimony, given in our consciousness to the thoughts God has embodied in creatures. Above all in religion, this testimony of the Holy Spirit to the truth is clear. God does not leave himself without witness. He reveals his δυναμις and θειοτης in the creatures and himself bears witness to it in our νους through his Spirit. All knowledge of the truth is essentially a testimony that the human spirit bears concerning it and, in the deepest ground, a testimony of the Spirit of God to the Word through whom all things were made.
