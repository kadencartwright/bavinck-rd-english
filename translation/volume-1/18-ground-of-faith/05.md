## 5.

This testimony of the human spirit to the truth is the presupposition and foundation, and at the same time also the analogy, of the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_. Calvin and others already pointed to this correspondence, _Instit._ II, 8, 1; Maresius, _Syst. theol._ I, §33; Alsted, _Theol. schol. did._, p. 31. But analogy is not identity. The _principium externum_ of the Christian religion is not the general revelation of God in nature but a special revelation of God in Christ. The _principium internum_ must correspond to it. The νους of the psychical man is insufficient to discern the things of the Spirit of God. God can be known only through God. Ὁ ὠν ἐκ του θεου τα ρηματα του θεου ἀκουει, John 8:47; 3:21; 7:17; 10:3 f.; 18:37. No one can speak about God except the one who speaks from and through him. Therefore only the same Spirit who spoke through prophets and apostles can bear witness to the truth in our hearts and thereby raise it above all doubt and bring it to absolute certainty. Such a testimony of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers is taught very clearly in Scripture. Objective revelation—that is, the person of Christ and Scripture as his Word—contains all that man needs for the knowledge and service of God. God’s revelation is completed in Christ and described with perfect sufficiency in Scripture. But this revelation in Christ and in his Word is a means, not the end. The end is the creation of a new humanity that fully unfolds the image of God. The whole of revelation must therefore be transferred from Christ into the congregation, from Scripture into consciousness; God seeks a dwelling in man. This great, divine work of the application of salvation, of guidance into all truth, is entrusted to the Holy Spirit. Already in the days of the Old Covenant he was the author of all religious-ethical knowledge and life, Pss. 51:13; 143:10; Isa. 63:10. But Israel existed in a state of minority and was placed under the guardianship of the law, Gal. 4:1 f. The Holy Spirit was not yet, because Christ was not yet glorified, John 7:39. The prophets therefore looked with longing toward the days of the New Covenant, in which all would be taught by the Lord and all would be led by the Holy Spirit, Jer. 31:34; Ezek. 36:25 f.; Joel 2:28 f. In accordance with the promise, this Spirit is poured out on the day of Pentecost. His entire activity is called by Jesus a testifying to and glorifying of himself, John 15:26; 16:14. The Holy Spirit is the truthful and almighty Witness for Christ. The entire world stands hostile over against Christ; no one takes up his cause. But before that world the Holy Spirit appears as the Paraclete, as the defender of Christ. He does this first of all in Scripture; Scripture is the testimony, the plea of the Holy Spirit for Christ, which he pronounces and maintains through all the centuries. This _testimonium Spiritus Sancti in S. Scriptura_ precedes and lies at the foundation of the testimony the Holy Spirit bears in the hearts of believers. Just as the thoughts of God are embodied objectively in the world and derived from it by the human spirit, so the Word of revelation is first perfectly described in Holy Scripture in order afterward to be sealed in our hearts by the testimony of the Holy Spirit. Here too the activity of the human spirit consists in nothing other than bearing witness to the truth and thinking after what God thought beforehand. The _testimonium Sp. S._ in believers already proves here to be no new revelation or communication of unknown truths. It is essentially distinct from prophecy and inspiration; it only causes the truth that exists outside and independently of us to be understood as truth, and thereby assures and seals it in human consciousness. _Mutatis mutandis_, the relation of the testimony of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers to the truth of revelation in Holy Scripture is no other than that of the human spirit to the object of its knowledge. The subject does not create the truth; it only acknowledges and affirms it.

But the analogy extends still further. The objects of human knowing are all αὐτοπιστα; they rest in themselves, and their existence can be acknowledged but not proved. Proofs in the strict sense are possible only with respect to derived propositions and then consist in reducing these to general propositions. To prove is to lead the unknown back to the known, the particular to the general, the uncertain to the established. Perhaps it is more accurate still to say that proof consists in reducing uncertain and disputed propositions to those that are generally accepted as established. For the _prima principia_ on which all proofs finally rest are themselves incapable of proof; they stand firm only through and for faith. Proofs therefore have force only for one who agrees with us concerning these _principia_. _Contra principia negantem non est disputandum._ Thus in morals, stealing can be proved unlawful to one who acknowledges the authority of the moral law, but every proof is powerless over against one who denies this authority. The moral law itself is αὐτοπιστος; it rests in itself, it is like the sun that is seen only by its own rays; it depends on no proof and reasoning; it is powerful because it is there, posits itself, and maintains itself; its power consists in its authority, in the divine majesty with which it causes its “You shall” to be heard in our conscience. The moral law would weaken itself if it began to reason with us and submitted to our judgment. It appears categorically and will know of no exceptions, no excuses. To the question “Why do you submit to the moral law?” only one answer is possible: “Because it reveals God’s will to me.” But if it is then further asked, “Why do you believe that this moral law is God’s will?” no conclusive answer can be given. Whoever nevertheless wishes to answer takes a side road and appeals to all manner of _notae_ and _criteria_ in which the divinity of the moral law makes itself known to him. But these are not conclusive proofs. So it is with all _principia_. They rest in themselves and stand firm only for faith. It is the same in theology. Proofs are possible here only with respect to derived propositions. The deity of Christ can be proved to one who acknowledges the authority of Scripture. But the authority of Scripture rests in itself and is incapable of proof. Holy Scripture is αὐτοπιστος and therefore the ultimate ground of faith. No deeper ground can be advanced. To the question “Why do you believe Scripture?” the only answer is “Because it is God’s Word.” But if it is then further asked, “Why do you believe that Holy Scripture is God’s Word?” the Christian must leave the question unanswered. He will indeed then appeal to the _notae_ and _criteria_ of Scripture, to the majesty of its style, the sublimity of its content, the depth of its thoughts, the blessedness of its fruits, and so forth; but these are nevertheless not the grounds of his faith. They are only properties and marks afterward discovered in Scripture by believing thought, just as the proofs for God’s existence do not precede faith and support it, but flow from faith and are conceived by it. All proofs for faith in Scripture derived from its _notae_ and _criteria_ demonstrate most clearly that no deeper ground can be indicated. The _Deus dixit_ is the _primum principium_ to which all dogmas, including that concerning Scripture, can be reduced. The bond of the soul to Scripture as the Word of God lies behind consciousness and beneath the proofs; it is mystical in nature, like faith in the _principia_ in the various sciences.
