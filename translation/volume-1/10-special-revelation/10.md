## 10.

The revelation deposited in Holy Scripture is a troublesome fact for everyone who denies it.
For even those who contest its possibility and reality must nevertheless strive to explain its historical origin.
It did not arise from deception, any more than religion did.
Belief in revelation is not something arbitrary or accidental that occurs here or there only under special circumstances, but is essentially inherent in all religion.
The question of revelation is not as simple as Rationalism sometimes imagined it to be.
This is immediately evident from the fact that all attempts made from the naturalistic side to explain the biblical miracle narratives in a natural way have thus far suffered shipwreck.
If revelation in all its forms—theophany, prophecy, and miracle—is not truly supernatural but comes from God only in the sense that all human activity has its ultimate cause in him, one is compelled to resort either to the so-called material or to the formal interpretation of the miracle narratives.
That is, on the one hand, one can leave the facts untouched to a certain degree and accept them as true; one then seeks to explain these facts from the people’s ignorance of natural causes and from the religious need to attribute everything directly to God, Spinoza, _Tract. theol. pol._ cap. 1 and 6; Hase, _Dogm._ § 19; _Leben Jesu_ § 15; Strauss, _Glaub._ I 280; Scholten, _Supranat. in verband met Bĳbel, Christ. en Prot._, 1867, p. 8 f.
Or one explains them physically from unknown forces of nature, Kant, _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 101; Morus, _Epitome Theol. Christ._ p. 23; Schweizer, _Glaub. der ev. ref. K._ I 324 f.; or psychologically from an exceptional ability to sense the future in advance, Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I S. 300; Hase, _Dogm._ § 137, and to heal the sick without remedies, Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I S. 115; Ammon, _Gesch. des Lebens Jesu_ 248; or teleologically from such arrangement and ordering of the physical and psychical forces present in nature that they produce an unusual result and prompt recognition of God’s providence and faith in the preacher, Wegscheider, _Inst. Theol._ § 48; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I 314.
On the other hand, one can seek a solution in the formal or genetic explanation, that is, in a particular interpretation of the accounts concerning revelation.
One then invokes the Oriental mode of presentation and garb, and the accommodation of Jesus and the apostles to popular conceptions, cf. Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw. aller in der Dogm. vork. Begriffe_, 4e Aufl., S. 135 f.; Herzog², art. “Accomodation.”
Or one seeks help in the allegorical interpretation, Woolston, _Discourses on the miracles of our Saviour_, 1727; in the natural interpretation, Paulus of Heidelberg, _Philol.-krit. u. histor. Commentar über das N. T._, 1800-1804; _Leben Jesu_, 1828; _Exeg. Handbuch über die 3 ersten Evang._, 1830-33; in the mythical interpretation, Eichhorn, Gabler, G. L. Bauer, Strauss, _Leben Jesu_, 1835; or in the symbolic interpretation, A. D. Loman, _Gids_, Febr. 1884.
Cf. Wegscheider, _Instit. Theol._ S. 214; Bretschneider, _System. Entw._ S. 248 f.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erkenntniss_ II 156 f., 335 f., 405 f.

But all these explanations have had little success.
Scripture simply cannot be interpreted naturalistically or rationalistically.
The facts of revelation themselves, which Scripture communicates to us, stand in the way of all such attempts.
It is true that revelation in Scripture has, in form, many points of agreement with those to which other religions appeal; nevertheless, in principle it stands opposed to them and makes a decisive distinction between them and itself.
Consciously and with complete assurance, it attributes its origin solely to an extraordinary operation of God.
Scripture forbids all divination and sorcery, Num. 19:16, 31; 20:6, 27; Deut. 18:10 f.; Isa. 8:9; Jer. 27:9; Acts 8:9 f.; 13:8 f.; 19:13; Rev. 21:8; 22:15.
The prophets and apostles want nothing to do with them.
They stand diametrically opposed to them and follow no cunningly devised fables, 2 Pet. 1:16.
Prophecy in former times was not brought forth by the will of man, but the Holy Spirit moved the prophets, 2 Pet. 1:21; 1 Cor. 2:11, 12.
The miracle is a σημειον of the presence of God.
The clear self-consciousness that we encounter everywhere among the prophets and the perfectly lucid self-testimony that accompanies revelation throughout Scripture present an insuperable stumbling block to the naturalistic explanation.
The psychological method too, Strauss, _Glaub._ I 77; Kuenen, _De profeten_ I 106 f., cannot do justice to this self-consciousness and self-testimony of prophets and apostles, indeed, of Christ himself.
It may be gratefully acknowledged that the modern conception of revelation no longer thinks for a moment of regarding the prophets and apostles as deliberate deceivers.
Yet it cannot escape the conclusion that all these men were poor dupes who erred in good faith, since they appealed to an alleged revelation and appeared with an imagined divine authority.
The issue, after all, is not as though revelation contained only certain facts whose interpretation it leaves to our insight.
Rather, it itself casts a distinctive light on those facts; it has, so to speak, its own view and its own theory of them.
In the revelation of Scripture, word and fact, prophecy and miracle, always go hand in hand.
Both are necessary so that both consciousness and being may be re-created and the whole cosmos redeemed from sin.
_The light needs the reality and the reality needs the light, to produce -- -- -- the beautiful creation of His grace. To apply the kantian phraseology to a higher subject, without God’s acts the words would be empty, without His words the acts would be blind_, Dr. Vos, _The idea of biblical theology as a science and as a theol. discipline_, New-York, Randolph and Co., 1894, p. 15.
See also Kuyper, _Uit het Woord_ I, 1873, pp. 69-160.
The two, word and fact, are so intimately interwoven in revelation that the one cannot be accepted or rejected without the other.
Every attempt to explain the facts of revelation naturalistically has therefore thus far always ended with the recognition that a deep gulf yawns between the supernatural worldview of Scripture and that of the naturalists, and that reconciliation is impossible.
Professor Scholten furnished a striking example of this.
At first he still accepted the sayings of the Johannine Jesus as truth.
Then he sought to explain those sayings according to his altered views and to make exegesis subservient to his heterodox dogmatics.
Finally, in 1864, he openly acknowledged that the worldview of the fourth Evangelist was different from his own, _Het Evangelie naar Johannes_, 1864, pp. III-VI.
Every negative tendency ultimately acknowledges that the revelation of Scripture is still understood and reproduced most purely in orthodoxy.
Radicalism leaves Scripture as it is and is done with revelation.
Thereby the question is reduced to its deepest principle.
Whether revelation is acknowledged or not is decided by our whole outlook on life and the world.
Not historical criticism but self-criticism, not science but faith, not the head but the heart, is decisive here.
From the heart also comes foolishness, Mark 2:22.
Our thinking is rooted in our being.
_Operari sequitur esse_ (Schopenhauer).
_Was für eine Philosophie man wähle, hängt davon ab, was für ein Mensch man ist. Unser Denksystem ist oft nur die Geschichte unseres Herzens_ (Fichte).
That the acknowledgment or denial of revelation is ultimately a question of faith is adequately proved by the fact that neither the supernatural conception nor the naturalistic one is capable of removing every difficulty or resolving every objection.
The naturalistic view appears strong when it takes individual miracle narratives by themselves and isolates them from the whole; but that whole itself—the system of revelation and, within it, the person of Christ—remains for it an insoluble riddle and a stone of stumbling.
Conversely, the supernatural view has not yet succeeded in fitting all the individual facts and words of revelation into the order of the whole.
But here there is nevertheless agreement with revelation as a whole, insight into its system, and a conception of its mighty harmony.
If the acknowledgment of revelation were a philosophical proposition, it would be of relatively little importance.
But a deep religious interest is at stake in it.
Religion itself is bound up with revelation.
Whoever gives up revelation also loses the religion built upon it.
The revelation of Scripture and the religion of Scripture stand and fall together.
