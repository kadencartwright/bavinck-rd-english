## 8.

In Roman Catholicism this supernaturalistic and dualistic system has been consistently elaborated. In God there are two conceptions of man, of his nature and destiny. Man _in puris naturalibus_, without the image of God, as he in fact still is after the fall, can have a pure knowledge of God from his works; can serve and fear him and stand in a normal relation of servanthood to him that is good in itself; can practice all the natural virtues, even the natural love of God; and can thereby attain a certain state of happiness in this life and in the life to come. If he fails to reach even this, it is his own fault and is attributable to his failure to use, or to his misuse of, the natural powers bestowed upon him. But God wills to give man a still higher, supernatural, heavenly destiny. For that purpose he must grant man _dona superaddita_, both before the fall and now after it. He must bestow on him a supernatural grace by which he can know and love God in another, better, higher way, can practice better and higher virtues, and can attain a higher destiny. That higher knowledge consists in _fides_; that higher love, in _caritas_; those higher virtues are the theological virtues—faith, hope, and love—which are essentially distinct from the _virtutes cardinales_ (_intellectuales et morales_); and that higher destiny consists in sonship with God, birth from God, the _unio mystica_, participation in the divine nature, θεωσις, _deificatio_, the _visio Dei_, and so forth. This doctrine was already prepared in some statements of the church fathers but was first developed by scholasticism, especially by Alexander of Hales, _Summa universae Theol._ II qu. 91 m. 1. a. 3; Bonaventure, _Breviloquium_ V cap. 1; Thomas Aquinas, _Disp. de verit._ qu. 27; _S. Theol._ I 2. qu. 62 art. 1. In the controversy against Baius and Jansenius it was ecclesiastically established, Denzinger, _Enchir. symbol. et definit._ num. 882 sq., and was expressly declared by the _Vaticanum_, Sess. III cap. 2: _revelatio absolute necessaria dicenda est, -- -- quia Deus infinita bonitate sua ordinavit hominem ad finem supernaturalem, ad participanda sc. bona divina. quae humanae mentis intelligentiam superant_, with an appeal to 1 Cor. 2:9. Cf. _Canones_ II 3; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_, 2e Aufl., 1872 II S. 3-151; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ I 105 f., II 75 f.; Scheeben, _Handb. der kath. Dogm._ II 1878, S. 240 f.; idem, _Natur u. Gnade_, Mainz 1861; Schäzler, _Natur u. Gnade_; Oswald, _Religiöse Urgeschichte der Menschheit_, 2e Aufl., 1887 § 1 f.

It must now be acknowledged that the difference between the condition before and after the fall does not lie in revelation as such. There was supernatural revelation in paradise as well, Gen. 1:28 f.; 2:16 f.; it therefore did not first become necessary through sin. Even in the _status integritatis_ there was a revelation of grace, for even then the relation of love into which God entered with man was a manifestation of goodness that he did not owe. What sin made necessary, therefore, was not revelation itself but the particular content of revelation, that is, the _gratia specialis_, the revelation of God in Christ, the ἐνσαρκωσις του θεου. Revelation from God was necessary for religion in the _status integritatis_ as well. But the _religio christiana_ is founded on a special, particular revelation. Paul has this in view when, in 1 Cor. 2:4-16, he speaks of the wisdom of God that had always been hidden and had not entered the heart of man. Rome itself cannot really deny this unless it accepts the doctrine that, on the supposition that God willed to grant man a _finis supernaturalis_, the whole supernatural order that now exists in the incarnation, the church, and the sacraments would have been necessary even without and apart from sin. But then the soteriological character of revelation would be entirely lost, the fall would lose its significance, and sin would have brought no change whatsoever. The doctrine of the _finis supernaturalis_ has therefore always encountered much opposition in the Roman Catholic Church, from Baius, Jansenius, Hirscher, Hermes, and Günther; but it is most closely connected with the entire Roman Catholic system, which is built not on the religious antithesis of sin and grace but on the distinction of degree within the good, on the ranking of creatures and virtues, and on hierarchy in a physical and ethical sense. The Reformation, however, had only one idea, one conception of man—namely, that of the bearer of God’s image—and this applied to all human beings. If that image in the narrower sense is lost, then the whole of human nature is thereby corrupted, and man can no longer have any religion or _ethos_ that answers to God’s demand and to his own ideal. Then his religion and his virtue, however beautiful they may appear, are nevertheless corrupt at the root. There is no _religio naturalis_. All religions have become superstitions. But for that very reason the _religio christiana_ is also essentially one with the _religio vera_ in the _status integritatis_. The Reformed carried this so far that they said that Adam too had possessed the knowledge of the Trinity and faith, that the Logos had even then been _mediator_, not _reconciliationis_ but _unionis_, and that even then the Holy Spirit had been the author of every virtue and power, and so forth. The true thought in this was that God’s will is not arbitrary, forming now one and then another idea of man; that the conception of man, the nature of the image of God, and therefore religion too must be one. And from this it finally followed that revelation was necessary not absolutely but relatively, not _quoad substantiam_ but _quoad modum_. The _religio_ is one before and after the fall; that it is _christiana_, however, became necessary through sin. The _religio christiana_ is a means, not an end; Christ is mediator, but the end is ὁ θεος τα παντα ἐν πασιν, 1 Cor. 15:28.

This further entails that revelation cannot stand in absolute opposition to nature. In Rome there is a quantitative antithesis: the _religio naturalis_ is essentially different from the _religio supernaturalis_; the two stand alongside each other; they are two entirely different conceptions, two wholly distinct systems and orders; the _ordo gratiae_ rises high above the _ordo naturae_. But the Reformation transformed that quantitative antithesis into a qualitative one. Revelation does not stand over against nature but over against sin. Revelation seeks to break the power of sin, but it restores and perfects nature. Creation itself is already revelation. There was revelation before the fall. Revelation still exists now in all the works of God’s hands in nature and history. His eternal power and divinity are understood and perceived from the creatures. Analogies of prophecy and miracle exist outside the _revelatio specialis_ as well. The inspiration of heroes and artists and the wondrous powers that sometimes come to light can serve to elucidate and confirm the facts of revelation recorded in Scripture. Even mantic practices and magic in the pagan religions are phenomena that, as a caricature, still display a resemblance to the original image in revelation. Indeed, revelation itself reaches back into nature, as it were, joins itself to nature, and prepares itself within it. The _gratia communis_ points toward the _gratia specialis_, and the latter takes the former into its service. _Natura commendat gratiam, gratia emendat naturam._ _Even creation itself is built up on redemption lines_, Orr, _The christian view of God and the world_, p. 323. See further my address _De algemeene Genade_, Kampen 1894.
