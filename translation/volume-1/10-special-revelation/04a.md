## 4. Miracles

Just as man makes himself known not only through his appearance and his word but also through his deeds, so God reveals himself not only through his words but also through his works. Word and deed are closely connected. God’s word is a deed, Ps. 33:9, and his doing is a speaking, Ps. 19:2; 29:3; Isa. 28:26. Both word and deed accompany one another in creation as well as in re-creation. Usually the word precedes, as a promise or a threat, but it contains the deed within itself in germ. His word does not return empty but accomplishes what pleases him, Isa. 55:10, 11. The word demands the deed; the miracle accompanies prophecy; not only consciousness but being too must be renewed. The words by which Scripture designates the deeds, the works of God, are diverse. With respect to their outward appearance, they are נִפְלָאוֹת, Exod. 3:20; 34:10; Ps. 71:17; פֶּלֶא, Exod. 15:11; Isa. 25:1, _insignia, ingentia_; or מוֹפְתִים, Exod. 4:21; 7:19; Ps. 105:5, _splendidum quid_; both rendered in Greek by τερατα, something special and unusual that is distinguished from ordinary events. They are called גְּבוּרוֹת, Deut. 3:24; Ps. 21:14; 54:3; 66:7, δυναμεις; מַעֲשִׂים, Ps. 8:7; 19:2; 103:22; Isa. 5:19; or עֲלִילוֹת, Ps. 9:12; 77:13, ἐργα μεγαλεια, because of the great divine power revealed in them. Above all, they are also called אוֹת, Exod. 3:12; 12:13, etc., because they are a proof and sign of the presence of God. These works of God are to be observed first of all in his creation and preservation. All God’s works are miracles. The works of nature too are frequently designated in Scripture by the name of miracles, Ps. 77:13; 97:3; 98:1; 107:24; 139:14. Yet it may not be inferred from this, as Scholten does, _Supranaturalisme in verband met Bĳbel, Christendom en Protestantisme_, Leiden 1867, p. 9 f., that Holy Scripture knows no distinction between nature and miracle. Certainly the thought that a miracle would conflict with the laws of nature and would therefore be impossible does not arise. Rather, the whole of Scripture proceeds from the faith that nothing is too wonderful for God, Gen. 18:14; Deut. 8:3 f.; Matt. 19:26. Yet that does not mean there is no distinction between the ordinary order of nature and the extraordinary acts of God’s power. The Old Testament knows a fixed order of nature, ordinances that apply to heaven and earth and are fixed by the decree of the Lord, Gen. 1:26, 28; 8:22; Ps. 104:5, 9; 119:90, 91; 148:6; Eccles. 1:10; Job 38:10 f.; Jer. 5:24; 31:25 f.; 33:20, 25. And the New Testament makes an equally clear distinction, Matt. 8:27; 9:5, 24, 33; 13:54; Luke 5:9; 7:16; 8:53; John 3:2; 9:32, etc. Miracles are a בְּרִיאָה, a creation, something new that is otherwise never seen, Exod. 34:10; Num. 16:30. The facts recorded as miracles in Holy Scripture are still regarded by us as miracles; there is no disagreement about the characterization of these facts, cf. Herzog² 17:360; Pierson, _Gods wondermacht en ons geestelĳk leven_ 1867, p. 10 f.; Gloatz in _Stud. u. Krit._ 1886, 3tes Heft, S. 403 f.; W. Bender, _Der Wunderbegriff des N. T._, Frankf. 1871, S. 100 f.; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._ 577 f. Furthermore, Scripture indeed acknowledges that unusual powers can operate and unusual things can occur even outside revelation, Exod. 7:11, 22; 8:7, 18; 9:11; Matt. 24:24; Rev. 13:13 f.; thus a sign or miracle is not in itself enough to authenticate a prophet, Deut. 13:1-3. Yet it is the God of Israel alone who performs miracles, Ps. 72:18; 77:15; 86:10; 136:4. Sometimes he brings these miracles about directly himself; sometimes he makes use of human beings or angels. But it is always God who performs them. His δυναμις is revealed in them, Luke 5:17; 14:19; Mark 7:34; Luke 11:20; John 3:2; 5:19 f.; 10:25, 32; Acts 2:22; 4:10. It is the Spirit of the Lord who works them, Matt. 12:28; Acts 10:38.
