As the final form of revelation, internal illumination must still be mentioned. Hengstenberg, _Christol. des A. T._ III² 2, S. 158; cf. also Kueper, _Das Proph._ 53 f., held that ecstasy was the ordinary condition in which the prophet found himself when receiving revelation. But this opinion has been opposed by many, including Riehm, _Mess. Weissagung_², S. 15 f.; König, _Der Off. begriff_ II 48 f., 83 f., 132 f., and is now generally rejected. Ecstasy is not the rule but the exception, Kuenen, _Prof._ I 98; _H. C. O._² II 11. Most revelations to the prophets, even in the Old Testament, took place without any vision—for example, in Isaiah, Haggai, Malachi, Obadiah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. The word “vision” is then still often used for the divine oracle, but this also happens where nothing is seen, Isa. 1:1; 2:1; Amos 1:1; Hab. 1:1; 2:1; 1 Sam. 3:15; Obad. 1; Nah. 1:1, etc. Revelation then occurs inwardly through the Spirit as the Spirit of revelation.

König, _Der Off._ I 104 f., 141 f., 155 f., indeed claimed that the Spirit is not the principle of revelation but only the principle of illumination—that is, that YHWH reveals but the Spirit merely makes the subject receptive to that revelation. König arrived at this because by this means too he wished to maintain the objectivity and externality of revelation and to bind the subjective Spirit to the objective word of YHWH. But Num. 11:25–29; Deut. 34:9; 1 Sam. 10:6; 19:20 f.; 2 Sam. 23:2; 1 Kings 22:24; 1 Chron. 12:18; 28:12; 2 Chron. 15:1; 20:14 f.; 24:20; Neh. 9:30; Isa. 11:1; 30:1; 42:1; 48:16; 59:21; 61:1; 63:10 f.; Ezek. 2:2; 3:24; 8:3; 11:5, 24; Mic. 3:8; Hos. 9:7; Joel 2:28; Zech. 7:12, cannot be understood exclusively of a formal, subjective equipping by the Spirit. They clearly teach that the prophets spoke not only through but from the Spirit, that prophecy proceeded from the Spirit within them.

There was indeed also an activity of the Spirit that subjectively equipped the prophet, but this is not the only activity. It cannot be separated as strictly from the other, revelatory activity as König does; and on König’s standpoint, where revelation is wholly external, it is also unnecessary, Kuenen, _H. C. O._² 14. The lying spirit, 1 Kings 22:22, clearly teaches that the Spirit is the source of the word, Herzog² 16:721. Jewish theology saw in the Spirit not only the source of illumination but also of revelation and prophecy, Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._ 184–187. The New Testament declares just as clearly that the Old Testament prophets spoke from and through the Spirit of God, Acts 28:25; 1 Pet. 1:11; 2 Pet. 1:21.

There is, however, a difference in the manner in which the Holy Spirit inwardly communicates revelation in the Old and New Testaments. Under the Old Testament the Holy Spirit descends from above and momentarily upon someone. He comes upon the prophets, Num. 24:2; 1 Sam. 19:20, 23; 2 Chron. 15:1; 20:14; rushes upon them, Judg. 14:19; 15:14; 1 Sam. 10:6; falls upon them, Ezek. 11:5; clothes them as with a garment, Judg. 6:34; 1 Chron. 12:18; the hand—that is, the power—of the Lord seizes them, Isa. 8:11; Ezek. 1:3; 3:22; 8:1; 37:1; 40:1. Over against this operation of the Spirit the prophets are also generally passive; they are silent, fall to the ground, are terrified, and for a time find themselves in an abnormal, ecstatic condition. The Spirit of prophecy is not yet the prophets’ permanent possession; there is still separation and distance between the two, and the order of prophets still stands apart over against the people.

The whole of prophecy is still imperfect. It therefore also looks forward and expects a prophet upon whom the Spirit of the Lord will rest, Deut. 18:18; Isa. 11:2; 61:1. Indeed, it foretells the fulfillment of Moses’s wish that all the Lord’s people might be prophets, Num. 11:29, and testifies to a future indwelling of God’s Spirit in all the children of the Lord, Isa. 32:15; 44:3; 59:21; Joel 2:28; Ezek. 11:19; 36:27; 39:29.

In the New Testament the highest, the only, the true prophet appears. As Logos he is the full and completed revelation of God, John 1:1 f., 18; 14:9; 17:6; Col. 2:9. He receives no revelation from above or without, but is himself the source of prophecy. The Holy Spirit does not come over him or fall upon him. He dwells in him without measure, John 3:34. From that Spirit he was conceived; through that Spirit he speaks, acts, lives, and dies, Matt. 3:16; 12:28; Luke 1:17; 2:27; 4:1, 14, 18; Rom. 1:4; Heb. 9:14. And he grants that Spirit to his disciples, not only as the Spirit of regeneration and sanctification but also as the Spirit of revelation and illumination, Mark 13:11; Luke 12:12; John 14:17; 15:26; 16:13; 20:22; Acts 2:4; 6:10; 8:29; 10:19; 11:12; 13:2; 18:5; 21:4; 1 Cor. 2:12 f.; 12:7–11.

Through that Spirit particular persons are still equipped for the office of prophet, Rom. 12:7; 1 Cor. 14:3; Eph. 2:20; 3:5, etc. Prediction proper is not lacking in the New Testament either, Matt. 24; Acts 20:23; 21:8; 1 Cor. 15; 2 Thess. 2; Revelation. Yet all believers partake of the anointing of the Spirit, 1 John 2:20, and are taught by the Lord, Matt. 11:25–27; John 6:45. All are prophets who proclaim the virtues of the Lord, Acts 2:17 f.; 1 Pet. 2:9. Prophecy as a special gift will pass away, 1 Cor. 13:8. In the new Jerusalem the name of God will be upon the foreheads of all. The lie is completely excluded from it, Rev. 21:27; 22:4, 15.
