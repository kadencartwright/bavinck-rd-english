But in Christian theology the concept of the supernatural was gradually delimited still more narrowly. It was distinguished on the one hand from creation and on the other from the spiritual miracles of regeneration and so forth. The first distinction was made because the supernatural exists not for God but only for us and presupposes the ordinary order called into being by creation. One can speak of the supernatural only if nature already exists beforehand. And on the other hand, regeneration, forgiveness, sanctification, _unio mystica_, and so forth, were indeed regarded as direct acts of God but were nevertheless not reckoned to supernatural revelation, since they are not unusual and rare but belong to the ordinary _ordo rerum_ in the church. The church itself is indeed supernatural but is nevertheless not a miracle. The supernatural and the miracle are also distinguished from each other. Not everything supernatural is a miracle, but the converse is true. Miracles are not only supernatural but are in addition unusual and rare events in nature or grace. They occur not only _praeter ordinem naturae alicujus particularis_ but _praeter ordinem totius naturae creatae_. Angels and devils cannot perform miracles in the proper sense, but only such things as appear marvelous to us and occur _praeter ordinem naturae creatae nobis notae_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 110 art. 4. _S. c. Gent._ I 6. III 112. Voetius, _Disp._ II. 973 sq. Thomas speaks of miracles not only _praeter_ and _supra_ but also _contra naturam_, _qu. de miraculis_, art. 2 ad 3, Müller, _Natur u. Wunder_, Strassburg 1892 S. 145. And Voetius said that miracles are indeed not _contra naturam universalem sed supra et praeter eam_, but nevertheless could sometimes also be _contra naturam aliquam particularem_, _Disp._ II 973; cf. Gerhard, _Loci Comm._ Loc. 22 § 271 sq. Miracles therefore had the following marks: _opus immediatum Dei, supra omnem naturam, in sensus incurrens, rarum, ad confirmationem veritatis_, Voetius, ib. II 965. Gerhard, ib. loc. 22 § 271. However much good there may be in these definitions and distinctions made by Scholasticism, they nevertheless brought with them no small danger. Special revelation was on the one hand detached from creation and nature; although it was acknowledged that supernatural revelation had taken place not only now but already before the fall, Calovius, _Isag. ad theol._ p. 49, and therefore could not in itself be in conflict with nature, this was nevertheless not thought through. On the other hand, special revelation was set over against the spiritual miracles, the works of grace that continually take place in the church of Christ, and was thus isolated from re-creation and grace. Special revelation therefore came to stand entirely by itself, without connection to nature and history. Its historical and organic character was misunderstood. It did not enter into the world and humanity but remained hovering outside and above them. Finally, it was conceived as a doctrine, as a proclamation of mysteries not understood and not comprehensible, whose truth was confirmed by miracles. In a word, it was and remained a _donum superadditum_ of the cosmos.
