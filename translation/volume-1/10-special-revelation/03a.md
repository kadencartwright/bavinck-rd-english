## 3. Prophecy

By prophecy we understand here God’s communication of his thoughts to man.
The name inspiration is often used for this, and to that extent it is also more precise, since the concept of prophecy is broader than that of inspiration and also includes the proclamation of those thoughts to others.
But on the basis of 2 Tim. 3:16, inspiration is used especially for written revelation.
The word prophecy was also formerly used repeatedly in our sense, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 171 art. 1.
It also includes the reception of thoughts from God, because only one who proclaims God’s word is a prophet.
And better than inspiration, it brings out God’s intention in communicating his thoughts, namely, that man himself should be a prophet, a proclaimer of his excellencies.
The thoughts of God communicated in prophecy can concern the past, as in the historical books of Scripture, or the present, or the future.
But prophecy always sets the thoughts of God over against those of human beings, his truth over against their lie, his wisdom over against their folly.
According to Scripture, this communication of God’s thoughts to man can take place in various ways.
Sometimes God himself speaks audibly, in a human voice and language, Gen. 2:16; 3:8-19; 4:6-16; 6:13; 9:1, 8 f.; 32:26 f.; Ex. 19:9 f.; Num. 7:89; Deut. 5:4; 1 Sam. 3:3 f.; Matt. 3:17; 17:5; John 12:28, 29.
In many passages God is represented as speaking without further description of how that speaking occurred—externally or inwardly, in a dream or vision, etc.
This speaking of God bears its most intimate character with Moses, who is neither terrified nor falls down when God speaks to him, but with whom God spoke mouth to mouth and dealt as a friend with his friend, Num. 12:6-8; Ex. 33:11; 34:29; Deut. 5:5; 18:15, 18; 2 Cor. 3:7; Gal. 3:19; Heb. 3:5.
Cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 174 art. 4; Witsius, _de proph._ I 7; Episcopius, _Instit. Theol._ III 2.
The Jews later spoke of a _Bath-Kōl_, a heavenly voice through which God revealed himself, but it stood lower than the earlier prophecy and had arisen after the spirit of prophecy had ceased, Weber, _System der altsyn. pal. Theol._ 187; Herzog² 2, 130.
But in communicating his thoughts, God frequently accommodates himself to those lower forms in which the gods were also thought by the pagans to make their will known.
There is then an almost complete agreement in form.
Chief among these forms are the lot, the Urim and Thummim, the dream, and the vision.
The lot was used on many occasions: on the great Day of Atonement, Lev. 16:9; in dividing the land, Josh. 13:6; 14:2, etc.; Neh. 11:1; in assigning the Levitical cities, Josh. 21:4; in dividing booty, Joel 3:3; Nah. 3:10; Obad. 11; in distributing garments, Matt. 27:35; John 19:23; in deciding difficult cases, Josh. 7; 1 Sam. 14:42; Prov. 16:33; 18:18; Jonah 1:7; and in election to an office, 1 Sam. 10:19; Acts 1:26; 1 Chron. 24:5; Luke 1:9, etc.
The ordeal, Num. 5:11-31, may also be included here, Herzog² 8, 762.
The Urim and Thummim—LXX δηλωσις και ἀληθεια; Vulg. _doctrina et veritas_; “light and right”—occur 7 times, Ex. 28:30; Lev. 8:8; Num. 27:21; Deut. 33:8; 1 Sam. 28:6; Ezra 2:63; Neh. 7:65.
The Urim and Thummim are not identical with the 12 precious stones on the high priest’s breastpiece, as Josephus, _Ant._ III, 8, 9, and many after him suppose, but, according to Ex. 28:30 and Lev. 8:8, were objects hidden in the breastpiece, Philo, _Vita Mosis_ 3.
But how they made God’s will known—by the glittering of the stones, by a voice, by inspiration, etc.—and what they consisted of—two stones with the tetragrammaton, figurines, a necklace made of precious stones, or stones for casting lots—is wholly unknown.
The last opinion has in modern times gained support from Thenius’s 1842 emendation, following the LXX, of the text of 1 Sam. 14:41.
The Urim and Thummim would then have been lots bearing yes and no and would also have been used in Judg. 1:1; 20:18; 1 Sam. 22:10, 15; 23:6, 9-11; 30:7 f.; 2 Sam. 2:1; 5:19, 23.
But in that connection, answers consisting not of yes and no but of lengthy description and elaboration are difficult to explain, Judg. 20:27; 1 Sam. 30:7 f.; 2 Sam. 5:23; 21:1; Judg. 1:1; 20:18; 2 Sam. 2:1; especially 1 Sam. 10:22b; 2 Sam. 5:23; 1 Chron. 14:14.
The Urim and Thummim certainly belonged to the same category of revelation as the lot; they occur especially in the time of Solomon and then seem to give way to prophecy proper.
Cf. art. “U. en Th.” in Herzog²; Winer, _Realwört._; Riehm, _Wörterb._; Keil, _Archaeol._ § 35; De Wette-Räbiger, _Archaeol._ S. 281 f.; Oehler, _Altt. Th._ S. 334 f.; Schultz, _Altt. Th._ 257 f.; Dosker, _Presbyt. and Ref. Rev._ Oct. 1892 p. 717 etc.

Next, dreams occur in Holy Scripture as a means of revelation.
They were regarded as such throughout antiquity, Homer, _Od._ 19:560 f.; _Il._ 1:63; 2:22, 56; Aristotle, περι της καθ’ ὑπνον μαντικης; Cicero, _de divinatione_ 1:29; Philo, _de somniis_, etc.; Herzog² 15:733.
Even now many attach great value to dreams, Splittgerber, _Schlaf und Tod_, 2e Aufl., 1881, I 66-205.
At all times, however, people also knew that dreams were highly deceptive, Homer, _Od._ 19:560 f.; Aristotle, loc. cit.; and Holy Scripture repeatedly points to the vanity of dreams, Ps. 73:20; Job 20:28; Isa. 29:7; Eccles. 5:2, 6; Sir. 31:1 f.; 34:1 f., and frequently ascribes them to false prophets, Jer. 23:25; 29:8; Mic. 3:6; Zech. 10:2.
Nevertheless, God repeatedly uses dreams to make his will known, Num. 12:6; Deut. 13:1-6; 1 Sam. 28:6, 15; Joel 2:28 f.
They occur among Israelites but repeatedly also among non-Israelites, Gen. 20; 31; 40; 41; Judg. 7; Dan. 2; 4, and contain either a word, a communication from God, Gen. 20:3; 31:9, 24; Matt. 1:20; 2:12, 19, 22; 27:19, or a representation of the imagination that then often requires interpretation, Gen. 28; 37:5; 40:5; 41:15; Judg. 7:13; Dan. 2; 4.
For literature, see Herzog² 15:734; G. E. W. de Wĳs, _De droomen in en buiten den Bĳbel_, 1858; Witsius, _de proph._ I cap. 5.
