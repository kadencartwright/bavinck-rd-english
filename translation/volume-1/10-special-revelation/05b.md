Hamann, Claudius, Lavater, Herder, Jacobi, and others pointed to the affinity between religion and art and connected revelation with the inspiration of genius. The concept of revelation was thus expanded until everything became revelation. Religion, poetry, philosophy, history, and language are different expressions of the same original life. _Omnia divina et humana omnia._ And at the center of all these revelations stands Christ; everything points to him, and everything groups itself around him. Ehrenfeuchter, _Christenthum und moderne Weltanschauung_, Göttingen, 1876, S. 243 f.

Schleiermacher too, _Glaub._ §§ 10, 13, decisively rejected the Rationalistic doctrine of revelation. He sought its distinctive character not in whether it bore a supernatural character but in the new and original quality with which a person or event appears in history. Revelation is therefore akin to poetic and heroic inspiration and consists in fact in awakening new, original affections of religious feeling. Schleiermacher prepared the way for the conception of revelation according to which it consists not in the communication of doctrine but of life. Rothe, _Zur Dogmatik_, 1862, S. 55–120, has the same thought concerning revelation as inspiration, but he further assumes as a constitutive element of revelation an external, historical manifestation, so that the internal revelation, inspiration, may not be magical and mechanical.

The distinctive features of the concept of revelation among theologians who attach themselves more or less to Schleiermacher consist especially in the following. Revelation is to be distinguished from theopneusty, from Holy Scripture; Scripture is not revelation but its record. Revelation is a religious, and more specifically a soteriological, concept, akin to but not identical with the inspiration of genius, poetry, and heroism. It is the correlate of religion alone. Proceeding from God as Redeemer, it does not have as its content doctrine concerning all manner of physical, historical, and metaphysical things, but only religious-ethical truth; it is communication of life, God’s self-communication. It is not supernatural in the strict sense but genuinely natural and human. Finally, it is not merely external (manifestation), but also internal and spiritual (inspiration).

There is still disagreement concerning the beginning, extent, and end of revelation, but in its main features this is nevertheless the conception found in Nitzsch, _System der chr. Lehre_ § 22; Twesten I 341 f.; Martensen §§ 11, 12; Lange I, § 56 f.; Dorner I 569 f.; Frank, _System der chr. Wahrheit_, 2e Aufl., II 8 f.; Kähler, _Wiss. der chr. Lehre_ I 192 f.; Saussaye, _Mĳne theologie_, 35 f.; Gunning and Saussaye, _Het ethisch beginsel_, 21 f., etc.

But it was not only Schleiermacher and his school who brought the concept of revelation to new life. Schelling and Hegel attempted the same in their own way. Through them Rationalism received a speculative character. They did not attempt to destroy Christian revelation by an intellectual critique, but sought speculatively to trace the profound idea underlying it and all Christian dogmas, and thus set themselves as speculative Rationalism over against the vulgar Rationalism of earlier times.

According to Schelling in his first period, the whole world was God’s self-revelation. Nature is visible spirit, spirit invisible nature. God’s being becomes knowable to man from the whole of nature, but especially from the development of the human spirit in art, religion, and science. Hegel likewise taught that God does not reveal himself to man through a passing event in time, but reveals himself in man himself and becomes conscious of himself in man. And this becoming conscious of himself by God in man is man’s knowledge of God, is religion, _Religionsphilosophie_, 1832, I 29; II 158; _Encyclop._, S. 576.

For Hegel, then, revelation is equivalent to necessary self-revelation, to the Absolute’s becoming conscious of itself in the human spirit. The history of religions is the history of the Absolute’s coming to itself in human consciousness and reaches its climax in Christianity, which expresses the unity of God and man. In its main features, the conception of revelation found in the following authors attaches itself to this thought of revelation as God’s self-communication to every human being: Marheineke, _Grundlehren der chr. Dogm._ § 206; Rosenkranz, _Encycl. der theol. Wiss._, 2e Aufl., 1845, S. 1 f.; Erdmann, _Glauben und Wissen_, 1837; Strauss, _Glaubenslehre_ § 19; Feuerbach, _Wesen des Christ._, 2e Ausg., S. 174; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ I 264 f.; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 16; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 52, _Philos. und Religion_, 1885, S. 266 f.; Scholten, _Initia_, ed. 2, pp. 26–39; _L. H. K._ I 165 f., 233, 299. They have in common the denial of the supernatural character of revelation, but otherwise differ greatly concerning its content, extent, and manner.

The concept of revelation in the school of Ritschl differs in turn from both groups. The distinctive feature of this conception lies in the very slight value attached to the distinction between natural and supernatural revelation, the emphasis placed upon the positive character of every religion and upon the historical, external element in revelation, and the fact that for Christianity this historical revelation is found especially or even exclusively in the person of Christ. Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III³ 190 f., 599 f.; Kaftan, _Wesen der chr. Religion_ 171 f., 295 f.; Herrmann, _Der Begriff der Offenbarung_, Giessen, 1887; G. v. Schulthess-Rettberg, _Der Gedanke einer göttlichen Offenbarung_, Zurich, 1893; Nitzsch, _Lehrbuch der ev. Dogm._, S. 131 f. Cf. the Groningen school in our country, Hofstede de Groot, _De Groninger Godgeleerden in hunne eigenaardigheid_, 42 f.

Dogmatics still lacks a distinct and clear concept of revelation. Among theologians there is disagreement about everything that enters into consideration in this concept. Perhaps a boundary can be drawn between those who assume a supernatural revelation and those who assume only a natural one. But even then the question arises whether the supernatural character of revelation lies in the manner in which it came to us or in the new and original character of its content (Schleiermacher). Further, in what does supernatural revelation differ from natural revelation in nature and history, especially from the religious, poetic, and heroic inspiration that is also encountered outside Christianity and has so often been connected with Christian revelation (Hamann, Herder, Jacobi, Schleiermacher)?

Where, next, is that supernatural revelation to be found: also in the religions of the pagans, or only in Israel, or even exclusively in the person of Christ (Schleiermacher, Ritschl)? How far does it extend after Christ? Is it limited to him, or must the operation of the Holy Spirit in regeneration, conversion, and so forth, also be included under the concept of revelation (Frank)? Is its content primarily knowledge, so that it enlightens the understanding (Hegel, Biedermann, Scholten), or is it principally mystical in character—an operation upon the disposition, an affection, an awakening of feeling (Schleiermacher, Lipsius, Opzoomer, the Ethical theologians)? In revelation is the external, the historical, the manifestation—whether in nature (Hegel, Scholten), in history (Schelling, Ritschl), or specifically also as miracle (Rothe)—the principal thing; or does the center of gravity lie in the subject, in the self-revelation of the absolute Spirit, of God, to man (Biedermann, Lipsius)?
