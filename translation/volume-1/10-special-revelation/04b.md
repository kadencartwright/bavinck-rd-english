Miracles have their beginning and foundation in the creation and preservation of all things, which is a continuous work and miracle of God, Ps. 33:6, 9; John 5:17. Everything that happens has its ultimate ground in the will and power of God. Nothing can resist him. He does according to his good pleasure with the host of heaven, Isa. 55:8f.; Ps. 115:3. This power and freedom of God are proclaimed by nature, Jer. 5:22; 10:12; 14:22; 27:5; Isa. 40:12; 50:2, 3; Ps. 33:13-17; 104; Job 5:9f.; 9:4f.; etc., but they come to expression especially in the history of his people, Deut. 10:21; 11:3; 26:8; 29:2; 32:12f.; Ps. 66:5f.; 74:13f.; 77:15f.; 78:4f.; 135:8f.; Isa. 51:2, 9; Jer. 32:20f.; Acts 7:2f. Miracles appear especially in this history. They occur for different purposes. At one time it is to punish the wicked, Gen. 6:6f., 11, 19; Ex. 5f.; Lev. 10:1; Num. 11:30f.; 14:21; 16:1f.; 21:6; etc.; Matt. 8:32; 21:19; Acts 13:11; etc. At another, it is to save and deliver God’s people, to bring salvation and healing, as with the plagues in Egypt, the passage through the Red Sea, the miracles in the wilderness, and the healings of Jesus. They also frequently have the direct or indirect purpose of confirming the mission of the prophets, the truth of their word, and thus faith in their testimony, Ex. 4:1-9; Deut. 13:1f.; Judg. 6:37f.; 1 Sam. 12:16f.; 1 Kings 17:24; 2 Kings 1:10; 20:8; Isa. 7:11; etc.; Matt. 14:33; Luke 5:24; John 2:11; 3:2; 5:36; 6:14; 7:31; 9:16; 10:38; 12:37; Acts 2:22; 10:38; etc. Prophecy and the gift of miracles go together. All the prophets, and the apostles as well, are conscious of being able to perform miracles. Moses was great also in his miracles, Ex. 5-15; Deut. 34:10-12. On one occasion his sin consisted in doubting God’s miraculous power, Num. 20:10f. A cycle of miracles gathers around Elijah and Elisha, 1 Kings 17-2 Kings 13. Among the later prophets, miracles no longer occupy so large a place. They frequently make use of so-called symbolic actions in order thereby to confirm their prophecy and, as it were, initially to realize it, 1 Kings 11:29-39; 20:35f.; 22:11; Isa. 7:3; 8:1; 20:2f.; 21:6; 30:8; Jer. 13; 16; 18; 19; 25:15; 27; 28:10f.; 32:6; 43:8; Ezek. 4; 5; 6:11; 7:23; 12:3; 17:1; Hos. 1-3; Acts 21:10f.; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._ 250f. Smend, _Lehrb. der altt. Rel. Gesch._ 88. König, _Der Offenbarungsbegriff_ II 111f. Nevertheless, miracles are still related of them too, and they have the conviction that they can perform miracles, Isa. 7:11; 16:14; 21:16; 38:7; cf. 2 Kings 20; Jer. 22:12, 30; 28:16; 29:22; 36:30; 37:7f.; Dan. 1-6. But all these miracles in the Old Testament did not effect an elevation, a renewal of nature. They had their effect. They alternately punished and blessed humanity and in every case preserved it from destruction. They created in Israel a distinct people, delivered it from the bondage of Egypt, preserved it from merging with the pagans, and protected it as the people of God against the oppressive power of nature. But they were momentary, passed away, diminished in effect, and were forgotten. Life resumed its ordinary course. Nature appeared to triumph. Then prophecy raised its voice and declared that Israel could not perish and dissolve into the natural life of the pagans. God will come anew and in greater glory to his people. God will not forget his covenant; it is an eternal covenant, Ps. 89:1-5; Isa. 54:10. With this coming of God the old age passes into the new. That is the turning point in world history. It is the יוֹם יהוה, the Day of the Lord, on which he will reveal his glory and display his miraculous power. God will then give miraculous signs in heaven, Amos 8:8f.; Joel 2:30. All nature, heaven and earth, will be moved, Amos 9:5; Isa. 13:10, 13; 24:18-20; 34:1-5; Joel 2:2, 10; 3:15; Mic. 1:3f.; Hab. 3:3f.; Nah. 1:4f.; Ezek. 31:15f.; 32:7f.; 38:19f. Judgment will pass over the wicked, Isa. 24:16f.; etc., but it will also purify and liberate. God will save his people through his miracles, Isa. 9:3; 10:24f.; 11:15f.; 43:16-21; 52:10; 62:8. He does a new thing on the earth, Isa. 43:19, brings Israel back from death, Ezek. 37:12-14, and causes it to share in a fullness of spiritual and material blessings. Forgiveness of sins, holiness, a new covenant, Isa. 44:21-23; 43:25; Ezek. 36:25-28; Jer. 31:31f.; Zech. 14:20, 21, but also peace, security, and prosperity will be its portion. Even nature will change into a paradise, Hos. 2:17f.; Joel 3:18; Jer. 31:6, 12-14; Isa. 11:6-8; 65:25; Ezek. 34:29; 36:29f.; Zech. 8:12. A new heaven and a new earth are coming, and the former things will no longer be remembered, Isa. 65:17; 66:22. This _Jom YHWH_, this עֹלָם הַבָּא, αἰων μελλων, in contrast to the עֹלָם הַזֶּה, αἰων οὑτος, has dawned, according to the representation of Scripture, with the New Testament. The coming of Christ is the turning point of the ages. A new cycle of miracles gathers around his person. He himself is the absolute miracle, descended from above and yet the true, perfect human being. In him creation has in principle been restored once more, raised from its fall to its former glory. His miracles are σημεια of the presence of God, proof of the Messianic age, Matt. 11:3-5; 12:28; Luke 13:16, a part of his Messianic labor. In Christ a divine δυναμις appears that is stronger than every corrupting and destructive power of sin. He attacks this power not only at the periphery by healing diseases and ailments and performing all kinds of miracles, but he penetrates to its center, breaks it, and overcomes it. His incarnation and satisfaction, his resurrection and ascension, are the great acts of deliverance wrought by God. They are the restoration in principle of the kingdom of glory. These saving facts are not only means of revealing something but are themselves the revelation of God. Here miracle becomes history, and history itself is a miracle. The person and work of Christ are the central revelation of God; all other revelation gathers around them. But even after Jesus’ departure, his miraculous power continues in the disciples, Matt. 10; Mark 16:18; Luke 8. And not only are many miracles related in Acts, 2:43; 3:5; 5:12-16; 6:8; 8:6, 7, 13; 9:34, 40; 13:11; 14:3; 16:18; 19:11; 20:10; 28:5, 8, but Paul also testifies to this miraculous power of the apostles, Rom. 15:18, 19; 1 Cor. 12:9, 10; 2 Cor. 12:12; Gal. 3:5; cf. Heb. 2:4. For a time this miraculous power continues in the church. But it ceased when Christianity was established and the church became the object in which God glorifies the miracles of his grace, Augustine, _De civ._ 22:8; _De util. cred._ 16; _De vera relig._ 25. It is in spiritual miracles that God now reveals his power and glory, Luther in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 249f., 341f. Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 143. Nevertheless, Scripture points forward to a future in which miracle will once again exercise its power. The αἰων μελλων is completed only in the new heaven and the new earth, in which righteousness dwells. Then miracle has become nature. _Ethos_ and _physis_ are reconciled.
