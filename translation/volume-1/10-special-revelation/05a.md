### B. The Concept of Special Revelation

## 5.

The system of revelation that Scripture makes known to us has been far too much misunderstood and neglected in Christian theology. Only in modern times did the concept and nature of revelation become the object of deeper investigation. Earlier no need for this was felt. The possibility of revelation was not in dispute between Christians and pagans. The conflict concerned only the truth of that revelation taught in the Old and New Testaments. The apologists argued for it on all manner of grounds against the attacks especially of Celsus and Porphyry.

For the rest, thoughts concerning revelation amounted to this schema: God can be known only through God. All knowledge and service of God therefore rest upon revelation on his part. But the revelation of God in nature and history is insufficient. A special revelation is therefore needed, which reaches its climax in Christ, Harnack, _Lehrb. der Dogmengesch._ I² 420 f., 436 f., 453 f. Subsequent theologians, especially the scholastics, devoted all their care to determining and defining the relation between nature and revelation, knowing and believing, philosophy and theology, but did not think through the concept of revelation and mentioned it only in passing; cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 57, art. 5 ad 3; II 2, qu. 2, art. 6; III, qu. 55, art. 3.

Protestant theologians too devoted too little attention to this concept. They immediately identified revelation with Holy Scripture and did not entirely escape the abstract-supernaturalistic and one-sidedly intellectualistic conception that had gradually been formed of it in theology. Cf. Gerhard, _Loci Theol._ I, § 12; Calovius, _Isagoge ad theol._, pp. 101 sq., 142 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ VI 9; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ I, § 15 sq.; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ XII 46. Socinianism carried this supernatural and intellectual concept of revelation to an extreme, Fock, _Der Socin._ 296 f., 314 f. Remonstrantism had essentially the same conception, Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II 9, 18. There was no difference between Rationalism and Supernaturalism concerning the concept of revelation; for both it consisted in an external communication of doctrine.

It was unsurprising—and fully deserved—that such a concept of revelation could not withstand the criticism of Deism and Rationalism. What was the religious value of a revelation that gave nothing but some intellectual truth which reason itself might perhaps later have discovered? Yet it became apparent that people had disposed of the concept of revelation far too quickly. On deeper historical and philosophical investigation, religion and revelation displayed a much closer affinity than had earlier been supposed. Thus the concept of revelation came once again to receive greater honor in modern theology and philosophy, and various attempts at reconstruction were undertaken.
