## 6.

The revelation that Scripture makes known to us does not consist of a few disconnected words and facts, but is one historical and organic whole, a mighty, world-governing and world-renewing system of acts of God. As we have seen, it appears in three forms: theophany, prophecy, and miracle, שְׁכִינָה, ‏נְבוּיָה‎ and ‏בְּרִיאָה‎. But these three do not stand loosely alongside one another; they form one whole and together intend one goal.

Already through the _revelatio generalis_ God does not leave himself without witness to man. He reveals himself in nature and history; he speaks in heart and conscience; he performs wonders of power. The _revelatio generalis_ too may be called revelation. For in the broad sense revelation is every action that proceeds from God to place man in a religious relation to him. But through sin another revelation becomes necessary, one that indeed stands in many connections with the _revelatio generalis_ but nevertheless differs from it essentially, in form and content. For it addresses fallen man and therefore must be a revelation of grace.

The _revelatio specialis_ is God’s seeking of and coming to man. He must now reveal himself, speak, and work in such a way that man is renewed again according to his image. God therefore comes to man in a human manner. Incarnation is the central fact in the _revelatio specialis_ and sheds light over its entire domain. Already in creation God makes himself like human beings. But in re-creation he becomes man and enters into our condition. And this incarnation, which is the proper content of the _revelatio specialis_, in a certain sense begins immediately after the fall.

God’s special revelation enters into history and forms a history that runs through the ages. It assumes such a historical character because humanity itself, to which it is directed, exists only in historical form. It shares humanity’s life, follows it along its paths, and walks with it through the ages to the end. It reaches deeply back into the life of creation, connects itself with providence, and causes its light to shine through the prism of human persons, conditions, and events. It employs every character and individuality, every disposition and gift given in creation. It clothes itself in the forms of type and shadow, image and symbol, art and poetry, epistle and chronicle. In religion it takes over practices found in other religions, such as circumcision and sacrifice, temple and priesthood. It considers even lot, dream, and vision not too lowly to employ as instruments. So deeply does the divine descend into the human that the boundaries between revelation and pseudoreligion seem to be effaced. Divine oracle and pagan oracle, prophecy and manticism, miracle and magic seem to approach one another.
