Each of those powers works according to its own nature, its own law, and in its own manner. The powers differ, and therefore so do their operations and the manner in which they work. The idea of a law of nature arose only gradually. Formerly _lex naturae_ was understood as an ethical rule known by nature. Later this term was transferred to nature in a highly improper sense, for no one prescribed those laws to nature, and no one is capable of obeying or transgressing them. Hence great disagreement still prevails concerning the concept and meaning of natural laws. _Im 17ten Jahrhundert giebt Gott die Naturgesetze, in 18ten thut es die Natur selbst, und im 19ten besorgen es die einzelnen Naturforscher_ (Wundt).

But this much is certain: the so-called natural laws are not themselves a power that exercises dominion over phenomena, but nothing more than an often very defective and always fallible description of the manner in which the powers present in nature operate. A natural law says only that definite powers under equal circumstances work in the same manner. Ed. Zeller, _Vorträge_, IIIe Sammlung, S. 194 f.; Wundt, _Philos. Studien_ III 195 f., IV 12 f.; Hellwald, _Culturgeschichte_, 3e Aufl., I 32; Hartmann, _Philos. des Unbewussten_, 9e Aufl., II 96; Lotze, _Mikrokosmos_, 4e Aufl., I 31 f., II 50 f., III 13 f.; art. “Naturgesetz” in Herzog².

The regularity of phenomena therefore rests ultimately upon the immutability of the various powers at work in nature and of the final elements or substances from which nature is composed. The laws differ as those elements and powers differ. Mechanical laws differ from physical laws; logical laws in turn differ from ethical and aesthetic laws. In the physical sense giving makes one poorer; in the ethical sense it makes one richer. The laws of nature—that is, of the whole cosmos, of every creature—are therefore not a cordon around things through which nothing can enter or leave, but only a formula for the manner in which, according to our observation, every power works in accordance with its nature.

According to the Theistic worldview, all these elements and powers with the laws indwelling them are maintained from moment to moment by God, who is the ultimate and highest, intelligent and free causality of all things. As creatures they possess no subsistence in themselves. It is God’s omnipresent and eternal power that maintains and governs everything. In him, in his thought and his government, lies the unity, the harmony, that holds and binds all things together in the richest diversity and leads them toward one goal. Thereby there are _unitas, mensura, ordo, numerus, modus, gradus, species_ in the creatures, as Augustine repeatedly says. _Aliis dedit esse amplius, aliis minus, atque ita naturas essentiarum gradibus ordinavit_, Augustine, _De civ._ 12, 2.

God is present in everything. In him all things live and move and have their being. Nature and history are his work. He is always working, John 5:17. Everything reveals God to us. His finger may be more clearly discernible for us in one event than in another; the pure in heart sees God in all his works. Miracles are therefore by no means necessary to make God known to us as sustainer and governor of the universe. Everything is his act. Nothing happens without his will. He is present with his being in all things. And therefore everything is also a revelation, a word, a work of God.
