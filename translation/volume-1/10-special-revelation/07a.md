### C. Special Revelation and Supernaturalism

## 7.

This organic conception of revelation has been misunderstood in the Christian church in two ways, both by Supernaturalism and by Naturalism (Rationalism). It must therefore be more fully elucidated and maintained over against both. First, over against Supernaturalism, which arose especially in Rome and then continued to work within various tendencies in Protestantism.

Scripture does indeed know a distinction between the ordinary course of things and extraordinary works of God, but does not yet make the opposition between natural and supernatural. This occurs first among the church fathers. Special revelation is identified with supernatural revelation and set over against natural revelation. Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ 2, 2, already speaks of a ὑπερφυης θεωρια obtained through faith. Chrysostom, _Hom._ 36 _in Gen._, calls miracles ὑπερ φυσιν and φυσει μειζονα. Ambrose, _De mysteriis_ c. 9, sets _gratia_, _miraculum_, _mysterium_ over against the _ordo naturae_. John of Damascus repeatedly speaks of miracles such as the conception of Christ, the Eucharist, and so forth, as ὑπερ φυσιν, ὑπερ λογον και ἐννοιαν, _De fide orthod._ IV 12–15. Cf. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ I 82 f.

Since then the distinction between natural and supernatural has found acceptance and acquired full citizenship throughout Christian theology. Without doubt this distinction also has a right to exist. Scripture may not make it in so many words, but it nevertheless acknowledges an ordinary order of nature and alongside it acts and works that have their cause solely in the power of God. The revelation in Holy Scripture presupposes that there is still another, higher and better world than this nature and thus that there is an _ordo rerum supra hanc naturam_. The concepts of natural and supernatural must therefore be clearly defined.

Nature, from _nasci_, to become, designates in general that which develops solely according to its internal powers and laws, without an alien power or influence from without. Nature then even stands over against art, education, culture, and history, which do not arise of themselves, spontaneously, but come about through human agency. Further, however, the concept of nature is extended to the whole cosmos insofar as it moves and develops not from without but from within, through immanent powers and according to its own implanted laws. Supernatural, then, is everything that transcends the powers of the _natura creata_ and has its cause not in creatures but in the omnipotence of God.

In this sense special revelation was conceived in Christian theology. Taken as a whole, it had its origin in a special act of God which manifested itself not in the ordinary course of nature but in an order of things of its own, distinct from it. A further distinction was then made between the supernatural in the absolute sense, as something that surpasses the power of every creature, and the supernatural in the relative sense, as something that exceeds the power of a particular cause under the given circumstances. A distinction was also made between the _supernaturale quoad substantiam_, when the fact itself is supernatural—for example, the raising of a dead person—and the _supernaturale quoad modum_, when only the manner of action is supernatural—for example, the healing of a sick person without means, Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ III 101.

In themselves these distinctions still involved no danger. They must even be defended over against a philosophy that denies or weakens the supernatural. The expression “supernatural,” namely, has often been understood in a greatly modified sense in later theology and philosophy and has been identified in turn with the supersensible (Kant), the free (Fichte), the unknown (Spinoza, Wegscheider), the new and original (Schleiermacher), the religious-ethical, the spiritual (Saussaye), and so forth. But such a modification of the settled and clear meaning of a word leads to misunderstanding. If by supernatural one means nothing other than the supersensible, the ethical, and so forth, one does better to avoid the term. And the confusion becomes still greater if the natural and supernatural are mingled together and this fusion is then given the name _Geistleibliche_, God-human, and so forth.

The concept of nature comprises everything created: not only substance, matter, but also soul and spirit; not only physical but also psychical, religious, and ethical life insofar as it arises of itself from the human disposition; not only visible but also invisible, supersensible things. The supernatural is not identical with the original, the genial, the free, the religious, the ethical, and so forth, but is the clear and settled name only for that which cannot be explained from the powers and according to the laws of created things. To this extent the distinction between natural and supernatural that arose in Christian theology is entirely correct, decisive, clear, and cuts off all confusion. Subject to what was said above, pp. 230 f., special revelation, in its three forms of theophany, prophecy, and miracle, is supernatural in the strict sense.
