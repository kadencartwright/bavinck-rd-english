# § 10. Special Revelation

### A. The Means of Special Revelation

## 1.

History teaches that no religion is satisfied with general revelation. The Christian religion too appeals to a special revelation. Scripture is the book of the _revelatio specialis_. The words by which it expresses the concept of revelation are principally the following: גלה, to uncover; Niphal, to be uncovered, show oneself, appear, be revealed, Gen. 35:7; 1 Sam. 2:27; 3:21; Isa. 53:1; 56:1; Hos. 7:1, etc.; ראה, to see; Niphal, to be seen, show oneself, appear, Gen. 12:7; 17:1; 18:1, etc.; ידע, to know; Niphal, Piel, Hiphil, Hithpael, to make known, instruct, Num. 12:6; ἐπιφαινειν, to appear, Luke 1:79; Titus 1:11; the noun ἐπιφανεια, appearance, especially of Christ at his return, 2 Thess. 2:8; 1 Tim. 6:14; 2 Tim. 4:1; Titus 2:13, and at 2 Tim. 1:10 of Christ’s first coming; ἐμφανιζειν, to make manifest or visible, and in the passive to show oneself, appear, Matt. 27:53; John 14:21, 22; γνωριζειν, to make known, Luke 2:15; Rom. 9:22; Eph. 3:3, 5, 10; δηλουν, to make known, 1 Pet. 1:11; 2 Pet. 1:14; δεικνυναι, to show, John 5:20; λαλειν, to speak, Heb. 1:1; 2:2; 5:5; and especially also ἀποκαλυπτειν and φανερουν.

These last two words cannot be distinguished as subjective, inward illumination and objective, outward appearance or manifestation, as Scholten, _L. H. K._, 4e dr., I 165 f., 299; _Dogm. Christ. Initia_ I 26 sq., supposed. For ἀποκαλυπτειν is repeatedly used of objective revelation, Luke 17:30; Rom. 1:17, 18; 8:18; Eph. 3:5; 2 Thess. 2:3, 6, 8; 1 Pet. 1:5; 5:1. Nor does the distinction lie in φανερουν designating God’s general revelation in nature and ἀποκαλυπτειν the special revelation of grace, Neander, _Gesch. der Pflanzung und Leitung der christl. Kirche durch die Apostel_, 5te Aufl., Gotha, Perthes, 1862, S. 131 f. For φανερουν is often used of special revelation, John 17:6; Rom. 16:26; Col. 1:26; 1 Tim. 3:16; 2 Tim. 1:10, etc.; and ἀποκαλυπτειν occurs at Rom. 1:18 also of general revelation. A constant difference in the use of the two words is difficult to demonstrate in the New Testament.

Etymologically, however, ἀποκαλυπτειν denotes the removal of the covering by which an object was hidden, and φανερουν the manifestation of a matter that was hidden or unknown. In the former, therefore, the emphasis falls on removing the impediment that prevented knowledge of the hidden thing; on the mysterious character of that which had not hitherto been understood; and on the divine act that removed that covering and caused the mystery to be understood. The latter word indicates in general that something which was hidden and unknown has now become manifest and entered into the light. Ἀποκαλυψις removes the cause by which something was hidden; φανερωσις makes the matter itself manifest.

Connected with this is the fact that φανερωσις is always used of objective revelation, while ἀποκαλυψις is used of both objective and subjective revelation; that φανερωσις repeatedly designates both general and special revelation, but ἀποκαλυψις almost always special and only on a single occasion general revelation. The two words in turn differ from γνωριζειν and δηλουν in this: the former two cause things to enter the light and bring them under perception, while the latter two, as a consequence, also make them the content of our thinking consciousness.
