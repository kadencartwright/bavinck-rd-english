The Christian religion thus agrees with all historical religions in appealing to revelation. But the agreement extends still further, even to the forms and modes in which revelation occurs. All means of revelation can be reduced to three. In the first place, religious faith desires a God who is near and not far away, Acts 17:27; it has therefore at all times been convinced of the appearance of the gods in one form or another, under one sign or another, in one place or another. Holy places, holy times, and holy images are found in almost every religion. The gods are not equal to human beings and do not live with them on equal terms; the profane sphere is separated from the sacred. Yet the gods dwell with and among human beings in particular places and special objects, and communicate their blessing at particular times. Idolatry, understood in the broadest sense, was born from the need for a God who is near, Ch. de la Saussaye, _Lehrb. der Rel. gesch._ I 54 f., 114 f.

In the second place, all religions possess the belief that the gods reveal their thought and will in one way or another, whether through human beings as their organs—such as soothsayers, oracles, dreamers, necromancers, spirit-seers, and so forth—or artificially and externally through the stars, the flight of birds, the entrails of sacrificial animals, the play of the flame, the lines of the hand, the chance opening and falling open of a book, and so forth: μαντικη, _divinatio_. _Nemo vir magnus sine afflatu divino unquam fuit_, Cicero, _De nat. deor._ 2, 66; cf. Cicero, _De divinatione_; Plutarch, _De defectu oraculorum_; A. Bouché-Leclercq, _Histoire de la divination dans l’antiquité_, 4 vol., 1879–82; Saussaye, op. cit., 93 f.

Finally, all religions contain belief in special intervention and help from the gods in times of need. Magic is universally widespread—that is, the art by which human beings, through mysterious means, sacred words, formulas, amulets, potions, and so forth, make divine power subservient to themselves and produce wondrous effects. Ennemoser, _Gesch. der Magie_, 2e Aufl., Leipzig, 1844; Alfr. Maury, _La magie et l’astrologie dans l’antiquité et au moyen-âge_, 1860; Lenormant, _Les sciences occultes en Asie_, 2 vol., 1874–’75; Saussaye, ibid. Theophany, manticism, and magic are the ways along which all revelation comes to man.

This universal religious belief in appearance, prediction, and miracle certainly cannot be explained—at any rate, not exclusively—from deception or demonic activity, nor from unfamiliarity with the order of nature; it is a necessary element in all religion. Religious need seeks satisfaction; and where it does not find this in a real revelation of God that comes to meet it, it seeks it by the way of ἐθελοθρησκεια. It enlists those mysterious powers in man himself or outside him in nature which can bring him into contact with a supernatural world. Superstition is the bastard form of genuine religion. Superstitious belief is the caricature of πιστις.

The present-day phenomena of Spiritism, Theosophy, telepathy, magnetism, hypnotism, and so forth provide evidence of this, and perhaps also show that in the so-called night side of human nature there lie concealed powers which can bring about a more immediate contact with a supersensible world and which, in any event, can sufficiently explain belief in such contact without the hypothesis of deliberate deception and so forth. _There are more things in heaven and earth, than are dreamt of in your philosophy_ (Shakespeare). Cf. art. “Modern Bĳgeloof” in _Tĳdspiegel_, Jan. 1895.

Holy Scripture does not appear to deny all reality to such phenomena, Gen. 41:8; Ex. 7:8; Deut. 13:1, 2; Matt. 7:22; 24:24; 2 Thess. 2:9; 2 Tim. 3:8; Rev. 13:13–15. But the religion in the Old and New Testaments decidedly wishes to have nothing in common with all these religious phenomena. It stands opposed to them in principle. It neither acknowledges nor tolerates them and prohibits them most emphatically, Lev. 19:26, 31; 20:27; Num. 23:23; Deut. 18:10, 11; Acts 8:9; 13:6; 16:16; 19:13 f.; Gal. 5:20; Rev. 21:8; 22:15. Prophets and apostles object most strongly to being placed on the same level as pagan soothsayers and magicians.

There may sometimes be correspondence in form—for example, in the appearances to the patriarchs—but there is a difference in essence. Theophany, manticism, and magic, like sacrifice, temple, priesthood, cultus, and so forth, are essential elements in religion. They therefore occur in all religions, including that of Israel and in Christianity. The Christian religion too has its sacrifice, Eph. 5:2; its priest, Heb. 7; its temple, 1 Cor. 3:16, and so forth. The difference between Christianity and the other religions does not lie in the absence there of all these necessary elements of religion, but in this: everything that occurs as caricature in paganism became shadow and image in Israel and true, spiritual reality here.

This explains how Israel’s religion, on the one hand, displays so much correspondence in form—circumcision, sacrifice, tabernacle, priesthood, and so forth—with the pagan religions and, on the other hand, differs from them in principle, so that the Messiah proceeded from Israel alone. This principial difference lies in the fact that in Holy Scripture the initiative in religion is taken not by man but by God. In the pagan religions it is man who seeks God, Acts 17:27. In all manner of ways he attempts to cause God to descend to him and draws him down into matter, Rom. 1:23; through all manner of means he endeavors to obtain power over God. But in Scripture it is always God who seeks man. He creates man in his image. He calls him after the fall. He preserves Noah. He chooses Abraham. He gives Israel his laws. He calls and equips the prophets. He sends his Son. He sets apart the apostles. He will one day judge the living and the dead.

Paganism teaches us to know man in his restlessness, his misery, his lack of peace, and also in his noble aspirations and his eternal needs; man both in his poverty and his wealth, in his weakness and his strength. Paganism cultivates its noblest fruit in Humanism. But Holy Scripture teaches us to know God in his coming to and seeking of man, in his mercy and grace, in his justice and his love. Yet here too theophany, prophecy, and miracle are the means by which God reveals and gives himself to man. Oehler, _Ueber das Verhältniss der altt. Prophetie zur heidn. Mantik_, 1861; idem, _Altt. Theologie_, 1882, S. 29 f., 753 f.; Tholuck, _Die Propheten u. ihre Weissagungen_, 1860, § 1; Staudenmaier, _Encycl._ § 231 f., § 271 f.; Schultz, _Altt.
