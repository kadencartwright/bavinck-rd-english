## 12.

Supernatural revelation is in no way in conflict with such a worldview. For here nature does not exist for a single moment independently of God but lives and moves in him. Every power that appears within it originates from him and operates according to the law that he has placed within it. God does not stand outside nature and is not shut off from it by a fence of laws, but is present within it and upholds it by the word of his power. He works from within and can cause new powers to appear that differ in nature and operation from those already existing. And these higher powers do not nullify the lower ones but nevertheless occupy a place of their own alongside and over against them. The human spirit seeks at every moment to counteract the lower powers of nature in their operation and to rule over them. The whole of culture is a power by which the human being rules over nature. Art and science are a triumph of spirit over matter. In the same way, in revelation, in prophecy and miracle, a new divine power appears that indeed occupies a place of its own in the cosmos but in no way conflicts with the lower powers in their laws. There is no question of a so-called suspension of the laws of nature by miracles. There is no _Durchlöcherung_ of nature. Thomas already said: _quando Deus agit aliquid contra cursum naturae, non tollitur totus ordo universi, sed cursus qui est unius rei ad aliam_, _de pot._ qu. 6 art. 1, in Müller, _Natur und Wunder_ 133. Indeed, even the _ordo causae ad suum effectum_ is not nullified; although the fire in the furnace did not burn the three young men, the _ordo ad comburendum_ nevertheless remained in that fire. No change is introduced by a miracle into the powers that lie in nature or into the laws according to which they operate. The only thing that happens in a miracle is that the operation of the powers present in nature is suspended at a particular point because another power enters, one that operates according to a law of its own and produces an effect of its own. Science therefore has nothing to fear from the supernatural. But every science should remain within its own field and not arrogate to itself the right to lay down the law for another. It is the right and duty of natural science to seek within its domain the natural causes of phenomena. But it must not rule over philosophy when the latter investigates the origin and destiny of things. It must also acknowledge the right and independence of religion and theology and must not undermine the foundation on which they rest. For here religious motives for belief in a revelation come into play, concerning which natural science as such cannot judge. Among the various sciences too, the goal lies not in uniformity but in harmony. Theology should honor natural science but itself lay claim to equal treatment. Every science should remain within its own field. The erasure of boundaries has already caused far too much confusion. From it also arose the assertion of Hume, Voltaire, and Renan that no miracle has ever yet been sufficiently established and that constant experience cannot be overthrown by a few testimonies. Renan, _Vie de Jésus_, p. LI, says: _nous ne disons pas, le miracle est impossible, nous disons, il n’ y a pas eu jusqu’ici de miracle constaté_, and refuses to believe in a miracle until a commission of all kinds of scientists—physiologists, chemists, and so forth—has investigated such a fact and, after repeated experimentation, established it as a miracle. Under such a condition the miracle is judged _a priori_, for in the case of the miracles of Scripture the opportunity for such an experiment is granted neither to Renan nor to any of us. Miracles belong once and for all to history, and in history a different method applies than in natural science. In the latter, experiment is in its proper place. But in history we must make do with testimonies. If, however, the experimental method must be introduced and applied in the historical domain, there is not a single fact that can stand the test. Then all history is at an end. Every science should therefore remain within its own field and investigate there according to its own nature. One cannot see with the ear, weigh with a measuring rod, or test revelation by experiment, Vigouroux, _Les livres saints et la critique rationaliste_, 3e ed. 1890 I 73. II 294.
