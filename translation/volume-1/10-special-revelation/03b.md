The vision is related to the dream, Gen. 15:1, 11; 20:7; Num. 12:6. The very names רֹאֶה‎, ‏חֹזֶה‎, ‏נָבִיא and perhaps also צֹפֶה by which the prophet is called—Kuenen, _De Profeten_ I 49, 51 f., 97; idem, _Godsd. v. Isr._ I 212; idem, _Hist. Cr. Ond._ II² 5 f.; König, _Der Offenbarungsbegriff_ I 71 f.; Delitzsch, _Genesis_³ 634; Schultz, _Altt. Th._ 239; Smend, _Lehrb._ 79 f.—and the names מַרְאֶה and חָזוֹן for the prophetic sight probably indicate that the vision was not an unusual means of revelation. But these words have often lost their original meaning and are also used where no vision proper takes place, 1 Sam. 3:15; Isa. 1:1; Obad. 1; Nah. 1:1, etc.

Visions are repeatedly mentioned and described in Scripture, from Genesis through Revelation: Gen. 15:1; 46:2; Num. 12:6; 22:3; 24:3; 1 Kings 22:17–23; Isa. 6; 21:6; Jer. 1:24; Ezek. 1–3; 8–11; 40; Dan. 1:17; 2:19; 7; 8; 10; Amos 7–9; Zech. 1–6; Matt. 2:13, 19; Luke 1:22; 24:23; Acts 7:55; 9:3; 10:3, 10; 16:9; 22:17; 26:19; 1 Cor. 12–14; 2 Cor. 12:1; Rev. 1:10, etc. The vision was often accompanied by a certain transport of spirit. Music, dance, and ecstasy go together; prophecy and poetry are related, 1 Sam. 10:5 f.; 19:20–24; 2 Kings 3:15; 1 Chron. 25:1, 25; 2 Chron. 29:30. When the hand of the Lord falls upon the prophets, Isa. 8:11; Ezek. 3:14; 11:5, or the Spirit comes over them, they often enter a state of rapture, Num. 24:3; 2 Kings 9:11; Jer. 29:26; Hos. 9:5, and fall to the ground, Num. 24:3, 15, 16; 1 Sam. 19:24; Ezek. 1:28; 3:23; 43:3; Dan. 10:8–10; Acts 9:4; Rev. 1:17; 11:16; 22:8.

In that condition the thoughts of God are given them to see or hear in symbolic form. In images and visions his counsel is revealed to them, Jer. 1:13 f.; 24:1 f.; Amos 7–9; Zech. 1–6; Revelation, etc.; especially concerning the future, Num. 23 f.; 1 Kings 22:17; 2 Kings 5:26; 8:11 f.; Jer. 4:23 f.; 14:18; Ezek. 8; Amos 7, etc. In that condition they also hear all manner of voices and sounds, 1 Kings 18:41; 2 Kings 6:32; Isa. 6:3, 8; Jer. 21:10; 49:14; Ezek. 1:24, 28; 2:2; 3:12; Rev. 7:4; 9:16; 14:2; 19:1; 21:3; 22:8, etc. They are even taken up and transported in the spirit, Ezek. 3:12 f.; 8:3; 43:1; Dan. 8:2; Matt. 4:5, 8; Acts 9:10; 10, 11; 22:17; 23:11; 27:23; 2 Cor. 12:2; Rev. 1:9, 12; 4:1; 12:18. After receiving a vision Daniel was ill for several days, 7:28; 8:27.

Yet the ecstasy in which the recipients of revelation often found themselves was not a state in which consciousness was wholly or partly suppressed. Such indeed was the condition in which the Greek μαντεις gave their oracles, Tholuck, _Die Propheten u. s. w._ 64 f. And Philo, _Quis rer. div. heres_; Justin Martyr, _Dial. c. Tryph._ c. 135; _Coh. ad Graecos_ c. 37; Athenagoras, _Leg. pro Christ._ c. 8; Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ 4, 22; and in modern times Hengstenberg in the first edition of his _Christol. des A. T._ III 2, 158 f., understood the ecstasy of the prophets in this way. But the prophets receive visions not in a sleeping but in a waking state, and not only in solitude but also in the presence of others, Ezek. 8:1. During the vision they remain conscious of themselves; they see, hear, think, speak, ask, and answer, Ex. 4–6; 32:7 f.; Isa. 6; Jer. 1; Ezek. 4–6, etc.; and later remember everything and communicate it accurately, König, _Der Offenbarungsbegriff_ I 160 f.; II 83 f.; Kuenen, _De profeten_ I 96 f.; Oehler, _Altt. Theol._ § 207 f.; Orelli in Herzog² 16:724.

The psychical condition of the prophets during the vision was therefore regarded by most theologians as a self-conscious, spiritual intuition, as an _alienatio mentis a sensibus corporis_, and not as an _alienatio a mente_. Thus, among others, Origen, _De princ._ III, 3, 4; Augustine, _Ad Simplic._ II, qu. 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 175; Witsius, _De proph._ I, c. 4; Buddeus, _Inst. theol. dogm._ I, 2, 5; and in modern times Hävernick and Keil in their introductions to the Old Testament; Oehler, _Altt. Theol._ § 210; Tholuck, _Die Propheten_, S. 64 f.; Kueper, _Das Profetenthum des Alten Bundes_, S. 51 f.; Orelli in Herzog² 16, 724; König, _Offenb._ II 132 f.

Only König, in order to maintain objectivity, added to this the peculiar opinion that all visions were external, corporeal, and sensibly perceptible. Indeed, many appearances, such as Gen. 18; 32; Ex. 3; 19, and so forth, are to be regarded as objective according to the intention of the writers. There is a distinction between theophany and vision. Yet the visions mentioned above—1 Kings 22:17 f.; Isa. 6; Jer. 1; Ezek. 1–3; Daniel; Amos 7–9; Zech. 1–6, and so forth—are certainly internal and spiritual. Many are of such a nature that they cannot be sensibly represented and perceived. König goes too far when he makes the objectivity and truth of revelation depend upon its externality and can conceive no operation of God’s Spirit in the human spirit except through the external senses. He forgets that there are also hallucinations of sight and hearing, that externality as such does not yet exclude self-deception, and that the certainty of revelation is therefore not sufficiently proved by its external character alone.
