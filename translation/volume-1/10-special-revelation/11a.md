## 11.

The worldview that stands over against that of Scripture and must oppose all revelation in principle can best be designated by the name Monism. Monism, in both its pantheistic and its materialistic form, strives to reduce all powers, substances, and laws discernible in nature to one single power, substance, and law. Materialism assumes only qualitatively identical atoms which work everywhere and always according to the same mechanical laws and through combination and separation cause all things and phenomena to arise and pass away. Pantheism likewise acknowledges nothing but one single substance, which is the same in all creatures and everywhere modifies and transforms itself according to the same logical laws.

Both are animated by the same impulse, the drive and desire for unity proper to the human mind. But whereas Materialism seeks to rediscover in all other historical, psychical, religious, ethical, and other phenomena the unity of substance and law that reigns in the physical world, and thus attempts to turn all sciences into natural science, Pantheism attempts to explain all phenomena, including the physical, from spirit and to transform all sciences into spiritual science. Both are Naturalism insofar as they may still make room for the supersensible, but in any case not for the supernatural, and insofar as for science and art, religion and morality, they are content with this cosmos, with the _diesseits_. Strauss, _Der alte u. der neue Glaube_, 2e Aufl., I 211 f.; E. Haeckel, _Der Monismus als Band zwischen Religion und Wissenschaft_, 6e Aufl., Bonn, 1893; Konrad Dieterich, _Philosophie u. Naturwissenschaft, ihr neuestes Bündniss und die monistische Weltanschauung_, 2tes Ausg., Freiburg, 1885; Dr. M. L. Stern, _Philosophischer und naturwissenschaftlicher Monismus_; T. Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_, 2e Aufl., Herder, Freiburg, II 8 f.; Stöckl, _Lehrbuch der Philos._ II, 1887, S. 117 f.; Schanz, _Apologie des Christ._ I, 1887, S. 249 f.

The worldview of Scripture and of all Christian theology is wholly different. It is not Monism but Theism, not Naturalistic but supernatural. According to this Theistic worldview there is a multiplicity of substances, of powers and materials and laws. It does not seek to erase the distinctions between God and world, spirit and matter, psychical and physical, ethical and religious phenomena, and so forth, but to discover the harmony that holds and binds all things together and that is the outflow of God’s creative thought. Not sameness or uniformity but unity in diversity is the goal of its striving.

Despite all the pretensions of Monism, this Theistic worldview has a right and reason to exist. For Monism has not succeeded in reducing all powers, substances, and laws to one. Materialism founders upon psychical phenomena (Du Bois-Reymond, _Die sieben Welträthsel_), and Pantheism cannot find the transition from thought to being and does not know what to do with multiplicity. Being itself is a mystery, a miracle. That something exists, and whence it exists, compels wonder from the thinking mind; wonder has therefore rightly been called the beginning of philosophy. And the more deeply being is thought through, the more wonder increases. For within the circle of being, of the cosmos, we see different powers appear in the mechanical, vegetative, animal, and psychical world, and further in religious, ethical, aesthetic, and logical phenomena.

Creation displays an ascending order. The laws of the conservation of energy, of causality, and of continuity (_natura non facit saltus_) are indeed interpreted and misused in the service of Monism. Nevertheless, powers continually appear in nature that cannot be explained from the lower ones. Already in mechanical nature causality reigns only in a hypothetical sense. Equal causes have equal effects, but only under equal circumstances. In the organic realm a power appears that does not originate from the inorganic. _Schon das Thier ist ein Wunder gegen die vegetabilische Natur und noch mehr der Geist gegen das Leben, gegen die bloss empfindende Natur_, Hegel, _Philos. der Rel._, _Werke_ XII 256.

In the understanding, the will, religion, morality, art, science, law, and history, powers are at work which differ essentially from the mechanical. The attempt to explain all these phenomena mechanically has thus far proved vain. The spiritual sciences have until now retained their independent place. Dilthey, _Einleitung in die Geisteswissenschaften_ I, 1883, S. 5 f.; Drummond, _Das Naturgesetz in der Geisteswelt_, aus dem Engl., Leipzig, 1886, S. 18 f. Reluctantly, Prof. Land, _Inleiding tot de Wĳsb._, 328, nevertheless has to acknowledge that for the present science is compelled to remain dualistic and even pluralistic.
