Further, nature, the cosmos, is still far too often conceived as a machine that is fully finished and is now driven by one force and always moves according to one law. Deism possessed this clumsy conception, but it is still unconsciously shared by many and serves in the opposition to revelation. Nature, the cosmos, however, is not a piece of work that is finished and now possesses a certain independence. Rather, it is φυσις, _natura_ in the proper sense; it is always becoming, finds itself in a continual teleological development, and in successive periods is led toward a divine destiny.

Within such a conception of nature miracles once again stand entirely in their place. On the final page of his _Kulturgeschichte_, Hellwald says that all life on earth will one day perish in the eternal rest of death and then concludes with the comfortless words: _Dann wird die Erde, ihrer Atmosphäre und Lebewelt beraubt, in mondgleicher Verödung um die Sonne kreisen, wie zuvor, das_ Menschengeschlecht _aber, seine Kultur, sein Ringen und Streben, seine Schöpfungen und Ideale sind_ gewesen. _Wozu?_ Naturally, in a system that ends with such an unanswered question, revelation and miracle would be nothing but absurdity. But Scripture teaches us that revelation serves to recreate the creation corrupted by sin into a kingdom of God. Here revelation occupies a fully proportionate and teleological place in the world plan that God has formed and realizes in the course of the ages.

In this sense Augustine already said, _portentum fit non contra naturam sed contra quam est nota natura_, _De civ._ 21,8; _C. Faustum_ 29,2; 26,3. This expression has often been interpreted in a wrong sense for the benefit of a theology that attempted to understand miracles as the operation of a power naturally present in man or in nature, or restored in him by regeneration or faith. This peculiar conception already occurs in Philo and Neoplatonism, repeatedly found an echo among various Christian theologians—Scotus Eriugena, Paracelsus, Cornelius Agrippa, Böhme, Oetinger; cf. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ II 182 f., 361 f.—and is also occasionally encountered in mediation theology: Twesten, _Vorles._ I 370 f., II 171 f.; Martensen, _Dogm._ § 16 f.; Schleiermacher, _Glaub._ § 13,1; § 129; Sack, _Apologetik_, S. 137 f.; Lange, _Philos. Dogm._ § 64; Saussaye, _Mĳne Theol. van Ch. d. l. S._ 36 f.; Gunning, _Blikken in de openbaring_ II 37 f.

Revelation, inspiration, and miracle then belong to the original disposition of human nature. This disposition has indeed been weakened by sin, but it nevertheless still emerges in poetic and heroic inspiration, in magnetism and other related phenomena. By an ethical way—through union with God, ascetic purification, regeneration, and so forth—this disposition can, however, be renewed and strengthened. All believers are therefore properly inspired and can perform miracles. _Si humana natura non peccaret eique, qui eam condiderat, immutabiliter adhaereret, profecto omnipotens esset_, Eriugena, _De div. nat._ 4,9. According to Zimmer, _Ueber den allgem. Verfall des menschl. Geschlechts_ III, n. 90 f., miracles are _Zeichen des über die Herrschaft der Natur erhobenen Menschen, in welchem die Herrlichkeit des ersten Menschenpaares vor seiner Sünde dargestellt wird_. When the soul is directed toward God in love, says Böhme, _so mag sie Wunder machen, was sie will_.

In a related sense C. Bonnet, _Recherches philosophiques sur les preuves du christianisme_, Geneva, 1771, held that prophecy and miracle were preformed in nature in advance and came about through the operation of ordinary natural powers. Some theologians in the previous century therefore spoke of _rationes seminales, primordiales_ and _radicales_ of miracles. But this attempt to explain miracles cannot be accepted. It confuses the natural with the supernatural, the supernatural with the religious-ethical, and erases the boundaries between prophecy and manticism, miracle and magic, inspiration and illumination.

The expression of Augustine cited above may not be explained in this way either. By _natura nota_ he understands nature in our sense. And with a view to this he even says that the miracle is _contra naturam_, just as Thomas and Voetius later did (above, p. 275). Yet that same miracle was from the beginning included by God in nature in the broader sense—that is, in the destiny of things determined by God, in the divine world plan, F. Nitzsch, _Augustinus’ Lehre vom Wunder_, Berlin, 1865. The same thought was later also expressed by Leibniz, _Théodicée_ §§ 54, 207. God included the miracles in his world plan from the beginning and brings them about at their appointed time. Miracles cannot be explained _par les natures des choses créées_, but _des raisons d’un ordre supérieur à celui de la nature le portent à les faire_.

According to Leibniz, then, miracles are not enclosed germinally, potentially, in the powers of nature, as Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, S. 146, explains Leibniz, but are constitutive elements in God’s world plan. To that extent miracles most certainly belong to nature. They do not enter the existing cosmos from without in order to disturb it, but are included in the world-idea itself and serve to restore and perfect fallen nature. Indeed, even without sin there would have been a place in the world for prophecy and miracle. The supernatural first became necessary not through the fall. Not revelation and miracle in themselves, but only the soteriological character that both now bear was caused by sin. To this extent even the miracle is no alien element added to fallen creation. Revelation and religion, prophecy and miracle, are not in themselves _dona superaddita_. They are entirely natural insofar as they belong to God’s world-idea and to the world plan that he brings to realization in time despite all resistance.
