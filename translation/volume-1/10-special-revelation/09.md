### D. Special Revelation and Naturalism

## 9.

The principial opposition to revelation began only in modern philosophy. Spinoza still retains the word revelation and even considers it necessary, _Tract. theol.-polit._ cap. 15, 27, but by it he understands nothing other than this: the simple cannot find true religion, the word of God, by the light of reason but must accept it on authority, ibid. cap. 15, 44; cap. 4, 22–37. For the rest, Spinoza acknowledges no revelation in the proper sense. All _decreta Dei_ are _aeternae veritates_ and identical with the _leges naturae_, ibid. cap. 4, 37; 3, 8; 6, 9, etc. Prophecy and miracle were subjected to a sharp critique and explained in a natural way, ibid. capp. 1–6.

This critique was continued by Deism and Rationalism. But Rationalism can appear in various forms and repeatedly changes in meaning; cf. Kant, _Religion innerhalb_ etc., ed. Rosenkranz, S. 185; Wegscheider, _Instit. Theol._ § 7 d; §§ 11–12; Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw. aller in der Dogm. vork. Begriffe_, § 34; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, S. 141 f.

In the first place, Rationalism designates the tendency that does indeed assume a supernatural revelation but assigns to reason the decision concerning the genuineness and meaning of that revelation. To this belonged many Cartesian theologians, such as Roell, Wolzogen, G. W. Duker, and also Leibniz, Wolff, and others. Next, Rationalism is the name for the opinion that still considers a supernatural revelation possible, but only of such truths as reason itself would sooner or later have been able to discover. Revelation is then needed only temporarily and contingently. It serves only as preparation and education for the universal dominion of the _Vernunftreligion_; it gives only more quickly and easily what reason otherwise would have attained by a longer and more difficult road. Such is the concept of revelation in Lessing, _Erziehung des Menschengeschlechts_, 1780; Fichte, _Versuch einer Kritik aller Offenbarung_, 1792; and Kant, _Religion_ etc., 1793.

In the third place, that theology is known as Rationalism which denies all supernatural revelation but nevertheless assumes that through special dispositions of his providence God equipped persons and prepared paths capable of bringing humanity to a better and purer knowledge of religious truth. Wegscheider is the foremost representative of this view, _Instit. theol._ I, § 12, p. 58, and further Röhr, Henke, Gabler, Paulus, Gesenius, and others. Finally, the name Rationalism is also given to the tendency which since the middle of the seventeenth century was called Naturalism, in England Deism, and also Atheism and Materialism, and which, denying all revelation, regarded the _religio naturalis_ as completely sufficient. To this belong Spinoza, Lud. Meyer, Voltaire, Rousseau, Reimarus, Nicolai, and others.

The arguments advanced by this Rationalism against revelation amount to the following. First of all, revelation is impossible from God’s side, for it would imply that God was changeable, that his creation was imperfect and defective and therefore needed improvement, and that he himself, otherwise a _Deus otiosus_, then worked only when he worked in an extraordinary manner.

Further, revelation is also impossible from the side of the world, since science has increasingly discovered that the world is always and everywhere governed by an inviolable system of laws which leaves no room for a supernatural intervention by God. Science proceeds from this causal connection of things and cannot do otherwise. Supernaturalism makes science impossible and puts arbitrariness in the place of regularity. Accordingly, as science has advanced, all phenomena have lost their supernatural character. There is not even any right, contrary to all experience, to regard a phenomenon as supernatural: _le surnaturel serait le surdivin_.

Next, even if revelation had occurred, it would nevertheless be unrecognizable and unprovable for the recipient himself, and still more for those who live after him. How can it ever be determined that a prophecy or miracle comes from God and not, for example, from the devil? By what can a revelation be recognized by the one who receives it and by those who live later? No such criteria can be given. Those who accept a revelation therefore believe solely on human authority and depend upon human beings in the highest and most important matters. _Que d’hommes entre Dieu et moi!_

Finally, revelation conflicts with human reason. Whatever one may say, every revelation that is _supra rationem_ is thereby also _contra rationem_; it therefore suppresses reason and leads to fanaticism. Moreover, if revelation communicates something that is _supra rationem_, it can never be received and assimilated and always remains outside our consciousness as an uncomprehended mystery. And if it communicates something that reason itself could have discovered, then it is unnecessary, gives at most earlier and more easily what would otherwise have been obtained anyway, and needlessly deprives reason of its power and energy. Cf. Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ §§ 10–12; Bretschneider, _Handbuch der Dogm._, 4e Aufl., I 188–329; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._ 141 f., 165.
