## 2.

Thus, although dogmatics belongs to the disciplines of dogmatic theology, it must be properly distinguished from its sisters. All the disciplines in this division of theology are concerned with dogma—that is, with the truth as God has revealed it—but each in its own way. It may be heard as the congregation confesses it clearly and forcefully in its confession, and then _theologia symbolica_ arises. It may be presented in a simple, intelligible form, as milk (1 Pet. 2:2), to the congregation’s younger members, its children, and then _theologia catechetica_ speaks—something to be carefully distinguished from catechetics, the art of presenting it in this way. It may be upheld in its truth and right against opponents, and then _theologia elenctica_ performs its task. It may also be expounded thetically and positively, but in a scientific, systematic form for the developed consciousness, and then dogmatics is practiced. All these disciplines have in common that they display the _thesauri Sacrae Scripturae_, but each in its own way. Dogmatics does so, as was formerly said, in a scholastic, schoolwise manner—that is, as ought to be done in the schools of scholarship. Naturally, if not everything, then certainly much depends here on the standpoint the dogmatician adopts. If, with the Pietists and _theologi biblici_, he assumes a sharp opposition between the teaching of Scripture and that of the church, he will adhere as closely as possible to Scripture, even in its words and expressions, and will process the material he finds as little as possible by means of thought. If in the church’s confession and in the _historia dogmatum_ he sees not a corruption but a development of scriptural truth, his dogmatics will bear an ecclesiastical and confessional character. And if he takes a rationalistic stance over against both Scripture and church, the convictions of faith he presents will above all have a negative character. All this is a difference of method and will be discussed later. For precisely that reason it is not right, with Prof. Doedes, _Encyclopaedie_ § 48, to distinguish three kinds of dogmatics—namely, New Testament or Christian, ecclesiastical, and critical dogmatics. For thereby all three are granted equal rank and right of existence; the confusion increases further; New Testament dogmatics, as Christian dogmatics _par excellence_, comes to stand over against the other two; the first two kinds of dogmatics can proceed entirely apart from one’s own conviction; and critical dogmatics alone becomes the dogmatics of one’s own independent view. Rather, the task of dogmatics is always one and the same. By its nature it is and can be nothing other than a scientific exposition of religious truth, an _enarratio verbi Dei_, a display of the _thesauri Sacrae Scripturae_, a παραδοσις εἰς τυπον διδαχης (Rom. 6:17), so that in it we possess a _forma_ and _imago_ of the _doctrina coelestis_. Dogmatics is therefore not itself the Word of God; it is always only a faint image and weak likeness of it. It is a fallible, human attempt to reflect on and repeat, in its own free manner, what God spoke long ago at many times and in many ways through the Prophets, and in these last days has spoken to us through the Son. Polanus, _Syntagma_, p. 539; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol. Christ._, Loc. I § 58. The question of how dogmatics can best fulfill this task is a question of method and must be treated separately later.
