## 3.

Dogmatics is most closely related to ethics. In former times the word _dogma_ embraced both the _articuli fidei_ and the _praecepta decalogi_, the _dogmata fidei_ and the _dogmata morum_. Ethics was either incorporated into dogmatics, as in Lombard, Thomas, Melanchthon, Calvin, Martyr, Musculus, Sohnius, etc.; or treated in a second part after dogmatics, as in Polanus, Amesius, Heidegger, Wollebius, Wendelinus, Maestricht, Brakel, etc.; or else treated wholly on its own, separately from dogmatics, as in Danaeus, _Ethices Christ. libri III_, 1577, Keckermann, Walaeus, Polyander, Amyraldus, Pictet, Driessen, Hoornbeek, Heidegger, Osterwald, J. A. Turretinus, Stapfer, Beck, Wyttenbach, Endeman; cf. A. Schweizer, _Die Entw. des Moralsystems in der ref. Kirche_, _Stud. u. Krit._ 1850, Heft 1; Gass, _Gesch. der chr. Ethik_ II Bd., 1te Abth., 1886, S. 131 f.; Luthardt, _Gesch. der chr. Eth._, 2te Hälfte, 1893. The influence of Aristotle, which began already with Melanchthon’s _philosophia moralis_ of 1539, hindered the development of Christian ethics. The separation that occurred later had the consequence that ethics lacked a principle of its own, went in search of one in philosophy at an unfortunate moment, came as a degenerate theological ethics to stand in hostility over against dogmatics and displaced it from its position, and finally erased entirely the boundaries between itself and dogmatics. The fruitless attempts to distinguish the two serve as evidence. In Kant, religion no longer has a content of its own but is merely the conception of the good as a command of God, _Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der blossen Vernunft_, ed. Rosenkranz, S. 184. Schleiermacher gave both the Christian life as their content, but dogmatics describes that life in relative rest and ethics in relative motion, _Die Christl. Sitte_, ed. Jonas, 2te Aufl., 1884, S. 12–24. Rothe recognized the inadequacy of this distinction and therefore made dogmatics a historical discipline that treated the dogmas of the church, but ethics a speculative science that develops its material dialectically out of the God-consciousness, _Theol. Ethik_ § 15. Dorner, _Glaubenslehre_ I 9 f., _Sittenlehre_ 47; Herzog² 4, 352; Wuttke, _Christl. Sittenlehre_ I 9; Palmer, _Moral des Christ._ 24 f., and others distinguish the two as knowing and doing, intellect and will. Doedes, _Encycl._ § 52 no. 4, and Van Oosterzee, _Chr. Dogm._ § 4 no. 4, distinguish them as doctrine of salvation and doctrine of life; Gunning, _Het ethische beginsel der Theologie_ 12, as the life of Christ in the church and in the individual; Daubanton, _Theol. Stud._ III 114 f., as the doctrine of God and the doctrine of man, etc. All these distinctions suffer from the defect that they seek a difference of principle between dogmatics and ethics. There is no such difference. Theological ethics, which must be carefully distinguished from philosophical ethics, has no principle of its own but is rooted entirely in dogmatics. The proposed lines of separation create a dualism between God and man, individual and community, salvation and life, rest and movement, intellect and will; and they prepare the way for ethics to seek a principle of its own along a speculative, philosophical path, as it does in Rothe, to lose its theological character, and from its speculative height to look contemptuously down upon historical, positive dogmatics. If dogmatics and ethics are to be treated separately—which is desirable for various reasons, though many still advise against it, such as Sartorius, Nitzsch, Beck; A. Dorner, _Ueber das Verhältniss der Dogm. u. Ethik_ in _Theol. Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, Oct. 1889; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I 14; Wendt, _Die Aufgabe der System. Theol._, 1894, S. 12 f.—the distinction between them can consist only in this: that man, however absolutely dependent on God at all times, is nevertheless also a free and independently acting being. Regenerated and renewed by the grace of the Holy Spirit, sinful man once again receives the desire and the power to live according to God’s commandments. Dogmatics describes the acts of God for, upon, and in man; ethics describes the acts that renewed man now performs on the ground and in the power of those acts of God. In dogmatics man is passive: he receives and believes; in ethics he himself steps forward as an actor. Dogmatics treats the _articuli fidei_; ethics, the _praecepta decalogi_. The former deals _de fide_; the latter _de caritate, obedientia, bonis operibus_. Dogmatics unfolds what God is and does for man and makes man know God as his Creator, Redeemer, and Sanctifier; ethics sets forth what man now is and does for God, how man dedicates himself wholly to God—with intellect and will and all his powers—out of gratitude and love. Dogmatics is the system of the knowledge of God; ethics, that of the service of God. Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 14; H. Schultz, _Grundriss der ev. Dogm._, 2te Aufl., 1892, S. 3; Wendt, ibid., 17. The two sciences do not stand independently over against each other but together form one system; they are mutually related members of one organism. Wendt, ibid., 19.
