# § 2. Encyclopedic Place of Dogmatics

## 1.

There is almost no disagreement about the place of dogmatics in the encyclopedia of theology. All assign it to systematic or dogmatic theology, that is, to the group of disciplines concerned with dogma and comprising, besides dogmatics, ethics, symbolics, the history of dogma, and elenctics. Schleiermacher alone, _Kurze Darstellung des theol. Studiums_, 2nd ed., 1830, § 195, assigned it to historical theology, because it is the science _von dem Zusammenhange der in der Kirche zu einer gegebenen Zeit geltenden Lehre_, _Der Christl. Glaube_ § 19; so too Rothe, _Zur Dogm._, 1863, p. 14; and thus it provides a statistical account of the present. Schleiermacher arrived at this peculiar view because he wished to separate dogmatics from apologetics as strictly as possible. Whereas apologetics, as part of philosophical theology, must demonstrate what Christian truth is, dogmatics has only to describe what is regarded as truth in one Christian church or another. Yet Schleiermacher by no means intended thereby to ascribe a merely reporting character to dogmatics. He certainly assigns it a critical task and requires that it be systematic, that it give expression to personal conviction, and that it describe not what once prevailed but what now prevails; Dr. Is. van Dijk, _Begrip en methode der Dogmatiek_, pp. 38f.; Hagenbach, _Encycl._ § 80. In doing so, however, it already goes beyond purely historical description. The Christian congregation cannot be satisfied with an objective report of the content of its faith, but requires that its faith also be unfolded and expounded as truth. Nor does the dogmatician stand neutrally outside and over against the faith of the congregation, as he might stand over against the religious teaching of Muslims and Buddhists. He belongs to that congregation, and therefore also describes what for him counts and ought to count as truth. Finally, there is an essential difference between works such as those of Hase, Schmid, Schweizer, and Heppe, which give an objective description of Lutheran and Reformed doctrine, and dogmatics, which seeks to demonstrate and present the truth of religious convictions. If, in calling dogmatics a historical discipline, Schleiermacher meant only that it is a thetic, positive science, one that does not seek its object but finds and then describes it, his intention is worthy of appreciation, though the designation remains incorrect. If, however, he held that dogmatics merely had to describe a historical datum and made no claim to normative truth, his conception was certainly incorrect and is now also generally rejected. Hagenbach, _Encycl._ § 80; Herzog² 3, 643.
