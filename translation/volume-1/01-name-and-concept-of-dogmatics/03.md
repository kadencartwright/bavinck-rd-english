## 3.

The usage of the word teaches us, first, that *dogma* can designate all kinds of commands, decrees, philosophical truths, theoretical propositions, practical rules, and so forth; but what they have in common is that a certain authority always underlies them. A dogma is a proposition that possesses authority, Harnack, *Lehrbuch der Dogmengeschichte*, 2te Aufl. 1888 I. 15. The word *dogma* does not, however, indicate in itself the authority on which it rests. That authority differs among the various dogmas. In political dogmas the authority is the government; in philosophical dogmas, internal evidence or the force of the proofs; in religious or theological dogmas, that authority lies solely in God's testimony, whether, according to Protestants, this testimony is heard only in Scripture or, according to Roman Catholics, also in the church. A proposition has authority in church and theology only because it rests on the testimony of God. It is therefore wrong, as Schleiermacher and those after him have done, to seek the essence and character of a dogma in its ecclesiastical definition and promulgation, Schleiermacher, *Christl. Sitte* 1884, S. 5; Rothe, *Zur Dogm.* 10; Schweizer, *Christl. Glaub.* I. 23; Daubanton, *Theol. Studien* 1885, pp. 136–145; Kuyper, *Encycl.* III 395f. From the Roman Catholic standpoint something might still be said for this, since the church is infallible. But the Reformation recognizes no truth except on the authority of God in Holy Scripture. *Verbum Dei condit articulos fidei, et praeterea nemo, ne angelus quidem*, *Art. Smalc.* Pars II art. 2. Dogmas and *articuli fidei* are only those truths *quae in scripturis proprie ut credenda proponuntur*, Hyperius, *Methodus theol.* pp. 34, 35. They are only such *sententiae, quibus credi aut obtemperari necesse est propter mandatum Dei*, Ursinus, *Tract. Theol.* 22. And therefore this proposition recurs again and again among Reformed theologians: *principium, in quod omnia dogmata theologica resolvuntur, est: Dominus dixit*, cf. Ritschl, *Lehre v. d. Rechtf. u. Vers.* 3te Aufl. II 2. But from the character of authority proper to a dogma it follows, second, that as such it is also acknowledged within a certain circle. However firmly established a truth may be, if it is not acknowledged, in the eyes of others it is nothing more than a *sententia doctoris*, a private opinion. The concept of dogma implies that the authority it possesses also knows how to gain and maintain recognition for itself. A distinction must therefore be made between *dogma quoad se* and *dogma quoad nos*. A proposition is dogma in itself, apart from all recognition, if it rests on the authority of God. But in order not to identify one's own opinion with the truth of God, it is of the highest importance for every believer, and especially for the dogmatician, to know which truths from Scripture have, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, been brought to general recognition in the church of Christ. The confession may therefore be called the *dogma quoad nos*. Yet even in this subjective sense, dogma is by no means limited to what has expressly been included in the creeds and formally defined by the church. The church has a life and a faith far richer than what comes to expression in its confession. The confession by no means formulates the entire content of the Christian faith. Nor does it belong to the essence of the church; it is incidental and has almost always arisen from particular circumstances. It usually includes only what has encountered opposition within or outside the church. Thus, just as wood does not burn because it smokes, although the smoke is nevertheless a sign of fire, so too a proposition is not a dogma because the church acknowledges it, even though the church's acknowledgment is an important, albeit always fallible, proof of its truth, Kleutgen, *Theologie der Vorzeit*, 2te Aufl. I, 97f. Third, the usage of the word *dogma* further teaches us that it was employed sometimes in a broader and sometimes in a narrower sense. Sometimes it designates the whole Christian religion, and Basilius M., *de Spiritu Sancto* c. 27, can understand by δογματα (in contrast to κηρυγματα, the *articuli fidei* drawn from Scripture) the ecclesiastical ceremonies and rites, Suicerus, *Thes. Eccl.* s. v. δογμα. Polanus, loc. cit., says that dogma in the broader sense encompasses everything contained in Holy Scripture, not only the *doctrina evangelii et legis*, but also all *conciones, historiae sacrae*, and so forth. Usually, however, the word was employed in the narrower sense for the *doctrina evangelii et legis*, for those *sententiae, quibus credi aut obtemperari necesse est propter mandatum Dei*, Ursinus, loc. cit., p. 22. It therefore included not only doctrinal but also ethical truth. Later, however, the word *dogma* was restricted still further when the *doctrina legis* was distinguished and separated from the *doctrina evangelii*; dogmas were now only those *sententiae, quibus credi necesse est propter mandatum Dei*. Polanus went still further and also distinguished between the *dogmata* and the *principia theologiae*. Thus dogma became the designation of the *articuli fidei* that rest on the authority of God's Word and therefore oblige everyone to believe. Dogmatics, then, is the system of the *articuli fidei*.
