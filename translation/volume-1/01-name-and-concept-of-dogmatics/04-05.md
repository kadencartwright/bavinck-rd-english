## 4.

Yet this establishes the concept of dogmatics only in a formal sense. A definition of dogmatics as the science of dogmas is of little use so long as we do not know the subject matter, the content, of the dogmas. To determine the material concept of dogmatics, we must recall that *dogmatic* was originally an adjective qualifying the principal concept, *theology*. Accordingly, in earlier times dogmatics was usually understood as *doctrina de Deo principaliter et de creaturis, secundum quod referuntur ad Deum ut ad principium vel finem*, Thomas, Summa Theol. I qu. 1 art. 3 and 7; Alb. Magnus, Sent. lib. I dist. 1 § 2; Petavius, Opus de theol. dogmatibus cap. 1. —Gerhard, Loci Comm., prooemium de natura theologiae; Hase Hutterus Redivivus § 11; H. Schmid, Dogm. der ev. luth. Kirche § 2. —Fr. Junius, Opera Omnia I fol. 1375-1424; Polanus, S. Theol. lib. I cap. 1-4; Gomarus, Disput. Theol., thesis 1; Owen, Θεολογουμενα, sive de natura, ortu.... verae theologiae, libri sex. Oxon. 1661, lib. 1 cap. 1-4; Voetius, Diatribe de theologia. Ultraj. 1668; Coccejus, Summa theol. cap. 1. Others, however, objected to making God the principal concept of dogmatics and preferred to designate its object differently. Lombardus, Sent. I dist. 1, followed Augustine, who says in doctr. christ. lib. I cap. 2: *omnis doctrina vel rerum est, vel signorum*, and therefore assigned two things to theology for treatment: *res*, that is, God, world, and man, and *signa*, that is, the sacraments. But this incomplete definition was soon abandoned and corrected by the commentators. Alexander Halesius and Bonaventura, Proleg. qu. I in 1 Sent. and Breviloquium Pars I cap. 1, named Christ and His mystical body, the church; Hugo a St. Victor, De sacr. lib. I c. 2, named the *opera reparationis* as the subject matter and content of theology or dogmatics. Lutheran and Reformed theologians likewise defined the content of dogmatics in this way. Calovius, Isagoge ad theologiam 1662, argues most vigorously against God being the proper object of theology: etymology decides nothing here; theology on earth is altogether different from theology in heaven; here we do indeed strive for knowledge of God, but we do not attain it. To make God the object of theology is as mistaken as making the sovereign, rather than the *res publica*, the object of *politica*, p. 283 s. 291 s.; the proper object of theology is *homo in quantum est perducendus ad salutem*, p. 252, 290, or religion, p. 299, 324. And so Amesius, Medulla Theol. I c. 5; Maestricht, Theor. pract. Theol. I c. 1 § 47; Marck, Merch der Christ. Godg. I § 34; Moor, Comment. in Marckii Compendium I p. 112; Burmannus, Synopsis Theol. I c. 2 § 30; Limborch, Theol. Christ. I c. 1, also made *Deo vivere per Christum*, *religio*, and *cultus Dei* the content of dogmatics. The subjective, practical conception of theology thus gradually began to gain wider acceptance. And when Kant’s philosophy restricted the human faculty of knowledge to visible things, Agnosticism pronounced God unknowable, and historical criticism undermined supernatural revelation, this subjective, practical conception became dominant. Schleiermacher conceived dogmatics as *Glaubenslehre* and defined dogmas as *Auffassungen der frommen Gemüthszustände*, Der Christl. Glaube § 15, 16. Since then, nearly all theologians have made this transition with him. Not God but religion is the object of theology—religion in general as a historical and psychological phenomenon, Tiele, Gids Mei 1866; Idem, Theol. Tĳdschr. 1ste aflev. 1867; Rauwenhoff, Theol. Tĳdschr. 1869, pp. 1f., 163f.; Wet op het Hooger Onderwĳs van 1876, or, more particularly, the Christian religion as it comes to expression in Scripture and the church. Accordingly, the content assigned to dogmatics is the *religio* or *doctrina christiana*, as it is either drawn from Holy Scripture, confessed by one church or another, or known through personal experience.

## 5.

Already in his day, Thomas observed concerning those who offered a different definition of theology that they arrived at it because they were *attendentes ad ea, quae in ista scientia tractantur et non ad rationem, secundum quam considerantur*, Summa Theol. I qu. 1 art. 7. Theology, and therefore dogmatics as well, treats far more than God alone; angels and human beings, heaven and earth—indeed, all creatures—are discussed in it. But the question is from what point of view and to what end they are treated in theology. After all, all these things are treated in other sciences too; what is distinctive about their treatment in theology is that they are considered in their relation to God, *ut ad principium et finem*, Kaftan, Wahrheit der Chr. Rel. S. 3. Furthermore, the definition of dogmatics as the science of the *religio christiana* would not in itself be so mistaken if, as in earlier times, this *religio* were understood to mean the religion objectively deposited in Scripture. But after Kant and Schleiermacher this definition acquired a different meaning, and dogmatics became the description of the historical phenomenon called the Christian religion, which also manifests itself in a distinctive faith and doctrine. When dogmatics is understood in this sense, it ceases to be dogmatics and becomes simply a description of what is held to be true in the sphere of religion within a particular circle. But science is concerned with truth. If dogmatics truly wishes to be a science, it is not satisfied with describing what is, but must indicate what ought to be regarded as truth. It must demonstrate not the ὁτι but the διοτι; not actuality but truth; not the real but the ideal, the logical, the necessary. Yet as soon as it attempts this, it immediately returns to God and once again becomes theology in the proper sense. For whoever makes Christianity the object of a distinct science proceeds not from the fact of Christianity’s *existence*—for then the treatment of that historical phenomenon in the faculty of letters would be entirely sufficient—but from a particular evaluation of that fact, Gunning en Saussaye Jr., Het ethische beginsel der Theologie 47. Theology as a distinct faculty discipline presupposes that in those historical facts of Christianity God has revealed Himself in a special way, and thus presupposes and requires the existence and revelation of God. That is, if theology is to be and remain theology, it requires that everything within it be considered *sub ratione Dei*. And if the unknowability of God is raised in objection, then the rule applies: *qui nimis probat, nihil probat*. If God is not knowable, then not only dogmatics and theology but also religion collapse, for religion is founded on the knowledge of God. Dogmatics, therefore, is the scientific system of the knowledge of God; that is, of the knowledge which, in His Word, He has revealed to the church concerning Himself and concerning every creature in its relation to Him.
