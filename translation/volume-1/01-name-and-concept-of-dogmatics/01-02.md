# Introduction

# § 1. Name and Concept of Dogmatics

## 1.

The name dogmatics is of comparatively recent date. Formerly, entirely different names were in use. Origen gave his principal dogmatic work the title περι ἀρχων. Augustine qualified the title of his Enchiridium ad Laurentium by adding sive de fide, spe et caritate. John of Damascus produced an Ἐκδοσις ἀκριβης της ὀρθοδοξου πιστεως. The name Sententiæ came into use with Isidore of Seville, yielding in the thirteenth century to Summa theologiæ. Melanchthon spoke of Loci communes rerum theologicarum, sive hypotyposes theologiæ. The expression loci is borrowed from Cicero and is a translation of the Greek τοπος. Aristotle understood τοποι to mean the general rules of dialectic, which were self-evident and firmly established and could therefore serve as στοιχεια των ἀποδειξεων, Rhet. lib. II c. 22 § 13. Metaph. lib. IV c. 3 § 3. Cicero transferred this doctrine of the τοποι from dialectic to rhetoric and took them to be the places where an orator could find the arguments he needed for his speech. He explains the expression by sedes, notæ argumentorum, and identifies as such sources the concept, definition, division, root meaning of a word, synonyms, etc., Top. II c. 2. This use of the word loci does not entirely agree with its meaning in Aristotle and was modified still further in later logic. Melanchthon himself, in his Dialectica, lib. IV, explains the expression loci by signa quædam, quibus rerum, quæ dici tractarique debent, capita indicantur. According to Cicero, de Orat. III 27. Brutus 12, Loci communes are propositions that relate to no definite object, are universal, and therefore hold always and everywhere, in distinction from loci proprii, which are concrete and therefore have force only with respect to a definite subject or in a particular science. In his loci communes Melanchthon intended to present only the principal topics of the Christian religion, as treated especially by Paul in his epistle to the Romans. He therefore describes them himself as hypotyposes—design, sketch, lineamenta of theology. And Spalatin’s German translation rendered the title as Hauptartikel und fürnehmste Punkte der ganzen Heiligen Schrift. Thus the name and content of Melanchthon’s dogmatics stand in contrast to the scholastic Sententiae and Summae. It was intended not to give the learned a complete dogmatic system, but rather to introduce the unlearned to the knowledge of Holy Scripture. Cf. Heppe, Dogm. des Deutschen Protest. 1857 I s. 4-14; Herzog u. Plitt, Real. Enc. 8, 708 f.; Sanseverino, Philosophia Christiana, ed. nov. Neap. 1878 III 286-315. This explains why, with a single exception, the name loci communes found no acceptance among Roman Catholic theologians. They do indeed use the expression loci, but not in Melanchthon’s sense; they use it in the sense of Cicero or Aristotle. They understand by it not the articuli fidei but the principia or sources of theology, Dens, Theologia ad usum Seminariorum. Mechl. 1828 I 5; Billuart, Summa S. Thomae sive Cursus Theologiae 1747 I 47; Daelman, Theologia seu Observ. theol. in Summam D. Thomae 1759 I 18. The famous work of Melchior Cano, Loci Theologici 1563, takes the expression in the same sense and treats not dogmatics itself but its sources, ten in number: Scripture, tradition, the pope, councils, the church, the church fathers, the scholastics, reason, philosophy, and history. By contrast, Melanchthon’s name loci communes was adopted by many Lutheran and Reformed theologians, such as Chemnitz, Hutter, Gerhard, and Calovius; Martyr, Musculus, Hyperius, Ursinus, and others. Yet it had fairly soon to yield to another title. Zwingli had published dogmatic writings under the titles Commentarius de vera ac falsa religione and Christianae fidei brevis et clara expositio. Calvin preferred the name Institutio religionis Christianae. And later theologians from the Lutheran and Reformed churches returned to the old name theologia. To distinguish it from other theological disciplines, which gradually increased in number and importance, this name Theologia had to be further qualified. For this purpose the additions didactica, systematica, theoretica, and positiva were used, and, since L. Reinhart’s Synopsis theologiae dogmaticae 1659, also dogmatica. This qualification was natural, since the truths of faith had long been designated by the name dogmata, and the separation of dogmatics and ethics begun by Danaeus and Calixtus required a distinct name for each discipline. Since then, this modifier has gained such ascendancy that it has displaced the headword theologia and come to stand on its own, has found acceptance among theologians of every confession, and could not be displaced by the more recent names doctrine of faith, doctrine of salvation, Christian doctrine, and so forth.

## 2.

The word δογμα, from δοκειν, to seem, denotes that which has been determined and decided and therefore stands fixed: το δεδογμενον, statutum, decretum, placitum. In Scripture it is used of commands issued by the civil authority, LXX, Esth. 3:9; Dan. 2:13; 6:8; Luke 2:1; Acts 17:7; of the ordinances of the Old Covenant, Eph. 2:15; Col. 2:14; and of the decisions of the assembly at Jerusalem, Acts 15:28; 16:14. Among classical writers it has the meaning of a decision or command, and in philosophy that of truths that stand firm either of themselves or by means of proofs, Plato, de Rep. VII c. 16. p. 538 C.; Arist. Phys. ausc. 4, 2; Cic. de fin. 2, 32; Acad. prior. 2, 9. § 27; Seneca Epist. 94, 95. The word was adopted into theology with these meanings as well. Josephus, c. Ap. I, 8, says that the Jews from childhood regard the books of the Old Covenant as θεου δογματα. In the same sense the church fathers speak of the Christian religion or doctrine as το θειον δογμα, of the incarnation of Christ as δογμα της θεολογιας, of the truths of faith that have authority in and for the church as τα της ἐκκλησιας δογματα, and likewise of the teachings of heretics as δογματα των ἑτεροδοξων, etc. Cf. Suicerus, Thesaurus Eccles. s. v.; Herzog² s. v. Dogmatik; H. Cremer, Wörterbuch der neutest. Gräcität s. v. The word retains this same sense among Latin writers, such as Vincent in his Commonitorium c. 29, and among Protestant theologians, such as Sohnius, Opera 1609 I 32; Ursinus, Tract. Theol. 1584 p. 22; Hyperius, Methodus Theol. 1574 p. 34; Polanus, Synt. Theol. 1625 p. 133.
