## Preface

_A brief word may serve to clarify the standpoint of this dogmatics. Not only the believer but also the dogmatician must confess the communion of saints. Only with all the saints can he comprehend what is the breadth and length and depth and height, and know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge. Only in and through their fellowship does he learn to understand the dogma in which the Christian faith expresses itself. Moreover, this communion of saints contains a strengthening power and an excellent consolation. Dogmatics is not held in honor today; Christian dogma does not share in the favor of the age. Hence, in the words of Groen van Prinsterer, Unbelief and Revolution, 1868, p. 17, there is sometimes a feeling of abandonment, of isolation. All the more, then, does it give occasion for gratitude that we can appeal to our alliance with the generations that have gone before us. For these reasons more attention has been devoted to patristic and scholastic theology than is otherwise customary among Protestant dogmaticians. Men such as Irenaeus, Augustine, and Thomas do not belong exclusively to Rome. They are fathers and doctors to whom the whole Christian church is indebted. Nor, furthermore, has Roman Catholic theology since the Reformation been forgotten. Protestants have often been too little acquainted both with what they hold in common with Rome and with what separates them from Rome. The revival of Roman Catholic theology under the auspices of Thomas makes it doubly necessary for the Protestant Christian to render a conscious and lucid account of his relation to Rome._

_This dogmatics, however, most closely follows the type that the Christian religion and theology received through the Reformation in the sixteenth century, particularly in Switzerland. It does so not because this type is the only true one, but because, in the author’s conviction, it is the relatively purest expression of the truth. In no confession has what is Christian in its religious, ethical, and theological character come so fully into its own; nowhere has it been conceived so deeply and broadly, so generously and freely, so truly catholic, as in the confession of the Reformed churches. It is therefore all the more regrettable that this reformation of religion and theology, like that of church and scholarship, was checked so soon. Despite the many good things displayed by later developments not only in this country but also in England, Scotland, and America, stagnation soon set in and deformation followed. Appealing by preference to the older generation, which far surpasses the later in freshness and originality, the present author considers it the right of the dogmatician to distinguish between wheat and chaff in the history of Reformed theology. To praise the old merely because it is old is neither Reformed nor Christian. And dogmatics describes not what has prevailed, but what ought to prevail. It is rooted in the past, but labors for the future._

_Finally, therefore, this dogmatics also wishes to bear the stamp of its age. It would be an impossible task to detach oneself from the present; but it would also not be right before God, who speaks to us no less loudly and earnestly in this century than in former generations. Attention has been paid to the many currents that cross one another in the field of theology. Amid them all, a place has been sought and a position taken. Wherever departure from them was a duty, an account has been given. Yet even then an effort has been made to appreciate the good wherever it could be found. Continued study often disclosed an affinity that at first appeared not to exist at all._

_Built on this foundation, this dogmatics seeks to be a handbook for those who devote themselves to its study. Even where it cannot win agreement, may it stimulate study. With this in view, the questions and the various solutions that have been attempted are presented as objectively as possible. Literature has been cited to such an extent that readers may readily orient themselves and join in laboring toward a solution._

_This first volume discusses the introduction and the principia. The second volume will treat dogma. It will probably appear in two parts, neither of which will be larger than this first volume, and they will follow as soon as possible. A comprehensive index will conclude the work._

H. Bavinck

_Kampen, April 1895._
