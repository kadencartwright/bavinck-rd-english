## 3.

From this _principium essendi_ the _principium cognoscendi_ must be distinguished. That theology exists we owe to God alone, to His self-consciousness and His good pleasure. But the means, the way by which this knowledge of God comes to us, is God’s revelation, here still understood in an entirely general sense. The nature of the case entails this. A human being becomes knowable to us only by revealing himself to us, that is, by showing himself, speaking, or acting. Appearance, word, and deed are the three forms in which one human being reveals himself to another. So it is also with the Lord our God: knowledge of Him flows to us only from His revelation, and that revelation too can consist only in appearance, word, and deed. The _principium cognoscendi_ of theology is therefore God’s self-revelation or self-communication to His creatures. Whether this self-revelation of God comes individually to every person or is deposited for the whole of humanity in Scripture or in the church can be investigated only later. For now it is enough that, by the nature of the case, God’s self-revelation can be the only _principium cognoscendi_ of our knowledge of God. Only this observation should be added: if that self-revelation of God is deposited in Scripture or in the church, Scripture and church can have only an instrumental and therefore, in a certain sense, contingent and transitory significance. Holy Scripture is therefore at most the _causa efficiens instrumentalis_ of theology.

For the end of theology can be nothing other than that the rational creature should know God and, knowing Him, glorify Him (Prov. 16:4; Rom. 11:36; 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 3:17). It is His εὐδοκια to be known by human beings (Matt. 11:25, 26). God’s self-revelation therefore intends to introduce knowledge of Him into human consciousness and, through that consciousness, to render glory and honor back to God Himself. But this self-revelation of God cannot therefore end outside, before, or alongside man; it must continue into man himself. That is, revelation cannot be merely external but must also be internal. A distinction was therefore formerly made between _principium cognoscendi externum_ and _internum_, _verbum externum_ and _internum_, _revelatio_ and _illuminatio_, the operation of God’s Word and of His Spirit. The _verbum internum_ is the _verbum principale_, for it brings the knowledge of God into man, and that is the end of all theology, of the whole self-revelation of God. The _verbum externum_, revelation deposited in Holy Scripture, serves as a means in this process. It is the _verbum instrumentale_, perhaps necessary for all kinds of incidental reasons in this dispensation, yet in its nature temporary and contingent.
