## 2.

This usage was adopted in theology as well. In Holy Scripture ἀρχη frequently has not only a temporal meaning (Mark 1:1; John 1:1, etc.) but occasionally also a causative one. In the LXX the fear of the Lord is called the ἀρχη της σοφιας (Prov. 1:7), and in Rev. 3:14 and Col. 1:18 Christ is called the ἀρχη of creation and of the resurrection. The church fathers frequently speak of the Father as ἀρχη, πηγη, αἰτιον of Son and Spirit, Athan., _C. Arianos_ II; Basilius, _adv. Eunom._ I; Damasc., _de fide orthod._ I 9, etc., just as Augustine calls the Father _principium totius divinitatis_, _de S. Trin._ IV, cap. 20. Thus God was the _principium essendi_ or _existendi_ of everything created, and therefore also of science, and more particularly still of theology. In this last domain it was always expressly repeated that God was the _principium essendi_ of theology. There was a special reason for this. No knowledge of God is possible except from and through God, Matt. 11:27; 1 Cor. 2:10 f. It was an axiom of earlier theology: _a deo discendum quid de ipso intelligendum, quia non nisi ipso auctore cognoscitur_. That there is any knowledge of God in the creature is due to God alone. He is knowable only because and insofar as He Himself wills it. Even the analogy of a human being proves the truth of this. To a certain extent a human being is the _principium essendi_ of our knowledge concerning his person, 1 Cor. 2:11. He must reveal himself, make himself visible through appearance, word, and deed, so that we may come to know him to some degree. But in a human being this is always relative. He often reveals himself wholly involuntarily and despite himself; he frequently reveals himself in traits of character and peculiarities unknown to himself; at times he also reveals himself otherwise than he is, falsely and untruthfully, and so forth. But none of this applies to God. In the absolute sense He is the _principium essendi_, the _causa efficiens principalis_ of our knowledge of God, for He is absolutely free, self-conscious, and truthful. His self-knowledge, His self-consciousness, is the _principium essendi_ of our knowledge of God. Without God’s self-consciousness there is no knowledge of God in creatures. Pantheism is the death of theology. The relation of this self-knowledge of God to our knowledge of God was formerly expressed by saying that the former was the _theologia archetypa_ of the latter, and the latter the _theologia ectypa_ of the former. Our knowledge of God is an imprint of the knowledge God has of Himself, but always in a creaturely sense. The knowledge of God in His creatures is only a weak likeness, a finite and limited sketch of God’s absolute self-consciousness, accommodated to human or creaturely consciousness. But however great the distance may be, the _principium essendi_ of our knowledge of God is God Himself alone, who reveals Himself freely, self-consciously, and truthfully.
