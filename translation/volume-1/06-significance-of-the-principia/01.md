# Part I. Principia of Dogmatics

## Chapter I. Principia in General

# § 6. Significance of the Principia

## 1.

According to Simplicius, the Neoplatonic commentator on Aristotle, _Phys._ 32, and likewise according to Hippolytus in his _Refutatio omnium haeresium_ I 6, cf. H. Ritter and L. Preller, _Historia philos. graecae_, 1886, pp. 16, 17, Anaximander was the first to designate the ground of things, which he held to be the ἀπειρον, by the name ἀρχη. Perhaps, however, by this he still meant only that the ἀπειρον was the beginning and first of all things, Ed. Zeller, _Die Philosophie der Griechen_, 4e Aufl., I 203. But in the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle this word acquired the meaning of the ultimate cause of things. Aristotle understands by ἀρχαι in general the first things in a series, and then especially the first causes that cannot be derived from any others. In _Metaph._ V. 1. 1013, a, 17 he gives this definition: πασων μεν οὐν κοινον των ἀρχων το πρωτον εἰναι ὁθεν ἠ ἐστιν ἠ γινεται ἠ γιγνωσκεται, _id unde aliquid est aut fit aut cognoscitur_. He assumed such causes especially in two domains, that of being and that of consciousness, in metaphysics and in logic. He derived the being of things from four ἀρχαι, namely, ὑλη, εἰδος, ἀρχη της κινησεως, and τελος, _Phys._ II, 3, 194b, 16, etc. But in the same way he also assumed such ultimate causes in logic. Aristotle observed, namely, that by no means can a proof be given of everything. Of many things we possess not mediate knowledge through demonstration but immediate knowledge through reason. If proofs themselves are not to form a _regressus in infinitum_, they must proceed from propositions that, as immediately certain, neither admit nor require proof. Aristotle called these propositions ἀρχαι ἀποδειξεως, ἀρχαι συλλογιστικαι, ἀρχαι ἀμεσοι, or also, as the universal presupposition of all demonstration, ἀξιωματα, _Anal. post._ I 2, 72a, 7, etc.; and there he himself says of them: λεγω δ’ ἀρχας ἐν ἑκαστῳ γενει ταυτας, ἁς ὁτι ἐστι μη ἐνδεχεται δειξαι. The Latin _principium_ was used in the same sense. Cicero speaks, for example, of _rerum principia_, _Acad._ IV 36; _principia naturae_, _Off._ III 12; _principia naturalia_, _Fin._ II 11; _principium philosophiae_, _Nat. D._ I 1; _principia juris_, _Leg._ I 6; and so forth. In accordance with Aristotle’s definition cited above, three kinds of _principium_ were later distinguished in logic: _principium essendi, existendi_, and _cognoscendi_, according as the being, becoming, or knowledge of a thing had to be derived from something else. _Causa_ was in turn distinguished from _principium_ in this way: _causa_ designated the _principium_ as _influxu suo determinans aliquid sibi insufficiens ad existendum_ and as _tempore_, or at least _natura_, _prius_ than the thing it caused. _Causa_ is therefore a particular kind of _principium_: every _causa_ is a _principium_, but not every _principium_ is a _causa_. J. F. Buddeus, _Elementa philosophiae instrumentalis_, ed. 5a, 1714, I pp. 140 sq., 288 sq.; Liberatore, _Instit. philos._, ed. 8a, Romae, 1855, I 217.
