## 8.

But this does not yet elevate the history of dogma and the confession of the church to infallible authority. There is a distinction between the path along which the dogmatician is formed and the principle from which dogmatics receives its material. In every science the practitioner begins by living from tradition. He always obtains his first knowledge of his field through authority. He must first absorb the history of his field and ascend to the height and present state of his science, in order afterward to work independently and acquire his own insight into the object of his investigation. But no one will therefore regard the tradition that was pedagogically of such great importance as the source of his science. It is no different with the dogmatician. Pedagogically, the church precedes Scripture. But in logical order Scripture is the *principium unicum* of church and theology. In the event of disagreement—the possibility of which can never be denied from the standpoint of the Reformation—church and confession must yield to Scripture.

Not the church but Scripture is αὐτοπιστος, *judex controversiarum, sui ipsius interpres*. Nothing may be placed on the same level with it. Church, confession, tradition—everything must conform to it and submit to it. The Remonstrants did indeed accuse the Reformed of detracting, through the confession, from the authority, sufficiency, and perfection of Holy Scripture. But the Reformed, although they considered a confession necessary in this dispensation of the church in order to explain the Word of God, ward off heresies, and maintain the unity of faith, opposed as strongly as possible the idea that the confession should have even the slightest authority alongside Scripture. Scripture alone is the *norma* and *regula fidei et vitae*; the confession deserves credence only because and insofar as it agrees with Scripture; as fallible human work, it remains revisable and subject to examination by Scripture. The confession is therefore at most a *norma secundaria, non veritatis sed doctrinae in aliqua ecclesia receptae*, and is consequently binding upon all who wish to live in fellowship with the church. Within the church the confession possesses authority as an accord of fellowship, as an expression of the faith of the congregation; but the church believes and maintains that confession only on the ground of Scripture, Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* IV 1–74; Turret, *Theol. El.* Loc. 18. cap. 30; *Examen van het Ontwerp van Tolerantie*, 8e Sam. pp. 59–136; Moor, *Comm. in Marckii Comp.* VI 353f., etc. All Christian churches are united in the confession that Holy Scripture is the *principium* of theology, and the Reformation unanimously acknowledged it as the *principium unicum*. The *Nederl. Confessie* states this in art. 5, and all Lutheran and Reformed theologians agree with it, Schmid, *Dogm. der ev. luth. K.* § 4; Heppe, *Dogm. der ev. ref. K.* loc. 2. It is true that art. 2 of the *Ned. Conf.* confesses that God is known by two means, namely, by nature and Scripture, and that all Reformed theologians maintained the truth and value of the *theologia naturalis*. But in the early period, before rationalism had corrupted Reformed theology, it was clearly understood that nature and Scripture did not stand independently and disconnectedly alongside each other, any more than did the *theologia naturalis* and *revelata*. Calvin incorporated the *theol. naturalis* into the *corpus* of Christian dogmatics and said that Scripture was the spectacles through which the believer beheld God more clearly even in the works of nature, *Instit.* I. 6, 1. The *theologia naturalis* originally had absolutely no purpose of gradually and progressively paving the way to the *theologia revelata*; in it one did not adopt the provisional standpoint of reason in order then, through reasoning and proof, to ascend to the standpoint of faith. Rather, from the outset the dogmatician stood on the foundation of faith and, as a Christian and a believer, now also viewed nature; and then, with his Christian eye armed with Holy Scripture, he discovered even in nature traces of that God whom he had learned to know through Scripture, in Christ, as his Father. Subjectively, therefore, natural reason did not speak first in dogmatics and faith afterward; it was always the believing Christian who expressed his faith in the catechism, in the confession, and in dogmatics. And objectively, likewise, nature did not stand as an autonomous and independent *principium* alongside Holy Scripture, with each supplying its own set of truths. Rather, nature was viewed in the light of Scripture, and Scripture contained not only truth revealed in the strict sense but also the truths that the believer can discover in nature. Thus Alsted, in his *Praecognita Theologiae* 1623. p. 115, does acknowledge a *theologia naturalis* in the unregenerate, but this is confused and obscure; for the believer the *principia et conclusiones theologiae naturalis* are clearly and distinctly repeated in Scripture, Hodge, *System. Theol.* I 11. 15; Shedd, *Dogm. Theol.* I 68.

Thus, even though there is a knowledge of God from nature, dogmatics nevertheless has only one *principium externum*, namely, Holy Scripture, just as it also has only one *principium internum*, namely, believing reason. And this is not merely in the sense that Holy Scripture would be only a *norma* and not a source, but specifically in the sense of *principium theologiae*. There is a great difference between earlier theologians and those of the present day. Since Schleiermacher turned from the object to the subject, a host of theologians have come to see the congregation and its confession as the source of dogmatic truth. They have not only—and rightly—recognized and expressed the confessional and ecclesiastical character of dogmatics, but have also made the confession of the congregation a source of knowledge and reduced Holy Scripture to a *norma*, Schleiermacher, *Glaub.* § 19; Rothe, *Zur Dogm.* 27; Schoeberlein 23; Gunning and Saussaye, *Het ethisch beginsel* 12; Dr. Van Dijk, *Begrip en methode der dogm.* 14f.; Dr. Daubanton, *Confessie en Dogmatiek* 29f.; or they have placed the confession alongside Holy Scripture as a source of knowledge, Lange, *Dogm.* II 3; Van Oost § 9; Von der Goltz § 18; Reiff, I 256. And Dr. Gunning, loc. cit., pp. 31–34, even asserted that the doctrine of Scripture as source instead of as *norma* was not Reformed but actually Remonstrant. But this conception rests on an incorrect understanding of the relation between church and Scripture. In the first period of the Christian congregation it was still possible to speak of a pure tradition that ran parallel with the writings of the apostles. But those two streams have long since flowed together. There is now no knowledge of Christian truth except from Holy Scripture. Even though religious life in the congregation is nourished far more by devotional works than by Scripture, Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* II² 12, they are nevertheless only channels through which the truth of Holy Scripture is conveyed to believers in a more comprehensible form.

Moreover, dogmatics is something wholly different from symbolics. The latter describes and explains the confession of the church. But dogmatics dogmatizes, that is, it sets forth not what is regarded as truth in the religious domain, but what must be regarded as truth. It must indeed stand in relation to the confession, as also to the school and the church. Yet dogmatics stands independently alongside the confession; it expounds the truth of God in its own distinctive way. Scripture has been given to the church, but also to the school, to scholarship; and both read and investigate, explain and describe its content. Confession and dogmatics thereby act upon each other; the confession, in turn, is just as dependent upon dogmatics as dogmatics is upon the confession. *Dogmata* are not produced by the church but are the fruit of theology. First there were the Apologists, then came Nicaea. First the Reformers appeared, and later the Protestant confessions followed, etc., Harnack, *Dogmengesch.* I 10, and my article, *Theol. Stud.* 1891, pp. 258–275. Finally, the doctrine of Holy Scripture as *unicum principium theologiae* is itself also purely Reformational and Reformed. The *Nederl. Conf.* expressly teaches in arts. 2 and 7 that the knowledge of God and of His service is drawn only from Scripture and that Scripture is therefore indeed a source and not merely a *norma*. Calvin, *Instit.* I. cap. 6, Melanchton in the preface to his *Loci*, and all *dogmatici* declare that the knowledge of God can be obtained clearly and fully only from Scripture. Almost every dogmatics begins with the doctrine of Scripture as *unicum principium theologiae*. The attributes *auctoritas, sufficientia, perfectio*, which the Protestants ascribed to Holy Scripture over against Rome, demonstrate the same thing, Daubanton, *Theol. Stud.* 1885, pp. 191–210. Their preference for speaking not of *fons* but of *principium* does not weaken this wholly unique significance of Scripture for dogmatics. The name *principium* is even preferable to *fons*. The latter expression represents the relation between Scripture and theology as mechanical and makes it appear as though the *dogmata* could be drawn from Holy Scripture like water from a spring. But *principium* points to an organic connection. The *dogmata* are not in Scripture, but their material is contained in it. Scripture contains the germs, the seeds, from which the *dogmata* grow up in the church under the instruction of the Spirit. Dogmatics is the truth of Scripture, having passed through the thinking consciousness of man and reproduced in scientific form.
