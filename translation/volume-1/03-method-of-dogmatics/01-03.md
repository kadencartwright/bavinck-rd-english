# § 3. Method of Dogmatics

## 1.

By the method of dogmatics, in the broad sense, is meant the manner in which dogmatic material is obtained and treated. Three things come into consideration here: Scripture, the confession of the church, and the personal faith of the dogmatician. The starting point—and therefore the course and outcome—of dogmatics differs according to whether these three are neglected or employed and, when employed, how they are related to one another. In fact, it always happens that every Christian, including the dogmatician, receives his convictions of faith from the circle in which he was born and raised. Through preaching the church is established and propagated from generation to generation. Faith comes from hearing. Tradition, declared infallible by Rome, was not wholly rejected by the Reformation either, and soon afterward it became a power that fettered all freedom. Dogmatic material was received from the hands of the church and the school. *Materia theologiae proprie sunt loci communes, materia remota est S. Scriptura*, say Alsted and Alting, Schweizer, Glaub. der ev. ref. K. I 210. And after the reaction of the so-called *Theologia Biblica*, the social element in religion and the ecclesiastical character of dogmatics have in this century once again been placed in the clearest light from various quarters. There is scarcely anyone who denies the confessionally defined character of religion and dogmatics, Schleiermacher, Glaub. §19; Rothe, Zur Dogm. S. 27; Lange, Dogm. I 659 f.; Herzog² 12, 651 f., et al. In the Netherlands, the Groningen theologians once again emphasized the church, H. de Groot, De Gron. Godg. 68, 71-74, 97f.; Instit. theol. nat. § 37; Voorlez. over de gesch. der opvoeding des menschdoms door God, II, lectures 16 and 17. Even modernist theologians ordinarily align themselves with ecclesiastical dogma in their treatment of the doctrine of faith, Biedermann, Chr. Dogm. 2te Aufl. II. 170 f.; Lipsius, Dogm. § 7; Scholten, Leer der Herv. Kerk.

## 2.

Yet the church with its confession cannot be the *principium* of dogmatics. The Greek and Roman churches have indeed declared tradition infallible, but at all times churches, sects, and movements whose Christian character could not be denied have appealed from that tradition to Holy Scripture. In *Theologia biblica* itself, an attempt was even made to arrive at a doctrine of faith from Scripture alone, without the aid of church and confession. This attempt had already been prepared by Erasmus, Joh. Jansen, Gesch. des deutschen Volkes II. 15; the Socinians, Herzog² 14, 389; and the Remonstrants, in the preface to the apology for their confession. It found support among the many sects that subsequently arose within and alongside the churches of the Reformation. It was introduced into the churches themselves by Calixtus and Coccejus and gained more and more ground in the eighteenth century. Its intention is evident, for example, from the works of A. F. Büsching, Epitome Theol. Christ. e solis S. S. verbis concinnatae et ab omnibus rebus et verbis scholasticis purgatae, Gott. 1755. Gedanken von der Beschaffenheid und dem Vorzüge der bibl. dogm. Theologie vor der scholastischen 1758. Even in this century, such a biblical tendency in dogmatics is advocated by Beck and his followers; by Doedes, Leer der zaligheid, 2de dr. 1876; and also by Ritschl and his school, which returns from dogmatics corrupted by Greek philosophy to God’s revelation in the person of Christ.

## 3.

But even this does not remove the possibility of conflict between the doctrine of Scripture and the personal conviction of the dogmatician. So long as truth is objectively deposited for the dogmatician in Scripture or in the doctrine of the church, it seems impossible to do justice to the dogmatician’s personality. Dogmatics then remains an exposition of the doctrine of Scripture or church, but perhaps without the dogmatician’s personal assent. That, however, is not dogmatics. For dogmatics does not consist in a historical report; it sets forth what ought to be regarded as truth and thus includes the dogmatician’s personal conviction. Since, for many, authority in matters of religion fell away entirely and the *religio subjectiva* was made independent of the *religio objectiva*, religious consciousness, conscience, feeling, reason—or whatever one wishes to call it—has become the source and standard of religious conceptions. Through and after Schleiermacher, all theology, among the orthodox as well as the modernists, has become a theology of consciousness. Scholten, Schweizer, Biedermann, and Lipsius may still take the ecclesiastical formulation as their starting point in treating the *dogmata*, but in the end they nevertheless give nothing other than their personal faith. Theologians such as Martensen, Dorner, Hofmann, Philippi, Frank, and others likewise take their starting point in the consciousness of the believer. In the Netherlands, Van Oosterzee, Dogm. § 10, included Christian consciousness among the sources of dogmatics. In Geloof des harten, Des Amorie van der Hoeven Jr. wrote: “The ineffable word lies written in our heart, and Christ gave it voice.” Beets, Dichtwerken IV 130, sang: “To be wholly objective is the demand—it is human. But could it be possible? Ah, do not delude yourself with appearances! Systems are personal or inhuman.” And Prof. Van Manen, in his inaugural address at Groningen, drew attention to the personal character of doctrinal theology, Gron. 1884. From this personal character of dogmatics, Doedes, Encycl. p. 168f., explains the boundless confusion that prevails in its field.
