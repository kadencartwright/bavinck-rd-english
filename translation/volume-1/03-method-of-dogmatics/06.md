## 6.

Thus it appears that the only true method is the one employed by biblical theologians. Yet this current too suffers from great one-sidedness. It imagines that it stands before Scripture entirely without preconceptions and renders its content purely and objectively. But it forgets that every believer and every dogmatician first receives his convictions of faith from the hand of his church. He therefore never comes to Scripture from the outside, without any knowledge or preconceived opinion, but already brings from home a certain conception of the content of revelation and thus also views Scripture through the spectacles the church places before him. When every dogmatician begins his work, whether or not he acknowledges it, he stands within that historical manifestation of Christianity in which he was born and raised, and he comes to Scripture as a Reformed, Lutheran, or Roman Catholic Christian. Here too one simply cannot divest oneself of one’s environment; one is always a child of one’s age, a product of one’s surroundings. Toorenenbergen, _De Chr. Geloofsleer_, p. 6; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, 1889, S. 8 f. The outcome also accords with this expectation: all dogmatic handbooks published by the biblical current faithfully reflect the author’s personal and ecclesiastical standpoint; they can make no claim to greater objectivity than the works of ecclesiastical dogmaticians. The pure gospel that Ritschl, for example, finds in Luther and in Jesus corresponds entirely to the conception he himself has formed of it. All these biblical currents are therefore continually judged by history. They serve their purpose for a time and reawaken a forgotten truth, but they do not alter the stream of ecclesiastical life and possess no permanence in themselves. Ordinarily they arise from a religious conviction that can no longer find itself even in Scripture. They always begin by appealing from the confession to Scripture, but soon go back from Scripture to the person of Christ and end by assailing even His authority. And history then shows again and again that Christianity has been preserved in relatively purest form in the confessions of the churches. But such a biblical conception is not only practically impossible; it is also theoretically incorrect. Scripture is not a statute book whose articles need only be consulted in order to discover its opinion in a particular case. It is composed of many books by different persons, from widely differing times, and with diverse contents. It is not an abstract but an organic, living unity. Nowhere does it contain an outline of the doctrine of faith; this must be derived from the whole organism of Scripture. Scripture is not designed for us to repeat it after it, but for us, as free children of God, to think its thoughts after it. But then all so-called _Voraussetzungslosigkeit_ and objectivity are also impossible. So much study and reflection by the subject is involved that one person is wholly incapable of it. Centuries are needed for it. For this the church has been appointed, and to her belongs the promise of the Spirit’s guidance into all truth. Whoever isolates himself from the church—that is, from Christendom and from the entire history of dogma—loses Christian truth. He becomes like a branch torn from the tree and withering away, like a member severed from the body and therefore destined for death. Only in the communion of saints can the length and breadth and depth and height of the love of Christ be comprehended (Eph. 3:18). In addition, advocates of this current forget that Christianity is universal and can and must enter into all forms and conditions. They set grace in hostility over against nature and do not reckon sufficiently with the incarnation of the Word. For just as truly as the Son of God became man, so too the thought of God laid down in Scripture becomes flesh and blood in human consciousness. Dogmatics is, and ought to be, the divine thought fully entering and being taken up into our human consciousness, uttered freely and independently in our language—in its essence the fruit of the ages, in its form belonging to this age (Da Costa). Therefore the contrast often drawn between biblical theology and dogmatics is also incorrect, as though the former presented the content of Scripture and the latter that of ecclesiastical dogmas. For dogmatics intends nothing other than to expound the thoughts of God that He has laid down in Holy Scripture. Maresius, _Syst. Theol._, loc. 1 § 8. But it does so, as it ought, in a scientific manner, in a form and according to a method such as scholarship requires. In this sense the Reformed formerly defended the legitimacy of so-called _theologia scholastica_. They had no objection at all to revealed truth also being presented in simpler form under the name of _theologia positiva_, _catechetica_, etc. But they vigorously denied that these differed in content; the only thing distinguishing them was a difference in form and method. By adopting this position, they maintained as strongly as possible, on the one hand, the unity and coherence of faith and theology, of church and school, while, on the other hand, they also held high the scientific character of theology. However lofty and wonderful the thoughts of God might be, they were nevertheless not aphorisms but formed an organic unity, a systematic whole that could also be thought through and rendered in scientific form. Scripture itself urges this theological labor when everywhere it places the strongest emphasis not on abstract knowing but on doctrine and truth, knowledge and wisdom.
