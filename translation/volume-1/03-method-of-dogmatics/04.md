## 4.

This survey of the three dogmatic currents already makes clear that none of the three can do without the others. Taken by itself, each becomes one-sided and lapses into error. The critical current, which rejects confession and Scripture as sources of knowledge and seeks to derive all religious truth from the subject, is first of all in conflict with experience. In religion too we are products of our environment. We receive our religious conceptions and impressions from those who care for and educate us, and at all times remain bound to the circle in which we live. A sound psychology demonstrates that intellect and heart, reason and conscience, feeling and imagination cannot serve in any domain as sources for the knowledge of truth. Just as physically we are bound to nature and must receive food and drink, shelter and clothing from it, so psychically—in art, science, religion, and morality—we are dependent on the world outside us. All our knowledge comes from without; there are no _ideae innatae_. Feeling especially cannot qualify as a source of knowledge, for feeling is never a _prius_ but always a _posterius_. It only reacts to what affects it and then produces a sensation of pleasure or displeasure, of what is agreeable or disagreeable. Tholuck, art. “Gefühl” in Herzog²; Bender, _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ 1872, S. 659 f.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Glaub._ I 60 f.; Hodge, _System. Theol._ I 65 f.; Hoekstra, _Godg. Bĳdr._ 1864, pp. 1–43; idem, _Wĳsgeerige Godsdienstleer_, Amst., 1894, pp. 59 f., 213 f. Declaring religious and moral man autonomous is always connected with deism or pantheism. Deism makes man independent of God and the world, teaches the all-sufficiency of his reason, and leads to rationalism; pantheism has God come to revelation and self-consciousness in man, and cultivates mysticism. Both destroy objective truth, leave reason and feeling, intellect and heart, to themselves, and end in unbelief or superstition. Reason criticizes away all revelation, and feeling gives the Roman Catholic as much right to picture Mary as the sinless queen of heaven as it gives the Protestant to contest this belief. Schweizer, _Glaub. der ev. ref. K._ I 94. It is therefore noteworthy that Holy Scripture never directs man to himself as the source and standard of religious truth. How could it, when it describes the natural man in his entirety as darkened and corrupted by sin—in his intellect (Ps. 14:3; Rom. 1:21–23; 8:7; 1 Cor. 1:23; 2:14; 2 Cor. 3:5; Eph. 4:23; Gal. 1:6, 7; 1 Tim. 6:5; 2 Tim. 3:8), in his heart (Gen. 6:5; 8:21; Jer. 17:9; Ezek. 36:26; Mark 7:21), in his will (John 8:34; Rom. 7:14; 8:7; Eph. 2:3), and also in his conscience (Jer. 17:9; 1 Cor. 8:7, 10, 12; 10:28; 1 Tim. 4:2; Titus 1:15)? For the knowledge of truth Scripture always directs him to objective revelation, to the word, the instruction that has gone out from God (Deut. 4:1; Isa. 8:20; John 5:39; 2 Tim. 3:15; 2 Pet. 1:19, etc.). And even where objective truth becomes our personal possession through faith, that faith is never like a fountain producing living water from itself, but like a channel conveying the water to us from elsewhere.
