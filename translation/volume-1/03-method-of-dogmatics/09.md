## 9.

This does not, however, nullify the personal character of doctrinal theology. It cannot and may not do so, since dogmatics is not a historical report but an indication of what ought to count as truth in the religious domain. Yet this personal character of dogmatics does not arise from severing every tie to the object and allowing each person simply to present whatever he pleases. For then dogmatics ceases to be a science and is nothing but a private opinion. If dogmatics, according to Prof. Van Manen, p. 23, must abandon the illusion that there is an objective truth given from without, then dogmatics no longer exists. There are then only subjective opinions, one as good as another. Every science worthy of the name must have its own object existing in the real world; it further presupposes that this object is knowable; and it is then bound as strictly as possible to that object. No different requirement applies to dogmatics. It too must have an object of its own; that object must be knowable; and to that object it is absolutely bound. But denial of such an object of its own, an object that can be known, may never be made good by appealing to the personal character of doctrinal theology. That confuses two wholly different questions. The personal character of dogmatics can be discussed only once it has first been established that dogmatics has an object of its own.

Every science—of nature, history, law, morality, and so forth—has, after all, an object present in the real world. Yet every science still bears a personal character, one certainly less than another: mathematics, for example, to a much smaller degree than history. But the less formal the sciences are and the nearer they lie to the center, the more the influence of personality increases. In the practice of science, no more than anywhere else, a person can divest himself of himself. He brings with him his upbringing, worldview, heart and conscience, sympathies and antipathies, and these naturally exert an influence on his investigation and reflection. This is not wrong in itself. The dualism that divides man into two halves and reduces him in the practice of science to pure intellect is practically impossible and theoretically misconceived. The only requirement is that always and everywhere, including when he practices science, a person be a good person, a man of God thoroughly equipped for every good work. It is no different in dogmatics; indeed, here all this applies _a fortiori_. For dogmatics deals precisely with man’s deepest convictions of faith and with the center of all science. Here above all the requirement is that a person be good, that he stand in the right relation to God, whom to know is eternal life.

It is therefore also the teaching of Scripture that objective revelation is completed in subjective illumination. The Reformed doctrine of Scripture stands in the closest connection with that of the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_. The _verbum externum_ does not remain outside us but becomes through faith a _verbum internum_. The Holy Spirit, who gave Scripture, also bears witness to that Scripture in the hearts of believers. Scripture itself secures its own triumph in the consciousness of Christ’s congregation. For this reason the believer feels himself bound to Scripture with his whole soul. Into it he is led by the Holy Spirit, the _Doctor ecclesiae_. His entire aim is to take into his consciousness those thoughts of God laid down in Scripture and to understand them through reflection. But in doing so he remains human, with his own disposition, upbringing, and insight. Faith itself does not arise in the same way in every person and is not of equal strength in all. Reason differs in sharpness, depth, and clarity of thought. The influence of sin continues to operate, also in his consciousness and intellect. As a result of all these influences, doctrinal theology continues to bear a personal character. This is the case here as in every science. Even prophets and apostles viewed the same truth from different sides. The unity of faith has no more been attained than the unity of knowledge. But it is precisely through diversity that God leads His congregation toward unity. When that unity of faith and knowledge has been attained, dogmatics too will have completed its task. Until then it remains entrusted with the calling to interpret, in the field of science, the thoughts God has laid down for us in Holy Scripture. The dogmatician will be most excellently equipped for this task if he lives in the fellowship of faith with the church of Christ and confesses Scripture as the sole and sufficient _principium_ of the knowledge of God. The dogmatician therefore receives the content of his faith from the hands of the church. In a pedagogical sense he comes through the church to Scripture. But he may no more stop there than may any believer. He has the calling to dissect to their very fibers the dogmas he has learned through the church and to investigate how they are rooted in Holy Scripture. His task might thus be thought to consist in first setting forth the dogmas objectively and then tracing them back to Holy Scripture; his method would then be historical-analytic. But although this method may in itself have much to recommend it for a single dogma, and although it is also of subordinate value in dogmatics, it has this disadvantage: it never arrives at a scientific system. The dogmatician therefore does better to follow another path, not from the river to the source but from the source to the river. Without detracting from the truth that in a pedagogical sense the church precedes Scripture, he can nevertheless take his position in Scripture itself as the _principium theologiae_ and then develop the dogmas from it. He then reproduces, as it were, the church’s labor of thought. He shows us how the dogmas have grown organically out of Scripture; how not one isolated text but Scripture as a whole is the firm, broad foundation on which the dogmatic edifice has risen. In the second place, this synthetic, genetic method gives him the advantage of being able to demonstrate the unity and mutual connection of the dogmas. The various dogmas are not isolated propositions but form a unity. There is really only one dogma, born from Scripture and branching and dividing into all kinds of particular dogmas. The dogmatician’s method cannot and may not be other than systematic. Finally, he also has the calling, in this genetic and systematic development of the dogmas, to draw attention to possible deviations, to fill possible gaps, and thus to labor for the future development of the dogmas. This is the critical task to which the dogmatician is called, but it is already implied of itself in the systematic treatment of the dogmatic material. In this way he seeks in dogmatics to give an exposition of the treasures of wisdom and knowledge that are hidden in Christ and displayed in Scripture.
