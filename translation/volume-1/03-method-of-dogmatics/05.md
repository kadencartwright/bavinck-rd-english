## 5.

Rome has understood exceedingly well the impossibility of such religious and moral autonomy and has therefore bound man, on pain of forfeiting the salvation of his soul, to the infallible church. For the Roman Catholic Christian, this church, and thus in the final instance the infallible pope, is the *principium* of his faith. The *Papa dixit* puts an end to every contradiction. But history teaches that this theoretical or practical infallibility of the church has at all times encountered contradiction and opposition, not only in Rome but also in the churches of the Reformation. It is not unbelieving but devout circles, in the first place, that have felt this power of the hierarchy as an oppressive bond upon the conscience. Throughout the centuries there has been not only scientific, social, and political opposition to the hierarchical power of the church, but also deeply religious and moral opposition. It will not do to explain this opposition as arising from unbelief and disobedience and, as though deliberately, to disregard the religious motives underlying the opposition of the various sects and movements. No one dares condemn all these sects because they resisted the church and its tradition. Even Rome recoils from this conclusion. The confession *extra ecclesiam nulla salus* is too burdensome even for the strongest faith. The law that we see governing every sphere therefore holds in religion and morality as well. On the one hand, there is a revolutionary spirit that wishes to raze everything that has developed historically to the ground and then begin building anew; but there is also a false conservatism that delights in leaving the existing order untouched merely because it exists and, in Calvin's familiar words, *malum bene positum non movere*. Everywhere and in every sphere a certain radicalism is needed at the proper time to restore the balance, advance development, and prevent the stream of life from silting up. In art and science, in state and society, and likewise in religion and morality, a deadening routine gradually develops that suppresses and violates the rights of personality, genius, invention, inspiration, freedom, and conscience. But then, at the proper time, the man always arises who can no longer endure under that pressure, who casts off the yoke of servitude, and who once again takes up the cause of human freedom, of Christian freedom. Those are the turning points in history. Thus Christ himself once appeared against the tradition of the elders and returned to the law and the prophets. And thus the Reformation once dared to protest against Rome's hierarchy, not for the sake of any scientific, social, or political interest, but in the name of the Christian. Even among the sects and movements that later arose in the Protestant churches, that religious and ethical motive often cannot be denied. The *theologia biblica* also defends an important part of religious truth. When church and theology prefer repose to struggle, they themselves awaken the opposition that reminds them of their Christian calling and task. Rome, by its nature, can never approve such opposition and must condemn it beforehand. The Reformation itself arose from such opposition and cannot withhold from others what it claimed for itself. And Holy Scripture, though far removed from all revolutionary resistance, has nevertheless sanctioned the right to oppose every human command that conflicts with God's Word, in Peter's royal declaration: πειθαρχειν δει θεῳ μαλλον ἠ ἀνθρωποις, Acts 5:29.
