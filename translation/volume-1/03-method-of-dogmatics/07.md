## 7.

A good method of dogmatics must therefore take account of all three data: Scripture, the church, and personal conviction. Only then is it possible to be preserved from gross one-sidedness. Yet it still remains necessary to determine the relation in which these three data stand to one another. As a rule, we receive our religious convictions from our environment. This is the case in all religions and also in Christianity. We are all born as members of a church. The covenant of grace receives us from our birth onward. God’s promises in Christ are for believers and their seed alike. In critical times such as ours, it frequently happens that a painful breach later arises between the faith of childhood and personal conviction. If this breach is such that one must indeed leave one’s own church but can nevertheless join another historically existing church, it is healed relatively quickly. There is then a change, but no loss of religion itself, of the Christian name, of fellowship, or of confession. A dogma still remains that stands firm and offers us support and comfort in life. From this standpoint, therefore, a dogmatics is still possible that describes the truth of God as it has found recognition in a particular church. But doubt frequently penetrates much more deeply into religious life. Many lose all faith and arrive at skepticism and agnosticism; here there can no longer be any question of dogmatics, faith, confession, or fellowship, for mere negation is incapable of establishing fellowship. Others, however, no longer able to maintain the faith of childhood, attempt through earnest exertion and struggle to acquire a religious conviction of their own. Here too, of course, the influence of one’s environment makes itself felt; no one ever arrives at a religious conviction in complete independence. The only difference is that what was no longer found in a church is now sought in a philosophical school. In this century every philosophy has been taken up in turn in order to awaken and maintain certain religious convictions. From this standpoint too, dogmatics is no longer possible. There remains only a religious faith, a *Glaubenslehre*, a philosophy of religion, a philosophical doctrine of religion.

Dogmatics is therefore possible only for one who stands in the fellowship of faith with one Christian church or another. This also lies in the nature of religious faith. Religious conceptions differ from scientific ones, among other respects, in that they rest not on one’s own insight or on the authority of any human being, but solely on the authority of God. But this implies that they have also found faith and recognition within a religious circle, that is, within a church. Only there does the religious authority of a religious conception become manifest. A church believes its confession not because it scientifically perceives its truth, but solely on the ground of the Word of God, whether that Word expresses itself only in Scripture or also through ecclesiastical organs. Whoever seeks his religious conviction from a philosophical school confuses religion with science and receives nothing but a *sententia* or *opinio doctoris* that is always uncertain and contested by many. Religious faith, however, is by its very nature bound to a community and its confession. Here too it is as it is everywhere else. Abstractions, *universalia*, do not exist in reality. *The* tree, *the* human being, *the* science, *the* language, *the* religion, and *the* theology are nowhere to be found. There are only particular trees, human beings, sciences, languages, and religions. Just as a language is connected with a people, and just as science and philosophy are always practiced within a particular movement and school, so religion and theology can be found and cultivated only within a community akin to them. A church is the natural soil for religion and theology. Just as there is not yet *the* church, neither is there *the* religion or *the* theology. There are only different churches and, likewise, different theologies. And this will continue until the congregation in Christ has reached its full maturity and all have come to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God. This unity cannot be seized by force, but it can best be promoted when each person thinks through the faith of his own church and presents it in its purest form. Not apart from the existing churches but through them Christ prepares for Himself His one holy, universal church. And not apart from the various ecclesiastical *dogmata* but through them the unity of the knowledge of God is prepared and attained. In this way the dogmatician will also be best able to labor fruitfully for the purification and development of the religious life and doctrine of his church. Connection with what already exists is the condition for improvement in the future. What shall be lies within what now is, Twesten, Vorles. über die Dogm. 2e Aufl. I 46. This significance of the church for theology and dogmatics is grounded in the connection that Christ Himself has established between them. He promised His church the Holy Spirit, who would guide her into the truth. The history of dogma thereby comes to stand in a glorious light. It is the explication of Scripture, Lange, Christl. Dogm. I 3; Schoeberlein, Princip. und System der Dogm. 26; the interpretation that the Holy Spirit has given within the church of the treasures of the Word. The dogmatician, therefore, must draw the material for his dogmatics not only from the written confession of his own church, but must consider it in connection with the entire distinctive faith and life of his church, and these in turn in connection with the history of the whole church of Christ. Thus he stands on the shoulders of his forebears. He feels himself surrounded by a cloud of witnesses and lets his testimony merge with that voice of many waters. Every dogmatics ought also to join in the hymn of praise that the church of all ages offers to God.
