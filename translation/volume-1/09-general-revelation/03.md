### B. General and Special Revelation

## 3.

Christian theology soon came to make an important distinction within this revelation.
On the one hand, the connection and agreement between the religion of Christians and that of the pagans, between theology and philosophy, could not be denied altogether; on the other hand, Christianity was nevertheless a distinct, independent religion, different in every respect from that of the pagans.
Thus theologians were led to distinguish between _revelatio (religio, theologia) naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_.
In substance, this distinction is already found among the earliest church fathers.
Justin Martyr speaks of an ἀνθρωπειος διδασκαλια, obtained through το ἐμφυτον παντι γενει ἀνθρωπων σπερμα του λογου, and of a γνωσις και θεωρια that becomes ours only through Christ, _Apol._ II 8, 10, 13.
Tertullian has a separate treatise, _de testimonio animae_, and speaks of one knowledge of God from the works of creation and another, more complete knowledge through men filled with God’s Spirit, _Apolog._ II c. 18.
Irenaeus frequently speaks in the same sense, _adv. haer._ II c. 6, 9, 28; III 25; IV 6.
Augustine acknowledges a revelation of God in nature, _de Gen. ad. litt._ 4, 32; _de civ. Dei_ 8, 11 sq.; 19, 1, etc., but alongside _ratio_ he places _auctoritas_ and _fides_, which alone lead to the true knowledge of God, _c. Acad._ 3,20; _de util. cred._ 11; _Conf._ 5,5; 7,26; _de civ._ 10,29.
In John of Damascus, _de fide orthod._ I. c. 1 sq., this distinction already bears all the marks of a dogma.
The later division of _theologia naturalis_ into _insita_ and _acquisita_ is likewise already found among the earliest ecclesiastical writers.
Tertullian appeals to the inward testimony of the soul and to the contemplation of God’s works.
Augustine says explicitly that God can be known from visible things, _de Gen. ad. litt._ 4,32, but points especially to self-consciousness and self-knowledge as the way to eternal truth, _de vera relig._ 72; _de mag._ 38; _de trin._ 4,1.
John of Damascus, _de fide orth._ I c. 1 and 3, already clearly places innate and acquired knowledge of God side by side.
The boundaries between the two kinds of revelation were not delineated so quickly.
For a long time theologians continued trying to prove the Christian _dogmata_ from nature and reason.
Augustine sought to prove the Trinity (_de trin._ lib. 9-15); Anselm, the incarnation and satisfaction in his _Cur deus homo_; and Albert the Great (cf. Stöckl, _Philos. des M. A._ II. 384 f.) and Thomas Aquinas (_S. c. Gent._ II. 15 sq.), creation _a posteriori_.
Raymond of Sabunde went farthest in this respect: in his _Liber naturae sive creaturarum_, later wrongly called _Theologia naturalis_ (ed. by J. Sighart, Solisbaci, 1852, without the prologue, which was condemned in 1595), he sought to construct the whole Christian doctrine from human nature, without the aid of Scripture and tradition and while avoiding the scholastic method.
But this rational argumentation was only an aid that came afterward; the _dogmata_ stood fixed _a priori_ on the ground of revelation: _adjuvantur in fide invisibilium per ea, quae facta sunt_, Peter Lombard, _Sent._ I dist. 3, 6; cf. 2, 1.
Furthermore, the knowledge obtainable from nature was restricted to certain _articuli mixti_, which centered on the three concepts of God, virtue, and immortality, Thomas Aquinas, _S. c. Gent._ Lib. 1-3.
In scholasticism, however, the distinction between _theol. naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_ was drawn ever more sharply and developed into an absolute opposition.
Through natural revelation, some strictly scientific knowledge of God and divine things could be obtained, Thomas Aquinas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 1 art. 5; cf. Bellarmine, _Controv._ IV p. 277 sq.
Thomas is so firmly convinced of this that he raises the question whether, in that case, assent to these truths known from nature does not lose all its merit.
Believing is meritorious only when it is not knowing but holding something true on authority, an act of the intellect _ex motu voluntatis motae per gratiam_, ib. II 2 qu. 2 art. 9.
The answer to this question is that knowledge does indeed diminish the _ratio fidei_, but that the _ratio caritatis_ nevertheless always remains in the believer, that is, the disposition continually to accept even what is known as true on God’s authority, ib. II 2 qu. 2 art. 10.
And this disposition to believe the _articuli mixti_ on authority always remains necessary because of special circumstances as well (see below under no. 7).
To this knowledge from nature and reason, supernatural revelation adds the knowledge of the _mysteria_, but this knowledge rests exclusively on authority and is and remains from beginning to end a matter of faith.
The mysteries of Christianity belong to an order that is supernatural in the proper sense, not for an incidental reason—because of sin—but by its nature for every human being, including the sinless human being and even the angels, and therefore can never be known otherwise than through revelation.
This distinctive Roman Catholic doctrine will be discussed more fully under special revelation.
But it should already be noted here that at Rome knowing and believing, _ratio_ and _auctoritas_, natural and supernatural revelation, stand dualistically side by side.
Rome therefore acknowledges, on the one hand, the right of rationalism in the domain of natural revelation and condemns the excessive supernaturalism that considers no knowledge possible even in the _articuli mixti_ except through revelation.
On the other hand, it embraces supernaturalism in the domain of the mysteries as strictly as possible and condemns every rationalism that seeks, _a priori_ or _a posteriori_, to escape authority and faith in the _dogmata_ and to transform them into knowledge.
It rejects both Tertullian and Origen and condemns both the traditionalism of de Bonald and the rationalism of Hermes (above, p. 94 f.).
According to the _Vaticanum_, sess. III, _Const. Dogm. de fide cath._ cap. 2, the Roman Catholic Church confesses _Deum.... naturali humanae rationis lumine e rebus creatis certo cognosci posse_, but also that it has pleased God _alia, eaque supernaturali via, se ipsum ac aeterna voluntatis decreta humano generi revelare_.
