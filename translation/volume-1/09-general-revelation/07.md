### C. The Insufficiency of General Revelation

## 7.

This _revelatio generalis_, however, is insufficient for various reasons. On this too all Christian theologians are agreed. Irenaeus, _Adv. haeres._ 2, 28, argues against the Gnostics for the limitation of human knowledge. Justin Martyr, _Dial. c. Tryph._, introduction; Tertullian, _De an._ c. 1; Lactantius, _Instit. div._ 3, 1; 4, 1; Arnobius, _Adv. nat._ I, 38; II, 6, portray the weakness of reason in very strong colors. Augustine does not deny that some truth is also found among the pagans from which Christians can profit, _De doctr. chr._ 2, 60. But philosophy is not the true way to salvation. It can teach only a few people and only a little, _De trin._ 13, 12; _De civ._ 12, 20; _De util. cred._ 10, 24. It indeed knows the goal but not the way that leads to the goal, _Conf._ 5, 5; 7, 26; _De civ._ 10, 29. It often leads astray and suppresses the truth in unrighteousness, _De trin._ 13, 24; does not seek it in a pious manner, _Conf._ 5, 4; lacks the love necessary for knowledge of the truth, _De civ._ 9, 20; and is hindered in knowledge of the truth by its own _superbia_, for _humilitas_ alone is the way to life, _De civ._ 2, 7. Another way to the truth is therefore still needed—namely, _auctoritas_, _De vera relig._ c. 24; _De mor. eccl._ 1, 2.

Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 1, art. 1; _S. c. Gent._ I, 4, argues for the necessity of revelation even for the _articuli mixti_ known by reason. The Roman Catholic Church clearly expressed the _insufficientia_ of _theol. nat._ in the preface to the _Catech. Romanus_, and in the _Vaticanum_, sess. 3, cap. 2 _de revelatione_, and can. 2, 2–4. The Protestant theologians judged no differently concerning this _insufficientia_ of general revelation: Calvin, _Inst._ I, 5, § 11 sq. and cap. 6; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ I, § 9–13; Trigland, _Antapologia_, cap. 17; Owen, Θεολογουμενα I, cap. 6; Turretin, _Theol. Elenct._ I, qu. 4; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 61, 39.

In earlier times the sufficiency of general revelation and of the _religio naturalis_ built upon it was taught only by the Pelagians, who assumed a threefold way to salvation—namely, the _lex naturae_, _lex Mosis_, and _lex Christi_. There were also always a few theologians in the Christian church who judged more favorably concerning the pagans and believed in the possibility of their salvation, such as Justin, Clement of Alexandria, Erasmus, Zwingli, and others; cf. Vossius, _Historia Pelag._, 1655, pp. 383 sq. But for them this belief generally rested not upon the doctrine of the sufficiency of general revelation but upon the supposition that God also worked with his special grace among the pagans, whether in this life or after it. By contrast, the complete sufficiency of general revelation and natural religion was taught in the eighteenth century by Deists and Rationalists such as Cherbury, Tindal, Collins, Rousseau, Kant, and others. Literature in Lechler, _Gesch. des engl. Deïsmus_, 1841, and art. “Deïsmus” in Herzog²; Bretschneider, _System. Entw. aller in der Dogm. vork. Begriffe_, 1841, S. 35 f.; Clarisse, _Encycl._, 1835, pp. 405 sq.; Doedes, _Inleiding tot de Leer van God_, 1880, pp. 197 f.

There can scarcely be any doubt concerning the _insufficientia_ of general revelation. First, it is apparent from the fact that this revelation supplies us at most with some knowledge of God’s existence and of certain of his attributes, such as goodness and justice; but it leaves us entirely ignorant of the person of Christ, who alone is the way to the Father, Matt. 11:27; John 14:6; 17:3; Acts 4:12. General revelation is therefore insufficient for man as sinner. It knows of no grace and forgiveness; often it is even a revelation of wrath, Rom. 1:20. Grace and forgiveness, which must be the essence of religion, are an act of good pleasure, not of nature and necessity. General revelation can at most make some truths known, but brings no facts, no history, and therefore changes nothing in being. It enlightens consciousness to some extent and restrains sin, but it does not recreate the nature of man and world. It can inspire fear, but not trust and love, Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 66, 218.

Second, the knowledge that general revelation can supply is not only meager and insufficient, but is also uncertain, always mixed with error, and inaccessible to by far the majority of human beings. The history of philosophy has been a history of mutually destructive systems; it ended among the Greeks in Skepticism, in the Middle Ages in Nominalism, and now in Agnosticism. The truths most necessary for religion—the existence and being of God, the origin and destiny of man and world, sin and forgiveness, reward and punishment—have by turns been taught and opposed. No sufficient certainty concerning all these questions can be obtained in philosophy. Cicero, _Tusc._ 1,5, therefore rightly asks: _ex philosophis nonne optimus et gravissimus quisque confitetur multa se ignorare, multa sibi etiam atque etiam esse discenda?_

But even if some thinkers arrived at a measure of true and pure knowledge, it was always still mixed with all manner of error. Every philosophical system has its gaps and defects. Plato, whose system according to Augustine, _De civ._ 8,5, is most closely related to Christianity, defends the exposure of weak children, pederasty, community of women, and so forth. Even in morality there is great difference and uncertainty: _vérité en deça des Pyrenées, erreur au delà_ (Pascal). _Nescio quomodo nihil tam absurde dici potest, quod non dicatur ab aliquo philosophorum_, Cicero, _De divin._ 2,58.

And even if the philosophers had possessed the most beautiful and purest doctrine, they would nevertheless have lacked the authority to gain acceptance for it among the people. In the practice of life they therefore often conform once again to popular belief and popular morals, or with an _Odi profanum vulgus et arceo_ withdraw from the people in haughty superiority. Their mutual conflict and the contradiction between their doctrine and life weakened their influence. And even if none of this had been the case, the doctrine of the philosophers could never have become or remained the religion of the people, because in matters of religion an intellectual clericalism and a scientific hierarchy are intolerable.

Thomas was therefore entirely right when he said that even in those truths which general revelation makes known to us, revelation and authority are still needed, because that knowledge is suited only to a few, would require too long a time of investigation, and would even then remain imperfect and uncertain, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 1, art. 1; II 2, qu. 2, art. 4; _S. contra Gent._ I, 4.

Third, the insufficiency of natural revelation is clearly demonstrated by the fact that no people has been satisfied with the so-called _religio naturalis_. The universal religion of the Deists, Kant’s _moralische Vernunftreligion_, and Spinoza’s _pietas_ and _obedientia_ are all nothing but pure abstractions that have never existed in reality. Even if Herbert’s five articles or Kant’s Rationalistic trilogy had been perfectly certain and demonstrable with strict scientific rigor, they would still have been incapable of founding a religion, a church. For religion is essentially different from science; it has another source and foundation. The eighteenth century could take pleasure in such truths of reason and empty abstractions. The nineteenth century, with its historical sense, quickly recognized that such a _religio naturalis_ existed nowhere and could exist nowhere. Today it is generally admitted that all religions are positive and rest upon revelation. Schleiermacher, _Glaub._ § 10, Zusatz; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 4, 500; idem, _Unterricht in der christl. Religion_³, S. 20; Frank, _System der chr. Wahrheit_ I² 512 f.; Doedes, _Encyclopaedie_, 190, 191; W. Bender, _Zur Geschichte der Emancipation der natürl. Theol._, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1883, S. 529⁴–592; R. Rütschi, _Die Lehre von der natürlichen Religion u. vom Naturrecht_, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1884, S. 1–48; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ I 19 f.
