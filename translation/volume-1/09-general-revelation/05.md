## 5.

Scripture does know the concept of a fixed order of nature, but in revelation it nevertheless makes no distinction between natural and supernatural. It uses the same words for both—for example, גלה, φανερουν, and ἀποκαλυπτειν also for the _revelatio naturalis_, Job 12:22; 33:16; 36:10; Rom. 1:18, 19. Nösgen, _Beweis des Glaubens_, Nov. 1890, S. 416–417, therefore objects wrongly to already giving the name revelation to God’s revelation in nature. Properly speaking, from Scripture’s standpoint all revelation, including that in nature, is supernatural. The word itself says nothing about the manner in which something is revealed, but says only that something which was hidden comes to light. In the religious sphere it indicates that God possesses a life of his own, independent and distinct from nature, and can now emerge from his hiddenness in one way or another before the eye of rational creatures.

Thus only one who acknowledges the supernatural, an _ordo supra hanc naturam_, can speak of revelation in the proper sense; and everyone who uses the word in this sense is in principle a supernaturalist, even if he assumes only a revelation by natural means. The distinction between _revelatio naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_ is derived not from the action of God expressed in the one and the other revelation, but from the manner in which that revelation occurs—namely, _per_ or _praeter hanc naturam_. In origin all revelation is supernatural. God is always working, John 5:17.

That working of God toward the outside began with creation. Creation is God’s first revelation, the beginning and foundation of all subsequent revelation. The biblical concept of revelation is rooted in that of creation, Oehler, _Theol. des A. T._, 1882, S. 21. Through creation God first stepped outward before creatures and revealed himself to creatures. When God creates the world through his Word and makes it alive through his Spirit, the basic lines of all subsequent revelations are already drawn therein. But providence is immediately joined to creation. It too is an almighty and omnipresent power and act of God. All that exists and happens is a work of God in the proper sense and, for the pious, a revelation of his virtues and perfections.

This is how Scripture views nature and history. Creation, preservation, and government are one mighty, continuing revelation of God. No nature poetry has surpassed or equaled that of Israel, Pierson, _Geestel. Voorouders_ I, _Israel_, pp. 389 f. Everything in nature speaks to the pious of God. The heavens declare God’s glory, and the firmament his handiwork. God’s voice is upon the great waters. That voice breaks the cedars, reverberates in the thunder, and roars in the tempest. Light is his garment, heaven his curtain, the clouds his chariot. His breath creates and renews the earth. He sends rain and gives sunshine upon the righteous and the unrighteous. Leaf and grass, rain and drought, fruitful and unfruitful years, and all things come to the believer not by chance but from God’s fatherly hand. Scripture’s view of nature and history is religious and therefore also supernatural.

For Scripture, even religion and supernatural revelation are most closely connected. It recounts such a revelation not first after the fall but already before it. The relation of God and man in the _status integritatis_ is portrayed as personal intercourse. God speaks to man, Gen. 1:28–30; gives him a command that he could not know from nature, Gen. 2:16; and, as though with his own hand, brings the woman to him as a helper, Gen. 2:22. The _foedus operum_ too is not a _foedus naturae_ in the sense that it arises of itself from man’s natural disposition, but is a fruit of supernatural revelation. And since the _foedus operum_ is nothing other than the form of religion in man created in God’s image who had not yet obtained the highest, it may be said that Scripture cannot conceive pure religion without _revelatio supernaturalis_.

The supernatural does not conflict with man’s nature or with the nature of creatures; it belongs, so to speak, to the essence of man. Man is the image of God and akin to God, and through religion he stands in a direct relation to God. The nature of this relation entails that God reveal himself both objectively and subjectively to man created in his image. There is no religion without tradition, dogma, and cultus; and all these are interwoven with the concept of revelation. All religions are accordingly positive and rest not only upon natural but always also upon actual or supposed supernatural revelation. And all human beings acknowledge the supernatural by nature.

Naturalism, like Atheism, is an invention of philosophy but has no support in human nature. As long as religion belongs to the essence of man, man will also be and remain a supernaturalist. Every believer, of whatever tendency, may be a naturalist with his head, but he is nevertheless a supernaturalist with his heart. Whoever wishes to banish the supernatural from religion, and therefore from prayer and the practice of communion with God, kills religion itself. For religion presupposes real kinship and communion with God and is supernaturalistic with heart and soul. It is inseparable from the faith that God stands _supra naturam_ and can deal with nature according to his good pleasure, that he makes the order of nature subservient to the moral order, the kingdoms of the world to the kingdom of heaven, _physis_ to _ethos_. It has therefore rightly been said that the prayer for a clean heart is just as supernaturalistic as the prayer for a healthy body (Pierson).

The Theist who truly wishes to be a Theist and yet opposes supernatural revelation has by no means finished with the matter through this denial. He must either go back to Deism or Pantheism, or go forward and also accept the possibility of supernatural revelation. There is no _religio naturalis_. The Rationalistic trilogy is untenable. The only true opposite of acknowledging the supernatural is therefore not Rationalistic Deism but Naturalism—that is, the belief that no other higher power exists than that which is present and reveals itself in the current order of nature.

But then every right to believe in the triumph of the good, in the final victory of the kingdom of God, and in the power of the moral world order also falls away. For the good, the true, the moral world order, and the kingdom of God are realities that possess no power in themselves to realize themselves. The hope that human beings will bring them to dominion and yield to the power of truth is disappointed every day by experience. Their triumph is assured only if God is a personal, almighty being and can lead the whole creation, despite all resistance, to the goal intended by him. Religion, morality, the acknowledgment of a destiny for humanity and the world, faith in the victory of the good, the Theistic worldview, and faith in a personal God are all inseparably connected with Supernaturalism. The idea of God and of religion involves that of revelation.
