## 9.

But however strictly Scripture may judge the character of paganism, precisely the general revelation it teaches enables and entitles us to acknowledge all the elements of truth that are also present in the pagan religions. Formerly the study of religions stood exclusively in the service of dogmatics and apologetics. Founders of religions, such as Muhammad, were simply regarded as deceivers, enemies of God, and accomplices of the devil. Cf. Dr. Snouck Hurgronje, _De Islam_, _Gids_, 1886, II 239 f. But since those religions have become more accurately known, this explanation has proved untenable; it conflicted with both history and psychology.

According to Holy Scripture there is also among the pagans a revelation of God, an illumination of the Logos, an operation of God’s Spirit, Gen. 6:17; 7:15; Ps. 33:6; 104:30; 139:2; Job 32:8; Eccles. 3:19; Prov. 8:22 f.; Mal. 1:11, 14; John 1:9; Rom. 2:14; Gal. 4:1–3; Acts 14:16, 17; 17:22–30. Many church fathers assumed an activity of the Logos in the pagan world, Justin Martyr, _Apol._ 1,47; Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ I 7, and others.

Augustine repeatedly speaks very unfavorably of the pagans, but nevertheless also acknowledges that they saw the truth in shadow, _De civ._ 19,1; _De trin._ 4,20; that the truth was not altogether hidden from them, _De civ._ 8:11 f.; and that we must therefore profit from and appropriate to ourselves what is true in pagan philosophy, _De doctr. chr._ 2,60. _Non usque adeo in anima humana imago Dei terrenorum affectuum labe detrita est, ut nulla in ea velut lineamenta extrema remanserint, unde merito dici possit, etiam in ipsa impietate vitae suae facere aliqua legis vel sapere_, _De spir. et litt._ c. 27, 28. Many impure persons too acknowledge much that is true, _Retract._ I, c. 4.

Thomas says not only that man as a rational being can know the _veritates naturales_ without supernatural grace, _S. Theol._ I 2, qu. 109, art. 1, but also testifies, II 2, qu. 172, art. 6, that it is impossible _esse aliquam cognitionem quae totaliter sit falsa, absque admixtione alicujus veritatis_, and in this connection appeals to the words of Bede and Augustine: _nulla falsa est doctrina, quae non aliquando aliqua vera falsis intermisceat_.

The Reformed were in a still better position through their doctrine of _gratia communis_. By it, on the one hand, they were preserved from the error of Pelagianism, which taught the _sufficientia_ of _theol. naturalis_ and attached salvation to observance of the _lex naturae_; but on the other hand they could nevertheless acknowledge all the true, beautiful, and good that was also present in the pagan world. Science, art, moral, domestic, social life, and so forth, were derived from this _gratia communis_ and gratefully acknowledged and praised. Calvin, _Inst._ II, 2, § 12 f.; II, 3, § 3 f.; Zanchi, _Opera_ VIII 646 sq.; Wttewrongel, _Christ. Huishoudinge_ I 288–299; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ III 12, § 52; idem, _Twist des Heeren met zĳn wĳngaart_, cap. 19; Turretin, _Theol. El._ 10:5; Vossius, _Hist. Pelag._ 3:3; Pfanner, _Systema Theol. Gentilis_, cap. 22, § 33; Trigland, _Antapologia_, cap. 17; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ IV 826–829; cf. my address _De algemeene Genade_, Kampen, 1894.

The operation of this _gratia communis_ was indeed commonly seen in moral and intellectual, social and political life, but less often in the religions. People then spoke only of some _religio naturalis, insita_ and _acquisita_, but did not demonstrate the connection between this and the religions. The religions were derived from deception or demonic influences. Yet an operation of God’s Spirit and of his common grace can be discerned not only in science and art, in morality and law, but also in the religions. Calvin rightly spoke of a _semen religionis_, a _sensus divinitatis_, _Inst._ I, 3, 1–3; I, 4, 1; II, 2, 18.

For the founders of religions were not deceivers and instruments of Satan, but men of religious disposition who had a calling to fulfill for their time and their people and who exercised a beneficial influence upon the life of the peoples. The various religions, however much mixed with error, have to some extent satisfied religious needs and provided comfort in the sorrows of life. From the pagan world come to us not only cries of despair but also tones of trust, hope, resignation, peace, submission, patience, and so forth. All the elements and forms essential to religion—the concept of God, consciousness of guilt, need for deliverance, sacrifice, priesthood, temple, cultus, prayer, and so forth—occur, though corrupted, in the pagan religions as well. Nor are unconscious prophecies and striking expectations of a better and purer religion entirely lacking here and there.

Christianity therefore does not stand exclusively in antithesis to paganism; it is also its fulfillment. Christianity is the true and therefore also the highest and purest religion; it is the truth of all religions. What is caricature in paganism is here the living original. What is appearance there is essence here. What is sought there is found here. Christianity is the explanation of paganism. Christ is the One promised to Israel and the Desire of all nations. Israel and the church are chosen for the benefit of humanity. In Abraham’s seed all the families of the earth are blessed.
