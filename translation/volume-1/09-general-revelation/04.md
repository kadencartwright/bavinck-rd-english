## 4.

The Reformation adopted this distinction between _revelatio naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_ and yet in principle gave it an entirely different meaning. The Reformers did indeed accept a revelation of God in nature. But through sin the human being was so darkened in his understanding that he could not rightly know and understand even this revelation. Two things were therefore necessary: namely, that God include once again in special revelation those truths that in themselves can be known from nature; and that the human being, in order to perceive God once more in nature, first be illumined by God’s Spirit. To understand God’s general revelation in nature, objectively there was need of special revelation in Holy Scripture, which Calvin therefore compared to a pair of spectacles, while subjectively the human being needed the eye of faith in order to behold God also in the works of his hands. Equally important was the modification that the Reformation introduced into the conception of supernatural revelation. This revelation was not supernatural in the first place because _an sich_ it belonged to another order and surpassed the understanding even of sinless human beings and of the angels; rather, it was supernatural chiefly because it far surpassed the thoughts and wishes of sinful, fallen humanity, as will be demonstrated later. Among the Reformers, therefore, _theologia naturalis_ lost its rational independence. It was not treated separately but incorporated into Christian doctrine, Zwingli, _Comm. de vera et falsa relig._, _Op._ ed. Schuler et Schulthess III 156 sq. Calvin, _Inst._ I c. 1-5. Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ I cap. 10. Martyr, _Loci Comm._ loc. 2 etc. But there were various causes that impeded this Reformation principle in its development and complete application. There was _excessus_, overstrain, on the one side. Anabaptism rejected the _ordo naturalis_ entirely and sought in revolutionary fashion to establish a kingdom of heaven on earth. The Socinians rejected _theologia naturalis_ altogether and derived all knowledge of God from revelation, _Catech. Racov._ qu. 46-49. Fock, _Der Socin._ 307f. Through his opposition to the Scholastic doctrine _naturalia mansisse integra_, Luther went so far that he denied Aristotle, reason, and philosophy any right to speak _in theologicis_ and called _Vernunft_ in religious matters _stock-, starr-und gar blind_, Köstlin, _Luthers Theologie_ II 287f. Luthardt, _Ethik Luthers_ 14f. Strauss, _Glaubenslehre_ I 311f. The strict Lutherans followed the master; and the _Formula Concordiae_, although acknowledging that _humana ratio seu intellectus naturalis hominis obscuram aliquam notitiae illius scintillulam reliquam habet, quod sit Deus, et particulam aliquam legis tenet_, II Pars. Sol. Decl. II. _de lib. arb._, J. T. Müller, _Die symb. Bücher der ev. luth. K._ 5e Aufl. 1882 S. 589, nevertheless emphasizes the darkness and incapacity of the natural human being in matters of religion so one-sidedly that the coherence and connection between special and general revelation is entirely broken; in _rebus spiritualibus et ad conversionem aut regenerationem_ the human being is nothing more than a _lapis, truncus aut limus_, ib., in Müller S. 594.

The reaction against this could not fail to come. In Anabaptism and Socinianism excessive supernaturalism turned into rationalism. Because Luther could not deny reason all insight and judgment, he was compelled to make a sharp separation between the spiritual and the secular, the heavenly and the earthly, the eternal and the temporal, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 244f. And in his footsteps the Lutheran theologians distinguished between _duo hemisphaeria, quorum unum inferius, alterum superius_; in earthly matters reason is still free and capable of much good, and here it is to a certain extent autonomous and independent of faith, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ 192f. Calvin too, although through his doctrine of _gratia communis_ in a much more favorable position than Luther, did not always rise above the old dualistic opposition between _revelatio naturalis_ and _supernaturalis_, _Inst._ II, 2, 12, 13. Reason thereby again received some authority alongside faith. It appeared that reason did not always need to be guided by faith but was free and independent within one domain, however small and indifferent it might be. It profited from this right granted to it, or at least not seriously disputed; gradually it extended its power. First in civil affairs, then in science, next in philosophy, and finally also in theology, it raised itself alongside and against faith. Alsted published a separate _Theol. naturalis_ in 1615 and enumerated seven _dogmata_ as its content: _deus est, super omnia diligendus, honeste vivendum est, quod tibi non vis fieri alteri ne feceris, suum cuique tribuendum est, nemo laedendus est, plus est in bono communi positum quam in particulari_; cf. _Praecognita_ 1623 p. 37-114. Many Reformed theologians followed this example, especially when the philosophy of Descartes gained influence, Doedes, _Inl. tot de leer van God_, 2nd ed., 1880 p. 200f. Through English deism and German rationalism, _theologia naturalis_ or _rationalis_ increased so greatly in power and prestige that it rejected _theologia revelata_ as wholly unnecessary. Herbert of Cherbury 1581-1648 gave the _religio naturalis_ five articles as its content: _esse deum summum, coli debere, virtutem pietatemque esse praecipuas partes cultus divini, dolendum esse ob peccata ab iisque resipiscendum, dari ex bonitate justitiaque divina praemium vel poenam tum in hac vita tum post hanc vitam_, Lechler, _Gesch. des engl. Deismus_ 42. But after it had banished _theologia revelata_, it was in turn itself judged. In his _Critique of Pure Reason_, Kant argued that reason is limited to phenomena perceptible by the senses and can penetrate neither to the supersensible nor to the supernatural. The history of religions demonstrated that no single religion was satisfied with _revelatio naturalis_, that a _religio naturalis_ existed nowhere, and that all religions were positive. And the criticism of Scripture undermined _revelatio supernaturalis_ and erased the boundaries between it and _revelatio naturalis_. The conviction thereby became general that, in order to acquire any knowledge of God, a path other than that of reason and scientific demonstration had to be taken—namely, that of faith, moral experience, or imagination. _Theologia_ and _religio naturalis_, and with them also _revelatio naturalis_, lost their value. The proofs concerning God’s existence, the soul, and immortality were abandoned and banished from dogmatics. Pierson, _Eene levensbeschouwing_ 83, even said that instruction in _theol. naturalis_ at the state universities was a misuse of the nation’s funds. Nevertheless, it was included again in the Higher Education Act under the names history of the doctrine concerning God and philosophy of religion. Prof. Doedes rejected it in his _Encyclopaedie_ p. 190f., yet in fact treated it again in his _Inleiding tot de leer van God_, 2nd ed., 1880, and _De Leer van God_ 1871. Everything indicates, as will be demonstrated in the _locus de Deo_, that the proofs for God’s existence are rising in value again. The good thought contained in the old _theologia naturalis_ is gradually being recognized more fully once again.
