## 6.

Supernatural revelation may not, however, be identified with immediate revelation. The distinction between mediate and immediate revelation has repeatedly been understood in different senses. Formerly, every revelation that came to the recipient himself without an intermediary was called immediate, and every revelation communicated to others through angels or human beings was called mediate, Witsius, _Misc. Sacra_ I 16. Insofar as revelation generally came personally to prophets and apostles, whereas it comes to us only through their writings, the former could be contrasted as _revelatio immediata_ with the latter as _revelatio mediata_. Among Rationalistic and modern theologians these designations have often received a wholly different sense and thereby increased the confusion in the conception of revelation, Rothe, _Zur Dogm._ 55 f., 64 f.; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._ 163 f.

In the strict sense there is no immediate revelation, either in nature or in grace. God always employs a means, whether taken from the creatures or freely chosen, through which he reveals himself to human beings. Through signs and symbols he causes them to feel his presence; through deeds he proclaims his virtues to them; through speech and language he makes his will and thought known to them. Even where he inwardly reveals himself through his Spirit in the consciousness, this revelation nevertheless always occurs organically and thus by a mediate way. The distance between Creator and creature is far too great for man to apprehend God directly. _Finitum non est capax infiniti._ Whether there will be in the _status gloriae_ a _visio Dei per essentiam_ can be investigated only later. But in this dispensation all revelation is mediate. As God is and speaks in himself, he can be beheld or understood by no creature.

Revelation is therefore always an act of grace; in it God descends to his creature made in his image. All revelation is anthropomorphism, is a certain incarnation of God. It always occurs in certain forms, in definite _modi_. In the _revelatio naturalis_ his divine and eternal thoughts are deposited in the creatures in a creaturely manner, so that they can be understood by man in thought. And in the _revelatio supernaturalis_ he binds himself to space and time, assumes human language and speech, and employs creaturely means, Gen. 1:28; 2:16 f., 21 f.; 3:8 f. Through these _media_ man heard and understood God just as well and as clearly as the pious now hears God’s speech in all of nature. Supernatural revelation, in which God employs unusual means but for which he also opens the eyes in a special manner, is no more impossible and deceptive for the believer than God’s revelation in nature and history.

According to the teaching of Scripture, therefore, natural and supernatural revelation go together in the _status integritatis_. They are not opposites but complement one another. Both are mediate and bound to definite forms and means. Both rest upon the thought that in grace God bends down to man and becomes like him. And both possess these _modi_: God causes his presence to be felt, his voice to be heard, and his works to be beheld. From the beginning God made himself known to human beings by appearance, word, and deed.

It is now remarkable that sin, which enters the world through the first man, brings no change in the fact of revelation itself. God continues to reveal himself; he does not withdraw. First of all, throughout Scripture we are taught a _revelatio naturalis_. God’s revelation began in creation and continues in the preservation and government of all things. He reveals himself in the nature around us, displays therein his eternal power and divinity, and in blessings and judgment reveals by turns his goodness and his wrath, Job 36, 37; Ps. 29; 33:5; 65; 67:7; 90; 104; 107; 145; 147; Isa. 59:17–19; Matt. 5:45; Rom. 1:18; Acts 14:16. He reveals himself in the history of peoples and persons, Deut. 32:8; Ps. 33:10; 67:5; 115:16; Prov. 8:15, 16; Acts 17:26; Rom. 13:1. He also reveals himself in the heart and conscience of every human being, Job 32:8; 33:4; Prov. 20:27; John 1:3–5, 9, 10; Rom. 2:14, 15; 8:16.

This revelation of God is general, in itself perceptible and intelligible to every human being. Nature and history are the book of God’s omnipotence and wisdom, of his goodness and justice. All peoples have acknowledged this revelation to some extent. Even idolatry presupposes that God’s δυναμις and θειοτης reveal themselves in the creatures. Philosophers, natural scientists, and historians have often spoken strikingly of this revelation of God—for example, Xenophon, _Memor._ I 4, 5; Cicero, _De nat. deor._ II 2, _De divinat._ II 72; Zöckler, _Gottes Zeugen im Reich der Natur_, 2 Th., Gütersloh, 1881.

Christian theology has at all times unanimously accepted and defended this general revelation: Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 6; Tertullian, _De testim. animae_, _Adv. Marc._ I 10; Augustine, _De civ. Dei_ 8:9 sq.; 19:1, _De trin._ 4:20, etc.; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ I, capp. 1 and 3; Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ lib. 1–3; _S. Theol._ I, qu. 2, etc.; cf. further H. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der relig. Erk._ II, S. 27–45. This general revelation was maintained and highly valued especially by the Reformed theologians, Calvin, _Inst._ I, c. 4; and cf. further Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. K._ I 241 f.; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, S. 1 f.; Scholten, _Leer der Herv. Kerk_ I⁴ 304–326; Doedes, _Inleiding tot de leer van God_, 2e dr., pp. 107–252.

But according to Scripture this general revelation is not merely natural in the strict sense; it also contains supernatural elements. The revelation that occurs immediately after the fall bears a supernatural character, Gen. 3:8 f., and through tradition becomes the possession of humanity. The original knowledge and service of God remain preserved for a long time in a more or less pure condition. Cain is granted grace instead of justice; he even becomes the father of a race that makes a beginning with culture, Gen. 4. The covenant established after the flood with Noah, and in him with all the new humanity, is a covenant of nature and yet no longer natural, but the fruit of unmerited supernatural grace, Gen. 8:21, 22; 9:1–17.

Scripture repeatedly speaks of miracles that God performed before the eyes of the pagans in Egypt, Canaan, Babel, and so forth, and of supernatural revelations granted to non-Israelites, Gen. 20; 30; 40; 41; Judg. 7; Dan. 2:4, etc. An operation of supernatural powers in the pagan world is _a priori_ neither impossible nor even improbable. There may be truth in the appeal to revelations common to all religions. Conversely, not everything belonging to the sphere of special grace is supernatural in the strict sense. Entire periods pass in the history of Israel, many days and years in the life of Jesus, and likewise in the life of the apostles, in which no supernatural revelation takes place and which nevertheless form an important part of the _historia revelationis_. When Jesus proclaims the gospel to the poor, this is of no less importance than when he heals the sick and raises the dead. His death, which appears natural, is of no less significance than his supernatural birth. The distinction between natural and supernatural revelation is therefore not identical with that between general and special revelation. To designate the twofold revelation underlying the pagan religions and the religion of Scripture, the latter distinction is preferable to the former.
