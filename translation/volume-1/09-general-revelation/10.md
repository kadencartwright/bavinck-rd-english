## 10.

But _revelatio generalis_ has significance not only for the pagan world but also within and for the Christian religion. Its value, however, does not lie in providing us with a _theologia_ or _religio naturalis_, a _moralischen Vernunftglauben_ that would be sufficient in itself and could dispense with every positive element in religion. Such a _religio naturalis_ is found nowhere and cannot exist. Nor is it the intention of _revelatio generalis_ that the Christian should draw from it his first knowledge concerning God, the world, and the human being and then later supplement this with the knowledge of Christ. Ritschl and his followers present the matter as though the dogmatician took the material for the _loci de Deo_ and _de homine_ solely from _revelatio generalis_ and only then drew the material for the following _loci_ from Holy Scripture. The dogmatician would then first stand outside and before the Christian faith and would take his position within that faith only with the later _dogmata_, Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 4. But this was not the method of Reformation dogmatics, at least not in the beginning. When the Christian confesses his faith in God the Father Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth, that is Christian faith in the fullest sense. And the dogmatician does not first divest himself of this faith in order to construct from general revelation a rational doctrine concerning God and the human being and later supplement it with the revelation in Christ. Rather, he draws his knowledge solely and exclusively from _revelatio specialis_, that is, from Holy Scripture. This is his _principium unicum_. But he does not restrict this special revelation to the person of Christ as portrayed in certain portions of Scripture—for example, in the Synoptic Gospels or only in the Sermon on the Mount. The entire revelation comprehended in Scripture is a _revelatio specialis_ that comes to us in Christ. Christ is the center and content of that entire special revelation, which begins in Paradise and is completed in the Apocalypse. Special revelation has acknowledged and valued general revelation and has even taken it up and, as it were, assimilated it. And so too does the Christian, and so too the dogmatician. He stands within Christian faith, within _revelatio specialis_, and from there also looks upon nature and history. He now discovers there traces of the same God whom he came to know in Christ as his Father. Precisely as a Christian, through faith, he sees God’s revelation in nature much better and more clearly than he could discern it before. The natural man does not understand God’s speech in nature and history. He searches the whole universe without finding God. But the Christian, armed with the spectacles of Holy Scripture, R. de Sabunde, _Theol. Natur._ _in den Prologus_, Calvin, _Inst._ I 6, 1, sees God in everything and everything in God. We therefore encounter in Scripture a nature poetry and a view of history such as are found nowhere else. With his Christian confession the believer thus also finds his bearings in the world; he is no stranger in it and sees no other power ruling there than the one he also invokes in Christ as his Father. Through this general revelation he feels at home in the world; it is God’s fatherly hand from which all things in the natural sphere also come to him.

In that general revelation he has, furthermore, firm ground upon which he can meet all non-Christians. He shares a common foundation with them. Through his Christian faith he may occupy an isolated position and may be unable to prove his convictions of faith to others; nevertheless, in general revelation he has a point of contact with everyone who bears the name of human being. Just as classical propaedeutics lays a common basis among all scholars, so general revelation holds all human beings together despite every difference in religion. Subjectively, for the believer, knowledge of God from nature is posterior to that from Holy Scripture. We are all born into a definite positive religion. Only the eye of faith sees God in creation; here too it holds true that only one who is pure in heart sees God. Objectively, however, nature precedes grace, and _revelatio generalis_ precedes _revelatio specialis_. _Gratia praesupponit naturam._ The denial that _religio_ and _theologia naturalis_ are sufficient and have an independent existence of their own detracts nothing from the fact that an utterance of God comes to every human being from creation, from nature and history, and from heart and conscience. No one escapes the power of _revelatio generalis_. Religion belongs to the essence of the human being. The idea and existence of God, the spiritual substantiality and eternal destiny of the human being, the origin and goal of the world, the moral world order and its final triumph—these are problems that give the human mind no rest. The _metaphysische Bedürfniss_ cannot be suppressed. Philosophy seeks again and again to satisfy it. It is _revelatio generalis_ that keeps this need alive. It prevents the human being from degrading himself to an animal. It binds him to the supersensible world. It maintains in him the consciousness that he was created in God’s image and finds no rest except in God. _Revelatio generalis_ preserves humanity in order that—and until—it is found and restored by Christ. In this respect _theologia naturalis_ was formerly rightly called _praeambula fidei_, a divine preparation and education for Christianity. General revelation is the foundation upon which special revelation rises.

Finally, the rich significance of general revelation also comes to expression in this: it holds nature and grace, creation and re-creation, the _Welt der Wirklichkeit_ and the _Welt der Werthe_, in an indissoluble connection with one another. Without general revelation, special revelation loses its connection with the whole of cosmic being and life. Then the link that joins the realm of nature to the kingdom of heaven is lacking. Anyone who, with critical philosophy, denies _revelatio generalis_ strives in vain to regain by way of practical _Vernunft_ or imagination what he has lost. He has lost the point of support for his faith. Religious life stands detached alongside ordinary human existence; the image of God becomes a _donum superadditum_; religion, as among the Socinians, becomes alien to human nature; Christianity becomes a sectarian phenomenon and is deprived of its catholicity; grace stands hostile over against nature. It is then consistent, with the ethical Modernists, to assume a radical separation between the power of the good and the power of nature. _Ethos_ and _physis_ lie totally separated. The world of _Wirklichkeit_ and that of _Werthe_ have nothing to do with one another. In principle, Parsism and Manichaeism are renewed. By contrast, _revelatio generalis_ maintains the unity of nature and grace, of the world and the kingdom of God, of the natural and the moral order, of creation and re-creation, of _physis_ and _ethos_, of virtue and happiness, of holiness and blessedness, and in all these the unity of the divine Being. It is the same God who in general revelation does not leave himself without witness to anyone and who in special revelation makes himself known as a God of grace. General and special revelation therefore interact with one another. _Praemisit Deus naturam magistram, submissurus et prophetiam, quo facilius credas prophetiae discipulus naturae_ (Tertullian). _Natura praecedit gratiam, gratia perficit naturam.
