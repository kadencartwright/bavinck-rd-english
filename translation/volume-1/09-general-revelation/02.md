## 2.

Revelation—as this concept is furnished to us by religion and is still taken here in its broadest sense—is every action that proceeds from God in order to bring and keep man in that distinctive relation to him which is designated by the name religion. The first and foremost concern here is always and everywhere to understand this revelation as an action, as an act on God’s part. God never does anything unconsciously; he does everything deliberately and has a purpose in everything. Revelation is never an unconscious emanation, an involuntary shining through of God in his works, but always God’s conscious, free, and active self-disclosure to man. Religion and revelation both rest by their nature on the foundation of theism, that is, on the belief that God and man are not separated from one another but do exist as distinct realities. Put strongly, revelation always presupposes that there are two worlds, a supernatural and a natural, a heavenly and an earthly. Revelation, then, is every operation that proceeds from that other invisible world into this visible world in order to cause man to set his mind on the things above. The ways and forms in which God reveals himself may differ, just as one person can make himself known to another in different ways. God can reveal himself directly and immediately, and in doing so he can make use of ordinary or extraordinary means. In a certain sense these forms are subordinate, since their significance is instrumental. Yet revelation is always an act on God’s part, whether it comes to us in an ordinary or unusual way. Whoever understands it thus is in principle a supernaturalist, whether or not he accepts the possibility of miracles. The issue between naturalism and supernaturalism is decided not first in connection with so-called supernatural revelation but really already here, at the threshold, in the concept of revelation in general. Deism is untenable. There is only a choice between theism and pantheism (materialism). Pantheism has no revelation and therefore no religion either. Theism is inherently supernaturalistic—not in the historical meaning of that word, but in the sense that it recognizes an _ordo supra hanc naturam_ and accepts an operation of that world in this one. Religion, revelation, supernaturalism, and theism stand and fall together. The purpose of revelation is none other than to awaken and cultivate religion in man. Everything that has this as its aim and serves it is revelation in the proper sense. Revelation coincides with all the works of God in nature and grace. It encompasses the whole of creation and re-creation. All that exists and occurs is for the devout a means of leading him upward to God. The usual definitions, according to which revelation consists in the communication of doctrine or life, and so forth, already prove to be much too narrow here. In his revelation God intends to place man in a religious relation to him. Religion, however, embraces the whole man with all his faculties and powers. In revelation God draws near to the whole man in order to win him wholly for his service of love. Indeed, revelation cannot have as its goal merely to place the individual man in a religious relation to God. Humanity is one whole. It is the object of God’s love. The final goal of revelation is therefore to make humanity itself, as one whole, into a kingdom, a people of God. Revelation is not an isolated fact that stands by itself in history. It is a system of acts of God, beginning with creation and ending in the new heaven and the new earth. It is instruction, education, guidance, government, renewal, forgiveness, and so forth; it is all these together. Revelation is everything God does in order to re-create humanity in his image and likeness.
