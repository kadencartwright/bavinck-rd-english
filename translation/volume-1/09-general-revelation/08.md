### D. The Value of General Revelation

## 8.

General revelation has not thereby lost its value and significance. First, it is of great importance for the pagan world. It is the firm and enduring foundation of the pagan religions. Holy Scripture passes a stern judgment on paganism and explains its origin as apostasy from the pure knowledge of God. To be sure, this knowledge, which was man’s original possession, continued to operate for some time, Gen. 4:3; 8:20, and creation revealed God’s eternal power and divinity, Rom. 1:20. But although human beings knew God, they became futile in their thoughts and darkened in their hearts and did not glorify or thank him as God. Moreover, the confusion of language and the dispersion of the peoples, Gen. 11, certainly also exercised great influence on the development of polytheism, Origen, _C. Cels._ V; Augustine, _De civ. Dei_ 16:6; Schelling, _Einleitung in die philos. der Mythologie_ I 94 f.; Delitzsch, _Comm._ on Gen. 11; Auberlen, _De goddelĳke openbaring_ I 187 f.; Fabri, _Die Entstehung des Heidenthums und die Aufgabe der Heidenmission_ 1859; Herzog² 12:108. The Hebrew גּוֹי, the multitude or nation united by descent and language, alongside גַּם, the people united by a common government, also points to this. For גּוֹיִים, ἐθνη, is generally used of the pagan peoples and means not only nations but also Gentiles; the word has a national and at the same time an ethical-religious meaning, like the Latin _pagani_ and our word _heathen_. The unity of God, and therefore also the purity of religion, was lost when humanity was divided into peoples. Each people acquired its own national god. And once the concept of the unity and absoluteness of God had been lost, other powers could gradually be acknowledged and worshiped as gods alongside that one national god. The idea of the divine is corrupted and debased; the various powers of nature come to the fore and increase in significance; the boundary between the divine and the creaturely is erased; and religion can even degenerate into animism and fetishism, sorcery and magic. According to Scripture, therefore, the character of the pagan religions consists in idolatry. The pagan gods are idols; they do not exist; they are falsehood and vanity, Isa. 41:29; 42:17; 46:1 f.; Jer. 2:28; Ps. 106:28; Acts 14:15; 19:26; Gal. 4:8; 1 Cor. 8:5. Even a demonic power operates in these religions, Deut. 32:17; Ps. 106:28; 1 Cor. 10:20 f.; Rev. 9:20. The condition in which the pagan world exists outside the revelation given to Israel, outside Christ, is described as darkness, Isa. 9:1; 60:2; Luke 1:79; John 1:5; Eph. 4:18; as ignorance, Acts 17:30; 1 Pet. 1:14; Rom. 1:18 f.; as imagined, vain wisdom, 1 Cor. 1:18 f.; 2:6; 3:19 f.; and as sin and unrighteousness, Rom. 1:24 f.; 3:9 f.

The pagan world is an immense problem in its origin, character, and destiny. In itself, the solution Scripture gives to this problem is far from absurd; indeed, it commends itself by its simplicity and naturalness. Nevertheless, the philosophy of history as well as the philosophy of religions has not been satisfied with that solution and has put forward another conception that stands diametrically opposed to that of Scripture. The glorification of the childlike condition of the peoples, such as was customary in the previous century, indeed no longer finds assent today. But the theory of evolution now used as an explanation conflicts with Scripture just as much. Just as natural science seeks to derive the living from the lifeless, the organic from the inorganic, man from the animal, the conscious from the unconscious, and the higher from the lower, so the modern science of religion seeks to explain religion from an earlier religionless condition and pure religion from the primitive forms of fetishism, animism, and so forth. David Hume already made a beginning with this in his _Natural history of religion_. In Hegel it fitted entirely within the framework of his pantheistic philosophy, _Vorlesungen über die Philosophie der Religion_ 1832. Since then it has found ever wider circulation and defense: Buckle, _History of civilization in England_ 1858; W. E. H. Lecky, _History of the rise and influence of the spirit of rationalism in Europe_ 1865; E. B. Tylor, _Researches into the early history of mankind and the development of civilization_, 2d ed., 1870; idem, _Primitive Culture_ 1872; Sir John Lubbock, _Prehistoric times as illustrated by ancient remains and the manners and customs of modern savages_ 1865; idem, _The origin of civilization and the primitive condition of man_ 1870; H. Spencer, _The principles of sociology_ 1876-’82; F. von Hellwald, _Kulturgeschichte in ihrer natürlichen Entwicklung bis zur Gegenwart_, 3e Aufl., 1883; O. Caspari, _Die Urgeschichte der Menschheit mit Rücksicht auf die natürliche Entwicklung des frühesten Geisteslebens_ 1873; G. Roskoff, _Das Religionswesen der rohesten Natürvolker_ 1880; Ed. von Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_, Leipzig; O. Pfleiderer, _Religionsphilos. auf geschichtl. Grundlage_, 2e Aufl., 1883-84; H. Siebeck, _Lehrb. der Religionsphilosophie_ 1893; A. Réville, _Prolégomènes de l’histoire des religions_, Paris 1881; C. P. Tiele, _De plaats v. d. godsd. der natuurvolken in de godsdienstgeschiedenis_ 1873; idem, _Over de wetten der ontwikkeling van den godsdienst_, _Theol. Tĳdschr._ 1874; idem, _Gesch. v. d. godsd._ 1876, etc. However widely this doctrine of evolution may be accepted, in any event it still has no higher rank than that of a hypothesis. But it does not explain the phenomena. In natural science it still founders on the facts of life, consciousness, speech, language, will, and so forth. And in the science of religion, the origin and essence, the truth and value of religion, continue to protest against it. Furthermore, the claims that primitive peoples represent the original condition of humanity, that fetishism and animism are the oldest forms of religion, and that the first human beings were like children or savages are opinions that lack sufficient grounds and therefore meet with increasing opposition. In his _Philosophie der Mythologie und Offenbarung_, Schelling assumed a relative monotheism as original. Max Müller recognizes a so-called henotheism as the primitive religion, _Vorlesungen über Ursprung und Entwicklung der Religion_, S. 292 f.; _Deutsche Rundschau_, Sept. 1878; cf. Rauwenhoff, _Wĳsb. v. d. godsd._ 95 f., 191 f., and Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ 146 f. The opinion that the various religions are successive moments in a single process of development is also much less probable than the view that regards them as degenerations of a single type, Kähler, _Wiss. der chr. Lehre_ I 185. The doctrine of Scripture concerning the origin and nature of paganism is therefore still defended more or less decisively by Lüken, _Die Einheit des Menschengeschlechts und dessen Ausbreitung über die ganze Erde_ 1845; Doedes, _De toepassing van de ontwikkelingstheorie niet aan te bevelen voor de gesch. der godsd._ 1874; E. L.
