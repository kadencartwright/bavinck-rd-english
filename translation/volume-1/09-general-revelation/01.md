# Chapter II. External Principle

# § 9. General Revelation

### A. The Concept of Revelation

## 1.

Religion, when investigated in its nature and origin, itself leads to the concept of revelation. The history of religions acquaints us with this concept as the necessary correlate of all religion. The philosophy of religion can no longer pass over this concept in silence. But the manner in which revelation is conceived in theology and philosophy does not always accord with the concept itself. The concept of revelation carries with it a certain content that must be acknowledged in its truth if we are still to speak of revelation. It may not be used as a flag covering a false cargo.

In the first place, revelation is a thoroughly religious concept. Not philosophy but religion, not reason but history, presents it to us. Science and philosophy must therefore be denied the right to determine this concept _a priori_ and then tailor the historical and religious phenomena comprehended under the name of revelation to fit it. It goes without saying that philosophical systems such as Pantheism and Materialism cannot acknowledge this concept in its rightful meaning. In these systems there is no place for revelation. By virtue of their principles both are incapable of judging according to their worth revelation and therefore also religion, with which it is inseparably connected.

If God does not exist and, according to Feuerbach’s saying, _Wesen des Christenthums_, 2e Aufl., 401, the secret of theology is anthropology, then religion and revelation are thereby self-evidently condemned and are nothing but a hallucination of the human mind. Pantheism likewise cannot by its nature assign any reality to revelation. If God and man are substantially one, a relation of man to God such as arises in religion is no longer possible. Religion is then at most God’s coming to self-consciousness in man, the return of the Absolute to itself in human consciousness. Revelation can here be nothing other than a name for religion in man considered from its objective side. Thus, for example, Ed. von Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_ II 71, 75, etc., says that revelation objectively and faith subjectively are one and the same act, conceived from the divine and the human side. There is then no external, objective revelation. Revelation is nothing but God’s dwelling and working in every human being.

Leaving aside the question whether this too contains a truth and whether objective revelation must not be completed subjectively in illumination, it is nevertheless clear that the idea of revelation made known to us by religion and the history of religions is thereby entirely lost. For religion is always a relation of man to a divine person whose objective and real existence is elevated above all doubt for the religious consciousness. As soon as man begins to doubt the distinct and independent existence of the object of his worship, his religion is finished.

This relation of man to God in religion is ethical in character (above, p. 193). Religion is no physical or metaphysical communion of God and man, as is so often said. It does not consist in a unity of being, a _unio_ or _communio physica_ of man with God. It is not a substance of man and does not constitute his being, his _essentia_. Religion always presupposes precisely that God and man, though akin, are nevertheless distinct. And religion itself then consists not in a relation of God to man, for God has no religion, but in a relation of man to God. This relation is by its nature not physical, metaphysical, _realis_, but ethical, moral in character. It consists in man’s knowing and loving God and living for him. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der religiösen Erkenntniss_, Würzburg, 1856, I, S. 1–10; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsdienstleer_ I 57 f., 64.

Religion indeed presupposes that God and man stand in kinship and, in a proper sense, also in communion with one another, but religion itself is nevertheless not two-sided but one-sided. However intimately religion and revelation are connected with one another, they are nevertheless two. They are not two sides of one and the same thing, but differ essentially and actually from one another. Just as the eye and light, the ear and tone, the _logos_ within us and the _logos_ outside us are akin and yet distinct, so it is also with religion and revelation. In the religious sphere it is as in every other domain. We come naked into the world and bring nothing with us. We receive all our nourishment, in both the spiritual and the natural sense, from without. In religion too the content comes to us from without through revelation.
