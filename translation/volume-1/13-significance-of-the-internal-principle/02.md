## 2.

Yet such inquiry has its good and useful side. Kant transformed the whole of philosophy into a critique of the faculty of knowledge. And no one will disdain the investigations that have since been devoted to the nature and certainty of our knowledge. Nevertheless, excessively high expectations here are always followed by disappointment. The problems are so complicated that a solution seems unattainable. Every attempt to reach a satisfactory answer to this question is burdened by serious difficulties. Voices are sometimes heard counseling against further inquiry because it is utterly useless. After all, these formal questions have no influence on science itself. Whether one is an idealist or empiricist, realist or nominalist, science can be obtained only by way of observation and thought. In theology and religion too there is reason to warn against exaggerated expectations. _Erkenntnistheorie_ cannot compensate for faith; the _pars formalis_ of dogmatics cannot replace the _pars materialis_. Indeed, inquiry into the grounds of faith is much more difficult still than inquiry into the grounds of knowledge. First, it is generally impossible for believers to give a scientific account of the reasons why they believe. Their life from faith is for them sufficient proof of the truth and worth of that faith. One who is hungry and eats thereby experiences for himself the nourishing power of bread and has no need to investigate its chemical constituents. Further, even for scientific theologians it is an excessive demand that, before they may begin theological labor, they must first prove the scientific legitimacy of the _Erkenntnistheorie_ from which they proceed. A. Ritschl, _Theologie und Metaphysik_, 1881, p. 38. A theologian is not, after all, a philosopher. Indispensable though philosophical formation is for the theologian, he need not first have investigated every philosophical _Erkenntnistheorie_ before he can act as a theologian. Theology brings its own theory of knowledge with it and is indeed dependent on philosophy, but not on any philosophical system. Finally, the object of inquiry here lies so deeply hidden in the life of the soul and is so intimately interwoven with the finest and tenderest stirrings of the human heart that it almost entirely escapes our own observation, and still more the observation of others. Religion is rooted more deeply in human nature than any other power. For it man will sacrifice everything: his money and possessions, his wife and child, his name, his honor, his life. Religion alone has witnesses unto blood, martyrs. Preserving it, man can lose everything and still preserve himself with it. But denying it, he himself is lost. Who, then, can trace the root of this life, which is one with man’s own life? Who can uncover the ground upon which believing rests? Faith itself is already such a wondrous and mysterious power. We define it by knowledge, assent, trust, and so forth, but immediately sense the weakness of this definition and, after lengthy reasoning, have finally said nothing or very little. The question “How and why do I know?” is so difficult that all the power of philosophical thought has not yet found the answer. But more difficult still is the question “How and why do I believe?” It is a riddle to ourselves, for we cannot descend into the depths of our own mind or penetrate with our gaze the darkness that lies behind our consciousness. And to others it is an even greater mystery. For ourselves, all kinds of moods and emotions, reflections and dispositions that accompany faith and bind us inseparably to the object of our faith still count among its grounds. But we cannot communicate these to others; they are not intended for disclosure and are incapable of communication. If we sometimes try, they lose their power and value in the telling; we ourselves feel least satisfied by them. And often the result is that what was offered as a ground cannot withstand examination and proves to be no ground at all. Nevertheless, despite all reasoning, faith stands firm and says: “I can do no other. God help me. Amen.”
