## 3.

It is therefore no cause for surprise that very different grounds are given for religious faith and thus also for Christian faith. To the question how someone comes to faith and why he believes, the answers diverge very widely. Some are of the opinion that man possesses within himself, in his own nature, sufficient data to discern, judge, and accept a religious conception, revelation, and Scripture. The organ for judging and accepting revelation is sought in turn in the understanding; faith then rests on historical-apologetic grounds. Or reason is designated as such an organ; faith is then built on speculative reasoning. Or conscience, the mind, and the heart are regarded as the organ for the divine; faith is then based on ethical-practical motives. But these tendencies always presuppose a person who may perhaps be prepared for revelation in one way or another but who actually still stands outside faith and for whom revelation must now be justified along an intellectual or moral path.

The following investigation will show that these grounds are insufficient and that the standpoint adopted by these tendencies cannot be accepted. But the inadequacy of these various methods can already be seen _a priori_. Religion, after all, is an independent reality; it is essentially distinct from science, art, and morality. It has its own _principium cognoscendi externum_, namely revelation, and therefore also requires its own _principium cognoscendi internum_. As the eye corresponds to light, the ear to sound, and the _logos_ within us to the _logos_ outside us, so a subjective organ in man must correspond to objective revelation. Religious capacity in general, the _semen religionis_, the νους, cannot qualify for this. For, first, this capacity never and nowhere occurs in a pure state and without content. From its first awakening it is always already integrated into a historical religion and accommodated to it. Next, the revelation in Scripture proceeds from the supposition that man is corrupted also in this religious disposition and needs re-creation. It would therefore destroy itself if it recognized the psychical man as its competent judge. It places itself in an entirely different relation to him. It does not place itself beneath him or submit to his judgment, but stands high above him and demands nothing but faith and obedience. Scripture even expressly declares that the psychical man does not understand the things of the Spirit of God, that they are foolishness to him, and that in enmity he rejects and disowns them. God’s revelation in Christ asks no support and no justification from human beings. It posits and maintains itself in high majesty. Its _auctoritas_ is _normativa_ but also _causativa_. It fights for its own triumph. It conquers hearts for itself. It makes itself irresistible. Revelation therefore divides into two great dispensations. When the economy of the Son, of objective revelation, has ended, that of the Spirit begins. God is also the author of this subjective revelation. The action proceeds from him. He is the first and the last. Man does not come to revelation and does not seek God. God seeks man. He seeks him in the Son; he seeks him also in the Spirit. When God has spoken to us in these last days through the Son, the Holy Spirit comes, takes up Christ’s cause before the world, pleads his case, defends his Word, and inclines human hearts to obedience. The Holy Spirit is the great and mighty Witness of Christ, objectively in Scripture and subjectively in man’s own spirit. In that Spirit man receives an adequate organ for external revelation. God can be known only through God; only in his light can light be seen. No one knows the Father except the one to whom the Son wills to reveal him, and no one can say that Jesus is Lord except through the Holy Spirit. God, then, is the _principium essendi_ of religion and theology; objective revelation in Christ, deposited in Scripture, is their _principium cognoscendi externum_; and the Holy Spirit, who has been poured out in the congregation, regenerates it, and leads it into the truth, is their _principium cognoscendi internum_. In this testimony of the Holy Spirit revelation closes itself and reaches its goal. For it is God’s good pleasure to re-create humanity according to his image and likeness. Objective revelation, therefore, is not sufficient; it must continue and complete itself in subjective revelation. Indeed, the former is only the means, the latter the goal. The _principium externum_ is _instrumentale_; the _principium internum_ is the _principium formale_ and _principale_, p. 143.

The Christian church has therefore at all times confessed the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_. God is the author of external revelation, but it is also he who chooses the congregation, establishes the church, and bears witness to Christ within her. Scripture is his Word; the congregation is his temple. To this extent there is unanimity in the confession of all churches concerning the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_. Not the natural but only the spiritual person knows the things that have been bestowed on him by God, 1 Cor. 2:12 f. In other respects, however, there is a great difference concerning this testimony of the Holy Spirit, especially between the Roman Church and the churches of the Reformation. According to Rome, Scripture was given to the church—indeed, to the church as institution—and only through it to believers. The church receives, preserves, authorizes, and explains Holy Scripture. All revelation of God to believers is mediated by the institution of the church. The church always stands between God and believers. The church is mediatrix, _medium gratiae_, _principium externum_. She is the temple of the Holy Spirit. At Rome the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ expresses itself only through the church as institution, through the _ecclesia docens_, through the _magisterium_, through the pope. According to the Reformation, however, revelation—Scripture—was indeed also given to the church, but to the church as organism, to the congregation, to believers. They are the temple of the Holy Spirit. The _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_ is the possession of all believers. Where two or three are gathered in Jesus’ name, he is in their midst. At Rome the institution is the essence of the church; according to the Reformation it is a temporary aid, while the essence of the church lies in the assembly of believers. This is God’s dwelling, the body of Christ, the temple of the Holy Spirit.
