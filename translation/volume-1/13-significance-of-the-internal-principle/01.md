# Chapter III. Internal Principle

# § 13. Significance of the Internal Principle

## 1.

To the _principium externum_ discussed in the preceding chapter there must correspond a _principium internum_ within the human being himself.
Herbert Spencer, _Principles of psychology_ § 120, defines life as “the continuous adjustment of internal relations to external relations”; and indeed all human life rests upon a reciprocal correspondence of subject and object.
The human being is in every respect dependent on the world outside him; he is autonomous in no domain and lives from what is given, that is, from grace.
Conversely, he is adapted to that entire world outside him and is connected with it by all manner of relations.
His body was taken from the dust of the earth, is composed of the same elements as other bodies, and is therefore akin to the physical world.
His vegetative life is nourished from the earth; food, shelter, and clothing are bestowed on him by nature; he needs light and air, the alternation of day and night, for his bodily life; he is a microcosm, earthly from the earth.
As _anima sensitiva_, he received the senses as organs through which he can perceive the world outside him in its various relations and form representations of it.
Through the _logos_ within him, he rises to the world of _intelligibile_ things and discovers the _logos_ embodied in the visible.
And in the same way, religiously and ethically, the human being is related to a real world of ideal and spiritual goods and has received a _facultas_ for apprehending and knowing this world.
Our earlier investigation into the nature of religion, pp. 177, 212, already led us to a certain religious aptitude in the human being, to a receptivity in his nature for apprehending the divine.
Scripture expresses this by saying that the human being was created in God’s image, that he is God’s offspring, and that in the νους he possesses an organ for discerning God’s revelation in his creation.
Religion presupposes that the human being is akin to God.

But although this religious receptivity may be proper to human nature, it is nevertheless an abstraction and never occurs in reality in a pure and contentless form.
However rich an aptitude for science or art may lie hidden in a child, the child is nevertheless born in a condition of helplessness.
It depends on the grace of its environment.
Food and drink, shelter and clothing, representations and concepts, sensations and desires—all these we receive from the circle into which we are born and in which we are raised.
Religion too is impressed upon us by our parents and caregivers.
Religion is like language in this respect.
We bring the faculty of speech with us at birth, but the language in which we will later express our thoughts is bestowed on us by our environment.
Schopenhauer, _Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung_, 6e Aufl., II 181, therefore makes the correct observation that religions enjoy a great advantage over philosophical systems because they are impressed upon children from an early age.
From childhood onward, religion grows together with the innermost and tenderest life and is for that reason too almost ineradicable.
As a rule, a person dies in the religion into which he was born.
The Muslim, the Christian, the Roman Catholic, and the Protestant generally remain faithful until death to the religion of their youth and their parents.
Except in times of religious crisis, such as the rise of Christianity, Islam, and the Reformation, conversions are rare; a change of religion is the exception, not the rule.
Indeed, most people live and die without ever being shaken in their religious faith by serious doubt.
The question why they believe in the truth of the religious ideas in which they were raised never occurs to them.
They believe, find more or less satisfaction in their faith, and do not reflect on the grounds upon which their conviction rests.
When faith is strong, there is no place for such an investigation into the grounds of faith.
Someone who is hungry does not first investigate the manner in which the bread set before him was prepared.
_Primum vivere, deinde philosophari._
There is a great difference between life and reflection.
It is not evidence of the richness but of the poverty of religious life when the greatest attention is devoted to formal questions.
When the power of philosophical thought is exhausted, people turn to the history of philosophy.
When people no longer live in the confession of the church, its origin and history are investigated.
And when faith loses its strength and confidence, an investigation is undertaken into the grounds upon which it rests.
