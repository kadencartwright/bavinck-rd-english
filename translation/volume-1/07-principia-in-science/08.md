## 8.

The point of departure for all human knowledge, then, is sensory perception. Οὐδε νοει ὁ νους τα ἐκτος μη μετ’ αἰσθησεως ὀντα, Aristotle, _De sensu_ c. 6; Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ III 198. _Omnis cognitio intellectualis incipit a sensu_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 84, art. 1 and 7. _Intellectus noster nihil intelligit sine phantasmate_, idem, _C. Gent._ III 41. And all Christian theologians were of the same mind. The error of scholasticism, among Protestants as well as Roman Catholics, lay only in this: it considered the work of perception finished far too quickly and thought that, in virtually every field of science, its results had been fully gathered and deposited in the books of Euclid and Aristotle, the church fathers, and the confession. Under that assumption people neglected perception and began at once with concepts already obtained, Spruyt, _Proeve_, p. 36. Hence it could almost be called a discovery when Bacon returned to sensory perception as the sole source of knowledge. Yet it was no discovery, though it was a necessary refreshment for science, for science must always return to the sources. Truth must be drawn not from books but from the real world. Intuition is the source of all genuine science. _Die Anschauungen sind die Kontanten, die Begriffe die Zettel_, Schopenhauer, _Die Welt u. s. w._, 6e Aufl., II 76; cf. pp. 76–98.

Now in this sensory perception each sense has its own nature and its own task; each seeks out what is akin to it in the phenomena. Touch acquaints us with mechanical properties; taste and smell with chemical properties; hearing opens to us the world of tones and sight that of colors. Aristotle in Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ III 533 f.; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 78, art. 3; Schopenhauer, _Die Welt u. s. w._ II 30–36; Land, _Inleiding_, pp. 63 f.; Bilderdĳk, _Taal- en Dichtk. Verscheidenheden_, 1821, II 39 f.; _Verhandelingen, ziel-, zede- en rechtsleer betreffende_, 1821, pp. 12 f.; _Brieven_ V 48. Thus none of the senses by itself perceives the whole object, but only certain properties of it. The perceptual image that arises in our consciousness is composed of many different impressions. These are received by the various senses, conveyed along the nerve fibers into our brains, there transformed in an inexplicable manner into sensations, and united into a whole. There are no simple sensations; they are all already composed of several others. Every object we see and every tone we hear is already a complex of perceptions.

The human mind is therefore active even in the simplest perceptions. It is no _tabula rasa_ on which the external world merely writes whatever it wishes, no mirror in which the object is simply reflected. Rather, every perceptual image is formed in consciousness itself from the factors supplied from the object through the various senses. Hence the question is of such great importance: what is the relation between the perceptual image—the representation (φαντασια, φαντασμα, _species sensibilis_, _perception_, _Vorstellung_) in our consciousness—and reality, the object outside us? Greek philosophy generally proceeded from the thought that like could be known only by like: φασι γαρ γιγνωσκεσθαι το ὁμοιον τῳ ὁμοιῳ, Aristotle, _De an._ I 2. Some inferred from this that the human soul had to consist of the same elements and atoms as the real world, and that in perception material atoms from the objects entered the soul. Aristotle, however, understood it to mean that the soul is not actually but potentially all that is thought—ἡ ψυχη τα ὀντα πως ἐστι παντα, _De an._ III 8—and that through perception and thought the objects acquired an ideal existence in the soul.

Scholasticism adopted this and said: _cognitum est in cognoscente per modum cognitionis, non per modum cogniti_; that is, the things themselves do not pass into the soul, but only their image, their form, εἰδος, _forma_, _species_, _similitudo_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 75; I 2, qu. 5, art. 5; II 2, qu. 23, art. 6 ad 1; _S. c. Gent._ I 77; II 77, 98. On the one hand, therefore, there is an essential difference between the thing and its representation, for the former is outside us and has real existence there, whereas the latter exists within us and has only ideal existence. On the other hand, however, there was nevertheless a perfect correspondence: the representation is an image, a faithful ideal reproduction of the object outside us.

Modern philosophy, however, attending to the activity of human consciousness in forming perceptual images, has dug an ever-widening chasm between thing and representation. Perceptual images are not _species_, _formae_, but at most signs, symbols, or diagrams of the external world, freely formed in our mind in response to the changes produced from without in our brain cells through the senses and nerves. If this is so, the objective world recedes ever farther from view and dissolves into appearance. For it is impossible to check the perceptual image against reality, since we can never approach reality itself and the perceptual image always inserts itself between reality and us, Land, _Inleiding_, p. 71, and the literature cited above.

The error underlying this theory appears to be the notion that the proper object of our perception is not the thing outside us but some impression or vibration of our nerves within us. It is certainly true that no representation can be formed in our consciousness unless vibrations are conveyed along the nerves to our brain cells. But neither the image cast upon the retina of the eye nor the changes in the brain cells caused by the nerve vibrations are the cause from which the sensation and representation arise in our consciousness. All psychometric investigations, important though they may be, have brought us not one step nearer to explaining this wondrous phenomenon. Here we confront what appears to be an insoluble riddle. The nerve vibrations can be traced into the center of the brain, and their strength and speed can be calculated; but the representation that subsequently arises in our consciousness differs from them _toto genere_. It is a psychical, spiritual act and can never be explained from physical phenomena such as nerve vibrations.

The representations, then, cannot be products unconsciously brought forth within our consciousness by the nerve vibrations. Nor can they be conscious creations of our mind prompted by changes in our brain cells, for the simple reason that in the act of perception no one knows anything whatever of this whole process of nerve vibrations and learns of it only through deliberate physiological investigation. Moreover, the nerve vibrations and changes in the brain cells may sometimes proceed entirely and purely mechanically—in seeing without noticing, hearing without understanding, and so forth—but in perception proper they are always already accompanied by a psychical act. It is not the case that the nerve vibrations are first transmitted to our brains and only afterward consciousness awakens and forms the representation from those changes in the brain cells. Rather, perception itself through the senses is an act of consciousness. It is the human spirit that sees through the eye and hears through the ear.

The object of perception, therefore, is not some phenomenon within me but the thing outside me. The same spirit that sees the object also forms the representation. Both are psychical acts. There is consequently no reason to doubt that in our representations we possess a faithful, ideal reproduction of the objects outside us. To a certain extent it is immaterial whether we call the representations εἶδη, _species_, _formae_, signs, symbols, and so forth, of the things; for these words too are images and are for the most part derived from visual perception. What matters is that the representations, in their whole and in their parts, are faithful interpretations of the world of reality outside us.
