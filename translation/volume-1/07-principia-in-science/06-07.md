### C. Realism

## 6.

The starting point of the theory of knowledge ought to be ordinary, everyday experience, the universal and natural human certainty concerning the objectivity and truth of our knowledge. Philosophy, after all, does not create the faculty of knowledge or knowing, but finds it and then tries to explain it; and every solution that does not explain the faculty of knowledge but destroys it, and does not comprehend knowing but turns it into an illusion, stands condemned by that very fact. Only such a theory of knowledge has a chance of succeeding as, on the one hand, does not abandon the ground of experience but, on the other, penetrates the problem in all its depth: _Prior homo, quam philosophus vel poeta_, Tertull. _de test. an_ 5. _Primum vivere, deinde philosophari._ Natural certainty is the indispensable foundation of science. Scientific knowledge is not the destruction but the purification, expansion, and completion of ordinary knowledge, Kaftan, _Die Wahrheit der Chr. Rel._, Basel, 1889 S. 317f. Every human being, after all, accepts the reliability of the senses and the existence of the external world, not by a logical inference from the effect—_in casu_, the representation in his consciousness—to the cause outside himself, nor by reasoning from the resistance his will encounters to an objective reality that offers this resistance; rather, prior to all reflection and reasoning everyone is fully assured of the world’s real existence. This certainty is not born of a syllogism and rests on no proof, but arises in me immediately and spontaneously with perception itself; it is not the product but the foundation and starting point of every other certainty. _Denn die ganze Welt der Reflexion ruht und wurzelt auf der anschaulichen Welt_, Schopenhauer, _Welt als W. u. V._ 6e Aufl. I 78. Here, however, a proper distinction must be made between the certainty that is given immediately with the actual perception of an object and the certainty that follows later, after the perception is long past, from the representation that remains behind. Only the former is under discussion here, and it is not the conclusion of a process of reasoning but is present in me immediately with perception itself. Cf. Ed. Zeller, _Ueber die Gründe unseres Glaubens an die Realität der Aussenwelt_ 1884. E. L. Fischer, _Die Grundfragen der Erkenntnisstheorie_ 1887 S. 392f. Paulsen, _Einl. in die Philos._ 385f. Flügel, _Die Probleme der Philosophie und ihre Lösungen_, Cöthen, Schulze, 2e Aufl. 1888 S. 104f. Schmid, _Erkenntnisslehre_ 1890 II 307f. Land, _Inl. tot de wĳsbeg._ p. 97f., and others.

## 7.

This one fact alone—the natural certainty concerning the reliability of the senses and the reality of the external world—proves that there is another certainty besides scientific, demonstrative certainty. The empiricists have wrongly denied this. Experience teaches only that something is, but not that it must be; it acquaints us only with the contingent, the mutable, and actuality. Yet we also have universal, necessary truths of which we are certain not through perception and reasoning but _a priori_. Most philosophers have therefore accepted, alongside scientific or mediate certainty, a metaphysical, intuitive, immediate certainty as well, also called a certainty of faith or of evidence. Aristotle was the first to see clearly that ἐπιστημη is built, in the last instance, upon indemonstrable, evident truths. Some, such as Plato, Descartes, Leibniz, and Rosmini, sought to explain this immutable, eternal character of truth by the doctrine of innate ideas; cf. Spruyt, _Proeve van eene gesch. van de leer der aangeb. begrippen_, Leiden 1879. But this doctrine rests upon an untenable dualism of subject and object, makes the human mind independent of the cosmos, is rationalistic in principle, and leads logically and also historically to absolute idealism. This was the reason why the doctrine of the _ideae innatae_ was unanimously rejected by the Scholastic as well as the Reformed theologians, Spruyt ib. 57-60. Frohschammer, _Die Philosophie des Thomas von Aquino_, Leipzig, Brockhaus 1889 S. 44f. Liberatore, _Die Erkenntnisstheorie des H. Thomas v. Aquin_, deutsch von Eugen Franz, Mainz 1861 S. 130f. Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ p. 325. Zanchius, _Opera_ III 636 sq. Voetius expressly contests Descartes’s doctrine in his _Disput. Sel._ V 477-525. They even adopted the empiricist proposition _nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu_ and spoke of the human being before he perceives as a _tabula rasa, in qua nihil scriptum est_, Thomas _S. Theol._ I qu. 79 art. 2. Voetius, _Disp._ V 459, 525. And they maintained this because the human being, in distinction from the angels, is corporeal, his body is not a prison but belongs to his nature, and through that body he is bound to the cosmos, Thomas ib. I qu. 84 art. 3. qu. 85 art. 1. Voetius ib. V 483. Thus, on the one hand, they rejected rationalism as decisively as possible, not only in the form of innate concepts in which it was taught by Plato, Descartes, and Leibniz, but also in the form of innate forms in which it appeared in Kant, and in that of the innate idea of being in which it appeared in Rosmini and the ontologists. On the other hand, however, the expressions cited above may not be understood in the sense of Locke’s empiricism. When Thomas calls the human mind a _tabula rasa_, he by no means intends thereby to deny that the _ipse intellectus_ is nevertheless innate in him. Leibniz added to the maxim _nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu_ the qualification _nisi ipse intellectus_. Thomas expresses himself more precisely still: _species aliorum intelligibilium non sunt ei innatae, sed essentia sua sibi innata est, ut non eam necesse habeat a phantasmatibus acquirere_, _qu. de mente_ art. 8 ad 1; cf. Liberatore, ib. 144. And Voetius explains the maxim cited above in such a way that it does not exclude the intellect’s ability to perceive and know in the sensibly perceived world also the eternal and immutable—for example, in the works of nature also their author, namely, God. And this is indeed the proper thought of their theory of knowledge: the human mind is not capable, or in any case not in a position, to produce apart from the sensible world, out of its own resources and by its own means, the knowledge of things, not even the knowledge of the _principia aeterna_, κοιναι ἐννοιαι. By nature it is bound to the body and through it to the cosmos, and therefore the _intellectus_ too cannot come to activity except through and on the ground of the _sensus_. At the beginning the _intellectus_ is pure _potentia_, a _tabula rasa_, without any content, and is first aroused from outside by the sensible world to activity, to actuality. The impulse therefore proceeds from the sensible world; it acts upon the human mind, awakens it, and impels it to action. But as soon as the _intellectus_ begins to operate, it immediately and spontaneously operates in its own manner and according to its own nature. And the nature of the _intellectus_ consists in its possessing the _vis, facultas, inclinatio, aptitudo_ to form in and with perception, immediately, spontaneously, involuntarily, _sine ullo labore, sine praevio studio, sine ratiocinatione_, those basic concepts and basic principles that are established _a priori_, prior to all reasoning and all proof, and therefore deserve to be called _veritates aeternae_. Thus thought itself, as soon as it begins to operate, spontaneously feels bound to the laws of thought; the laws of thought lie enclosed within thought itself and emerge from it. Thus experience teaches us what a part is and what a whole is, but the intellect understands immediately that a whole is greater than its part. Thus experience teaches us what is good and what is evil, but the _intellectus practicus_ knows immediately that the one must be done and the other left undone. This does not mean that every human being can give himself or others a clear account of these basic concepts and basic principles; but everyone, even the simplest person, nevertheless applies these basic concepts and basic principles in life, without any scientific reflection, unconsciously and with the greatest certainty. The difference between this doctrine of the faculty of knowledge and those of rationalism and empiricism lies in these two points: first, in a distinctive conception of the _intellectus_, which brings with it a nature of its own and accordingly also begins to operate in a manner of its own; and second, in the fact that this _intellectus_, while operating according to its own nature, nevertheless does nothing other than abstract from perceived things that logical element which also lies hidden in those things by nature. Rationalism as it were forces things to conform to the intellect, fits them into forms without knowing whether they fit those forms, and constructs the world according to a play of concepts. Empiricism forces the mind to conform to the sensible world, clips its wings in its ideal flight, and ultimately explains the mind itself from matter. But the theory of knowledge that gradually arose in Christian theology and was first thought out in its basic outlines by Augustine maintains both the freedom and the boundedness of the human mind: the freedom to ascend to the world of the ideal, the boundedness by which, even in this flight, it does not lose the world of reality beneath its feet.
