## 5.

This Empiricism has powerful support in man’s dependence on the nature surrounding him, but it is nevertheless burdened by serious objections.
First of all, it is certain that the human mind in its intellectual activity is never passive, or even receptive, in an absolute sense, but always acts to a greater or lesser degree.
After all, it is not the eye that sees and the ear that hears, but the human being himself, who sees through the eye and hears through the ear.
Even the simplest sensation and representation already presuppose consciousness and thus an activity of the soul.
The human mind is never a _tabula rasa_ on which the external world may write whatever it wishes; it is the mind itself that perceives and that connects, compares, and judges its perceptions.
But there is more.
Kant, _Kritik der reinen Vernunft_, Einleitung § 2, rightly says: _Erfahrung lehrt uns zwar, dass etwas so oder so beschaffen sei, aber nicht, dass es nicht anders sein könne._
We possess, however, not only particular and contingent truths but also universal and necessary truths in logic, mathesis, etc., which the empiricists have tried in vain to derive from experience.
The principle of causality, for example, has rightly been called the bulwark of the intuitive school; and every effort made to explain this principle and foundation of all science from volition, habit, etc., has been fruitless: Dr. G. Heĳmans, _Schets eener kritische gesch. van het causaliteitsbegrip in de nieuwere wĳsbegeerte_, Leiden, Brill, 1890; Dr. E. Koenig, _Die Entwicklung des Causalproblems von Cartesius bis Kant_, Leipzig, Wigand, 1888; Spruyt, _Proeve van eene geschiedenis van de leer der aangeboren begrippen_, Leiden, Brill, 1879.
Indeed, all sciences proceed from a series of unproved and unprovable propositions that are accepted _a priori_ and serve as the starting point for all reasoning and proof.
Aristotle already recognized this: there is no _regressus in infinitum_;
precisely in order to have probative force, proofs must ultimately rest on a proposition that requires no proof, that rests in itself, and that can therefore serve as an ἀρχη ἀποδειξεως, a _principium argumentationis_: Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ III 190 f., 235 f.; Schopenhauer, _Die Welt als Wille u. Vorstellung_, 6e Aufl., Leipzig, 1887, I 78.
A building cannot stand in the air, and a line of reasoning can rest only on a foundation made firm by evidence, not by proof.
With this the starting point of Empiricism stands condemned, but its conception of science is also open to serious objection.
For science by its nature seeks knowledge of the universal, the necessary, and the eternal—the logical, the idea.
Knowledge of phenomena, persons, facts, etc., is good, but it is nevertheless only preparatory labor; analysis must precede, but synthesis must follow.
Science exists only when we comprehend things in terms of their cause and essence, their end and destiny, when we know not only the ὁτι but also the διοτι, and thus _rerum dignoscimus causas_.
Empiricism, however, is compelled to deny all disciplines the name of science and to reserve it solely for the _sciences exactes_.
But this restriction is impossible for two reasons.
First, apart from—and even then only in a certain sense—the purely formal sciences (logic, mathesis, mechanics, astronomy, and chemistry), no science is possible without a philosophical element; and thus in every science discovery, intuition, imagination—in a word, genius—and, in connection with it, scientific hypothesis occupy a very important place.
Second, the name of science could then ultimately be retained only for a few subsidiary disciplines, while precisely that knowledge which is most important to man and which is his primary concern in inquiry would be banished from the domain of science.
What Thomas Aquinas said following Aristotle (_Ethic._ 10,7; _de part. an._ 1,5; _de coelo et mundo_ 2,5) remains true (_S. Theol._ I qu. 1 art. 5, ad. 1): _Minimum quod potest haberi de cognitione rerum altissimarum, desiderabilius est quam certissima cognitio, quae habetur de minimis rebus._
And Schopenhauer spoke in a similar spirit: _Sie hören nicht auf, die Zuverlässigkeit und Gewissheit der Mathematik zu rühmen. Aber was hilft es mir, noch so gewiss und zuverlässig zu wissen, daran mir gar nichts gelegen ist_, cited in Van Oosterzee, _voor Kerk en Theol._ I 101.
Moreover, the world of spiritual things, the _Welt der Werthe_, of good and evil, law and ethics, religion and morality, of everything that inspires love and hatred in us, that uplifts and comforts us or depresses and grieves us—that entire rich, invisible world is just as much a reality for us as the _Welt der Wirklichkeit_ that we perceive with our senses.
Its power over our life and in the history of humanity is much greater still than that of the visible things around us.
One may indeed demand that man limit himself in his inquiry because no knowledge is possible in this domain; that demand founders on what Schopenhauer called _das metaphysische Bedürfniss_ of the human mind.
Man is not only an intellectual being but also a willing and feeling being; he is no thinking machine but has, besides his head, a heart, a world of emotions and passions.
He brings these with him into his scientific inquiry; in his work in the study and laboratory he cannot exclude himself.
It cannot be demanded that man, in scientific labor—that is, in one of the noblest and highest activities of his mind—impose silence on his emotional nature, his heart, and the best that is in him, and thus mutilate himself.
Only this may always be demanded, including of the practitioner of science: that he be a good and true human being, a man of God thoroughly equipped for every good work, including this work of science (cf. above, p. 29).
If, however, one limits science in both the subjective and the objective senses, the only result will be that people seek to meet the _metaphysische Bedürfniss_ by other paths.
Kant took the path of the _practische Vernunft_; Comte instituted a cult of humanity and consecrated himself as its high priest; Spencer bows humbly before _The Unknowable_.
All of them seek, in one way or another—even in spiritualism, magic, and theosophy—compensation for what science does not grant them.
And religion, together with all spiritual knowledge, after first being disgracefully driven out through the front door, is admitted again through the back door, though often in a superstitious form.
_Naturam expellas furcâ, tamen usque recurret._
The only inevitable consequence is that science is surrendered defenseless and unarmed to Materialism.
Empiricism has in fact led to this.
If the content and then also the intellectual faculty of the soul derive entirely from the external world, why could the substance of the soul not ultimately be explained from it as well?
Against this, however, the “sieben Welträthsel” still stand as a torment and an offense to materialistic thought.
The spiritual has not yet been explained from the material, any more than Rationalism has succeeded in deriving being from thought.
The transition between the two has not been found.
Here is a gulf that neither Idealism nor Materialism can bridge.
It is not even rash to speak here not only of an _Ignoramus_ but also of an _Ignorabimus_.
But when we see how Empiricism and Rationalism, despite their great promises and still greater expectations, have in this century issued in nothing other than Materialism and Illusionism, and despite their opposition have nevertheless promoted and abetted one another—the Idealism of Hegel ended in Materialism in Feuerbach and Strauss, and among many natural scientists Materialism in turn passes into partial or complete Idealism—then there is certainly reason to ask whether the whole of modern philosophy, in both its Cartesian and its Baconian orientations, needs revision; whether there are not other and better _principia_ of science that protect us from both Materialism and Idealism.
