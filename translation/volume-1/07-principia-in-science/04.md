### B. Empiricism

## 4.

Diametrically opposed to Rationalism stands Empiricism, which already had its precursors among the Greeks in the Atomists, then appeared in the Middle Ages as Nominalism, subsequently entered the modern period as a philosophical current with Francis Bacon, and through Locke, Hume, and the French Encyclopedists culminated in this century in the Positivism of A. Comte, the experiential philosophy of Stuart Mill, the Agnosticism of H. Spencer, and the Materialism of Büchner, Czolbe, Moleschott, and others. Empiricism too appears in various forms and systems, but it nevertheless always takes as its starting point this principle: sensible perception alone is the source of our knowledge. Whereas Rationalism causes the objective world to conform wholly or partly to the human mind, Empiricism subjects consciousness entirely to the world outside us. In striving for knowledge man brings with him nothing but the faculty of perception; from this all intellectual activity takes its beginning and origin. There are therefore no innate concepts; the scientific investigator must set aside all preconceived opinions. From the temple of truth that he wishes to build in his consciousness, he must remove all _idola_. No _anticipatio mentis_, but _interpretatio naturae_, _mera experientia_, must guide him (Bacon). The human mind is and must be a _tabula rasa, in qua nihil scriptum est_, completely _voraussetzungslos_. Knowledge is reliable only when it is built solely and exclusively from perception. _Begriffe ohne Anschauungen sind leer._ The further man removes himself from experience and rises above it, the less he can be trusted in his scientific endeavor. Therefore no science of the supersensible (_noumena_) or the supernatural is possible. Metaphysics, theology, the spiritual sciences in general, and according to Comte even psychology, are not science in the proper sense. Science is limited to the _sciences exactes_. Even within the circle of observable phenomena, our knowledge is restricted to the that and the how; the what and the why remain hidden. The cause and purpose, origin and destiny, of things lie beyond our reach. Only the mutual relations of things, the _relations invariables de succession et de similitude_, are the object of scientific investigation, A. Comte, _Cours de philos. positive_ I, pp. 8 s. Whether behind and above observable phenomena there is anything else, whether the soul, God, and the _jenseits_ exist, may perhaps be made plausible by another way—through practical reason, faith, imagination, and so forth—but scientifically all this is and remains a _terra incognita_. The goal of science can therefore no longer lie in providing a _Welterklärung_, but extends only to such knowledge of reality that we can order our life accordingly and derive practical benefit from it. _Savoir, c’est prévoir; science, d’où prévoyance; prévoyance, d’où action._ But this absolute binding of the mind to the world of perception led others to attempt to explain from the world not only the mind’s content of thought but also consciousness and the mind itself; Empiricism ended in Materialism. Here too, therefore, progression, history, and development can be observed. First the content of thought, then the faculty, and finally also the substance of the mind is derived from the material world. E.
