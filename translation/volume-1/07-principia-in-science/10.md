## 10.

Plato clarified this process of science with a beautiful and striking image. Just as the sun objectively illuminates the object and subjectively illuminates our eye, so God, or the Idea of the Good, is the light by which the truth, the essence of things, becomes visible and by which at the same time our mind can behold and recognize that truth; cf. Siebeck, _Geschichte der Psychologie_, I Theil, 2 Abth., Gotha, Perthes 1880, ’84 I 226. Aristotle, ib. II 70. Augustine adopted this image: God is the sun of minds. In the immutable light of truth our mind beholds and judges all things: _in ipsa incommutabili veritate mens rationalis et intellectualis intuetur, eaque luce de his omnibus judicat_, _de Gen. ad litt._ L. 8 cap. 25. Just as we can see nothing with the bodily eye unless the sun spreads its light over it, so too we can see no truth except in the light of God, who is the sun of our knowledge, _Solil._ I c. 8, 13, _de Trin._ 12 c. 15. _Deus intelligibilis lux, in quo et a quo et per quem intelligibiliter lucent, quae intelligibiliter lucent omnia._ Thomas frequently speaks in the same way and uses the same simile, _S. Theol._ I qu. 12 art. 11 ad 3, qu. 79 art. 4, qu. 88 art. 3 ad 1. II 1 qu. 109 art. 1 ad 2. _S. c. Gent._ 3 c. 47. Thomas did point out, however, that this may not be understood pantheistically, as Averroes taught under Neoplatonic influence and was later followed in this by Malebranche and the ontologist school. Just as in natural vision, says Thomas, we see not by being in the sun itself but by the light of the sun that shines upon us, so too we see things not in the divine essence but by the light that shines from God into our own _intellectus_. The reason within us is that divine light; it is not the divine Logos himself but participates in him. To God belong _esse, vivere, intelligere per essentiam_; to us, _per participationem_, _S. Theol._ I qu. 79 art. 4. This image of the sun led theologians to speak in a sound sense of the natural light of reason; cf. Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ p. 325. Zanchius, _Opera_ III 636 sq. By this they understood nothing other than that permanent property or power of the human mind by which it is enabled, immediately upon its first perceptions, to form those basic concepts and basic principles that thereafter guide it in all perception and thought. The light of reason is thus in the first place equivalent to the _intellectus agens_, to the faculty of abstraction that shines upon objects and causes the _intelligibile_ to emerge from them, and further to that fund of κοιναι ἐννοιαι which our mind appropriates precisely through its power of abstraction. But in both senses that light is owed to God, or, more specifically, to the Logos, Ps. 36:10; John 1:9. It is he who causes this light to dawn within us and continually sustains it. And thus, when the rays of that light disclose the truth to our minds, the credit belongs not to the human being, who is only an instrument, but to God, Liberatore, _Die Erkenntnisslehre des h. Thomas v. A._ 185f. Kleutgen, _Die Philosophie der Vorzeit_, 2e Aufl., Innsbrück 1878 I 89f.

This beautiful imagery makes clear to us what the _principia_ are from which all science proceeds. Not only in theology, as the preceding section showed us, but in every science three _principia_ may be distinguished. Here too God is the _principium essendi_; in his self-consciousness lie the ideas of all things; all things rest upon thoughts and were created through the Word. But it is his good pleasure to transfer from this _cognitio archetypa_ in his divine consciousness an ectypal knowledge to the human being, who is made in his image. He does this, however, not by allowing us to behold the ideas in his _essentia_ (Malebranche), nor by giving all of them to us already at birth (Plato, doctrine of the _ideae innatae_), but by spreading them out before the human mind in the works of his hands. The world is an embodiment of the thoughts of God; it is a beautiful book in which all creatures, great and small, are like letters that enable us to behold the invisible things of God. It is not a book in which, according to the conception of the idealists, we ourselves must fill in the words, but a book to be read, from which God enables us to know what he has written in it for us. The created world is therefore the _principium cognoscendi externum_ of all science. But that is not enough. To see, an eye is necessary. _Wär’ nicht das Auge sonnenhaft, wie könnten wir das Licht erblicken?_ There must be correspondence, kinship, between object and subject. The same Logos who shines in the world must also cause his light to shine in our consciousness. This light is the _intellectus_, the _ratio_, which, itself proceeding from the Logos, discovers and acknowledges the Logos in things. It is the _principium cognoscendi internum_. _Sicut scientia in nobis est sigillatio rerum in animabus nostris, ita e converso formae non sunt nisi quaedam sigillatio divinae scientiae in rebus_, Thomas, cited in Liberatore, _Die Erkenntnisstheorie des h. Thomas v. Aq._ S. 148. Thus it is God alone who, from his divine consciousness and through the creatures, brings the knowledge of the truth into our minds: the Father, who through the Son in the Spirit reveals himself to us. _Multi dicunt: quis ostendet nobis bona? Signatum est super nos lumen vultus tui, Domine!_
