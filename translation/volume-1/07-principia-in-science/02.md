## 2.

In whatever different forms this Rationalism has appeared, it has nevertheless always had one fundamental thought, namely, that the origin of knowledge is to be sought in the subject. It is easy to understand how people arrived at this thought. Quite apart from the unreliability of sensible perception, there is such an essential difference between the representations within us and the things outside us that the former cannot be explained from the latter. Matter cannot act upon spirit; spiritual phenomena, such as representations, can be explained only from spirit; like can be known only by like. It follows from this that the existence and interaction of matter and spirit, of things outside us and representations within us, can either be maintained only through hypotheses such as occasionalism (Geulinx), the _harmonia praestabilita_ (Leibniz), the contemplation of the ideas in God (Malebranche), and so forth; or else the duality of matter and spirit must simply be denied, and thing and representation, being and thought, must be regarded as essentially one. For if thing and representation are two, idealism says, then we must despair of knowing the thing. We can never test our representation of the thing against the thing itself; we can never get outside ourselves, outside our world of representations; _nous ne pouvous nous mettre à la fenêtre, pour nous voir passer dans la rue_ (Scherer). We always remain within the circle of our representations and come into contact never with the thing itself, but always again with our representation of the thing. Only the conscious exists for us. I can think only what is thought, not the thing. What is not my thought is for me unthinkable and unknowable; for me it does not exist. And this idealism has been further strengthened by what the physiology of the senses now teaches. Democritus already distinguished between such properties as weight, density, and hardness, which are objective and reside in things themselves, and others such as heat, cold, taste, and color, which are present only subjectively in our sensations, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ I⁴ 783. This distinction between objective and subjective, quantitative and qualitative properties was adopted by Descartes, Hobbes, and Locke—the latter first calling them primary and secondary properties, _An essay concerning human understanding_ II, ch. 8 § 19, etc.—and was then elaborated in this century especially by Helmholtz in his _Handbuch der physiol. Optik_, Leipzig, 1865–66, and _Lehre von den Tonempfindungen_, Braunschw., 1862, 4e Aufl., 1877; cf. also Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._, 4e Aufl., 1882, S. 712 f.; Pierson, _Gids_, June 1871. According to this so-called semi-idealism, outside us in the world there are only mechanical movements of atoms; matter is without quality. Our senses receive impressions only through the movement and undulation of the atoms; those impressions are qualitatively alike, but in our brains we produce from those uniform movements the infinite diversity of the world of perception. Light, sound, color, taste, heat, cold—all the qualitative properties that we believe we perceive in things—do not exist outside us but arise and exist only in the human mind. One and the same movement of matter, affecting our sense of touch, produces the impression of heat; entering the eye, it supplies us with the sensation of light. The world is not in its substance, but nevertheless in its form, a product of man. Thus idealism has increasingly gained ground in philosophy and has even received powerful support from natural science.
