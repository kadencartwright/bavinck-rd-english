# § 7. Principia in Science

### A. Rationalism

## 1.

Science always consists in a logical relation between subject and object. The relation in which we place these two to one another determines our conception of science. At all times there have been two currents that stand diametrically opposed in this respect: Rationalism and Empiricism. They already arose in Greek philosophy. There the contrast was already drawn between αἰσθησις and λογος, sensible perception and thought, and therefore also between δοξα and ἐπιστημη. The Eleatic school, Plato, and the Neoplatonists stood on the side of Rationalism. Sensible perception gives no knowledge; its object is changing phenomena, and it teaches us only that something is and is such, but not why it is so. Moreover, it frequently deceives us and supplies us with false representations—for example, the bent stick in water, the rising sun, and so forth—which only thought can purge of their untruth. Thought therefore stands far above sensible perception. It alone supplies us with ἐπιστημη; science does not come from without but is a product of the human mind. In modern philosophy this rationalistic current arose again with Descartes, who, casting everything aside, finally found his fixed starting point in thought and inferred being from it: _cogito ergo sum_. With this, necessity of thought, logical connection, and the mathematical order of ground and consequence became in Spinoza the standard of truth. The sensible world is at most the occasion but not the source of our knowledge; the human mind can produce all knowledge from itself, by its own means, through thought. _Nos idées, même celles des choses sensibles, viennent de notre propre fond_, Leibniz, _Nouveaux essais sur l’entendement humain_ I, ch. 1. Kant did indeed temper this Rationalism insofar as he derived from the human mind not the matter but only the forms of perception (transcendental, critical idealism). But Fichte rightly recognized that such a distinction was impossible and therefore declared that all the elements of our knowledge, even perception itself, were _a priori_ and posited by the Ego (absolute idealism). In these philosophers this Rationalism was still always limited to the domain of the faculty of knowledge and was therefore intended only in an epistemological sense. But this subjective Rationalism was extended by Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel into an objective Rationalism. Not only knowledge but also being, not only representations but also things themselves, have proceeded solely from thought; thought and being are one (metaphysical idealism). There is a progression in this history of Rationalism: thought, not sensible perception, gives truth; for this purpose it brings within itself the _principia_, the _semina_ of all knowledge; it creates the form of our world of thought (Kant), and also its matter and content (Fichte); indeed, it creates and constructs the whole world, not only of thought but also of being.
