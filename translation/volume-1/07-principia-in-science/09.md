## 9.

But the human mind does not stop with these representations. Scientific knowledge does not arise from the senses but from the understanding. It was not mere observation, but earnest reflection on the phenomena observed, that made Copernicus the father of astronomy and Newton the discoverer of gravity. The observation of phenomena is necessary and good, but it is not the only or the highest thing. The object of science is not the particular but the universal, the logical, the idea. Greek philosophy already recognized this. Socrates was the first consciously to think through the idea of knowledge and to make it the principle of his philosophy: science is knowledge not of the appearance but of the essence of things, Xenophon, _Memor._ IV, 6.1. Plato distinguished between δοξα, whose content was ordinary empirical knowledge, and ἐπιστημη, whose content was the true being of things, _Rep._ V 476 D–478 D; _Sympos._ 202 A, etc. Aristotle likewise described science as knowledge του ὀντος, του καθολου, των πρωτων αἰτιων και ἀρχων (passages in Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ III 161 f.).

Augustine in particular placed this intellectual knowledge in the foreground. He does not reject knowledge through the senses; in a separate work he defends its truth against the Academics and acknowledges that we understand the _invisibilia Dei per ea quae facta sunt_, _De Gen. ad litt._ 4,32. Nevertheless, like Plato, he underestimates its value. The sensible yields only δοξα, _C. Acad._ 3,26; it is not truth itself, but only an image of it, _Solil._ 2,32. Knowledge of nature is without value, _nihil prodest_, _Conf._ 5,7; 10,55; _Enchir._ 3,5. Properly speaking, only two things are important to know: God and ourselves. _Deum et animam scire cupio. Nihilne plus? Nihil omnino_, _Solil._ 1,7. And he acquires this knowledge not by looking outward but by looking inward: _noli foras ire, in te ipsum redi, in interiore homine habitat veritas_, _De vera relig._ c. 39, n. 72; not through sensory perception but through thought: _aliud est sentire, aliud nosse_, _De ord._ 2,5; cf. _Solil._ 2,33.

Scholasticism, attaching itself more closely to Aristotle, better appreciated the value of sensory perception, yet it too fully acknowledged the significance of the _intellectus_ for science. Thomas expressed this briefly and clearly: _scientia non est singularium_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 1, art. 2; _intellectus est universalium_, _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 44. Science has as its object the universal and the necessary, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 14, art. 13 ad 3; I, qu. 84, art. 1 ad 2; I, qu. 86, arts. 1 and 3, and can therefore be supplied only by the _intellectus_. For whereas sensory perception considers things _quantum ad exteriora ejus accidentia_, it is precisely the distinctive character of the _intellectus_ that it penetrates _ad interiora rei_, _S. c. Gent._ I 58; III 56; IV 11, and _penetrat ad essentiam rei_, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 8, art. 1. Its proper object is the _quidditas rei materialis_, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 85, art. 5 ad 3.

The understanding, namely, as _intellectus agens_—that is, as the faculty of abstraction, as we would call it—releases the universal from the sensible representations. It causes the particular to fall away from them, shines over them as a light, renders them intelligible, allows the universal in them to become knowable, and then, as _intellectus possibilis_—that is, as the faculty of intellectual cognition—receives that universal into itself and makes it the possession of the mind, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 79, arts. 3 and 4; I, qu. 84, art. 6; _S. c. Gent._ II 76, 77; III 45.

Now there is actually no dispute that the organ of science is not sensory perception but the understanding. Empiricism has not denied this either. Bacon, Hume, and Stuart Mill fully acknowledge that sensory perception is indeed the first but not the only thing, and that by induction the understanding seeks to derive the universal from the particular. Bacon’s very concern was to find a reliable method by which universally valid judgments could be formed from particular observations. But with the concepts that the understanding forms from the representations, the question already asked above concerning perceptual images returns with redoubled seriousness: what is the relation between these concepts of the understanding and the world of reality? Here Nominalism and Realism part ways.

In substance, both tendencies already occur in Greek philosophy. Plato and Aristotle were realists, though in different ways. And we already find the proper thought of Nominalism, among others, in the Cynic philosopher Antisthenes, who denied the reality of universal concepts and said to Plato: ἱππον μεν ὁρῶ, ἱπποτητα δε οὐχ ὁρω, Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ II⁴ 295; and among the Stoic philosophers, who regarded the ἐννοηματα, the thoughts, only as φαντασματα διανοιας, ibid. IV³ 79, 125. In the Middle Ages this view of universal concepts was revived and received the name Nominalism. Roscellinus held that universal concepts were only _flatus vocis_, _Gedankendinge_, to which no reality corresponds. In reality there are no universal but only particular, individual things; there is no humanity, but only human beings, and so forth.

The conflict between Realism and Nominalism continued into the fifteenth century. Cf. A. Stöckl, _Gesch. der Philos. des Mittelalters_, 3 Bde., Mainz, 1864–66, I 135 f., II 986 f.; Hauréau, _De la philosophie scolastique_, 2 vol., Paris, 1850; Schwane, _Dogmengeschichte der mittl. Zeit_, Freiburg, 1882, S. 4 f.; further, the histories of dogma by Bach, Thomasius, Münscher, Baur, Harnack, and others, and the histories of philosophy by Ueberweg, Erdmann, Windelband, and others; A. Pierson, _De realismo et nominalismo_, 1855; idem, _Gesch. van het R.-Katholic._ III, 1871, pp. 53 f., 87 f., 183 f.; Spruyt, _Proeve_, pp. 66 f. But even afterward the issue did not disappear from philosophy. The dispute between Realism and Nominalism is no quarrel over scholastic subtleties but is of far-reaching significance. In modern philosophy Nominalism has reappeared in a new form as Empiricism, Spruyt, _Proeve_, passim; Hugo Spitzer, _Nominalismus und Realismus in der neuesten deutschen Philosophie mit Berücksichtigung ihres Verhältnisses zur modernen Naturwissenschaft_, Leipzig, 1876; Janet, _Traité élémentaire de philos._, Paris, Delagrave, 1887, pp. 162 s.; Land, _Inleiding_, pp. 107 f.; Pierson, _Wĳsgeerig Onderzoek_, Deventer, ter Gunne, 1882, pp. 200 f.

If Nominalism is right, all science is at an end. For it must be one of two things. If we can comprehend the corresponding characteristics of a group of things in one concept and word, either this occurs without any ground, in which case those words and concepts represent nothing of value in reality; or the things actually resemble one another and possess common characteristics. In the latter case, however, the concepts are not empty _Gedankendinge_, but the sum of essential properties of the things, and thus not _nomina_ but _res_. Realism was therefore undoubtedly right when it assumed the reality of universal concepts—not in the Platonic or ontological sense _ante rem_, but in the Aristotelian sense _in re_, and therefore also _in mente hominis post rem_. The universal that we express in the concept does not exist outside us in precisely that manner, as a universal (cf. above, p. 23); yet, individualized and specified in each particular specimen of the species, it does have its ground in the things and is abstracted from them by the activity of the understanding and expressed in a concept, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 85, art. 2 ad 2; _S. c. Gent._ I 65.

With concepts, therefore, we do not remove ourselves from reality but draw ever nearer to it. It may seem that, in forming concepts, judgments, and conclusions, we increasingly lose the firm ground beneath the structure of our knowledge and soar high into the air. It appears strange and wondrous that, by converting representations into concepts and then working these up according to the laws of thought, we obtain results that accord with reality. Yet anyone who surrenders this conviction is lost, Land, _Inleiding_, p. 250. But this conviction can rest only in the faith that it is one and the same Logos who created both the reality outside us and the laws of thought within us, placed the two in organic connection, and caused them to correspond to one another.

Only in this way is science possible—that is, knowledge not merely of changing appearance but of the universal, of the logical in things. Certainly, the being of things, their existence, remains outside us; the things themselves never enter into us _realiter_. Being is therefore never accessible to us; it is a _factum_ that must be accepted and that forms the foundation of thought. But insofar as things also exist logically, have proceeded from thought, and rest in thought, John 1:3; Col. 1:15, they are also comprehensible and thinkable for the human mind.
