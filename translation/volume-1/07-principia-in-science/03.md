## 3.

Yet there are very serious objections to idealism. In the first place, it conflicts with all experience. By nature we are all realists, and even idealists are realists in practice. In fact, idealism is a school doctrine, an opinion directly at odds with life and experience. It does not explain how and why every human being spontaneously and involuntarily comes to ascribe objectivity and independent reality to perceived phenomena rather than conceiving them merely as inner states of consciousness, even though we clearly distinguish between inner states and external things, between what is within us and what is outside us, and between dream (hallucination) and reality. Moreover, the human being is never autonomous in any sphere but is always and everywhere dependent on nature around him. Through his body he is bound to the earth. From it he receives shelter, food, and clothing; it would be strange if matters stood otherwise with him intellectually. Just as we may prepare food and clothing with our hands but still derive their material from nature outside us, so too with the intellect we receive the material from without. Here too the intellect is an instrument, not a source. Idealism identifies the organ with the source of knowledge; it makes the eye, as it were, the source of light and derives the content of thought from the act of thinking. But this is impossible, because a thing and its representation, being and thought, _esse_ and _percipi_, are two and cannot be identified. After all, they differ _toto genere_. A thing does not arise within us like a dream, nor does it follow logically from preceding representations; rather, it often comes to me suddenly from without and interrupts the sequence of my representations. It is independent of me and exists outside me; it has properties that cannot be ascribed to its representation. The thing that is called a stove is, for example, warm, but the representation of that thing in my consciousness has no such property. If thing and representation are nevertheless identified, idealism must lead to absolute illusionism: not only does the world outside me become mere illusion, but I myself am nothing but a representation, a phenomenon to myself. Everything becomes a dream; there is no longer either reality or truth. Idealism may exercise no influence on the actions and conduct of its adherents, Land, _Inleiding tot de wĳsbegeerte_, ’s Grav. 1889, p. 92 f.; Fr. Paulsen, _Einleitung in die Philosophie_, Berlin 1892, S. 363, because life is often better and stronger than doctrine. Yet it is impossible to see how, for example, religion and morality can still be justified theoretically if both are not real relations to beings existing outside me but only relations to representations within me. The dualism of thought and being from which idealism proceeds in Plato, Cartesius, and Kant therefore repeatedly turns into the identity of the two in Spinoza, Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel; subjective Rationalism leads to absolute and objective Rationalism. But abstract, contentless thought—the most universal principle of substance, the absolute, being, thought—from which idealistic philosophy then takes its point of departure is incapable of producing the rich fullness of being. The living world cannot be explained from that arid _abstractum_, nor the multiplicity of phenomena from that lifeless unity. The rock on which all pantheism founders is multiplicity; no transition can be found from the abstract to the concrete, from the universal to the particular. Schelling therefore openly stated _dass es schwer sei an die Wirklichkeit heran zu kommen_, as cited in Liberatore, _Die Erkenntnisstheorie des h. Thomas v. Aquin_, deutsch von Eugen Franz, Mainz 1861, S. VII. Finally, a distinction such as Kant’s between the form that we ourselves bring to perception and the matter that comes to us from without, or such as semi-idealism’s distinction between objective (primary) and subjective (secondary) phenomena, is untenable because no boundary between the two can be indicated. As Fichte also stated, the matter of a representation belongs just as much to the representation as its form does. And the primary quantitative properties—indeed, the bodies themselves—are perceived phenomena just as much as the qualitative properties of tone, color, and so forth. There is no reason to accept the testimony of one sense, the sense of touch, and reject that of the other four, thus making an exception solely for the properties of extension, hardness, and so forth. There is no objection to ascribing objective reality in the things themselves also to the forms of perception, time and space, and to the categories of thought.
