## 3.

But this standpoint has proved untenable through the history of Supernaturalism itself and through the sharp criticism of Rousseau and Lessing, Kant and Schleiermacher.
Apologetics undoubtedly has a right to exist; an important task has been entrusted to it.
It must maintain and defend the truth of God against every attack, both from within and from without.
Through various circumstances it has unjustly fallen into disrepute.
In the first place, it lost the sympathy of many through the weakness and subtlety of the arguments it advanced against the serious and scientific opposition to the Christian faith.
Next, it increasingly aroused aversion because of the assumption from which it often proceeded, that Christianity was a doctrine capable of being demonstrated intellectually.
Further, fear of science, which so often spoke in a lofty tone and proclaimed its _dogmata_ so infallibly, frequently deterred believers from offering a defense.
They continued to believe but withdrew anxiously and avoided all contact with science; sometimes they eagerly took refuge in Mysticism or Agnosticism.
Yet there is no reason to despise apologetics.
The apologists of the second century, the church fathers, the Scholastic theologians, the Reformers, and so forth, all stood in the firm faith that the truth of God had to be and could be defended against the attacks to which it was exposed on every side.
They did not leave the attacks unanswered.
They sought out the enemy and did not rest until they had overcome him.
That faith is itself a power and almost half the victory.
Doubt and mistrust in the cause we champion make us powerless in the struggle.
But this itself already indicates the only standpoint from which a sound defense of the truth can be undertaken.
Apologetics cannot precede faith and does not attempt to demonstrate the truth of revelation _a priori_.
It presupposes the truth and faith in that truth; it stands on the foundation of dogmatics and now seeks to maintain and defend the dogma against the attacks to which it is subjected.
If, however, Christian revelation, which presupposes the darkness and error of the psychical man, were to submit itself in advance to his reason for judgment, it would thereby contradict itself.
It would thereby place itself before a tribunal whose competence it had first denied.
And once it acknowledged the right of reason in the _principia_, it could no longer afterward contest that right in the _articuli fidei_.
Supernaturalism must always lead to Rationalism, since in principle it is already rationalistic.
But apart from this principial objection, the historical-apologetic mode of proof does not lead to the desired result either.
It could still make some headway in a time when the authenticity of the biblical books and the historical truth of their content were still fairly generally established.
But the miracles and predictions of Scripture now themselves require so much defense that they can no longer possibly serve as arguments.
To prove anything, apologetics would first have to treat the entire so-called science of biblical introduction and incorporate numerous other disciplines before it could begin the exposition of the truth.
In this way it would never arrive at faith or dogmatics; the _pars formalis_ would expand so greatly that no time or place would remain for the _pars materialis_.
This long road might still be taken by someone who possessed sufficient time, strength, and gifts to undertake such an investigation into the truth of the Christian faith; but it would be wholly inaccessible to the simple person, who needs the peace and comfort of faith just as much as the scholar does, and needs it not first tomorrow but already now, at this very moment, and who would therefore become dependent for the salvation of his soul upon an intellectual and hence all the more intolerable clericalism.
And suppose that this were still no overriding objection and that historical investigation were the only road to the knowledge of the truth for all people; even then the result obtained under the most favorable circumstances would be nothing other than a _fides humana_, which could be shaken and overthrown again tomorrow by other and better investigations.
Eternity truly cannot hang upon a cobweb.
In religion no human being and no creature may stand between God and my soul.
It is impossible to live and die in comfort and blessedness so long as I rest on human, fallible testimony.
In religion there is need not of a lesser but of a much stronger and firmer certainty than in science.
Here there is rest only in the testimony of God.
The testimony of the church too is insufficient.
It is of great value not only in the origin but also in the continuance of Christian faith.
It remains a support until the end of life.
It is indeed a _motivum perpetuum_ for faith.
Throughout our whole life we are bound to a community.
A human being is a ζωον πολιτικον.
The community sustains us whenever we are in danger of stumbling.
The cloud of witnesses surrounding us encourages us in the struggle.
It requires unusual courage and strength of spirit to stand firm when everyone abandons us and stands opposed to us.
But community strengthens personal conviction.
Yet the testimony of the church cannot therefore be the last and deepest ground of faith.
Roman Catholic theologians themselves acknowledge this as well, as will appear later.
In the investigation into the grounds of faith, they are in no better position whatsoever with their infallible church than the Protestants are.
For they too must ask the question: on what does faith in the church rest?
If it rests on apologetic proofs, then they face the same objections advanced above.
And if it rests on the _testimonium Spiritus Sancti_, then this doctrine is the cornerstone of Christian faith.
