# § 14. The Historical-Apologetic Method

## 1.

No sooner had Christianity penetrated the Greco-Roman world than it found itself called to a serious struggle.
Jews and pagans attacked it and raised all kinds of objections against it.
Then, in the 2nd century, the Apologists arose, seeking to repel these attacks and defend Christianity.
Justin Martyr, _Dialogus c. Tryphone_; Tertullian, _adv. Judaeos_; and Eusebius, _Demonstratio evangelica_, wrote against the Jews.
The number of apologetic writings against the pagans was much greater.
The most important are Justin Martyr, _Apologia maior_ and _minor_; Tatian, _Orat. adv. Graecos_; Athenagoras, _Legatio sive supplicatio pro Christianis_; Theophilus, _ad Autolycum_; Clement of Alexandria, _Coh. ad gentes_; Origen, _contra Celsum_; Tertullian, _Apologeticus_ and _Ad nationes_; Arnobius, _Disput. adv. gentes_; Minucius Felix, _Octavius_; Eusebius, _Praeparatio evangelica_; Athanasius, _Orat. adv. gentes_; Cyril, _adv. impium Julianum_; and Augustine, _de civ. Dei_.
In these works the following arguments are advanced for the truth of Christianity:
a) of two uncertain things, it is safer to believe _quod aliquas spes ferat quam omnino quod nullas_, Arnobius, _adv. gentes_ II 4;
b) the agreement of Christianity with what is best and most beautiful, with the _σπερματα ἀληθειας_ that are still found even among the pagans through the operation of the Logos, Athenagoras, _Leg._ 6; Justin, _Apol. maior_ 20 sq.; Minucius, _Octav._ 19, 20;
c) the excellence of Christianity above the pagan religions, so that every human soul must involuntarily bear witness to it, Justin, _Apol. min._ 10; Tertullian, _de testim. an._ 1; Arnobius, _adv. gentes_ II 2;
d) the moral influence of Christianity upon doctrine and life, so that the horrible sins of idolatry, sorcery, hatred, avarice, cruelty, and so forth have disappeared through it, _Epist. ad Diognetum_ 5; Justin, _Apol. maior_ 14; Athenagoras, _Leg._ 11; Origen, _c. Cels._ I 26; Arnobius, _adv. gentes_ I 63; Lactantius, _Instit. div._ III 16; Eusebius, _Praep. evang._ I 4;
e) the steadfastness of the martyrs, Justin Martyr, _Apol._ II 12, and the holiness of the ascetics, Justin, _Apol._ I 15; Athenagoras, _Leg._ 33, 34; Eusebius, _Dem. Evang._ III 6;
f) the predictions and their fulfillment, Justin, _Dial. c. Tryph._ 7, 8; Justin, _Apol._ I 31; Origen, _c. Cels._ I 2;
g) the miracles, not only in former times but also in the present, Justin, _Dial. c. Tryph._ 39, 82, 88; Irenaeus, _adv. haer._ II 31, 32; Tertullian, _Apol._ 23; Origen, _c. Cels._ III 24;
h) the holy character and life of Jesus and the apostles, Arnobius, _adv. gentes_ I 63; Eusebius, _Dem. evang._ III 3, 5;
i) the testimony of Scripture, the mutual agreement of the Scriptures, the simple language, and the divine content that could be produced by no human reason, Justin Martyr, _Coh. ad Graec._ 8; Origen, _de princ._ IV 1, together with the marvelous origin, preservation, and dissemination of Scripture, Tertullian, _Apol._ 19;
and finally j) the testimony of tradition and the church, Irenaeus, _adv. haer._ I 10, III 3; Tertullian, _de praescr._ 20; Cyprian, _de unitate eccles._; Augustine, _de civ. dei_, and so forth.
Cf. Münscher-v. Coelln, _Lehrb. der christl. Dogm. Gesch._ I, 1832, S. 103 f.; Hagenbach, _Dogm. Gesch._ § 29 f.; Harnack, _Dogm. Gesch._ I² 413 f.
These arguments have since acquired a generally recognized place in Christian theology.
The content of the supernatural truths was incomprehensible to reason, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 32 art. 1; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 9.
It was therefore all the more important to prove that God had revealed himself.
All the proofs that could be adduced for this were gathered under the name _rationes inductivae_ or _motiva credibilitatis_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 2 art. 9 ad 3, art. 10; Duns Scotus, _Prol. Sentent._ qu. 2; Ludovicus Vives, _de veritate fidei christ._, 1543; cf. Frohschammer, _Die Philosophie des Thomas v. Aquino_, Leipzig, 1889, S. 130 f.
After the Reformation, the argument drawn from the church in particular was developed further.
At Rome the church then became increasingly the _fundamentum et regula fidei_.
Augustine had already said that he was moved by the church to believe Scripture.
Roman Catholics after the Reformation made the church the strongest ground for belief in Scripture, in revelation.
The _motiva credibilitatis_ were frequently divided into three kinds, Becanus, _Theol. Schol._ Tom. II pars. II tract. 1 cap. 6, Mogunt. 1623, p. 93: those that apply against Jews and pagans; others that have significance especially for Roman Catholics themselves; and a third group that are effective against heretics.
To this last group belongs above all the church with her 15 _notae_, as enumerated by Bellarmine, _de Conciliis et Ecclesia_, lib. IV.
What the Reformers said of Scripture is applied to the church.
She is like the sun, which spreads her rays and can easily be known by her own light.
Among the proofs for revelation, the church occupies the first and highest place; of them all she is the most powerful motive to faith.
The _Conc. Vatic._, sess. III, cap. 3, _de fide_, declared: _ad solam enim catholicam Ecclesiam ea pertinent omnia, quae ad evidentem fidei christianae credibilitatem tam multa et tam mira divinitus sunt disposita. Quin etiam Ecclesia per se ipsa, ob suam nempe admirabilem propagationem, eximiam sanctitatem et inexhaustam in omnibus bonis foecunditatem, ob catholicam unitatem invictamque stabililatem, magnum quoddam et perpetuum est motivum credibilitatis et divinae suae legationis testimonium irrefragabile._
The value of all these proofs, including that of the church, consists in their ability to demonstrate the credibility of revelation.
They are capable of producing a _fides humana_ and of proving the reasonableness of believing.
They make the truth of revelation so clear that all reasonable doubt is excluded.
If no sinful selfishness and no hostility of the heart intervened on the human side, those motives would be powerful enough to move one to faith in revelation.
They do not, to be sure, make revelation _evidenter veram_, for if that were the case, faith would no longer be necessary and would also lose all merit; but they do make it _evidenter credibilem_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 1 art. 4 ad 2, 4, art. 5 ad 2, qu. 2 art. 1 ad 1; Bellarmine, _de Conc. et Eccl._ IV cap. 3; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae hodiernis acad. moribus accommodata_, VIII p. 25 sq.; P. Dens, _Theologia ad usum seminariorum_ II 275 sq.
Roman Catholic theologians therefore generally incorporate all these apologetic arguments into dogmatics and treat them at length, Perrone, _Prael. Theol._ I; Jansen, _Prael. Theol._ I 117 sq.; Hake, _Handbuch der allgem. Religionswissenschaft_, Freiburg, 1887, II 1 f.; Heinrich, _Dogm._ I 279 f.; Liebermann, _Dogm._ I p. 33 sq., and so forth.
Some even went so far as to regard these proofs as sufficient for the unbeliever too, Billuart, I p. 28, 29; Dens, II 292.
But most acknowledged that all these proofs were only motives and did not constitute the final and deepest ground of faith.
That could only be the authority of God, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 2 art. 1 ad 3 and art. 10; _S. c. Gent._ I c. 9; Billuart, VIII p. 1 sq.; Becanus, ib., p. 3-17; Dens, II 280 sq.; Jansen, _Prael. Theol._ I 701-706.
And that faith in revelation on the ground of God’s authority is brought about not by those proofs but by an _auxilium dei_, an _instinctus interior_, which moves the will to faith, Thomas II 1 qu. 109 art. 6, qu. 112 art. 2 and 3, qu. 113 art. 4.
The _Conc. Vatic._, sess. III, cap. 3, _de fide_, likewise acknowledged, on the one hand, that faith was a _virtus supernaturalis, qua Dei aspirante et adjuvante gratia ab eo revelata vera esse credimus, non propter intrinsecam rerum veritatem naturali rationis lumine perspectam, sed propter auctoritatem ipsius Dei revelantis_.
But on the other hand, it nevertheless attached great value to the apologetic arguments and therefore immediately added: _ut nihilominus fidei nostrae obsequium rationi consentaneum esset, voluit Deus cum internis Spiritus Sancti auxiliis externa jungi revelationis suae argumenta..... quae.... divinae revelationis signa sunt certissima et omnium intelligentiae accommodata_.
In canon 3, 3, it even condemned anyone who said: _revelationem divinam externis signis credibilem fieri non posse, ideoque sola interna cujusque experientia aut inspiratione privata homines ad fidem moveri debere_.
This high estimation of apologetics at Rome is again connected with the entire system.
Supernatural revelation is erected upon the foundation of natural revelation.
The former is reached only successively and by stages.
The human being _in puris naturalibus_ first arrives through proofs at the _theologia naturalis_.
This is _praeambula fidei_.
Here even science is possible.
The proofs are conclusive.
At this point faith itself is not yet in view.
Whoever has come this far and stands upon the foundation of the _theol. naturalis_ can then, through the _motiva credibilitatis_, and especially through the _signa_ and _notae ecclesiae_, perceive the credibility of revelation and acknowledge the reasonableness of believing.
And when the _fides humana_ has thus been obtained and the human being has prepared himself through the _actus praeparatorii_, he is admitted into the supernatural order through the _gratia infusa_ itself and through good works prepares himself in turn for heaven, for the _visio Dei_.
The human being ascends from the state of nature by stages.
At each stage he stands one degree higher.
The _pura naturalia, theol. naturalis, motiva credibilitatis, actus praeparatorii, gratia infusa, bona opera, visio Dei_ form the different rungs of the ladder that stands upon earth and reaches into heaven.
