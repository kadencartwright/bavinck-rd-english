## 4.

This speculative method had important advantages over the apologetic method of the rationalistic age. The clarity that in the previous century had been regarded as the standard of truth had changed revelation into a doctrine, the church into a school, regeneration into moral improvement, and the crucified Christ into the sage of Nazareth. Rationalism had falsified the entire Christian religion. The cultivated turned away with contempt from revelation and religion, from the congregation and faith. It took courage to return, as Schleiermacher and Hegel did, to the congregation and its dogmas and to discover in them, even if only in a certain sense, profound religious truth. It was a demonstration of moral strength to break with the rationalistic demand for clarity, to champion the despised religion of the congregation, and once again to affirm the right and value of the Christian faith. More than that, a glorious truth lay in the point of departure of Hegel and Schleiermacher. Thought and being are most intimately related and correspond to one another. Rationalism tried to justify religion before the incompetent tribunal of common sense. But Hegel and Schleiermacher both recognized that religion occupies a place of its own in human life, that it constitutes an independent reality and therefore also requires its own corresponding organ in human nature. Hegel and Schleiermacher differed from one another in identifying that organ: the one sought it in reason, the other in feeling. But both rose above vulgar rationalism, and both pointed to the harmony of subject and object. If by their subjective point of departure they had meant nothing more than this, their victory over rationalism would have deserved only assent. For the objective does not exist for us except insofar as it enters our consciousness. It can be approached in no other way than through consciousness. And religion is therefore no reality for me except insofar as I have received it into feeling or reason, or whatever its organ in the human being may be. But Hegel and Schleiermacher were not content with the proposition that thought and being correspond to one another; they identified the two. This identification of thought and being is the _πρωτον ψευδος_ of speculative philosophy. Plato already proceeded from it when he regarded the ideas as the true world. Descartes adopted it in his _cogito ergo sum_. Spinoza spoke in the same sense of a _causa sui, cujus essentia involvit existentiam_. Fichte brought it to dominance in modern philosophy. The great question here is this: do we think something because it exists, or does something exist because we must necessarily and logically think it? Speculative philosophy answered the latter. But however great the correspondence between thought and being may be, there is a no less essential distinction. No conclusion can be drawn from thought to being, since the being of all creatures is not an emanation of thought but rests upon an act of power. The essence of things is due to thought, but their existence to the will of God alone. Human thought therefore presupposes being; it first rises upon the foundation of the created; we can only think after what has been thought before us and comes to our consciousness through the world. If, however, with modern philosophy one rejects all material that has come to us from without and takes pure reason or abstract feeling as one’s point of departure, one is left with nothing, or at most with a principle so general, devoid of content, and vague that nothing—let alone the whole world or the entire Christian revelation and religion—can be deduced from it; cf. above, p. 148 f.

Hegel’s philosophy was therefore not as innocent as it could originally appear. It was the elaboration and application of Fichte’s proposition that the ego posits the non-ego, that the subject creates the object. Schleiermacher returned to this principle in theology because all authority in religion had fallen away for him, the rational and historical proofs for Christianity did not satisfy him, and, in his opinion, God too was unknowable to reason. Just as Kant wished to restore through practical reason what he had lost through the critique of pure reason, so Schleiermacher too saw no way to save religion except by proceeding from the religious subject, from feeling, from consciousness. It followed that dogmatics could be nothing other than a description of states of mind and thus properly belonged in historical theology. Theology became anthropology, pisteology, ecclesiology, and ceased to be what it had always claimed to be: the knowledge of God. Yet Schleiermacher could not stop there; in religion too our concern is not reality but truth. The justification of Christianity was therefore entrusted to philosophy in the first part of the encyclopedia. Since there was no longer any other ground on which the Christian faith rested, philosophy received the task of maintaining religion in its right and value. The _Vermittelungstheologie_ adopted Schleiermacher’s subjective point of departure, followed the path he had traced for the defense of religious truth, and thus naturally entered into an alliance with Hegel’s dialectical, speculative method. It could not be satisfied with empirical knowledge of the content of the Christian consciousness. Such knowledge was, after all, not science. Not only did the fact of faith have to be established; the right and truth of faith also had to be demonstrated. And since no other proof was available, recourse was taken to speculation. The speculative theology that arose after Schleiermacher strove for a higher knowledge of Christianity than one that rested upon authority and was obtained through faith. It was a renewal of ancient Gnosticism. Christian dogmas such as the Trinity, the incarnation, and satisfaction were not merely to be confessed as articles of faith but also to be seen and understood in their necessity. The “that” is not enough; the “how” and “why” must also be comprehended. The speculative _Vermittelungstheologie_ therefore sought to escape the lower standpoint of authority and strove to make Christianity, as absolute truth, rest in itself. It did indeed proceed from faith, but it set knowledge as its goal. Necessity of thought was its proof of truth.

That this method would lead to no desirable result in either philosophy or theology could be foreseen and has been strikingly demonstrated by history. Refutation is almost superfluous. Speculation had its day long ago. Hegel’s philosophy led Feuerbach and Strauss to reject the whole Christian faith. The philosophical reworking of orthodox dogmatics by Schweizer, Scholten, and Biedermann concealed the dogmatic poverty of modern theology for only a short time. The more orthodox _Vermittelungstheologie_ can rightly boast of the works of Rothe, Dorner, Lange, Martensen, Müller, and others, which are full of profound and beautiful thoughts; yet it did not at all fulfill the expectation it had aroused. It did not succeed in changing the foolishness of the cross into the wisdom of the world; through its profound reflections it failed to win the children of this age back to Christ. On the contrary, mediation resulted in an even more radical separation of faith and knowledge, of theology and philosophy, of the congregation and the world. The speculation pursued by one part of the _Vermittelungstheologie_ also proceeded from the incorrect assumption that Christianity was a logical system of thought from whose first member all the following members could be derived by thought and reasoning. But if the being of things in general already rests not upon thought but upon will, and if history, however much it may be the execution of a counsel of God, is nevertheless something essentially different from an arithmetic problem, then still more is the Christian religion distinct from a logical system of thought. For Christianity is history; it is a history of grace, and grace is something different from and more than a logical conclusion. In the Christian religion, therefore, even the most profound thinker never rises above the childlike standpoint of authority and faith.
