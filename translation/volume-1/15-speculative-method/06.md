## 6.

For Frank, all this is rooted in a peculiar _Erkenntnisstheorie_.
He goes a considerable way with the idealism of modern philosophy, I 58 f.
The object as object, that is, for the subject, is present only through this _Setzung durch das Subject_, ibid. 61.
Frank does indeed acknowledge the reality of the objective world, although not in an empiricist and sensualist sense, ibid. 59, 60.
But our knowledge never deals with the _Ding an und für sich_, but always with the _Ding für uns_.
We ascribe existence in itself to the object because we find ourselves compelled to posit the object in this way and no other.
Both tendencies, Empiricism as well as Idealism, do this; they differ only in the manner in which they do it, ibid. 61.
Certainty is therefore always certainty concerning an object.
It consists precisely in _des Objectes inne zu sein als der Wahrheit_, ibid. 63.
Through this reasoning we advance no further than the conclusion that our mind is so constituted that it must assume the objective reality of the objects concerning which it is certain; whether the human mind does this rightly and is not suffering from hallucination is no question for Frank.
The necessity of the _Setzung_ is for him the ultimate ground of objective reality.
Certainty is for him—not essentially but _erkenntnisstheoretisch_—the guarantee of truth.
Reality is indeed the ontological ground of certainty, but certainty is the epistemological ground of reality.

Frank also applies this _Erkenntnisstheorie_ to the Christian faith.
The objective truths and facts of Christianity do precede faith essentially and causally, but in an _erkenntnisstheoretischen_ sense they follow it.
Just as in philosophy he takes the self-consciousness of the human being as his point of departure, so in theology he proceeds from the self-consciousness of the Christian, from the experience of regeneration.
But there are many objections to this point of departure and this method.
a) This regeneration of the Christian, and likewise all his other spiritual experiences, including even his certainty, did not arise spontaneously within the Christian but from the beginning and continually stand in connection with the objective factors of Scripture, church, and so forth.
Frank himself repeatedly acknowledges this, I 123, 124, 191, etc.; but he then wrongly detaches spiritual experience _a priori_ from those objective factors in order to set it on its own and make it rest in itself.
Frank’s point of departure, namely, the Christian’s certainty, is a pure abstraction; from the beginning and continuously, that certainty rests upon the objective factors of salvation that come to the believer from without.
b) By acknowledging, as noted above in the _System der chr. Gew._ II 281 f., that the order now reverses, Frank himself admits that regeneration offers no sufficient certainty for the objective truth of the Christian faith.
For if the objective, causal order is in fact as Frank presents it in his _System der christlichen Wahrheit_, namely, such that the objective precedes, then this must also be the order of the entire system.
The system must be an imprint of the order of being, not of the manner in which someone arrives at knowledge and certainty of the objective truth.
For this manner varies so greatly that it is incapable of systematic description; cf. above, p. 50.
c) The method by which Frank constructs the objective _dogmata_ from the Christian’s certainty is one that does not fit within the Christian religion and theology.
It is borrowed from speculative philosophy.
Just as speculative philosophy took its point of departure in a general, vague principle abstracted from everything, so the Christian’s self-consciousness detached from all objective factors—his certainty _an sich_—is for Frank the _cogito ergo sum_, the δος μοι που στω.
From this he first infers the immanent truths of faith.
Next he calls the method of natural science to his aid and reasons regressively from the effect to the cause, seeking to explain the Christian’s new life entirely according to the empirical method, I 39.
Just as the natural scientist seeks by spectral analysis to learn the chemical constituents of the sun, so Frank attempts by analysis to reduce the life of regeneration to its objective factors, I 315.
The Christian, reflecting on his spiritual life, can explain it in no other way than by assuming that God is triune, that Christ became incarnate and made satisfaction, and so forth.
d) This method also conflicts with all Christian experience.
No Christian ever arrived in this way at certainty concerning objective truth.
It bypasses reality entirely.
Moreover, it is impractical, for in doubt and unbelief the Christian lacks precisely that certainty which, according to Frank, can alone guarantee him the objective truth of his faith.
At such times he needs precisely an objective word, an objective deed, that sustains him, to which he can cling, and by which he can raise himself again from the depths of doubt and temptation.
e) Finally, various other objections can be advanced against Frank’s system.
Thus, in Frank, the transition from natural to spiritual knowing, and likewise the connection between the two, remains obscure.
The distinction of two egos in the regenerate person is susceptible to all manner of misunderstanding; in regeneration no new ego is created in the human being, but the ego of the psychical man is renewed.
The division of dogmatics into a system of certainty and a system of truth cannot be maintained, since the Christian’s certainty cannot be described without the truth to which it relates.
But the foregoing is enough to show that the charge of subjectivism, even if vague, has not been brought entirely without justification against Frank’s theology, just as it has against Ritschl’s.
