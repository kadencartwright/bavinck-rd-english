# § 15. The Speculative Method

## 1.

Supernaturalism fell under the blows of Rousseau and Kant, of Lessing and Schleiermacher. A mighty reversal followed. In every domain classicism yielded to romanticism, to the dominion and autonomy of the subject. At first, amid the sense of freedom, this reaction went so far that it effaced everything objective and the subject regarded itself as sufficient. If the subject did not produce the matter of the world (Fichte), it nevertheless produced its form (Kant). The non-ego is a product of the ego, the moral world order is brought about by man himself, and the moral law is freely and royally set aside by genius. Schleiermacher too initially adopted this standpoint. But this absolute idealism led to all manner of dreadful consequences. The French Revolution demonstrated the danger of this human autonomy. There nevertheless had to be something objective that stood firm and possessed authority. Thus came the restoration, that is, the attempt, while preserving the same point of departure, nevertheless to return from the subject to the objective. Hegel was the philosophical interpreter of that restoration. He elevated Fichte’s subjective, ethical idealism into an objective, logical idealism and replaced the idea of being with that of becoming. The whole world became a process, a development of the logical idea. Religion too has its place in this evolution. But that religion clothes itself in forms and symbols whose profound meaning can be understood only by speculative reason. Rationalism understood nothing of this and simply set aside the dogmas of the church, not knowing what to do with them. But those dogmas are full of deep philosophical meaning. Hegel’s spirit turned to those dogmas, stripped them of their historical, symbolic forms, and traced the idea within them. History is only the shell, the covering; the kernel itself is deep, true philosophy. Not the rationalistic doctrines of God, virtue, and immortality, but the highest and deepest dogmas of Christianity—such as the Trinity, the incarnation, and the atonement—became the objects of bold philosophical speculation. Apart from Scripture and apart from any authority, these dogmas were deduced as necessary from reason and proved to be supremely rational. Theology and philosophy were apparently reconciled; faith was transformed by speculative reason into absolute knowledge. This speculative method was adopted and applied to dogmatics, though with widely varying results, by Daub, Marheineke, Strauss, Feuerbach, Vatke, Weisse, and most recently Biedermann. Biedermann departs from Hegel on important points and does not accept his _a priori_ method; but, taking the Christian dogmas as his point of departure, he nevertheless tries in the same manner as Hegel to analyze them into the religious principle underlying them and the historical expression they have assumed, and then to develop them further speculatively and practically. _Christl. Dogm._, 2e Aufl., I 15, 16. Likewise, in this country Scholten attempted to distinguish between the clothing and the idea in the Reformed dogmas and to interpret them in a monistic and deterministic sense.
