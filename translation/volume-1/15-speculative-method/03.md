## 3.

Even outside the circle of those theologians who in the narrower sense align themselves with Hegel and Schleiermacher, this method found acceptance. Hofmann does not conceive dogmatics as a description of states of mind, nor as a reproduction of the doctrine of Scripture or church, nor again as a development of Christian knowledge from a general principle, but like Schleiermacher he nevertheless proceeds from Christian piety. Dogmatics is the unfolding of that which makes the Christian a Christian, of the personal fellowship of God with man mediated by Christ. _Die Erkenntniss und Aussage des Christenthums ist vor allem Selbsterkenntniss und Selbstaussage des Christen_, _Schriftbeweis_, 2e Aufl., I, pp. 5–16. Philippi, _Kirchl. Glaub._ I 52 f., 108 f.; Kahnis, _Luther. Dogm._ I 7, 8, have this same subjective starting point. Ebrard tries in dogmatics to demonstrate the truth and necessity of the _Erlösungsthatsachen_ by comparing them at every point with the scientifically developed _Erlösungsbedürfniss_, _Dogm._ §52, and so forth. Frank especially adopted and elaborated this thought in his _System der Christl. Gewissheit_, 2e Aufl., 1884. Hofmann took his standpoint in the _Thatbestand_ of God’s fellowship with man. This rests in and carries its certainty within itself, and Scripture bears witness to it. Here, therefore, there was no place for a system of Christian certainty. The Christian is conscious of himself, and with that everything is said. But Frank does not stop here; he tries to give an account of why the Christian believes all that his faith contains. He therefore constructs a _System der christlichen Gewissheit_. Theology, he says, in distinction from philosophy, I 26 f., must take its place not outside but in the midst of Christian consciousness, and from there survey and judge everything, including the natural, pp. 31, 32, 119, 137. For Christian certainty has its ground and essence not in all manner of external proofs, nor in an external authority, but in the Christian himself, in his moral experience and self-determination. This particular moral experience is regeneration and conversion, p. 113. The Christian knows that a change has taken place and continues to take place within him, so that two different directions, two different selves, now dwell in him, p. 120 f.; and he is as certain of this as a sick person who has recovered is conscious of his former illness and his present health, p. 129. From this experience of regeneration he now naturally and immediately posits the entire content of Christian truth; he does so by virtue of the nature of the new life bestowed on him and can no more divest himself of this truth of faith than he can of himself, p. 193. Christian truth groups itself in three circles around the experience of regeneration. First, by virtue of his new life, the Christian posits those truths of faith that are given directly and immediately with the fact of regeneration, namely the reality of sin, righteousness, and future perfection. This is the sphere of the central, immanent truths, which still belong entirely to the Christian’s _self_-consciousness. Around this a second circle of truths forms: the believer can explain this new state only by the reality of the personal God, the existence of God as triune, and the reconciliation accomplished by the God-man. These three together form the group of transcendent truths and identify the factors that brought about this moral change of regeneration in the Christian. Finally, around these a third circle of truths forms, namely the transeunt truths, which identify the means by which the transcendent factors named above effect the experience of salvation in the Christian; these are church, Word of God, Scripture, sacrament, miracles, revelation, and inspiration. Finally, Christian certainty also entails a particular relation of the regenerate person to natural life, to world and humanity, p. 191 f.
