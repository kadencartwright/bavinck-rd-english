## 5.

The theology of Dr. Fr. H. R. Frank, professor at Erlangen, † February 1894, deserves separate discussion. Frank opposes as strongly as possible the so-called prolegomena of dogmatics, _System der christl. Gewissheit_, 2e Aufl., 1884, I 36 f., and gives in their place a _System der christlichen Gewissheit_. This title, however, is not particularly clear. Certitude, certainty, is a state of the soul and as such excludes a system. If certainty is here taken, however, for the objects of faith concerning which the Christian is certain, then _System der christl. Gewissheit_ is equivalent to _System der christl. Wahrheit_. That cannot be the intention, for Frank gave precisely this title to his second major work. In his _System der christl. Gewissheit_ I 48 he explains himself more precisely and says that his system has as its object the _Gewissheit_ insofar as it extends to the _zu verbürgenden Wahrheitsgehalt_, that is, certainty as a psychological state together with all that it assures, insofar as it assures it. The system of Christian certainty consequently amounts to a formal schema of the different kinds and degrees of certainty that flow from Christian self-consciousness with respect to the objects of faith. It is not Frank’s intention, however, that the objects of Christian faith would not previously exist and be known. He expressly declares the contrary, _Gew._ I 313. Nor does he deny that Christian certainty arises through the Word, _Dogmatische Studien_, Erlangen and Leipzig, 1892, p. 56. He does not wish to derive those objective truths from the regenerated subject, ibid., pp. 68, 69. More than this: when he has derived from Christian self-consciousness all that follows from it, and has developed and completed the whole system of Christian certainty, he says in clear words that a reversal now follows and that the last becomes the first. From and through his own certainty, the Christian has found the objective ground on which he rests with his life of faith, namely the grace of God in Christ, and from there he now constructs the system of Christian truth, _Syst. der chr. Gew._ II 281 f. Frank’s intention is only to answer the question: how does a person come to rely on those objective factors of salvation—God, Christ, Holy Scripture, and so forth—and to accept Scripture unconditionally as God’s Word? _S. der chr. Gew._ II 285 f.; _Dogm. Stud._ 56. Thus in the system of Christian certainty Frank develops not so much the grounds of faith as the paths by which a person arrives at certainty concerning the Christian truths of faith. And in general Frank now says that a person obtains this certainty not through historical or rational proofs, nor through authority, church, tradition, and so forth, but solely through the experience of regeneration, _S. d. chr. Gew._ I 314.

The question Frank set himself to answer is therefore of the highest importance; it is also necessary and proper, for Christian certainty does not speak for itself and is often subject to doubt. The answer Frank gives to that question is also correct to a certain extent. Just as the eye is necessary for beholding light, so regeneration is necessary for seeing the kingdom of heaven. But doubt may arise whether Frank’s _System der christlichen Gewissheit_ fulfills this good intention of his. And there is ground for this doubt. For the entire system as such conflicts with that stated intention. If Frank had wished to do nothing but describe how the believer arrives at certainty, he would only have brought the origin and nature of that certainty to light and would have ended there. Just as _Erkenntnistheorie_ develops only the grounds upon which belief in the external world rests but does not derive every object in that world from this certainty, so the system of Christian certainty should have contented itself with identifying the grounds of certainty and should not have discussed its content. But then no system in the proper sense of the various objects to which certainty relates would have been possible. Frank, however, does much more: he gives a system. He successively derives all the truths of faith from regeneration—not in a temporal but nevertheless in a logical, causal sense, _Gew._ I 47, II 290. He has the Christian advance gradually from the experience of regeneration to all Christian dogmas “as though” he previously knew nothing of them and had learned nothing of them by another way. Frank does this with all dogmas: sin, guilt, God, Trinity, incarnation, resurrection, and so forth. We receive the impression that, apart from Scripture and church, the Christian can derive all these truths from his regeneration. Frank continually confuses the _causa essendi_ and the _causa cognoscendi_, the objective ground of the truth of faith and the subjective path by which someone arrives at certainty of it. He interchanges and identifies objective truth and subjective certainty. He repeatedly expresses himself as though, by virtue of his spiritual experience, the regenerate person simply posits the objective truths of faith as realities, _Gew._ I 94, 193. He speaks of an _Autonomie des christlichen Subjektes als Garanten der Wahrheit_, I 151.
