# § 12. The Properties of Scripture

### A. The Properties of Scripture in General

## 1.

The doctrine of the _affectiones S. Scr._ developed entirely out of the struggle against Rome and Anabaptism. There was agreement in the confession of the inspiration and authority of Scripture, but otherwise there was a great difference between Rome and the Reformation in the _locus de S. Scr._ The relation in which Rome had placed Scripture and church was changed in principle in the Reformation. Among the church fathers and Scholastics, Scripture still stood, at least in theory, far above church and tradition; it rested in itself, was αὐτοπιστος, and was the _norma normans_ for church and theology. Augustine said, _scriptura canonica certis suis terminis continetur_, in Harnack, _D. G._ II 85, and reasons in _Conf._ 6, 5; 11, 3, as though the truth of Holy Scripture depended upon itself alone. Bonaventure, _de sept. don._ n. 37-43, cited in the _Breviloquium_, ed. Freiburg 1881 p. 370, declares: _ecclesia enim fundata est super eloquia Sacrae Scripturae, quae si deficiant, deficit intellectus........ Cum enim ecclesia fundata sit in Sacra Scriptura, qui nescit eam, nescit ecclesiam regere._ More testimonies of this kind are cited by Gerhard, _Loci theol._ I cap. 3 §§ 45, 46, from Salvian, Biel, Cajetan, Hosius, Valentia, and others. Canisius says in his _Summa doctrinae christ._, in cap. _de praeceptis ecclesiae_ § 16: _Proinde sicut scripturae propter testimonium Divini Spiritus in illa loquentis credimus, adhaeremus ac tribuimus maximam auctoritatem, sic ecclesiae fidem, reverentiam obedientiamque debemus._ And Bellarmine too, _de Verbo Dei_, lib. 1 cap. 2, still declares: _Sacris Scripturis, quae propheticis et apostolicis litteris continentur, nihil est notius, nihil certius, ut stultissimum esse necesse sit qui illis fidem habendam esse negat._ All were of the opinion that Scripture could sufficiently prove itself to be the truth from and through itself; it does not depend on the church, but conversely the church depends on it; the church with its tradition may be _regula fidei_, but it is not yet _fundamentum fidei_. Scripture alone is that.

But at Rome the church with its office and tradition began increasingly to occupy an independent place and to acquire authority alongside Holy Scripture. At first the relation between the two was not defined more precisely, but it nevertheless soon demanded better regulation. And as the church continually increased in power and self-sufficiency, authority was increasingly transferred from Scripture to the church. Various moments in history indicate the process by which the church raised itself from the place below Scripture to the place alongside it and finally also to the place above it. The question which of the two, Scripture or church, had priority was first posed clearly and consciously in the time of the reforming councils. Despite the opposition of Gerson, d’Ailly, and especially Nicholas of Clémange, _Herz._² 3:247, it was decided in favor of the church. Trent sanctioned this against the Reformation. In the struggle against Gallicanism, the question was defined still more precisely, and at the Vatican Council in 1870 it was resolved by declaring the church infallible. The subject of this infallibility, however, is not the _ecclesia audiens_, nor the _ecclesia docens_, nor even the bishops together, assembled in council, but specifically the pope. And he in turn is infallible not as a private person, nor as bishop of Rome or patriarch of the West, but as Supreme Shepherd of the whole church. He does possess this infallibility as head of the church and not apart from it, but he nevertheless possesses it not through and with the church but above and in distinction from it. Even bishops and councils participate in infallibility not apart from the pope but only in unity with and submission to him. He stands above all and alone makes the church, tradition, councils, and canons infallible. Councils without the pope can err and have erred, Bellarmine, _de Conc. et Eccl._ II c. 10-11. The whole church, both _docens_ and _audiens_, is infallible only _una cum et sub Romano pontifice_, Jansen, _Theol._ I 506. With this, the entire relation of church and Scripture is reversed. The church, or more concretely the pope, precedes and stands above Scripture. _Ubi papa, ibi ecclesia_, Jansen, I 511. The infallibility of the pope makes that of the church, the bishops and councils, and likewise that of Scripture unnecessary.
