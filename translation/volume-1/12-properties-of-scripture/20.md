## 20.

The development of tradition into papal infallibility, and the degradation of Scripture that necessarily follows from it, already prove in themselves the Reformation’s right to oppose tradition. It did not, however, stop at attack, but set the doctrine of the _perfectio_ or _sufficientia Scripturae_ over against that of Rome. Luther in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._, 2e Ausg., 1883, II 56 f., 246 f.; Gerhard, _Loci theol._, loc. I, capp. 18, 19; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, §9; Calvin, _Inst._ IV, c. 10; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ I, capp. 46, 47; Zanchius, _Op._ VIII, col. 369 sq.; Ursinus, _Tract. Theol._, 1584, pp. 8 sq., 22 sq.; Chamier, _Panstratia Cathol._, loc. 1, lib. 8 and 9; Amesius, _Bellarminus enervatus_, lib. I, c. 6; Turretin, _Theol. El._, loc. 2, qu. 16; Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._, p. 11 f.; Holtzmann, _Kanon und Tradition_, Ludwigsburg, 1859; A. W. Dieckhoff, _Schrift und Tradition_, Rostock, 1870; J. L. Jacobi, _Die kirchl. Lehre von der Tradition u. h. Schrift_, 1 Abth., Berlin, 1847; P. Tschackert, _Evang. Polemik gegen die röm. Kirche_, Gotha, 1885, §23 f.; idem, art. “Tradition” in Herzog²; Hase, _Protest. Polemik_, 5te Aufl., Leipzig, 1891, p. 77 f.; Harnack, _Dogm. Gesch._ III 593 f., 623 f.; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 104, etc. This property of Holy Scripture must likewise be properly understood. It does not assert that everything spoken or written by the prophets, by Christ, and by the apostles has been included in Scripture; for many prophetic and apostolic writings have been lost, Num. 21:14; Josh. 10:13; 1 Kings 4:33; 1 Chron. 29:29; 2 Chron. 9:29; 12:15; 1 Cor. 5:9; Col. 4:16; Phil. 3:1, and Jesus and the apostles certainly spoke many more words and performed many more signs than have been recorded, John 20:30; 1 Cor. 11:2, 14; 2 Thess. 2:5, 15; 3:6, 10; 2 John 12; 3 John 14, etc. Nor does this property mean that Holy Scripture contains all the customs, ceremonies, provisions, and regulations that the church needs for her organization; it means only that it contains the _fidei articuli_ completely, the _res necessariae ad salutem_. And even then this property of Scripture does not imply that these _articuli fidei_ are contained in it literally and in so many words, _αὐτολεξει_ and _totidem verbis_, but only that they are contained in Scripture either _explicite_ or _implicite_ in such a way that, without the aid of another source, they can be derived from it by comparative investigation and reflection alone. Finally, this _perfectio S. Scr._ must not be understood as though Holy Scripture had always been the same _quoad gradum_. In the different periods of the church, Scripture was unequal in extent until its completion. But in every period the Word of God that existed, whether unwritten or written, was sufficient for that period. The Reformation too distinguished between a _verbum ἀγραφον_ and _ἐγγραφον_, Belgic Confession, art. 3. But Rome accepts the two alongside each other and regards them as species of one genus; in this distinction the Reformation sees only one and the same Word of God, which first existed unwritten for a time and afterward was recorded. The dispute between Rome and the Reformation therefore concerns only this: whether now, after Scripture has been completed, another Word of God still exists alongside it in unwritten form—in other words, whether the written Word of God contains _explicite_ or _implicite_ everything we need to know for our salvation and is thus the _regula totalis et adaequata fidei et morum_, or whether another _principium cognoscendi_ alongside it must be assumed in religion and theology. But when put this way, the question scarcely seems capable of two different answers. The Roman Catholic Church too acknowledges that Scripture is complete, that it forms an organic whole, and that the canon is closed. However highly it esteemed tradition, it has not yet dared in theory to place the decisions of the church on one level with Scripture. It still distinguishes between _verbum Dei_ and _verbum ecclesiae_. But how can the insufficiency of Scripture ever be taught so long as the _verbum Dei_ is taken seriously? The church fathers never thought of doing so and plainly affirmed the complete sufficiency of Holy Scripture. Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ III, praef. and cap. 1, says that we know the truth through the apostles, _per quos Evangelium pervenit ad nos, quod quidem tunc praeconaverunt, postea vero per Dei voluntatem in Scripturis nobis tradiderunt fundamentum et columnam fidei nostrae futuram_. Tertullian, _Adv. Hermog._, c. 22, and _De carne Chr._, c. 8, admires the _plenitudo Scripturae_ and rejects everything _quod extra Scripturam_ is. Augustine, _Sermo de Past._, c. 11, testifies: _quidquid inde audieritis, hoc vobis bene sapiat; quidquid extra est respuite_. And many others speak in the same way; cf. the passages collected by Chamier, _Panstratia Cathol._, loc. I, lib. 8, cap. 10. They certainly also acknowledge tradition alongside Scripture, but in doing so they admit an element that undermines their conviction concerning Scripture’s sufficiency and culminates in the later Roman doctrine of the _insufficientia S. Scr._ and the _sufficientia traditionis_. Scripture and tradition cannot both be maintained alongside each other; what is withheld from the one is bestowed on the other. Tradition can rise only when and insofar as Scripture falls. It is therefore exceedingly strange that Rome, on the one hand, holds Scripture to be complete and the canon closed, and even acknowledges Scripture as the Word of God, yet on the other hand regards that Scripture as insufficient and supplements it with tradition. Many Roman Catholic theologians today rightly say that Scripture is the unnecessary and at most useful supplement to tradition.
