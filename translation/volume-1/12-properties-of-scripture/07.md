## 7.

But although religion can be satisfied only with an _auctoritas divina_, the nature of that authority must still be investigated more closely. Authority in general is the power of someone who has something to say, the right to speak in some matter—hence in Middle Dutch, force, power, _Woordenboek der Ned. Taal_, s.v. Authority can be spoken of only between unequals; it always expresses a relation of a superior to his inferior, of a higher to a lower. Because there is no equality among human beings but all manner of distinction, authority can be spoken of among them. And since this inequality is so great and so manifold, authority occupies a very broad place among human beings. It is even the foundation of all human society. Whoever undermines it labors at the destruction of society.

It is therefore foolish and dangerous to cast belief on authority in a ridiculous light. Augustine already asked: _Si quod nescitur credendum non est, quomodo serviant parentibus liberi eosque mutua pietate diligant, quos parentes suos esse non credant....... Multa possunt afferri quibus ostendatur, nihil omnino humanae societatis incolume remanere, si nihil credere statuerimus, quod non possumus tenere perceptum_, _De util. cred._ 12.

In every sphere we live by authority. Under authority we are born and raised in family, society, and state. Parents have authority over their children, the teacher over his pupils, the government over its subjects. In all these cases authority is clear. It expresses a power that rightfully belongs to someone over another. It therefore appears with commands and laws, demands obedience and submission, and in case of rebellion even has the right of compulsion and punishment.

But we extend this concept of authority further and apply it also in science and art. Here too there is difference of gift, and there arises the relation of superiors and inferiors, of _magistri_ and _discipuli_. There are men who through their genial disposition and diligent labor have acquired mastery in some field and can therefore speak with authority in that domain. The inferiors, the laypeople, live and learn from the discoveries of these _magistri_. Indeed, because of the enormous expansion of science, even the most excellent person can be _magister_ only in a very small field; in all else he is _discipulus_ and must trust the investigation of others.

This authority in science and art, however, bears another character than that of parents, teachers, and government. It is not juridical but ethical in nature; it cannot and may not compel, and has no right of punishment. However distinguished and weighty the persons who appear here with authority may be, their testimony counts only for as much as the grounds they can advance for it. Authority here therefore rests ultimately not in the persons, so that an _ipse dixit_ would be decisive, but in the proofs upon which their claim rests. And since all human beings have received some understanding and judgment, blind faith is impermissible here, while the pursuit of independent insight, so far as necessary and possible, is a duty.

This is also the case in history. Knowledge of history rests properly and entirely upon authority, upon the testimonies of others. But these testimonies need not be believed blindly; they may and must be seriously investigated, so that one’s own insight may come into its own as far as possible. In a word, _in scientiis tantum valet auctoritas humana, quantum rationes_.

Finally, we find this concept of authority again in religion and theology. Here authority is needed not to a lesser degree but to a much higher degree than in family and society, in science and art. Here it is a necessity of life. Without authority and faith, religion and theology cannot exist for a moment. But authority here bears a wholly distinctive character. By its nature it must be an _auctoritas divina_. By this alone it already differs from authority in society and state, in science and art.

It differs from the latter especially in this respect: in science and art one’s own insight may judge and decide. But with a _divina auctoritas_ this is out of place. When God has spoken, all doubt is at an end. The _divina auctoritas_ should therefore not be called ethical, at least not in the sense in which we speak of the moral ascendancy of a person. For religion is not a relation of an inferior to his superior, but of a creature to his Creator, of a subject to his Sovereign, of a child to his Father. God has the right to command man and demand unconditional obedience from him. His authority rests in his being, not in the _rationes_. To this extent the authority of God and his Word corresponds to that of the government in the state and of the father in the family.

There is nothing degrading in it and nothing that in any way detracts from human freedom when man listens as a child to the Word of God and obeys it. To believe God on his Word—that is, on authority—conflicts with human dignity as little as it dishonors a child to rely with unconditional trust upon the word of his father. So far is the Christian from gradually outgrowing this authority, Schweizer, _Christl. Glaub._ I 186 f., that increasingly, with denial of all wisdom of his own, he comes to believe God on his Word. Here on earth the believer never rises above the standpoint of faith and authority. As he increases in faith, he clings all the more firmly to the authority of God in his Word.

On the other hand, there is nevertheless also a great difference between God’s authority in religion and that of a father in his family and a government in the state. A father compels his child if necessary and brings it to submission through punishment; and the government does not bear the sword in vain. Compulsion is inseparable from the authority of earthly government. But God does not compel. His revelation is a revelation of grace. In it he comes to man not with commands and demands, compulsion and punishment, but with invitation, exhortation, and entreaty to be reconciled to him.

God could appear as Sovereign over against man. He will one day judge as Judge all who have been disobedient to the gospel of his Son. But in Christ he descends to us, becomes like us in all things, and deals with us as rational and moral beings—only then, when he encounters enmity and unbelief, to reassume his sovereignty, execute his counsel, and prepare glory for himself from every creature. The authority with which God appears in religion is therefore altogether unique. It is not human but divine. It is sovereign and yet works in an ethical manner. It does not compel and yet knows how to maintain itself. It is absolute and yet is resisted. It invites and entreats and yet is invincible.

Such too is the authority of Scripture. As the Word of God it stands far above all human authority in state and society, in science and art. All else must yield before it, for one must obey God rather than men. Every other authority is limited within its sphere and applies only in its own domain. But the authority of Scripture extends over the whole man and all humanity. It stands above understanding and will, above heart and conscience; it can be compared with no other authority. Its authority is absolute because it is divine. It has the right to be believed and obeyed by everyone at all times. In majesty it far surpasses every other power.

But to bring itself to acknowledgment and dominion, Scripture calls no one to its aid. It does not need the strong arm of the government. It does not require the support of the church. It does not call upon the sword and the Inquisition. It does not wish to rule by compulsion or force. It desires free and willing acknowledgment. And therefore it itself brings this about in an ethical manner through the operation of the Holy Spirit. Scripture watches over its own authority. Formerly people therefore also spoke of an _auctoritas causativa_ of Scripture, _qua Scriptura assensum credendorum in intellectu hominis generat et confirmat_, Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ § 8.
