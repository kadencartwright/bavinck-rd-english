## 5.

Alongside this difference between Rome and the Reformation concerning the ground of the authority of Scripture, an important controversy arose in the 17th century within the Protestant churches themselves concerning the nature of that authority. There was agreement that Scripture, since it had God as its author, possessed an _auctoritas divina_. More particularly, this authority was defined by saying that Scripture had to be believed and obeyed by all and was the sole rule of faith and life. This definition, however, naturally led to the distinction between an _auctoritas historica_ and an _auctoritas normativa_. For God’s revelation was given in the form of a history; it has passed through different periods. By no means everything recorded in Scripture has normative authority for our faith and life. Much of what God once commanded and instituted, or prophets and apostles prescribed and ordained, no longer concerns us directly but pertained to persons living in earlier times. The command to Abraham to sacrifice his son; the command to Israel to kill all the Canaanites; the ceremonial and civil laws that were in force in the days of the Old Testament; the provisions of the Jerusalem council; and so much more are certainly still useful as history for teaching and admonition, but may no longer be followed by us. And not only that: in its written account revelation has recorded not only the good works of the saints but also the evil deeds of the wicked. Thus words and deeds occur repeatedly in Scripture that are indeed presented as historically true but not as normative; so far from being permitted to serve as a rule for our faith and life, they must instead be rejected and disapproved. The sins of the saints as well—of Abraham, Moses, Job, Jeremiah, Peter, and so forth—are reported for warning, not for imitation. And finally, in the case of many persons—the patriarchs, Deborah, the judges, the kings, Job’s friends, Hannah, Agur, Lemuel’s mother, the poets of certain psalms, such as the imprecatory psalms, Ps. 73:13, 14; 77:7-9; 116:11, and further Zechariah, Simeon, Mary, Stephen, and so forth—the question may be asked whether their words were inspired only formally, as far as their recording is concerned, or also materially, as far as their content is concerned. Voetius held that many of these persons, such as Job and his friends, could not be numbered among the prophets, and he maintained this opinion against Maresius; Voetius, _Disp._ I 31, 40-44; V 634-640; Maresius, _Theologus paradoxus_, p. 83-87; and further Maccovius, _Loci Comm._, p. 31-32; Cloppenburg, _de canone theol._, disp. 3, _Op._ II 18-23; Witsius, _Misc. Sacra_ I 316-318; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 131-134; Carpzovius, _Critica S. Vet. Test._ I c. 2 § 3. This issue admittedly had no further consequences, but it was nevertheless important in many respects. It first brought clearly to consciousness the distinction between the word of God in a formal sense and the word of God in a material sense, and it compelled reflection on the relation in which the two stand to each other. Now this relation was certainly construed in an overly dualistic fashion by most of the theologians mentioned above. The _auctoritas historiae_ and the _historia normae_ cannot be separated in Scripture in such an abstract manner. The formal and material meanings of the expression “word of God” are far too closely connected. Even in Satan’s lying words and the evil deeds of the wicked, God has something to say to us. Scripture is useful not only for teaching but also for warning and admonition. It instructs and corrects us, both by deterring and by urging on, both by putting us to shame and by comforting us. Yet that distinction did make clear that Scripture cannot and may not be understood as a code of law filled with articles. An appeal to a text outside its context is not sufficient for a dogma. The revelation deposited in Scripture is a historical and organic whole. As such it wishes to be read and explained. Therefore dogma, which comes to us with authority and claims to be a rule for our faith and life, must be grounded in and derived from the whole organism of Scripture. The authority of Scripture is of a different kind from that of a law of the state.
