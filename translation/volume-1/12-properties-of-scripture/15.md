## 15.

Indeed, over against Rome the churches of the Reformation possess no more powerful weapon than Scripture. It deals the deadliest blows to ecclesiastical tradition and hierarchy. The doctrine of the _perspicuitas S. Scr._ is one of the strongest bulwarks of the Reformation. It certainly brings serious dangers with it. Protestantism has been hopelessly divided by it. Individualism has developed at the expense of communal feeling. The free reading and investigation of Scripture has been and continues to be most grievously misused by all manner of parties and tendencies.

Yet the disadvantages do not outweigh the advantages. For denial of the clarity of Scripture entails the subjection of the layperson to the priest, of conscience to the church. With the _perspicuitas S. Scr._ fall freedom of religion and conscience, of church and theology. It alone is capable of maintaining the freedom of the Christian person; it is the origin and guarantee of religious and also political freedoms. Stahl, _Der Protest. als polit. Princip._, 2e Aufl., 1853; Saussaye, _Het Protest. als politiek beginsel_, Rotterdam, 1871; Kuyper, _Het Calvinisme, oorsprong en waarborg onzer constitutioneele vrĳheden_, Amsterdam, 1874.

A freedom that can be obtained and possessed only with the danger of lawlessness and arbitrariness is still always preferable to a tyranny that suppresses all freedom. In creating man, God himself chose this way of freedom, which brought with it the danger and indeed the fact of sin, above the way of compelled submission. In the government of world and church he still always follows this royal way of freedom. It is precisely his honor that through freedom he nevertheless attains his goal, creates order from disorder, light from darkness, cosmos from chaos.

Rome and the Reformation agree in this: the Holy Spirit alone is the true interpreter of the Word, Matt. 7:15; 16:17; John 6:44; 10:3; 1 Cor. 2:12, 15; 10:15; Phil. 1:10; 3:13; Heb. 5:14; 1 John 4:1. But Rome thinks that the Holy Spirit teaches infallibly only through the pope. The Reformation believes that the Holy Spirit dwells in the heart of every believer; every child of God has the anointing of the Holy One. It therefore gives Scripture into everyone’s hand, translates and disseminates it, and uses no language other than the vernacular in the church.

Rome boasts of its unity, but this unity appears greater than it is. The division of the Reformation into Lutheran and Reformed has its analogy in the schism between the Greek and Latin churches. Under the appearance of external unity, Rome conceals an almost equally great internal division. The number of unbelievers and indifferent persons is no smaller in Roman Catholic countries than in Protestant ones. Rome has been no more capable of stemming the current of unbelief than the churches of the Reformation. Already before the Reformation unbelief had spread in wide circles, for example in Italy. The Reformation did not call it forth but precisely checked it and awakened Rome itself to vigilance and opposition.

Descartes, the father of Rationalism, was Roman Catholic. The German Rationalists are counterbalanced by the French Materialists; Rousseau by Voltaire, Strauss by Renan. The Revolution struck its deepest roots and bore its bitterest fruits in Roman Catholic countries. Further, it remains to be seen whether the number of parties, tendencies, and sects that continually appear in Rome would not be as great as in Protestantism if Rome did not have the power and courage to suppress every tendency by censorship, ban, interdict, and if necessary by the sword. It is truly not thanks to Rome that so many flourishing Christian churches have arisen alongside it.

Whatever shadow sides the division of Protestantism may have, it nevertheless also proves that religious life here is a power which repeatedly creates new forms for itself and amid all diversity nevertheless reveals a deeper unity. In any case, Protestantism with its division is preferable to the dreadful superstition in which the people in the Greek and Roman churches are increasingly entangled. Marian devotion, veneration of relics, image worship, and adoration of saints increasingly displace the service of the one true God. Cf. Trede, _Das Heidenthum in der römischen Kirche_, Gotha, Perthes, 4 Theile, 1889–92.
