## 14.

The doctrine of the _perspicuitas S. Scr._ has repeatedly been misunderstood and incorrectly represented by both Protestants and Roman Catholics. It does not mean that the matters and subjects with which Scripture deals are not mysteries that far surpass human understanding. Nor does it claim that Holy Scripture is clear in all its parts, so that scientific exegesis would be unnecessary. And it means just as little that Holy Scripture, even in the doctrine of salvation, is plain and clear to every human being without distinction.

It entails only that the truth whose knowledge is necessary for everyone unto salvation, though not equally clear on every page of Holy Scripture, is nevertheless presented throughout Scripture in so simple and intelligible a form that a person concerned for the salvation of his soul can easily learn to know that truth from Scripture through his own reading and investigation, without the help and guidance of church and priest. The way of salvation is plainly deposited there for the reader who desires salvation—not with respect to the matter but to the _modus tradendi_. He may not understand the πως, but the ὁτι is nevertheless clear. Zwingli, _De claritate et certitudine verbi Dei_, _Op._ ed. Schuler et Schulthess I 65 sq.; Luther in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._, 2e Ausg., 1883, II 58 f.; Zanchi, _De Scr. Sacra_, _Op. Omnia_ VIII 407 sq.; Chamier, _Panstratia catholica_, 1626, loc. 1, lib. I, capp. 13–32; Amesius, _Bellarminus Enervatus_, Amsterdam, 1630, lib. 1, capp. 4 and 5; Turretin, _Theol. El._, loc. 2, qu. 17; Trigland, _Antapologia_, cap. 3; _Synopsis pur. Theol._, disp. 5; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, loc. 1, cap. 20 sq.; Glassius, _Philologia Sacra_, 1691, p. 186 sq.

Understood in this way, _perspicuitas_ is a property that Holy Scripture repeatedly predicates of itself. The Torah was given by God to all Israel, and Moses conveys all the words of the Lord to the whole people. The law and Word of the Lord are not far from any of them but are a light on the path and a lamp for the foot, Deut. 30:11; Ps. 19:8, 9; 119:105, 130; Prov. 6:23. In speaking and writing the prophets address the whole people, Isa. 1:10 f.; 5:3 f.; 9:1; 40:1 f.; Jer. 2:4; 4:1; 10:1; Ezek. 3:1. Jesus speaks openly to all the crowds, Matt. 5:1; 13:1, 2; 26:55, etc.; and the apostles write to all the called saints, Rom. 1:7; 1 Cor. 1:2; 2 Cor. 1:1, etc., and themselves ensure the circulation of their letters, Col. 4:16.

The written Word is commended to all for investigation, John 5:39; Acts 17:11, and is written precisely to grant faith, patience, hope, comfort, instruction, and so forth, John 20:31; Rom. 15:4; 2 Tim. 3:16; 1 John 1:1 f. Nowhere is there any mention of withholding Scripture from laypeople. Believers themselves are mature and able to judge, 1 Cor. 2:15; 10:15; 1 John 2:20; 1 Pet. 2:9. The words of God are entrusted to them, Rom. 3:2.

The church fathers accordingly know nothing of the obscurity of Scripture in the later Roman Catholic sense. They indeed frequently speak of the depths and mysteries of Holy Scripture; cf. passages in Bellarmine, _De verbo Dei_ III, c. 1. But they just as frequently praise its clarity and simplicity. Thus Chrysostom says, _Hom._ 3 _De Lazaro_, when he compares the writings of the prophets and apostles with those of the philosophers: Οἱ δε ἀποστολοι και οἱ προφηται τοὐναντιον ἁπαν ἐποιησαν· σαφη γαρ και δηλα τα παρ’ αὐτων κατεστησαν ἁπασιν, ἁτε κοινοι της οἰκουμενης ὀντες διδασκαλοι, ἱνα ἑκαστος και δι’ ἑαυτου μανθανειν δυνηται ἐκ της ἀναγνωσεως μονης τα λεγομενα. Elsewhere, _Hom._ 3 _in 2 Thess._, he says: παντα σαφη και εὐθεα τα παρα ταις θειαις γραφαις, παντα τα ἀναγκαια δηλα.

Likewise we read in Augustine, _De doctr. chr._ 2, 6: _nihil de illis obscuritatibus eruitur, quod non plenissime dictum alibi reperiatur_; and ibid. 9: _in iis quae clare in scripturis tradita sunt, inveniuntur omnia, quae continent fidem moresque vivendi_. Also well known is the word of Gregory I in which he compares Scripture to a _fluvius planus et altus, in quo et agnus ambulet et elephas natet_. Even now Roman Catholic theologians must acknowledge that much in Holy Scripture is so clear that not only can the believer understand it, but the unbeliever who rejects its clear sense is inexcusable, Heinrich, _Dogm._ I 819.

The church fathers therefore did not think of forbidding laypeople to read Scripture. On the contrary, they repeatedly urge investigation of Holy Scripture and tell of the blessing they themselves received from reading it, Vigouroux, _Les livres saints et la critique rationaliste_, 3 ed., I 280 s. Gregory I still commended the reading of Scripture to all laypeople, Herzog² 2, 376. Restrictions on Bible reading arose only when, from the twelfth century onward, various sects began to appeal to Scripture against the church. The opinion then gained currency that Bible reading by laypeople was the principal source of heresy. In self-defense Rome increasingly taught the obscurity of Scripture and bound its reading to the consent of ecclesiastical authority.
