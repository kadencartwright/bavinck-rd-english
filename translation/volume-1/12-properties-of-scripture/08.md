### C. The Necessity of Scripture

## 8.

There is great agreement among the Christian churches concerning the authority of Scripture, but significant disagreement concerning the three properties that now follow. Because of the relation it assumes between Scripture and church, Rome can neither understand nor acknowledge the _necessitas S. Scripturae_. In Rome the church is αὐτοπιστος, self-sufficient, living from and through the Holy Spirit; it possesses the truth and faithfully and purely preserves it through the pope’s infallible teaching office. Scripture, by contrast, having proceeded from the church, may be useful and good as _norma_, but is not the _principium_ of truth. It is not necessary _ad esse ecclesiae_. Properly speaking, the church does not need Scripture; Scripture needs the church for its authority, supplementation, interpretation, and so forth.

The grounds for this doctrine are derived from the fact that the church before Moses and the first Christian community had no Scripture, and that many believers under the Old and even under the New Testament never possessed and read Scripture but lived solely and exclusively from tradition. Bellarmine, _De verbo Dei_ IV, c. 4; Heinrich, _Dogm._ I 735 f.; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._, 1857, I, pp. 449 sq.; Dieringer, _Lehrb. der kath. Dogm._, 4e Aufl., 633; Gutberlet, _Lehrb. der Apol._ III, 1894, S. 221 f.; Jansen, _Prael. theol._ I 786 sq., etc.

But it is not only Rome that in this way opposes the necessity of Holy Scripture. All manner of mystical tendencies have also weakened and misconceived the significance of Scripture for church and theology. Gnosticism not only rejected the Old Testament but applied the allegorical method to the New Testament and thereby attempted to bring its system into agreement with Scripture. Sensible forms and historical facts have only symbolic significance; they are a clothing necessary for people at a lower standpoint but discarded by the more highly developed, the πνευματικοι. Scripture is not a source of truth but only a means of elevating oneself to the higher standpoint of _gnosis_, Herzog² 5, 209 f.; Harnack, _D. G._ I 214 f.

In Montanism a new revelation appeared that supplemented and improved the revelation in the New Testament. Montanism, especially in its moderate form in Tertullian, wished on the one hand to be nothing new and fully to maintain the authority of Scripture; yet it greeted in Montanus a prophet in whom the Paraclete promised by Jesus, the last and highest revelation, had appeared. Scripture had in this way to yield to the new prophecy proclaimed by Montanus, Harnack, _D. G._ I 353 f.; Herzog² 10, 258 f.

The church indeed condemned these tendencies, and the church fathers opposed this spiritualism. Augustine wrote against it in the prologue to his book _De doctrina christiana_. Yet Augustine too assumed that the pious, especially monks, could be equipped with so great a measure of faith, hope, and love that for themselves they could dispense with Scripture and live without it in solitude, _De doctr. christ._ I, c. 39.

Spiritualism repeatedly arose again and reacted against the constricting power of church and tradition. Various sects—the Cathars, Amalric of Bena, Joachim of Fiore, the Brothers and Sisters of the Free Spirit, and later the Libertines in Geneva—held that after the era of the Father and the Son, that of the Holy Spirit had dawned, in which all lived through the Spirit and no longer needed the external means of Scripture and church. Kurtz, _Lehrb. der Kirchengesch._ §§ 108, 116; Reuter, _Gesch. der relig. Aufklärung im M. A._ II 198 f.; Herzog² 2, 677; 6, 786; 8, 652 f.; Hahn, _Gesch. der Ketzer im M. A._ II 420 f., III 72 f.; Gieseler, _Kirchengeschichte_ II, 2, 1826, S. 437 f.; Hagenbach, _Kirchengesch. in Vorlesungen_ II, 1886, S. 480 f.

The mysticism that flourished in France and Germany during the Middle Ages sought by means of asceticism, meditation, and contemplation to attain a communion with God that could dispense with Scripture. Scripture was indeed needed as a ladder, as it were, to climb to this height, but became superfluous when unity with God, the _visio Dei_, had been attained, Herzog¹ 12, 427 f.; Harnack, _D. G._ III 374 f.

The Anabaptists especially elevated the internal word at the expense of the external. Already in 1521 an opposition was made between Scripture and Spirit, and this opposition became a permanent characteristic of Anabaptism, Sepp, _Kerkhist. Stud._ 12. Holy Scripture is not the true Word of God but only a testimony and description. The genuine, true Word is that spoken in our hearts by the Holy Spirit. The Bible is only a book with letters; Bible is Babel, full of confusion. It cannot work faith in hearts; only the Spirit teaches us the true Word. And when that Spirit instructs us, we can dispense with Scripture. It is a temporary aid but is not necessary for the spiritual person. A. Hegler, _Geist u. Schrift bei Sebastian Franck_, Freiburg, 1892; J. H. Maronier, _Het inwendig woord_, Amsterdam, 1890; Vigouroux, _Les livres saints et la critique rationaliste_, 3e ed., I 435–453.

Hans Denck already identified this internal word with natural reason and pointed to many contradictions in Scripture. Ludwig Hetzer considered Scripture altogether unnecessary. In Münster Knipperdolling demanded that Holy Scripture be abolished and that people live only according to nature and spirit, Herzog² 10, 362. Mysticism turned into Rationalism. We later see the same phenomenon among the Anabaptist and Independent sects in England in the time of Cromwell, among the Quakers, and in Pietism. Elevating the internal above the external Word always led to identification of the Spirit’s instruction with the natural light of reason and conscience, and thus to complete rejection of revelation and Scripture.

Accordingly, no one opposed the necessity of Scripture more sharply than Lessing in his _Axiomata_ against Goeze. He too distinguishes between letter and spirit, Bible and religion, theology and religion, the Christian religion and the religion of Jesus, and says that the latter existed and can exist independently of the former. For religion existed before there was a Bible. Christianity existed before evangelists and apostles wrote. The religion taught by them can continue to exist even if all their writings were lost. Religion is not true because evangelists and apostles taught it; they taught it because it is true. Their writings may and must therefore be explained according to the internal truth of religion. An attack on the Bible is not yet an attack on religion. Luther delivered us from the yoke of tradition; who will deliver us from the still far more intolerable yoke of the letter?
