## 19.

In fact, by this doctrine of tradition Scripture is robbed of all its authority and power. Roman Catholics predicate infallibility of both Scripture and tradition (the pope), but acknowledge that a great difference nevertheless exists between them. In both cases the cause of infallibility is indeed sought in a special, supernatural operation of the Holy Spirit; for Rome understands very well that the infallibility of tradition cannot be derived from believers as such, from the power and spirit of Christianity that dwells and works in believers. After all, many errors occur in the church and among believers, errors that often prevail for a long time and lead many astray. The infallibility of the pope, therefore, just like that of Scripture, is explained from an extraordinary operation of the Holy Spirit on the basis of Matt. 16:18; 28:20; John 14:16 f.; 15:26; 16:12 f.; _Concil. Vatic._, sess. 3. Yet there is a difference. The activity of the Holy Spirit in the apostles consisted in revelation and inspiration; that in the pope consists in assistance. The _Vaticanum_, cap. 4, says: _neque enim Petri successoribus Spiritus Sanctus promissus est, ut eo revelante novam doctrinam patefacerent, sed ut eo assistente, traditam per Apostolos revelationem seu fidei depositum sancte custodirent et fideliter exponerent_. Scripture is therefore the Word of God in the proper sense, inspired—at least according to many theologians—down to the _singula verba_; the decisions of councils and popes are words of the church that reproduce God’s truth purely. Scripture _is_ the Word of God; tradition _contains_ the Word of God. Scripture preserves the words of the apostles in their original form; tradition reproduces the apostles’ doctrine only with respect to its substance. The books of prophets and apostles were often written without investigation, solely from revelation; but with the _assistentia divina_ promised to the church, the persons themselves are always active: they investigate, deliberate, judge, and decide. In inspiration the activity of the Spirit was supernatural in the strict sense, but in assistance it often consists in a complex of providential cares by which the church is preserved from error. Finally, inspiration in Scripture extends to all matters, including history, chronology, and so forth, but through the assistance of the Holy Spirit the pope is infallible only when he speaks _ex cathedra_, that is, when he speaks as Pastor and Doctor of Christendom and when he _doctrinam de fide vel moribus ab universa ecclesia tenendam definit_. Scripture thus still has for Rome certain prerogatives over tradition. Bellarmine, _De Conciliis et Ecclesia_, lib. 2, c. 12; Heinrich, I 726 f., II 220–245; Jansen, I 616.

But in fact tradition greatly detracts from Scripture. First, Trent determines that Scripture and tradition must be venerated _pari pietatis affectu et reverentia_. Next, the inspiration of Holy Scripture is understood by most Roman Catholic theologians as an _inspiratio realis_, so that it is not the _singula verba_ but the matters themselves that are inspired. Further, infallibility _quoad formam_ and _quoad substantiam_ are so closely connected with each other that the boundary between them cannot be drawn. Moreover, strictly speaking, the pope is infallible only in matters of faith and life; but in order to be so, he must also be infallible in judging the sources of faith and in their interpretation—that is, in determining what Scripture and tradition are; in determining the authority of the church fathers, the councils, and so forth; in judging errors and heresies and even the _facta dogmatica_; in the prohibition of books; in matters of discipline; in the approval of orders; in the canonization of saints; and so forth. Heinrich, II 557 f. And although the pope is not, strictly speaking, infallible in all other things, his power and authority nevertheless extend also over all things _quae ad disciplinam et regimen ecclesiae pertinent_, and this _potestas_ is _plena_ and _suprema_ and extends over all _pastores_ and _fideles_, _Conc. Vatic._, cap. 3. Many Roman Catholics even demand that, in order to exercise this spiritual sovereignty, the pope must be a temporal prince; and it is asserted that he possesses, if not directly then at least indirectly, the _summa potestas disponendi de rebus temporalibus omnium christianorum_. Bellarmine, _De Romano Pontifice_, lib. 5; de Maistre, _Du Pape_, livre 2; Jansen, I 651 sq. The pope’s power and authority far surpass those of Scripture. He stands above it, judges its content and meaning, and by his own authority establishes the dogmas of doctrine and life. Scripture may be the principal means for demonstrating the agreement of present-day doctrine and tradition with the doctrine of the apostles; it may contain much that otherwise would not be known so well; it may be a divine instruction in doctrine that surpasses every other, Heinrich, I 732 f.; yet for Rome it always remains only an aid, useful but not necessary. The church existed before Scripture, and the church contains not a part but the full truth, whereas Scripture contains only a portion of the doctrine. Scripture indeed needs tradition, the confirmation of the pope, but tradition does not need Holy Scripture. Tradition is not a supplement to Scripture; Scripture is a supplement to tradition. Scripture alone is insufficient, but tradition alone is sufficient. Scripture rests on the church, but the church rests in herself. Heinrich, I 730 f.
