### D. The Clarity of Scripture

## 13.

Another important property that the Reformation ascribed to Scripture over against Rome was _perspicuitas_. According to Rome, Scripture is obscure, Ps. 119:34, 68; Luke 24:27; Acts 8:30; 2 Pet. 3:16. Even in those matters that pertain to faith and life it is not so clear that it can dispense with interpretation. For it deals with the deepest mysteries—with God, the Trinity, the incarnation, predestination, and so forth—and even in its moral precepts, for example Matt. 5:34, 40; 10:27; Luke 12:33; 14:33, it is often so obscure that misunderstanding and misconception have occurred at every moment in the Christian church.

For a right understanding of Scripture all manner of knowledge is also needed concerning history, geography, chronology, archaeology, languages, and so forth, which is inaccessible to laypeople. Protestants themselves accordingly write countless commentaries and differ from one another in the exegesis of the most important texts. An interpretation of Holy Scripture is therefore necessary. This cannot be given by Scripture itself; Scripture cannot be its own interpreter.

Plato already said, _Phaedrus_, p. 274, that the letter is mistreated and cannot help itself, and that it needs the help of its father. It is mute and can give no decision in a dispute. It is like the law according to which the judge renders a verdict, but it is not law and judge at the same time. The learned Jesuit Jakob Gretser made a deep impression at the religious colloquy at Regensburg in 1601 when he spoke as follows: _Sumus in conspectu sacrae Scripturae et Spiritus Sancti. Pronuntiet sententiam. Et si dicat: tu Gretsere male sentis, cecidisti causa tua, tu Jacobe Heilbrunnere vicisti; tunc ego statim transibo ad vestrum scamnum. Adsit, adsit, adsit et condemnet me!_

There must therefore be an interpreter and a judge who renders a decision according to Scripture. If there is no such judge, interpretation becomes wholly subjective; everyone judges according to his own pleasure and regards his own individual conception as infallible. Every heretic has his letter. Everyone, according to Werenfels’s well-known distich, seeks precisely his own dogmas in Scripture. Scripture is handed over to all manner of arbitrariness. Individualism, Enthusiasm, Rationalism, and endless division are the result.

Worst of all, if there is no infallible interpretation, there is also no absolute assurance of faith. The foundation upon which the Christian’s hope rests is then pious opinion, scientific insight, but no divine, infallible testimony. And so far is it from being possible for anyone to form his own conviction or doctrine from Holy Scripture that Protestants in practice, just as much as Roman Catholics, live from tradition and rely upon the authority of church, synods, fathers, writers, and others.

Such an infallible, divine interpretation of Scripture, however, has been given by God in his church. Not dead, uncomprehended, obscure Scripture left to itself, but the church—the living, ever-present church, always renewed by the Spirit—is the mediatrix of truth and the infallible interpreter of Holy Scripture. For everyone is the best interpreter of his own word. The true interpreter of Scripture is therefore the Holy Spirit, who is its author. And he has his infallible organ in the church, or better still, in the pope. Through tradition the church possesses the truth. It is led by the same Spirit who brought Scripture into being; it is akin to Scripture; it alone can understand Scripture’s meaning; it is the pillar and foundation of the truth.

This has also always been the practice. Moses, the priests, Christ, and the apostles interpreted and decided for the congregation, Ex. 18; Deut. 17:9 f.; 2 Chron. 19:9 f.; Eccles. 12:12; Hag. 2:2; Mal. 2:7; Matt. 16:19; 18:17; 23:2; Luke 22:32; John 21:15 f.; Acts 15:28; Gal. 2:2; 1 Cor. 12:8 f.; 2 Pet. 1:19; 1 John 4:1; and popes and councils followed that example.

The Council of Trent therefore established in sess. 4 that no one may interpret Holy Scripture _contra eum sensum, quem tenuit et tenet sancta mater ecclesiae, cujus est judicare de vero sensu et interpretatione Scripturarum Sanctarum, aut etiam contra unanimem consensum Patrum_. It thereby bound exegesis not merely negatively, as some Roman Catholics attempt to understand it, but very decidedly also positively. No one may give another exegesis than that which the church has given through its _patres_, councils, or popes. The _professio fidei_ of Pius IV, in Denzinger, _Enchir. symb. et defin._ n. 864, and the _Vaticanum_, sess. 3, cap. 2, alin. 4, leave no doubt concerning this.

But this doctrine of the obscurity of Scripture not only subjects scientific exegesis to the pope. The layperson is still more dependent and bound by it. Because of its obscurity Scripture is not suitable reading for laypeople. Without interpretation it is unintelligible to the people. Rome therefore increasingly restricted the translation of Scripture into the vernacular and the reading of the Bible by the people after the misuse made of it in the Middle Ages and later. Reading Scripture is not permitted to laypeople except with the consent of ecclesiastical authority. Protestant Bible societies have repeatedly been condemned by the popes and in the encyclical of December 8, 1864, were placed on one line with socialist and communist associations, Denzinger, _Enchir._ n. 1566. The present pope indeed commends the study of Scripture in his encyclical _De studiis Sacrae Scripturae_, but not to laypeople.
