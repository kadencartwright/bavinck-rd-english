## 6.

The nature and ground of Scripture’s authority, however, have been brought into dispute especially in modern theology. In earlier times the authority of Holy Scripture rested upon its inspiration and followed from it as a matter of course. But once inspiration was surrendered, Scripture’s authority could no longer be maintained. Attempts were indeed made to do so in all manner of ways, but theologians found themselves compelled to conceive both the grounds and the character of Holy Scripture’s authority in an entirely different way. Scripture’s authority, insofar as it was still acknowledged, was based on the considerations that Scripture is the authentic document of revelation; that it expresses the Christian idea in its purest form, just as water too is purest at its source; that it is the fulfillment of the Old Testament idea of salvation and contains Christian doctrine in its entirety, even if only in germ; and that it is the origin and continual renewal of the Christian spirit in the congregation. These and similar considerations in favor of Scripture’s authority may be found among theologians of the most diverse schools, such as Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 78 f.; Saussaye, _mijne theol. van Ch. d. l. S._ 53 f.; Schleiermacher, _Gl._ § 129 f.; Rothe, _Zur Dogm._ S. 166 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 193 f.; Biedermann, _Dogm._ § 193 f.; Schweizer, _Christl. Gl._ I S. 178 f.; Hofmann, _Weiss. u. Erf._ III 98 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II 5 f., 9 f.; etc. Yet all these grounds are not firm enough to sustain an authority such as religion requires. They may merit consideration as _motiva credibilitatis_, but as grounds they are untenable. For, first of all, by distinguishing between revelation and its document, between the Word of God and Scripture, they make Scripture’s authority entirely illusory in practice. For if it is not Scripture as a whole that has authority, but only the Word of God in it—the religious-ethical element, revelation, or whatever one wishes to call it—then everyone must determine for himself what that Word of God in Scripture is, and each determines this as he sees fit. The center of gravity is transferred from the object to the subject; Scripture does not judge the human being, but the human being judges Scripture; Scripture’s authority depends on human approval; it exists only insofar as one is willing to acknowledge it and is therefore completely destroyed. But even if all these grounds could vindicate some authority for Scripture, it would still be nothing other than a merely historical authority. And this is insufficient in religion. Here a historical—that is, human and fallible—authority is not enough for us. Because religion concerns our salvation and is connected with our eternal interests, in it we can make do with nothing less than divine authority. We must know not only that Scripture is the historical document for our knowledge of Christianity and that it contains and represents the original Christian ideas in their purest form; in religion we must know that Scripture is the Word and truth of God. Without this certainty there is no comfort in life or in death. And not only does every Christian need this certainty, but the church itself as an institution cannot do without it. For if a preacher lacks the conviction of the divine truth of the word he proclaims, his preaching loses all authority, influence, and power. If he has no divine message to bring, who then gives him the right to stand before people subject to the same passions as he is? Who gives him the freedom to place himself above them in the pulpit, to occupy their attention with the highest interests of the soul and of life, and even to announce to them eternal weal or eternal woe? Who dares and who is able to do that except one who has a word of God to proclaim? Christian faith and Christian preaching both require a divine authority on which they rest. _Titubabit fides, si divinarum scripturarum vacillat auctoritas_, Augustine, _De doctr. christ._ I 37.

For that reason, describing the character of Scripture’s authority as moral cannot be approved either. Lessing had already made a beginning in this direction when he said that something is not true because it stands in the Bible, but that it stands in the Bible because it is true. Since his plea for deliverance from the authority of the letter and from the paper pope, belief based on authority has been ridiculed in all manner of ways. Christian theologians have allowed themselves to be influenced by this and have modified or opposed such belief. Doedes, for example, _Inl. tot de leer van God_, 1880, pp. 29–40, rejects belief based on authority and speaks only of moral authority in religion. Saussaye, _mijne Theol. van Ch. d. l. S._ 53 f., declares that there is no authority other than moral authority and that the moral constitutes the whole of authority. There is no intellectual authority, but moral authority is morality, religion itself. One does not believe the truth on authority; rather, the truth has authority—that is, it has the right to be obeyed. This conception, however, suffers from a confusion of concepts. The truth has authority, certainly; no one denies it. But the question is precisely what truth is in the religious domain and where it is to be found. Only two answers are possible. On the one hand, the apostles tell us what truth is—or, if one prefers, who Christ is; Scripture tells us. On the other hand, each person determines this for himself by his own judgment, reason, or conscience. In the latter case, Scripture no longer has any authority; it is subjected entirely to the criticism of the subject. Nor does it help, then, to say with Rothe, _Zur Dogm._ 287, that the Bible is the fully sufficient instrument for arriving at a pure knowledge of God’s revelation. For every objective standard is lacking by which that revelation can be judged and found in Scripture. There is in fact only one ground on which Scripture’s authority can rest, and that is its inspiration. If inspiration falls, then Scripture’s authority is at an end as well. It then contains only human writings, which as such can claim no title whatever to serve as the _norma_ for our faith and life. And with Scripture, for the Protestant, all authority in religion falls. All attempts to recover some form of authority—for example, in the person of Christ, in the church, in religious experience, in reason or conscience—end in disappointment: Stanton, _The place of authority in matters of religious belief_, London, Longmans, 1891; James Martineau, _The seat of authority in religion_, London, Longmans, 1891; C. A. Briggs, _The authority of H. Scriptures. Inaugural address_, 4 ed., New-York, Scribner, 1892; L. Monod, _Le problème de l’autorité_, Paris, Fischbacher, 1892; E. Doumergue, _L’autorité en matière de foi_, Lausanne, Payot, 1892; E. Ménégoz, _L’autorité de Dieu, réflexions sur l’autorité en matière de foi_, Paris, Fischbacher, 1892; G. Godet, _Vinet et l’autorité en matière de foi_, _Revue de théol. et de philos._, Mars 1893, p. 173–191. They prove only that a religion without authority cannot exist. Religion is essentially distinct from science. It has a certainty of its own—not one that rests on insight, but one that consists in faith, in trust. And this religious faith and trust can rest only in God and in his Word. In religion a _testimonium humanum_ and a _fides humana_ are insufficient; here we need God’s testimony, on which we can rely in life and death. _Inquietum est cor nostrum, donec requiescat in Te!_ Harnack therefore also rightly says, _D. G._ III 73: es hat in der Welt keinen starken religiösen Glauben gegeben, der nicht an irgend einem _entscheidenden_ Punkt sich auf eine _äussere Autorität_ berufen hätte. Nur in den blassen Ausführungen der Religionsphilosophen oder in den polemischen Entwürfen protestantischer Theologen wird ein Glaube construirt, der seine Gewissheit lediglich den eigenen inneren Momenten entnimmt. Cf. also P. D. Chantepie de la Saussaye, _Zekerheid en twijfel_, 1893, p. 138 f. The right and value of authority in religion are gradually being acknowledged once again.
