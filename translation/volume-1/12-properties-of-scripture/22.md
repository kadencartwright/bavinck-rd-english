## 22.

Yet none of this denies the good and true elements contained in the doctrine of tradition. The word “tradition” has a broader meaning than the one Rome gives it. By it Rome understands a doctrine handed down by the apostles, preserved by the bishops—specifically by the pope—and defined and promulgated by him; but this conception proved untenable. Tradition, however, can also be understood as all that religious life, thought, feeling, and action which is found in every religious community and finds expression in all manner of forms, morals, customs, usages, religious language and literature, confession and liturgy, and so forth. In this sense there is tradition in every religion. The concept can even be extended further to include all those rich and manifold bonds that connect later generations with those that preceded them. In this sense no family, no lineage, no society, no nation, no art, no science, and so forth, can exist without tradition. Tradition is the means by which all the treasures and goods of our forebears are transmitted to the present and the future. Over against the individualism and atomism of the previous century, de Bonald, Lamennais, and others—and, in the Netherlands, Bilderdijk—once again placed the significance of community, authority, language, tradition, and so forth, in the clearest light. A tradition of this kind assuredly exists in religion and in the church as well. Its very universality already indicates that we are not dealing here with an accidental phenomenon. We find tradition not only in the Roman Church but also among Jews, Muslims, Buddhists, and so forth. In the higher religions there is an additional reason for the necessity of tradition. They are all bound to a sacred scripture that originated at a particular time and in that sense stands at an ever greater distance from the generation now living. The Bible too is a book written in centuries long past and under all manner of historical circumstances. The various books of the Bible bear the character of the time in which they originated. However clear Scripture may be in the doctrine of salvation and however much it is and remains the _viva vox Dei_, cf. above, p. 305, for its proper understanding it nevertheless often requires all manner of historical, archaeological, and geographical knowledge. The times have changed, and with the times, people—their life and thought and feeling. Tradition is therefore necessary to preserve the continuity between Scripture and the religious life of the present age. Tradition in the good sense is the interpretation and application of eternal truth in the speech and life of the present generation. A Scripture without such a tradition is impossible. Many sects in earlier and later times have indeed attempted this. They wished to know nothing but the words and letters of Scripture; they rejected all dogmatic terminology that was not used in Scripture; they condemned all theological training and scholarship; and they sometimes went so far as to demand a literal application of the civil laws under Israel and of the precepts of the Sermon on the Mount. But in doing so all these movements condemned themselves to certain destruction, or at least to a languishing existence. They place themselves outside society and forfeit all influence on their people and their age. Scripture is not intended to be memorized and merely repeated, but to enter into the full, rich life of humanity, to form and guide it, and to make it independently active in every sphere. The Reformation therefore adopted a different standpoint. It did not reject all tradition as such; it was reformation, not revolution. It did not seek to create everything anew but rather to cleanse everything of error and abuse according to the standard of God’s Word. It therefore continued to stand on the broad Christian foundation of the Apostles’ Creed and the first councils. It therefore favored a theological science that thoughtfully rendered the truth of Scripture in the language of the present. The difference between Rome and the Reformation in their conception of tradition consists in this: Rome desires a tradition that proceeds independently alongside Scripture, a _traditio juxta Scripturam_, or rather a _Scriptura juxta traditionem_. The Reformation acknowledges only a tradition that is grounded in and flows from Scripture, _traditio e Scriptura fluens_, Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 351. In the conception of the Reformation, Scripture was an organic principle from which the entire tradition—living on in preaching, confession, liturgy, _cultus_, theology, religious literature, and so forth—grows and by which it is nourished; a pure spring of living water from which all the streams and channels of religious life are fed and sustained. Such a tradition is grounded in Scripture itself. When Jesus has completed his work, he sends the Holy Spirit, who indeed adds nothing new to revelation but nevertheless leads the church into the truth, John 16:12–15, until through all diversity it attains the unity of faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, Eph. 3:18, 19; 4:13. In this sense there is a good, true, glorious tradition. It is the way by which the Holy Spirit brings the truth of Scripture into the consciousness and life of the church. Scripture, after all, is only a means, not the end. The end is that the church, instructed from Scripture, should freely and independently proclaim the excellencies of him who called her out of darkness into his marvelous light. The _verbum externum_ is the instrument; the _verbum internum_ is the end. Scripture has fulfilled its purpose when all shall be taught by the Lord and filled with the Holy Spirit.
