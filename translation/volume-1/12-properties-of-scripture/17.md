### E. The Sufficiency of Scripture

## 17.

Finally, the Reformation also confessed the _perfectio_ or _sufficientia S. Scripturae_.
The Roman Church holds that Scripture is incomplete _in partibus_ and must be supplemented by tradition.
At Trent, sess. 4, it declared that it _traditiones ipsas, tum ad fidem tum ad mores pertinentes, tanquam vel ore tenus a Christo vel a Sp. S°. dictatas et continua successione in Ecclesia Catholica conservatas, pari pietatis affectu et reverentia suscipit et veneratur_; and at the _Vaticanum_, sess. 3, cap. 2, it stated that supernatural revelation is contained _in libris scriptis et sine scripto traditionibus, quae ipsius Christi ore ab Apostolis acceptae, aut ab ipsis Apostolis Spiritu Sancto dictante quasi per manus traditae ad nos usque pervenerant_.
The grounds Rome advances for this doctrine of tradition are various.
First, it is pointed out that the church before Moses was entirely without Scripture and that even after that time, down to the present, many believers live and die without ever reading or studying Scripture.
The great majority of the children of God live from tradition and know little or nothing of Scripture.
It would also be strange if matters were different in the religious and ecclesiastical sphere than in every other domain.
After all, in law and morals, in art and science, in family and society, tradition is the bearer and nurse of our life.
Through it we are connected with preceding generations, receive their treasures, and in turn leave them to our children.
Analogy itself requires that there also be a tradition in the church; but here it must be so much more glorious and certain than elsewhere, because Christ has given the Holy Spirit to his church and through this Spirit infallibly leads his congregation into all truth, Matt. 16:18, 28:20; John 14:16.
To these are added the many statements of Scripture that acknowledge the right and value of tradition, John 16:12, 20:30, 21:25; Acts 1:3; 1 Cor. 11:2, 23; 2 Thess. 2:14; 1 Tim. 6:20; 2 John 12; 3 John 13, 14.
Jesus taught his disciples orally and through his Spirit many more things that were not recorded by them but were transmitted from mouth to mouth.
Church fathers, councils, and popes likewise acknowledged such an apostolic tradition from the beginning.
In fact, the church still draws its life from this oral, living tradition.
Scripture alone is insufficient.
For, besides the fact that by no means everything was recorded, various writings of prophets and apostles have also been lost.
The apostles did receive a command to bear witness, but not to do so in writing.
They came to write only because of circumstances, _necessitate quadam coacti_; their writings are therefore mostly occasional writings and by no means contain everything needed for the doctrine and life of the church.
Thus we find little or nothing in Scripture concerning the baptism of women, the observance of Sunday, the episcopate, the seven sacraments, purgatory, the immaculate conception of Mary, the salvation of many pagans in the days of the Old Testament, the inspiration and canonicity of the various biblical books, and so forth; indeed, even _dogmata_ such as the Trinity, eternal generation, the procession of the Holy Spirit, infant baptism, and so forth, are not found literally and in so many words in Scripture.
In a word, Scripture is useful, but tradition is necessary: Bellarmine, _de Verbo Dei_, lib. IV; Melchior Canus, _Loci theol._ lib. 3; Perrone, _Praelect. Theol._ IX 228 sq.; Klee, _Dogm._ I 277 f.; Heinrich, _Dogm. Theol._ II S. 1 f.; Jansen, _Praelect. theol. dogm._ I 788 sq.; Möhler, _Symbolik_ § 38 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_, 2e Aufl., I 72 f.; Dieringer, _Dogm._ § 126; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._ I p. 448 sq. For the Greek church, see Kattenbusch, _Confessionskunde_ I 292.
