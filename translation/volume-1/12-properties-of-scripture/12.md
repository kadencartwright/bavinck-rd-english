## 12.

Even when the necessity of Scripture is acknowledged, there can still be disagreement about how long that necessity endures. Even those who believe that Scripture has had its day often readily concede that in its own day it was of great significance for the education of individuals and peoples. But the duration of this necessity has been restricted in all sorts of ways. Gnosticism acknowledged its necessity for the _ψυχικοι_ but denied it for the _πνευματικοι_. Mysticism considered Scripture necessary at the stage of cogitation and meditation, but no longer at that of contemplation and _visio Dei_. The rationalism of Lessing and Kant assigned a pedagogical place to revelation, Scripture, and the _statutarische Religion_, so that the dominion of the _Vernunftreligion_ might thereby be prepared. Hegel likewise judged that the form of intuition in religion was necessary for the people, but that the philosopher with his concepts no longer had any need of it. And one hears it said repeatedly that religion is good for the masses in order to keep them in check, but that the educated and cultured are elevated far above it.

There is an undeniable and glorious truth in this conception. Revelation, Scripture, the church—in short, the whole Christian religion—does indeed bear a temporary, preparatory, and pedagogical character. Just as the Old Testament economy of the _foedus gratiae_ has passed away, so too this dispensation of the covenant of grace in which we live will one day belong to the past. When Christ has gathered his church and presented her to the Father as a pure bride, he delivers the kingdom to God. Moreover, the duality of grace and nature, of revelation and reason, of authority and freedom, of theology and philosophy, cannot endure forever. For the highest point in religion consists in our serving God without compulsion and without fear, from love alone, in accordance with the dictates of our own nature. In his revelation God himself aims to form human beings in whom his image has once again been fully restored. He gave us not only his Son but also the Holy Spirit, so that the Spirit might regenerate us, write his law in our hearts, and equip us for every good work. Regeneration, sonship, sanctification, and glorification are the proofs that God educates his children for freedom, for a service of love that never becomes wearisome. In this respect the conceptions mentioned above may be called an anticipation of the future ideal. Nevertheless, their tendency is very dangerous. They all proceed from a confusion between the dispensation of the present and that of the hereafter. Although the new Jerusalem will no longer need sun and moon, both remain necessary here on earth. Although we shall one day walk by sight, faith remains indispensable in this dispensation. Although the church militant and the church triumphant are one, there remains a distinction between their respective condition and life. The boundary line may not and cannot be erased. We never attain the heavenly life here on earth. We walk by faith and not by sight. Now we see in a mirror, dimly; only in the hereafter shall we see face to face and know as we are known. The _visio Dei_ is reserved for heaven. We never become autonomous and independent on this earth. We remain bound to the cosmos that surrounds us. The standpoint of authority can never be transcended here on earth.

Furthermore, this doctrine of the temporary necessity of Scripture digs a deep chasm between psychical and pneumatic human beings, between the cultured and the masses, between philosophers and the people. Such a chasm has no right to exist in any respect. If religion consisted in knowledge, scholars would enjoy a privilege over the uneducated. But religiously all human beings are equal; they have the same needs. In Christ there is no distinction between Greek and barbarian. Religion is one and the same for all human beings, however different they may be in station, rank, development, and so forth, for in religion—that is, before the face of God—all those distinctions of rank and privileges by which one person excels above others among human beings are worthless. The separation between these two kinds of human beings therefore testifies to a spiritual pride that is itself in direct conflict with the essence of the Christian religion, with the humility, lowliness, and so forth that it requires. The tax collectors enter before the Pharisees, and the least is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven.

There would, furthermore, still be something to be said for this separation if rationalism were right and revelation consisted in nothing but _Vernunftwahrheiten_. For then, although these were initially known from revelation, they could later be derived by thought from reason itself. But revelation has a content altogether different from a rational doctrine. It is history, has grace as its content, the person of Christ as its center, and the re-creation of humanity as its goal. None of this can ever be discovered by thought or derived from reason. To know such a revelation, Scripture remains necessary at all times. Even a revelation of God to every human being individually could not give what the revelation in Christ now offers all human beings through Scripture. The historical character of revelation, the fact and idea of the incarnation, and the organic conception of the human race require a Scripture in which God’s revelation is deposited for the whole of humanity (cf. p. 299). Therefore it can now also be decided what was left unresolved on p. 143, namely, whether revelation comes individually to each human being or is given to all through Scripture. Just as one sun with its rays illumines the whole earth, so Christ is the dawn from on high that appears to those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death, and so the one and the same Scripture is the light on every person’s path and the lamp before every person’s foot. It is God’s Word to the whole of humanity. History itself bears powerful witness to this necessity of Scripture. Hyper-spiritual mysticism has repeatedly turned into the most vulgar rationalism, and enthusiastic spiritualism has often ended in the crassest materialism. The _necessitas S. Scripturae_ is proved negatively just as powerfully by the movements that oppose it as it is proved positively by the churches that confess it.
