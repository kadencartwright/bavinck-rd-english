## 21.

But this doctrine directly contradicts Scripture itself. In the Old and New Testaments the congregation is never referred to anything other than the Word of God then at hand, whether written or unwritten. By that Word alone can man live spiritually. In the Scripture present at each time, the congregation finds all that it needs. The later Scriptures presuppose the earlier ones, join themselves to them, and are built upon them. The prophets and psalmists presuppose the Torah. In Isa. 8:20, Isaiah summons all to the law and the testimony. The New Testament regards itself as the fulfillment of the Old and refers back to nothing other than the existing Scripture. Still more forceful is the fact that everything lying outside Scripture is excluded as decisively as possible. Traditions are rejected as commandments of men, Isa. 29:13; Matt. 15:4, 9; 1 Cor. 4:6. The tradition that arose in the days of the Old Testament led the Jews to reject the Christ. Against it Jesus sets his “But I say to you” in Matt. 5, and against the Pharisees and scribes he joins himself precisely to the law and the prophets. The apostles appeal only to the Old Testament Scripture and never refer the congregations to anything other than the Word of God proclaimed by them. Insofar as tradition in the earliest period intended to be nothing other than the preservation of what had been personally taught and instituted by the apostles, it did not yet have a dangerous character. But Roman tradition has completely degenerated from this. No doctrine or practice can be proved to derive from the apostles except insofar as this can be demonstrated from their writings. The tradition of Rome, from which the mass, Mariolatry, papal infallibility, and so forth have arisen, is nothing but the sanction of the actual condition of the Roman Church, the justification of the superstition that has penetrated it.

The _sufficientia_ of Holy Scripture follows, further, from the nature of the New Testament dispensation. Christ became flesh and completed the entire work. He is God’s last and highest revelation. He has made the Father known to us, John 1:18; 17:4, 6. Through him God has spoken to us in these last days, Heb. 1:1. He is the highest, the only prophet. Even the Vatican Council, cap. 4, acknowledges that the _assistentia divina_ bestowed on the pope did not consist in revelation and in the disclosure of a new doctrine. And Rome, however new its dogmas may be, still always tries as far as possible to demonstrate them from Holy Scripture and to present them as the development and explication of what is present in Scripture in germ. Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 25; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 1, art. 7; qu. 174, art. 6; Schwane, _Dogmengeschichte_ I, 2e Aufl., 1892, p. 7 f.; Heinrich, II 23 f. But by doing so it involves itself in no small difficulty. For either all the dogmas, in the same sense as, for example, the Trinity, the two natures of Christ, and so forth, are explications of elements contained in Scripture, in which case tradition is unnecessary and Scripture sufficient; or they are in fact new dogmas that have no support in Scripture, in which case the pope’s _assistentia divina_ is indeed essentially a revelation and disclosure of new doctrine. The latter may still be denied theoretically, but in practice it is nevertheless accepted. For this reason Roman Catholic theologians after the Reformation are generally more generous than before it in acknowledging that some dogmas are grounded solely in tradition. And for this reason arguments are now advanced for tradition that were not formerly used, or at least not in the same sense and to the same extent. The insufficiency of Scripture and the right of tradition are now proved from the facts that prophetic and apostolic writings have been lost, that Christ did not teach everything to his apostles, that the apostles also commanded many things orally to the congregations, and so forth. But that writings have been lost, and whether they were inspired (Bellarmine, _De verbo Dei_ IV, c. 4) or not (Augustine, _De civ._ 18, 38), is beside the point. For the question is only whether the extant Scripture contains everything that it is necessary to know for our salvation, not whether it contains everything that prophets and apostles wrote and Christ himself spoke and did. Even if further writings of prophets and apostles were discovered, they could no longer render service as Holy Scripture. The same holds for the instruction of Jesus and the apostles. They spoke and did more than has been recorded for us. Knowledge of that would be historically important but religiously unnecessary. For our salvation Scripture is sufficient, and we need no other writing, even if it came from Jesus himself. That was the doctrine of the Reformation. Quantitatively, revelation was much richer and greater than Scripture has preserved for us, but qualitatively and substantially Holy Scripture is perfectly sufficient for our salvation. Rome can name no dogmas other than Mariolatry, the infallibility of the pope, and the like that arose from tradition apart from Scripture; but all those that concern God, man, Christ, salvation, and so forth are, according to Rome as well, to be found in Scripture itself. What further witnesses do we need? Roman tradition serves only to prove the specifically Roman dogmas, but the Christian, the catholic dogmas are, according to Rome itself, all grounded in Scripture. This fact too demonstrates that Scripture is sufficient, and that the nature of the New Testament dispensation entails and demands this _sufficientia S. Scr._ Christ revealed everything fully to the apostles, either personally and orally or also through his Spirit. Through their word we believe in Christ and have fellowship with God, John 17:20; 1 John 1:3. The Holy Spirit reveals no new doctrine; he takes everything from Christ, John 16:14. In Christ, God’s revelation is complete. And likewise the Word of salvation is perfectly comprehended in Scripture. Scripture forms one whole; it itself gives the impression of an organism that has reached its full maturity. It ends where it begins. It is a circle that returns into itself. It begins with creation and ends with the re-creation of heaven and earth. The canon of the Old and New Testaments was closed only when all the new beginnings of the history of salvation were present. Hofmann, _Weissagung u. Erfüllung_ I 47. The Holy Spirit has no other task in this dispensation than to apply the work of Christ and likewise to interpret the Word of Christ. He adds nothing new to either. The work of Christ does not need to be supplemented by the good works of believers; the Word of Christ does not need to be supplemented by the tradition of the church; Christ himself does not need to be succeeded and replaced by the pope. The Roman doctrine of tradition is the denial of God’s perfect incarnation in Christ, of the all-sufficiency of his sacrifice, and of the perfection of his Word. The history of the Roman Church shows us the gradually advancing process by which a false principle intrudes itself, at first still placing itself under Christ and his Word, then placing itself alongside them, shortly afterward exalting itself above them, and finally ending in the complete replacement of Scripture by tradition, of Christ by the pope, and of the congregation by the institution. The development is surely not yet at an end. It seems an anomaly that the pope, who has gradually exalted himself above Scripture, church, council, and tradition, is appointed by fallible men, even though they are cardinals. Who can better designate his successor than the one who is himself infallible? It is therefore entirely possible that papal sovereignty will in the future prove incompatible with the power of the cardinals. In any case, Rome has not yet reached the end of the road of man’s deification.
