## 10.

Although the Reformation thus sought its strength over against Rome in Scripture and maintained its necessity, it did not thereby deny that before Moses the church had existed for centuries without Scripture. It is also true that the New Testament church was founded through the preaching of the apostles and existed for a long time without a New Testament canon. Further, the church is still always nourished and planted in the pagan world through the proclamation of the gospel. The books of the Old and New Testaments arose gradually; before the printing press they circulated in small numbers; many believers in earlier and later times died without ever having read and investigated Scripture; and even now religious life seeks satisfaction of its needs not only in Scripture but at least as much in all manner of devotional literature.

All this can be freely acknowledged without in any way detracting from the necessity of Scripture. Indeed, if it had pleased him, God could certainly have preserved the church in the truth in another way than by means of a written Word. The necessity of Scripture is not absolute but _ex hypothesi beneplacentiae Dei_.

But understood in this way, this necessity is elevated above all doubt. Man has at all times lived only by the Word that proceeds from the mouth of God, Matt. 4:4. From the beginning the Word of God was the seed of the church. The church certainly existed before Moses without Scripture. But there was nevertheless a _verbum_ ἀγραφον before it became ἐγγραφον. The church has never lived from itself or rested in itself, but always lived through the Word of God. Rome does not teach this either, but assumes a tradition that infallibly preserves the Word of God. This must, however, be stated over against those who cause revelation to consist only in life, in the infusion of divine powers, in the awakening of religious affections. The church may therefore be older than the written Word, but it is nevertheless younger than the spoken Word. Zanchi, _Op._ VIII 343 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. theol._ I, c. 15; _Synopsis pur. theol._, disp. 2; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ I, cap. 1, § 5 sq.

The common claim that the New Testament church existed for a long time without Scripture must also be correctly understood. It is true that the canon of New Testament writings was generally acknowledged only in the second half of the second century. But the Christian churches had the Old Testament from the beginning. They were founded by the spoken word of the apostles. Very soon many churches came into possession of apostolic writings, which were also given to others to read, soon served for public reading in the churches, and spread very quickly. It goes without saying that, as long as the apostles lived and visited the churches, no distinction was yet made between their spoken and written word; tradition and Scripture were, as it were, still one.

But when the first period had passed and the distance from the apostles grew greater, the writings of the apostles rose in significance and their necessity gradually increased. The _necessitas S. Scripturae_ is indeed not a stable but a steadily growing property. Scripture was not always necessary in its entirety for the whole church. Scripture arose and was completed gradually. As revelation advanced, Scripture too increased in extent. Every period of the church had enough in that portion of Scripture that then existed, just as it had enough in the revelation that had occurred up to that point.

Scripture, like revelation, is an organic whole that grew; the plant was contained in the seed, the fruit in the germ. Both revelation and Scripture kept pace with the state of the church, and conversely. No conclusion for the present can therefore be drawn from earlier conditions of the church. Grant that the church before Moses was without Scripture; grant that before the completion of revelation the church never possessed the whole of Scripture. Nothing follows from this for the dispensation of the church in which we live, in which revelation has ended and Scripture is complete. For this dispensation Scripture is not merely useful and good, but also decidedly necessary _ad esse ecclesiae_.
