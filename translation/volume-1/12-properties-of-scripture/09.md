## 9.

These views that Scripture is not necessary were introduced into modern theology especially by Schleiermacher.
In his _Glaubenslehre_ §§ 128, 129, he says that faith in Christ does not rest on the authority of Scripture but precedes faith in Scripture and is precisely what causes us to accord Scripture a special esteem.
Among the first Christians faith in Christ did not arise from Holy Scripture, and therefore it cannot arise from it among us either, for faith must have the same ground among them and among us.
Scripture is thus not a source of religion but is indeed its _norma_; it is the first member in the series of Christian writings, stands closest to the source—that is, revelation in Christ—and therefore faced little danger of taking impure elements into itself.
But all the writings of the evangelists and apostles, like all subsequent Christian writings, proceeded from one and the same Spirit, the _Gemeingeist_ of the Christian church.
The church is not built on Scripture, but Scripture proceeded from the church.
Through Schleiermacher these ideas became the common possession of modern theology.
They seem so true and so self-evident that no thought is given to doubt or criticism.
Among almost all theologians one can find the conception that the church existed before Scripture and therefore can also exist independently of it.
The church rests in itself and lives from itself, that is, from the Spirit who dwells within it.
Holy Scripture, which proceeded from the church at the outset in the freshness of the church’s youth, is indeed the _norma_ but not the source.
The source is the personal, living Christ who dwells in the congregation; dogmatics is a description of that life, an explication of the congregation’s religious consciousness, and takes as its guide Scripture, which first and most clearly gave expression to that life of the congregation.
The church is thus properly the _Verfasserin der Bibel_, and the Bible is the _Reflex der Gemeinde_; Lange, _Philos. Dogm._ § 77; Rothe, _Zur Dogm._ 333 f.; Frank, _Syst. der chr. Gewissheit_ II 57 f.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Glaub._ I³ 190 f.; Hofstede de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._ 71 f., 97 f.; Saussaye, _Mĳne Theol. van d. l. S._ 49 f.; Gunning and de la Saussaye Jr., _Het ethisch beginsel der theol._ 34, etc.

All these ideas—those of Rome, Anabaptism, Mysticism, Rationalism, Lessing, Schleiermacher, and so forth—are most closely related to one another.
Schleiermacher especially, through his reversal of the relation between Scripture and church, furnished powerful support to Rome.
All agree that Scripture is not necessary but at most useful, and that the church can also exist from and through itself.
The difference lies only in this: Rome seeks the ground and possibility for this continued existence of the Christian religion in the institutional church, that is, in the infallible pope; Schleiermacher _c. s._ in the congregation as organism, that is, in the religious community; and Mysticism and Rationalism in religious individuals.
All locate the continued existence of the church in the guidance of the Holy Spirit and the indwelling of Christ, but this guidance finds its organ in the pope at Rome, in the organism of the congregation according to Schleiermacher, and in each individual believer in Anabaptism.
It is easy to see that Rome occupies the strongest position here.
For certainly there is a guidance of the Holy Spirit in the congregation; Christ has risen from the dead, lives in heaven, and dwells and works in his church on earth.
There is a mystical union between Christ and his body.
The word alone is insufficient; the _principium externum_ also requires a _principium internum_.
Protestantism knew all this very well and confessed it wholeheartedly.
But the question was whether or not the church, for the _conscious_ life of religion, was bound to the word, to Scripture.
Religion, after all, is not only a matter of the heart, the emotions, and the will, but also of the head.
God must also be served and loved with the understanding.
For its conscious life, therefore, the church must have a source from which it draws the truth.
Now Rome, with its infallible pope, can assert that Scripture is not necessary.
The infallibility of the church does in fact render Scripture superfluous.
But Protestantism has no infallible organ, whether in the institution, the organism, or the individual members of the congregation.
When it denies the necessity of Scripture, it weakens itself, strengthens Rome, and loses the truth, which is an indispensable element of religion.
For that reason the Reformation insisted so strongly on the necessity of Holy Scripture.
Scripture was the δος μοι που στω of the Reformation.
It succeeded because, over against the authority of church, councils, and pope, it could set the authority of God’s holy word.
Whoever abandons this standpoint of the Reformation unwittingly labors at the building up of Rome.
For if not Scripture but the church is necessary for the knowledge of religious truth, then the church becomes the indispensable _medium gratiae_.
The word loses its central place and retains only a preparatory, pedagogical significance.
Scripture may be useful and good, but it is not necessary, either for the church as a whole or for believers in particular.
