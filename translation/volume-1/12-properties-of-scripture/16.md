## 16.

Because of this _perspicuitas_, Scripture also has the _facultas se ipsam interpretandi_ and is the _supremus judex controversiarum_. _Synopsis pur. theol._, disp. 5, §20 sq.; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, lib. I, c. 45; Turretin, _Theol. El._, loc. II, qu. 20; Amesius, _Bellarm. enervatus_, lib. I, c. 5; Cloppenburg, _De Canone Theol._, disp. 11–15, _Op._ II 64 sq.; Moor, _Comm. in Markii Comp._ I 429 sq.; Gerhard, _Loci Theol._, loc. I, capp. 21, 22; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._, 6e Aufl., p. 42 f.

Scripture interprets itself: obscure passages are explained by clear ones, and the fundamental thoughts of Scripture as a whole serve to illuminate its parts. This was the _interpretatio secundum analogiam fidei_, which was also advocated by the Reformers. The Reformers too did not approach Scripture _voraussetzungslos_. They adopted the doctrine of Scripture, the Apostles’ Creed, and the decisions of the first councils almost without criticism. They were not revolutionaries and did not wish to begin again from the very start, but protested only against the errors that had crept in. The Reformation was not the emancipation of the natural man but of the _Christenmensch_.

From the beginning, therefore, the Reformers had an _analogia fidei_ in which they themselves stood and according to which they interpreted Scripture. By that _analogia fidei_ they originally understood the sense derived from the clear passages of Holy Scripture itself, which was afterward set down in the confessions. Voetius, _Disp._ V 9 sq., 419 sq.; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 436, VI praefatio; Turretin, _Theol. El._ I, qu. 19; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ I 217 f.; Zöckler, _Handbuch_ I 663 f.; Luz, _Hermeneutik_, 154–176.

In connection with this, the church too had a calling with respect to the interpretation of Holy Scripture. By virtue of the _potestas doctrinae_ granted to her by Christ and the gift of interpretation bestowed on her by the Holy Spirit, 1 Cor. 14:3, 29; Rom. 12:6; Eph. 4:11 f., the church has the duty not only to preserve Scripture but also to interpret and defend it, to formulate the truth in her confession, and to expose and resist errors. The church too, therefore, is within her sphere and on her own terrain a _judex controversiarum_, and she must test and judge every opinion by Holy Scripture.

For this she need not be infallible, since the judge in the state is likewise bound by the law but fallible in his decisions. So it is also in the church. Scripture is the _norma_; the church is the judge. But here too there is a higher appeal. Rome denies this and says that the church’s judgment is the last and highest. From her judgment not even an appeal to the divine judgment remains possible. She binds the conscience.

The Reformation, however, maintained that a church, however venerable, could nevertheless err. Her interpretation is not _magisterialis_ but _ministerialis_. It can bind the conscience only insofar as someone recognizes it as divine and infallible. Whether it actually agrees with God’s Word cannot be decided by any earthly power; each person can decide that only for himself. _Synopsis pur. theol._ 5, 25 sq. The church may then cast someone out as a heretic, but in the end he stands or falls before his own Lord. The simplest believer may and, if necessary, must oppose an entire church with Scripture in hand, as Luther did against Rome.

Only in this way are both the freedom of the Christian and the sovereignty of God maintained. From Scripture there is no higher appeal. It is the highest tribunal. No power or judgment stands above it. It is Scripture that finally decides for everyone in his conscience. And therefore it is the _judex supremus controversiarum_.
