## 4.

Over against this Roman Catholic doctrine the Reformation set the self-attestation of Scripture: Calvin, _Inst._ I, c. 7; Ursinus, _Tract. Theol._, 1584, pp. 8 sq.; Polanus, _Synt._ I, capp. 23–30; Zanchi, _De S. Scriptura_, _Op._ VIII 332–353; Junius, _Theses Theol._, capp. 3–5; _Synopsis pur. theol._, disp. 2, § 29 sq.; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ I, c. 3, etc. In this dispute the question was not whether the church had no calling to fulfill with respect to Scripture. It was generally admitted that the church is of great significance for Scripture. Its testimony carries great weight and is a _motivum credibilitatis_. The church of the first centuries possesses in its testimonies a strong support for Scripture. For every person the church is the guide to Scripture.

In this sense Augustine’s statement that he was moved by the church to believe Scripture is and remains true. Protestant theologians—Calvin, _Inst._ I 7, 3; Polanus, _Synt._, p. 30; Turretin, _De S. Scr. auctoritate_, disp. 3, § 13 s.; Gerhard, _Loci theol._ I, c. 3, § 51—have weakened this statement of Augustine by applying it only to the past, to the origin of faith. But Augustine’s reasoning in the passage cited is clear. He places his Manichaean opponent before this dilemma: you must either say to me, “Believe the Catholics”—but these very people warn me not to believe you—or, “Do not believe the Catholics”—but then you cannot appeal to the gospel over against me either, _quia ipsi Evangelio, catholicis praedicantibus, credidi_.

For Augustine the church is indeed a motive of faith, which he here uses over against the Manichaean. But there is a difference between a motive and the final ground of faith. Elsewhere, _C. Faustum_, lib. 32, c. 19, he himself elucidates how he sees a motive of faith in the church when he says: _cur non potius evangelicae autoritati, tam fundatae, tam stabilitae, tanta gloria diffamatae atque ab apostolorum temporibus usque ad nostra tempora per successiones certissimas commendatae, non te subdis?_ Cf. _De util. cred._ c. 14. The church with its dignity, power, hierarchy, and so forth, always made a deep impression upon Augustine. It continually moved him to faith, supported and strengthened him in doubt and conflict, and was the firm hand that repeatedly led him to Scripture. But Augustine does not thereby mean that the authority of Scripture depends upon the church, that the church is the final and deepest ground of his faith. Elsewhere he says clearly that Holy Scripture possesses authority by itself and must be believed for its own sake. Clausen, _Augustinus S. Scr. interpres_, 1827, p. 125; Dorner, _Augustinus_, Berlin, 1873, S. 237 f.; Reuter, _Augustinische Studien_, Gotha, 1887, S. 348 f.; Schmidt, _Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._ VI 235 f.; Hase, _Protest. Polemik_, 5te Aufl., Leipzig, 1891, S. 81; Harnack, _D. G._ III 70 f.

The church has and retains for every believer until death a rich and profound pedagogical significance. The cloud of witnesses surrounding us can strengthen and encourage us in the conflict. But this is altogether different from the authority of Scripture depending upon the church. Even Rome does not yet dare to state this openly. The _Vaticanum_ acknowledged the books of the Old and New Testaments as canonical also because _Spiritu S. inspirante conscripti Deum autorem habent_ and were as such handed over to the church. Roman Catholic theologians distinguish between the authority of Scripture _quoad se_ and _quoad nos_. But that distinction cannot apply here.

For if the church is the final and deepest ground why I believe Scripture, then the church, and not Scripture, is αὐτοπιστος. And now it must be one of two things. Scripture contains a testimony, a doctrine concerning itself, concerning its inspiration and authority, in which case the church does nothing but receive and confirm that testimony. Or Scripture itself does not teach such an inspiration and authority, in which case the church’s dogma concerning Scripture is condemned for the Protestant.

Roman Catholic theologians accordingly find themselves in no small contradiction. On the one hand, in the doctrine of Scripture they attempt to demonstrate its inspiration and authority from Scripture itself. On the other hand, upon reaching the doctrine of the church, they attempt to weaken those proofs and demonstrate that only the testimony of the church provides an unshakable certainty. If, by contrast, authority _quoad se_ depends upon Scripture itself, then it is also _quoad nos_ the final ground of our faith. The church can only acknowledge what exists; it cannot make what does not exist.

The accusation that in this way circular reasoning is used and Scripture is proved by Scripture itself can be cast back upon Rome, since it proves the church by Scripture and Scripture by the church. If Rome objects that in the former case it uses Scripture not as God’s Word but as human, credible, and reliable testimony, the Protestant theologian can adopt this observation as well: first inspiration is derived from Scripture as reliable testimony, and afterward Scripture as God’s Word is proved by this inspiration.

But of much greater significance is this: in every field of science, and therefore also in theology, the _principia_ stand firm by themselves. The truth of a _principium_ cannot be demonstrated but only acknowledged. _Principium creditur propter se, non propter aliud. Principii principium haberi non potest nec quaeri debet._ Gerhard, _Loc. theol._ I, cap. 3; Zanchi, _Op._ VIII 339 sq.; Polanus, _Synt._ I, cap. 23 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._, loc. 2, qu. 6; Trelcatius, _Schol. et method. loc. comm. S. Theol. Institutio_, 1651, p. 26.

Scripture itself also clearly teaches that not the church but the Word of God, written or unwritten, is αὐτοπιστος. The church at all times has been bound to that Word of God, insofar as it existed and in the form in which it existed. Israel receives the law at Horeb. Jesus and the apostles subject themselves to the Old Testament Scripture. From the beginning the Christian church is bound to the spoken and written word of the apostles. The Word of God is the foundation of the church, Deut. 4:1; Isa. 8:20; Ezek. 20:19; Luke 16:29; John 5:39; Eph. 2:20; 2 Tim. 3:14; 2 Pet. 1:19, etc.

The church can indeed testify to the Word, but the Word stands above it. It can give no one faith in the Word of God within the heart. The Word of God alone can do this, through itself and the power of the Holy Spirit, Jer. 23:29; Mark 4:28; Luke 8:11; Rom. 1:16; Heb. 4:12; 1 Pet. 1:23. From this alone it already appears that the church stands beneath Scripture. The church and believers can therefore learn from Scripture itself its inspiration, authority, and canonicity, but they can never arbitrarily decree and establish these.

The Reformation preferred some uncertainty to an assurance obtained only by an arbitrary decision of the church. For Scripture indeed nowhere gives a catalog of the books it contains. Concerning some books there was disagreement in the earliest Christian church and later as well. The text does not possess the _integritas_ that Lutheran and Reformed theologians so gladly wished for. Nevertheless, the Reformation maintained the self-attestation of Scripture over against the claims of Rome, subordinated the church to the Word of God, and thereby saved the freedom of the Christian.
