## 18.

Over against this Roman doctrine of tradition, the Reformation set the doctrine of the perfection and sufficiency of Holy Scripture. The legitimacy of this opposition to Rome has been placed in a clear light by the development of the concept of tradition itself. The earliest Christian churches, like churches among the Gentiles even today, were founded through the preached Word. The teaching and practices they had received from the apostles or their companions were handed down for a considerable time from mouth to mouth and from generation to generation. This concept of tradition was clear: it denoted the teaching and practices that had been received from the apostles and were preserved and propagated in the churches. But as the distance separating the churches from the apostolic age grew greater, it became increasingly difficult to determine whether something was truly of apostolic origin. The African church therefore protested against the exaggerated value attached to this tradition, especially in the latter half of the second century in opposition to Gnosticism. Tertullian, _de virg. vel._ c. 1, said: _Dominus noster veritatem se, non consuetudinem cognominavit._ Likewise Cyprian, _Epist._ 74, set over against the tradition invoked by the bishop of Rome the texts Isa. 29:13; Matt. 15:9; 1 Tim. 6:3-5, and said: _consuetudo sine veritate vetustas erroris est._ Christ called himself not custom but truth. The former must yield to the latter. It therefore became necessary to define tradition more precisely and to specify its marks. Vincent of Lérins found in his _Commonitorium_, cap. 2, the criteria of an apostolic tradition in the fact that something _ubique, semper et ab omnibus creditum est. Hoc est etenim vere proprieque catholicum._ At first, then, the mark of tradition consisted in its apostolic origin. Now it was added that something could in that case be regarded as apostolic in origin if it was truly universal, catholic. The _apostolicitas_ becomes recognizable by the _universitas, antiquitas_, and _consensio_. The _Tridentinum_, the _Vaticanum_, and the theologians too follow these criteria of Vincent in their definition of tradition. But materially there is a departure; the logic of the position led further. It could not be maintained that something was apostolic only when it actually had been believed always, everywhere, and by all. Of what doctrine or practice could such absolute catholicity be demonstrated? The three criteria were therefore gradually weakened. The church may indeed declare nothing new to be dogma and must hold to tradition, but the preservation of that tradition is not to be conceived mechanically, like a treasure in a field, but organically, as Mary treasured the shepherds’ words and pondered them in her heart, Heinrich II 12. A truth, then, may very well not have been believed previously or universally; it is nevertheless infallible apostolic tradition if only it is now universally believed. The two criteria _antiquitas_ and _universitas_ are thus not copulative but distributive marks of tradition; they are not both required together and at the same time, but one of the two is enough. In fact, the _antiquitas_ has thereby been sacrificed to the _universitas_. But the latter in turn is also restricted. The question arose as to who was the organ for the preservation and recognition of tradition. The church in general could not be this organ. Möhler, _Symbolik_, 6e Aufl. 357, did still identify tradition with _das fortwährend in den Herzen der Gläubigen lebende Wort_, but this answer was conceived far more along Protestant than Roman lines. The task of preserving and defining doctrine could not and might not be entrusted to the church in general, that is, to the laity. Within the church a distinction must be made between the _ecclesia audiens_ and the _ecclesia docens_. The two indeed belong together and are imperishable, but the former possesses only an _infallibilitas passiva_; that is, it is infallible only in its believing, because and for as long as it remains connected with the _ecclesia docens_. But the latter too is still not the proper organ of doctrine. Gallicanism, the Old Episcopal Clergy, and the Old Catholics went no further than this and ascribed infallibility to the bishops collectively. But this position is untenable. When are those bishops infallible? Apart from a council, or only when assembled in council? If the latter, are they infallible only when unanimous, or is the majority alone infallible? How large must this majority be? Is a majority of one vote sufficient? Is the council infallible without the pope, and even over against him, or only in agreement with him? All these were questions that placed Gallicanism in serious difficulty. The papal system therefore went a step further and ascribed infallibility to the pope. This primacy of the pope is the product of a centuries-long development, the consequence of a current of thought that was present in the church from a very early time. Gradually the pope came to be regarded as the infallible organ of divine truth and therefore also of tradition. Bellarmine, _de Verbo Dei_, lib. 4, c. 9, included among the marks of tradition this rule as well: _id sine dubio credendum esse, ex apostolica traditione descendere, quod pro tali habetur in illis ecclesiis, ubi est integra et continuata successio ab Apostolis._ In antiquity, he goes on to say, there were many such churches besides Rome. Today, however, this succession remains only in Rome. And therefore _ex testimonio hujus solius ecclesiae sumi potest certum argumentum ad probandas Apostolicas traditiones_. The church of Rome defines and decides what apostolic tradition is. Later theologians, especially among the Jesuits, developed this further. And on July 18, 1870, in the fourth session of the Vatican Council, infallibility was openly promulgated as dogma. Now it is indeed certain that the pope in this infallibility of his is not conceived apart from the church, especially not from the _ecclesia docens_. Furthermore, the creeds, decrees, liturgies, _patres_, _doctores_, and the entire history of the church are so many monuments of tradition with which the pope associates himself when defining a dogma and which he must take into account. Nevertheless, tradition is not formally identical with the content of all those monuments. Tradition is infallible; but what tradition is, is determined in the final instance by the pope alone, with, without, or if necessary over against the church and the councils. The judgment whether and to what extent something has been believed _semper, ubique et ab omnibus_ cannot rest with the church, either the _ecclesia audiens_ or the _ecclesia docens_, but belongs as a matter of course to the infallible pope alone. When the pope promulgates a dogma, it is _eo ipso_ apostolic tradition. The criterion of tradition was thus sought successively in apostolicity, in catholicity, in episcopal succession, and in papal decision. With that the endpoint was reached. The infallible pope is the _principium formale_ of Romanism. _Roma locuta, res finita._ Pope and church, pope and Christianity, are one. _Ubi Papa, ibi ecclesia, ibi religio Christiana, ibi Spiritus._ From the pope there is no higher appeal, not even to God. Through the pope God himself speaks to humanity; Perrone I 229, IX 279; Jansen I 804, 822 s. 829; Heinrich I 726 note, II 148 f. 537; Maistre, _du Pape_, _Oeuvres Choisies de Joseph de Maistre_ III, Paris, n.d., 71.

This outcome of the development of tradition demonstrates the falsity of the principle that was operative in it from the beginning. Papal infallibility can be treated at length only later, in the doctrine of the church. But it is clear that the good and true element at stake in maintaining tradition during the earliest centuries has been entirely lost. At that time the concern was to preserve what, by virtue of apostolic institution, was believed and practiced in the churches. It was obvious that people then attached great weight to tradition and did not yet recognize the indispensability and necessity of the apostolic writings. But the mark of apostolicity, which at that time naturally belonged to tradition, had to disappear as the apostolic age receded further into the distance. The relative independence of tradition alongside Scripture disappeared more and more. The streams of Scripture and tradition flowed together. And soon after the death of the apostles and their contemporaries, it became impossible to demonstrate that something was of apostolic origin except by an appeal to the apostolic writings. The apostolic origin of no dogma that the Roman church confesses outside and apart from Scripture can be proved. Rome’s doctrine of tradition serves only to justify deviations from Scripture and from the apostles. The veneration of Mary, the seven sacraments, papal infallibility, and so forth—these are the _dogmata_ that cannot dispense with tradition. In an evil hour apostolic tradition was identified with ecclesiastical customs and papal decisions. At Rome, tradition is _die gemeine Superstition, das Heidenthum_; Harnack, _D. G._ III 559 note.
