### B. The Authority of Scripture

## 3.

The authority of Scripture has at all times been acknowledged in the Christian church. Jesus and the apostles believed the Old Testament to be the Word of God and ascribed divine authority to it. The Christian church was born and grew up under the authority of Scripture. What the apostles wrote must be accepted as if Christ himself had written it, Augustine, _De cons. evang._ I 35. And in his exposition of 2 Tim. 3:16 Calvin declares that we owe Scripture _eandem reverentiam_ as to God. Until the previous century this authority of Scripture was established in all churches and among all Christians.

By contrast, a serious disagreement arose between Rome and the Reformation concerning the ground on which that authority rests. Church fathers and scholastics still frequently taught the self-attestation of Scripture, but the driving force of the Roman Catholic principle has increasingly placed the church before Scripture. The church, so runs the current general Roman Catholic doctrine, precedes Scripture temporally and logically. It existed before Scripture and owes its origin, existence, and authority not to Scripture but exists in and through itself—that is, through Christ or the Holy Spirit who dwells in it. Scripture, by contrast, proceeded precisely from the church and is now acknowledged, confirmed, preserved, interpreted, defended, and so forth, by it.

Scripture therefore needs the church, but the church does not need Scripture. Without the church there is no Scripture, but without Scripture there is indeed a church. The church with its infallible tradition is the original and sufficient means for preserving and communicating revelation. Holy Scripture was added later, is insufficient in itself, but is useful and good as support and confirmation of tradition.

In practice Scripture becomes entirely dependent on the church in Rome. The authenticity, integrity, inspiration, canonicity, and authority of Scripture are established by the church. A distinction is then made, however, that Scripture depends entirely on the church not _quoad se_ but _quoad nos_. By its acknowledgment the church does not make Scripture inspired, canonical, genuine, and so forth, but it is nevertheless the only body that can know these properties of Scripture infallibly.

For the self-testimony of Scripture does not establish that precisely these books of the Old and New Testaments—and no others and no fewer—are inspired. Nowhere does Holy Scripture provide a catalog of the books belonging to it. The texts that teach inspiration never cover the whole of Scripture; 2 Tim. 3:16 applies only to the Old Testament. Moreover, an appeal to Scripture itself for the inspiration of Scripture is still only a circular proof. Protestants are also divided among themselves concerning the books belonging to Scripture; Luther’s judgment concerning James differs from Calvin’s, and so forth.

The proofs derived from the church fathers and others for Scripture are not firm and solid enough. As _motiva credibilitatis_ they possess great value, but they nevertheless yield only probability, human and thus fallible certainty. The church alone gives divine, infallible assurance, as Augustine also said: _ego vero Evangelio non crederem, nisi me catholicae ecclesiae commoveret auctoritas_, _C. epist. Manich._ cap. 5; _C. Faustum_, l. 28, capp. 2, 4, 6. Protestants too could therefore accept and acknowledge Scripture as God’s Word because they received it from the hand of the church, Bellarmine, _De Verbo Dei_ IV, cap. 4; Perrone, _Praelect. Theol._ IX 71 sq.; Heinrich, _Dogm._ I 775 f.; Jansen, _Theol._ I 766 sq.

The _Vaticanum_, sess. 3, cap. 2, acknowledged the books of the Old and New Testaments as canonical _propterea quod Sp. S°. inspirante conscripti Deum habent autorem atque ut tales ipsi ecclesiae traditi sunt_. Guided by these thoughts, Rome at Trent, sess. 4, and at the Vatican, sess. 3, cap. 2, established the canon, included in it—following the example of the Greek translation and the practice of the church fathers—the Apocrypha of the Old Testament as well, and moreover declared the _editio Vulgata_ to be the authentic text, so that it possesses decisive authority in church and theology.
