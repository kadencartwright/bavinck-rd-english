## 5.

Finally, the same standpoint is also adopted by R. A. Lipsius, _Lehrbuch der ev. prot. Dogm._, 1876, 2e Aufl., 1879, 3e Aufl., 1893; _Dogmatische Beiträge_, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1878, also published separately, Leipzig, Barth, 1878; _Neue Beiträge_, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1885, and published separately under the title _Philosophie und Religion_, Leipzig, 1885; _Die Hauptpunkte der christ. Glaubenslehre_, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1889, S. 1–41, and published separately, Braunschw., 1889, 2e Aufl., 1891. Lipsius too restricts science to the domain of internal and external experience, _Lehrb. der ev. prot. Dogm._ § 1; _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1885, S. 181 f.; 1889, S. 1 f. But in distinction from Ritschl and Herrmann, Lipsius nevertheless acknowledges a certain legitimacy of metaphysics. Our reason feels a need and an impulse to ascend from the world of experience to an _unbedingten Grund_, and thus forms the concepts of the Absolute, the soul, and so forth, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 200, 278 f., 372, 395; ibid., 1889, S. 3. Our spirit strives for unity, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 283. But this metaphysical or philosophical thinking is no longer science proper; it produces no positive content and does not increase our possession of truth, as Biedermann thinks; it yields only _Formbestimmingen_, _Grundbegriffe_, _regulative Prinzipien_, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 275–283. If, nevertheless, we wish to invest it with some content and obtain transcendent knowledge through it, we transfer sensible forms to it and involve ourselves in an antinomy. The logical and the symbolic conceptions of the metaphysical conflict with one another, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 281–283; ibid., 1889, S. 4. Along this way, therefore, no knowledge of the supersensible can be obtained. But there is another way, that of practical _sittliche Nöthigungen_. For the human being is a thinking but also a willing and acting being, and he feels compelled to maintain himself as such over against the world. From the _Widerspruch_ between the human spirit and nature, religion is born in him. Religion is a practical concern of the spirit and arises not primarily from theoretical but from practical needs. The practical, ethical impulse to maintain himself in his existence impels the human being to faith in God, to the postulation of a supersensible world. Faith therefore begins where science ends, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 377 f., 557 f. This faith brings with it a certainty of its own—not scientific but moral certainty, a certainty of experience resting on the _erlebte Gewissheit_ of my own person, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 385 f., 438 f. For another this certainty may be nothing but imagination, but for the subject himself it is certain, ibid., 387 f. Such experience is also the sole criterion for the truth of the Christian religion, of the revelation in Christ. The appropriation of historical revelation as a personal possession is the only direct proof of the truth of the Christian religion. The personal experience of believers confirms historical revelation, just as the latter awakens and strengthens the former, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 600 f. And the Christian, thus believing, then confesses supersensible realities; in the dogmas he gives not merely a description of states of mind and _Werthurtheile_, but _Seinsurtheile_ concerning the relation of the human being to God and of God to the human being and the world. These _religiöse Aussagen_ are objectively true if they stand in an indissoluble, logically necessary connection with the realization of our highest destiny in life. Not accidental but necessary _Werthurtheile_ prove the objective truth of religious statements, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 440, 445. For the believer himself these proofs are sufficient; and over against unbelievers he still has an indirect apologetic proof in the fact that practical _Nöthigungen_ compel the human being to religion. Religion is no compulsion, no natural force, but it is practically and psychically necessary and is grounded to that extent in human nature, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 627 f., 639 f. Dogmatically, moreover, the believer has the task of joining the content of faith with all his knowledge of the world into a single whole. Herrmann kept faith and knowledge separated as rigorously as possible and to the very end. But although Lipsius allows the content of each to arise from its own source, he nevertheless ultimately wishes to unite them in an _einheitliche Weltanschauung_. Dogmatics indeed cannot prove the content of faith or elevate faith into knowledge, but it can and must bring the Christian, teleological worldview into connection with the causal worldview, with the knowledge of the world obtained from elsewhere, and then it must demonstrate that there is no conflict, that the conflict is only apparent, that there is in fact unity. Although the dogmas contain no scientific truth, no theoretical _Erkenntnisse_, and although they are all _bildlich_ and _anthropomorphistisch_, they nevertheless may not conflict with the established results of science. The unity of our spirit forbids the acceptance of a double truth. The religious idea of God and the concept of the Absolute, freedom and necessity, the teleological and causal worldviews, and so forth, must be compatible, _Jahrb._, 1885, S. 648 f., 658 f., 662 f.; ibid., 1889, S. 6; _Dogm._ § 3. Cf. on Lipsius, among others, Ed. von Hartmann, _Die Krisis des Christ. in der mod. Theol._, Berlin, 1880, S. 69 f.; Biedermann, _Christl. Dogm._, 1884, I 58 f., 160 f.; Flügel, _Die spekul. Theol. der Gegenwart_, 1888, S. 95 f.; Pfleiderer, _Entw. der prot. Theol._, 1891, S. 241 f.; Bruining, _Theol. Tijdschr._, Nov. 1894; etc.
