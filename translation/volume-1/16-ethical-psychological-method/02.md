## 2.

Related to this method is the moral proof as presented by Kant. Kant assumed two sources for our knowledge: _Sinnlichkeit_ for its matter and the understanding for its form. But above these there stands reason, _reine Vernunft_, whose synthetic _a priori Grundsatz_ is that it ascends from the _Bedingte_ to the _Unbedingte_, _Kr. d. r. Vern._, ed. Kirchmann, 5te Aufl., p. 300; cf. 310, 312, 319, 347. By virtue of this peculiarity, theoretical reason forms various _Grundsätze_ or ideas, ibid., 308, which are absolute, _unbedingt_, transcendent, and are produced by reason not arbitrarily but in accordance with its nature, ibid., 312 f. These ideas are chiefly three: God, freedom, and immortality, ibid., 317 f. These ideas, however, cannot be demonstrated in their objective truth but can only be derived subjectively from the nature of reason. The objects of these ideas are not perceptible and therefore not knowable. We arrive at those ideas only through a _nothwendigen Vernunftschluss_, ibid., 321. All three are theoretically indemonstrable. They do not increase our knowledge but merely regulate and order it, causing us to view and consider everything “as if” those ideas possessed reality, ibid., 337, 425, 512, 530, 535, 537, 539, 540 f. Theoretical reason cannot teach us that they possess reality or what they are. But reason is not only theoretical; it is also practical. That is, it bears within itself a moral law, a categorical imperative, and holds duty before us. And it demands that we fulfill this duty unconditionally, without ulterior motive, solely from respect for duty as such. It thus reveals that man belongs to another order besides that of nature, namely to a moral world order; that he must strive for a highest good, which far surpasses the sensible goods of life and consists in nothing other than the unity of virtue and happiness. If this command of duty is not an illusion but can be carried out, and if the highest good, the unity of virtue and happiness, will actually be attained one day—if, in other words, the moral world order within and outside us will one day triumph over the order of nature—then God, freedom, and immortality must exist. These three are therefore postulates of practical reason; their reality is demanded by the moral law, practical reason needs their existence. _Kr. der prakt. Vernunft_, ed. Kirchmann, 3e Aufl., 1882, p. 159 f.

This is, in essence, Kant’s famous theory of postulates. But it is not above criticism. First, it is unclear who derives these postulates from practical reason. Kant seems to think that practical reason itself does so. But then there are two practical reasons, an antecedent and a subsequent one: one that holds duty before us and one that by reasoning from it concludes to the existence of the ideas. Practical reason then itself becomes theoretical again, Rauwenhoff, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._, 325, 328. Another objection is of greater weight: whatever the ground may be upon which theoretical or practical reason concludes to the existence of these ideas, what is the compelling motive for those postulates? If that ground, that motive, lies in practical reason by itself, in the categorical imperative, in the command of duty as such, then even the most immoral person can infer from the phenomenon of that practical reason the reality of these three ideas. Now it sometimes seems that Kant actually means this. He repeatedly gives the impression that practical reason by itself, without anything more, must posit these postulates in order to realize itself. He says not only that every morally developed person is competent to postulate in this way, but that it is unavoidable for every rational being to reason in this way; that the suppositions of the existence of God, freedom, and immortality are as necessary as the moral law itself, _Kr. d. pr. V._, 173 note; and he argues precisely at pp. 146 f., 149 f., that in order to be practicable the moral law must assume those three ideas as existing. But if this is Kant’s meaning, then we are dealing here not with psychologically mediated but with objectively logical postulates; and the question arises: why, then, is such a logical postulate not knowledge, not an element of theoretical reason? Why does Kant call this knowledge only practical? There is therefore more reason to think that the ground and motive for these postulates lie not in practical reason by itself but in the moral disposition, in the moral will of the person who earnestly, though with weakness, tries to obey the duty prescribed to him by practical reason. He therefore declares in _Kr. d. r. Vernunft_, p. 638 f., that _moralische Glaube_ is a holding-for-true on psychological, moral grounds; this moral faith therefore does not say “it is certain,” but “I am morally certain”; it therefore rests on the _Voraussetzung moralischer Gesinnungen_, ibid., 639. Likewise he says, _Kr. d. pr. V._, p. 172: the _Rechtschaffene_ may say, “I will that there be a God; I will not let this faith be taken from me; my moral interest demands the existence of God”; and at p. 173: moral faith is no command—a faith that is commanded is an absurdity; and at pp. 173–175: the consciousness of duty is objective, and so too is the possibility of acting morally and being happy, but whether this connection between virtue and happiness is established by a personal God or by the coherence of nature, we do not know. Moral interest decides the matter here; it stands _in unserer Wahl_, but practical _Vernunft_ decides in favor of faith in a wise Creator of the world. Indeed, a double striving is perceptible in Kant: on the one hand to maintain the rationality and on the other the freedom of faith. Faith in God, freedom, and immortality is objective insofar as it is postulated by the practical reason proper to all, but it is nevertheless also dependent on the moral disposition of the individual person.
