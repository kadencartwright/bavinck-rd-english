## 8.

A final objection to the tendency described above is that it must always lead to a certain dualism of believing and knowing. Some have expressed this very starkly. In the Middle Ages, scholasticism ended among some theologians with the proposition that something could be true in theology and false in philosophy. Jacobi confessed himself to be a pagan with the understanding but a Christian with the heart. Herrmann called the reality of faith an illusion from the standpoint of science. The ethical Modernists demanded a complete separation between philosophy and religious life. Most, however, do not dare to go so far and recognize the impossibility of saying no as philosophers and yes as religious persons. They acknowledge that in the end there must nevertheless be agreement between the declarations of faith and the results of science. Philosophy may not be able to justify religious conceptions, but it must nevertheless be unable to demonstrate their untruth. Both—the results of scientific investigation and the declarations of religious faith—must be capable of being joined into one whole, or at least of existing alongside each other without conflict and hostility. People then try to obtain this agreement at the end, in the results, by making science agnostic with respect to the supersensible and supernatural on the one hand, and by strictly restricting the declarations of religious faith to the religious-ethical on the other. But although in this way they try to avoid dualism in the result, in fact they continue to honor it in the organ through which, and in the way or method by which, they arrive at knowledge of the truth. Within its own domain the heart is as much an organ for truth as the head. Religious-moral experience guarantees the reality of invisible things as strongly as sensory perception guarantees that of the visible world. Faith with its grounds possesses as much right as science with its proofs. But it is difficult to see how one can escape at the end, in the result, the dualism accepted at the beginning, in principle. If head and heart have lives of their own, in the sense that each forms an independent organ for the knowledge of truth, then the unity of truth, of the world, indeed of God himself, can no longer be maintained. Just as subjectively man falls apart into two—on the one hand a religionless being enclosed within the nexus of nature, and on the other a religious-ethical being, a citizen of a moral world order—so objectively science and religion, _erklärbare_ and _erlebbare Wirklichkeit_, the world of being and the world of value, visible and invisible things, the absolute power of nature and the religious idea of God, stand separately alongside each other and soon in hostility over against each other.

There is undoubtedly a certain truth in this dualism that may not be ignored. Besides a logical certainty there is also a certainty of faith. But this distinction, as will become evident later, is entirely different from the separation proposed by the dualism described above. For this twofold certainty divides neither man nor the world into two halves, but within the sphere of science itself distinguishes between that which comes to our knowledge immediately and that which comes through the mediation of proofs. The dualism that has come to dominion in modern philosophy, however, causes all created things to fall apart into two entirely separate spheres, and thereby conflicts with the unity of man’s spirit, with the unity of science and truth, with the unity of the world, and with the unity of the divine Being himself. For this reason it is also incapable of reconciling believing and knowing; rather, it causes the conflict between them to increase. Faith cannot be satisfied with this separation, since it sees itself deprived of all the historical and metaphysical elements with which it is always connected to a greater or lesser degree, and must therefore either become wholly dependent on science or retreat into vague, mysterious feeling. And science can have no peace with it, because the decision concerning the reality of things is assigned to a faculty that utterly lacks the right and competence for it. Neither faith nor science allows itself to be restricted in this way. At every moment they impinge upon each other. Nor can it be a proper demand that the religious person cease serving God when he devotes himself to science, or that the man of science silence his thought when he enters the domain of religion; cf. above, pp. 29, 155 f.
