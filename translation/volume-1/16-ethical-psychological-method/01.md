# § 16. The Ethical-Psychological Method

## 1.

Alongside historical proofs and speculative reasoning there is a third way in which people have sought to justify Christian faith: the ethical-psychological method. This method conceives Christianity not as a doctrine that can be demonstrated or as a historical fact that can be proved, but as a religious-ethical power that addresses heart and conscience. It therefore does not believe that Christianity can be made acceptable to every person without distinction and under all circumstances, but requires in man an antecedent moral disposition, a sense for the good, a need for redemption, a feeling of dissatisfaction, and so forth. And when Christianity then comes into contact with such a person, without reasoning or proof it commends itself to his conscience as divine truth. For it satisfies his religious-ethical needs, answers to his higher, noble inspirations, reconciles him with himself, frees him from the guilt and burden of sin, grants him peace, comfort, and blessedness, and in all this proves itself the power and wisdom of God.

This line of proof for the truth of Christianity is also very old. Tertullian already appealed to the testimony that the soul involuntarily bears to Christ, _De test. an._ 1; cf. _Apol._ c. 17; Arnobius, _Adv. gentes_ II, 2. In many Apologists we find the thought that pagan philosophy and mythology are incapable of giving a satisfactory answer to the questions concerning God, man, and world, of satisfying religious needs, and of cultivating a truly moral life. Christianity, by contrast, embraces all that is true and good which still lay scattered throughout the pagan world; it gives material for thought, renews the heart, and cultivates every kind of virtue; Christianity is the true philosophy. Ὁσα οὐν παρα πασι καλως εἰρηται, ἡμων των χριστιανων ἐστιν, Justin Martyr, _Apol._ II 13; cf. Harnack, _D. G._ I 421 f., 436 f. Likewise, in the prologue to his _Sententiae_, Duns Scotus points to the rationality of the content of revelation, its moral effect, and its sufficiency for man to reach his destiny. The arguments advanced by Roman Catholic and Protestant theologians for the truth of revelation were derived not only from miracles and predictions, and so forth, but also from the beauty and majesty of Scripture’s style, the mutual agreement of all its parts, the sublimity and divinity of its content, and the effect and influence the Christian religion had exercised upon the intellectual, moral, aesthetic, social, and political life of individuals, families, and nations. Bellarmine, _De Conc. et Eccl._, lib. 4, cap. 11 sq.; Perrone, _Praelect. Theol._ I 129 sq.; Jansen, I 158 sq., 269 sq.; Hake, _Handbuch der allgem. Religionswiss._, 1887, II 228 f.; Calvin, _Inst._ I, c. 8; Maresius, _Syst. theol._, loc. I, §31; _Synops. pur. theol._, disp. 2, §17 sq., 25; Vitringa, _Doctr. christ. relig._, cap. 2, §§26–29; Hoornbeek, _Theol. pract._ I, p. 48; Quenstedt, _Theol. didact-pol._ I, cap. 3, sect. 2, qu. 16; Glassius, _Philol. Sacra_ I, tract. 3; etc. Even Rousseau could not withhold his praise in _Émile_ from the life and teaching of Jesus: _oui, si la vie et la mort de Socrate sont d’un sage, la vie et la mort de Jesus sont d’un Dieu_. Supernaturalism divided the proofs for Christianity into external and internal proofs and understood by the latter precisely those that demonstrated Christianity’s agreement with man’s rational and moral nature and were especially powerful for his heart and conscience. The theologians of this tendency also realized that the proofs were not sufficient for every person without distinction, that they furnished not mathematical but moral certainty, and therefore also presupposed a certain moral disposition on man’s part. Knapp, _Glaub._ I, p. 71 f.; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I, p. 281 f.; idem, _Entwicklung aller in der Dogm. vork. Begriffe_, 4e Aufl., pp. 219–230; Muntinghe, _Theol. Christ._, pars theor., §38; Vinke, _Theol. Christ. Dogm. Comp._, p. 17 sq.; Voigt, _Fundamentaldogm._, p. 269 f.; Oosterzee, _Dogm._ I, pp. 199, 234 f., 252 f.; Gretillat, _Exposé de theol. systém._ II 163 s., 176 s.; A. B. Bruce, _Apologetics_, Edinburgh, 1892, p. 42.

The ethical-psychological method came into favor especially through Pascal and Vinet. In both of them, however, it did not yet stand in opposition to the historical line of proof. Indeed, the historical proofs form a necessary element in Pascal’s apology and were very highly esteemed by him. Wijnmalen, _Pascal als bestrijder der Jezuiten en verdediger des Christendoms_, 1865, pp. 164–188. Yet he gave those historical proofs a different place and significance. His apology is anthropological; it proceeds from man’s misery and seeks to awaken in him a need for redemption. It then demonstrates that this need finds satisfaction not in pagan religions and philosophical systems but only in the Christian religion prepared by Israel’s religion. Vinet too did not disdain the historical proofs, _Discours sur quelques sujets religieux_, 6e ed., Paris, 1862, p. 29; _Essais de philos. morale et relig._, 1837, p. 36 s., but he nevertheless considers them insufficient and attaches greater value to the internal proof. He wishes the apologist to walk the ethical path and commend Christianity to man’s conscience from its ethical side, as true humanity; cf. his “L’Evangile compris par le coeur” in the _Discours_, loc. cit., pp. 29–41, and “Le regard” in his _Études Evangéliques_, 1847; cf. Dr. J. Cramer, _Alex. Vinet_, Leiden, Brill, 1883, pp. 99 f., 117 f. Since then this method, with neglect and sometimes even contempt for the historical proofs, has been adopted and honored by Astié, _De theol. des verstands en de theol. des gewetens_, from the French by D. Ch. de la Saussaye, 1866; Pressensé, _Les Origines_, Paris, 1883, pp. 114–128; Sécrétan, _La civilisation et la croyance_, Paris, Alcan, 1887; Saussaye, my _Theol. van d. l. S._, pp. 55 f., 64 f. We further find this same line of proof for Christianity in Delitzsch, _System der christl. Apol._, Leipzig, 1869, pp. 30, 34 f.; Baumstark, _Christl. Apol. auf anthropol. Grundlage_ I, 1872, pp. 34–36; Köstlin, _Die Begründung unserer sittlich-relig. Ueberzeugung_, Berlin, 1893, p. 58 f.; and so forth.
