## 3.

Kant’s significance for theology consisted chiefly in his separation between _phaenomenon_ and _noumenon_, _erklärbare_ and _erlebbare Wirklichkeit_, the world of being and of values, knowing and believing, science and religion, theoretical and practical reason. Religion came to stand alongside and outside science and rested on its own foundation, that of man’s moral nature. The influence of Kant’s philosophy on theology was immediately perceptible. On the ground of the unknowability of the supersensible, supernaturalism argued for the necessity of revelation. Rationalism aligned itself with Kant’s rational morality and moralistic religion. Schleiermacher adopted Kant’s doctrine of the unknowability of the supernatural and his separation of religion and science, but sought a safe refuge for religion in feeling. Yet especially after the vanity of the historical-apologetic and speculative lines of proof had become evident, many returned to Kant. In this country Hoekstra already did so. Dissatisfied with Scholten’s intellectualism and determinism, he sought with Kant the foundation of religious faith in practical reason and its postulates. According to Hoekstra, faith is a moral act of the will, a postulate of our inward spiritual man over against the experiences of life; it rests on faith in the truth of our own inner being. That worldview alone is true which answers to our inner being, to our moral needs. _Bronnen en grondsl. van het godsd. geloof_, 1864, pp. 23 f., 45 f. Later, in an article in _Theol. Tijdschr._, 1872, p. 1 f., Hoekstra defined his thought more precisely and grounded religious faith not in our needs, ideals, and aspirations in general, but specifically in the unconditional sense of duty, ibid., 32; cf. _Wijsgeerige Godsdienstleer_ I 220. Following Hoekstra, Ph. R. Hugenholtz, _Studien op godsdienst- en zedekundig gebied_, Amsterdam, 1884–89, e.g., II 142 f.; Rauwenhoff, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._, 335 f.; Leendertz, _Het ethisch-evang. standpunt en het christ. godsd. geloof_, Rotterdam, 1891, I 7, 10, 57 f.; and others cause religious faith to be rooted in moral self-consciousness. The ethical Modernists went a step further still; abandoning the attempt to arrive at a philosophical system, they separated faith and science as sharply as possible and restricted religion to the moral domain. Religion is moral idealism, devotion to the moral ideal, faith in the power of the good—a power which, according to most, still has an objective existence, but which for some is nothing but a conception of the human mind. Cf. Rauwenhoff, _Wijsb. v. d. godsd._, pp. 116 f., 366 f., who also provides literature at p. 116; further, J. W. van der Linden, in _De Gids_, Dec. 1883, and _Bijblad van de Herv._, Sept. 9, 1887; Dr. A. Bruining, “Verschillende schakeeringen van modernen,” _Bijblad v. d. Herv._, Feb. 10, 1885; De Bussy, _Ethisch idealisme_, 1875; idem, _Over de waarde en den inhoud van godsd. voorstellingen_, 1880; idem, _De maatstaf van het zedelijk oordeel_, etc., 1889; idem, _Theol. Tijdsch._, Jan. 1895, pp. 1–14. A similar dualism is also found in Dr. J. H. Gunning, _Overlevering en wetenschap_, 1879; Dr. J. W. Gunning, in the introduction to _Christendom en Natuurwetenschap_, eight lectures by Fred. Temple, Haarlem, 1887; and likewise in Doedes when he distinguishes between faith, which rests on grounds, and science, which rests on proofs, _Inl. tot de Leer van God_, 1880, p. 7 f.
