## 7.

Others have sought the content and proof of the Christian religion not in the satisfaction of heart and conscience but in so-called _Werthurtheile_.
Yet this is only another name for the same thing.
If nothing more were meant by this than that a dogma must always contain a religious-ethical value, no one could raise any objection to these _Werthurtheile_.
In every dogma, as was said earlier, one must hear the heart of religion beating.
But in Ritschl’s theology these _Werthurtheile_ acquired an entirely different meaning.
Detached from everything metaphysical, they became the foundation and content of dogmatics.
And that is impossible.
Religion includes the conviction of the reality of its object.
Religious and ethical valuation presupposes the truth of the person or thing to which it relates.
_Werthurtheile_ therefore depend on _Seinsurtheile_; they stand and fall with them.
If the valuation of an object is not grounded in its reality, it becomes nothing but an imagining, a creation of fantasy, or an ideal formation in the sense of Lange and Pierson.
It is therefore impermissible to say, “Let the facts be one way or another; valuation decides,” for valuation too changes with the facts.
If the people and religion of Israel developed as modern criticism represents them, the valuation of the Old Testament must be altered in principle.
A modern view of history and an orthodox valuation fit poorly together.
If Christ is not truly God, then for the Christian he cannot have the value of God either.
And so it is with all _dogmata_.
Religious valuation stands in the closest connection with objective truth.
Many mists would disappear if, in accordance with Biedermann’s wish, the _Modestichwort: Werthurtheile_ were banished.
Cf. also Scheibe, _Die Bedeutung der Werthurtheile für das relig. Erkennen_, Halle, 1893; O. Ritschl, _Ueber Werthurtheile_, Freiburg, Mohr, 1895.
Gradually the conviction is gaining ground that religious-ethical experience and valuation, however weighty they may be, can neither guarantee the truth of their object nor serve as the standard for the content of dogmatics.
This conception cannot be justified psychologically either.
For the decision concerning the reality of things belongs not to will or feeling, heart or conscience, but to consciousness.
Only when the reality of a thing has been established for consciousness can the other faculties of the soul become active with respect to it.
Wishing, feeling, experiencing, and imagining in no way include the reality of their objects.
The way to the heart lies paved through the head.

Yet many, though recognizing the weakness of the proof from experience and valuation, have still tried to save something of it by means of Kant’s postulate theory.
It is then indeed conceded that man’s moral nature may not lead one to infer the reality of all manner of religious conceptions or the truth of the Christian religion; nevertheless, from that moral nature is derived the right to postulate the actual existence of everything necessarily connected with it and without which it would be undermined and destroyed.
But no one can indicate what this comprises; opinions diverge widely.
Who can determine in the abstract what is given immediately and directly with man’s moral nature?
In each person that moral nature differs according to the environment in which it was formed.
Each person postulates precisely as much as he needs for his religion.
Kant derived from practical reason that which constituted the content of his _moralische Vernunftreligion_: the existence of God, freedom, and immortality.
Fichte was satisfied with the moral world order.
And Rauwenhoff claims that belief in ourselves necessarily postulates belief in such a constitution of the world that the law of the sense of duty can rule within it, _Wĳsb. v. d. godsd._, p. 343.
We are not told more precisely what this constitution consists in.
The moral world order postulated by Rauwenhoff is nothing other than the good within us, p. 536 f.
But with this we advance not a single step.
For this power of the good is already present in the human being now and entirely coincides with that moral nature upon which the postulate was built.
Silence is maintained concerning whether the postulate includes anything more—for example, that the good will triumph, that one day all people will be able and willing to accomplish it.
Rauwenhoff expects this triumph of the good, but he does not say on what ground.
If, however, the present constitution of the world is compatible with the dominion of the moral law within us, why could it not also be so in the future?
What right does man’s moral nature have to demand in the end the complete victory of the good, the harmony of virtue and happiness, and the correspondence of the natural and moral world orders?
In Rauwenhoff the postulate theory thus ended by returning to its point of departure; at the end it gave no more than it already contained at the beginning.
But apart from its meager results, the question arises whether the foundation upon which the postulate is built is reliable.
For the modern philosopher of religion this foundation is not beyond all objection.
In religion he refuses to acknowledge an original religious nature in man.
That would conflict with a scientific explanation.
Religion must be derived from factors that are not yet religious when each is considered by itself.
But upon reaching man’s moral nature, he seems entirely to forget the demand of his scientific explanation.
Without concerning himself with the objections advanced by materialistic science, he proceeds from man’s moral nature as an established, immutable, original property and builds upon it the reality of a moral world order or even of an entire metaphysical world.
His right to do so should at least have been demonstrated.
If this investigation had been conducted beforehand, it might have become evident that man’s moral nature itself already presupposes religion.
In an _erkenntnisstheoretischen_ sense, as with the moral proof for the existence of God, one may ascend from the moral to the religious; logically and in reality, however, religion precedes the ethical.
There is no morality without _metaphysica_.
The idea of duty involves that of an absolute power that binds in the conscience.
