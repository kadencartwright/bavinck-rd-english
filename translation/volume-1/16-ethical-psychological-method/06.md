## 6.

This ethical, practical method of justifying religion and Christianity certainly deserves to be preferred by far over the historical and speculative lines of proof. It conceives religion neither merely as a doctrine that must be justified before the understanding, nor as a state of the subject that must be analyzed by thought. Rather, it sees religion as a historical, objective power that answers to man’s moral disposition and finds its proof and justification therein. Yet this method too is subject to serious objections. In the first place, the agreement of a religion with man’s moral needs is certainly of great significance. The satisfaction of heart and conscience is the seal and crown of religion. A religion that cannot comfort in grief and pain, in life and death, cannot be the true religion. We do not expect other sciences—logic, mathematics, physics, and so forth—to offer comfort to the guilty conscience and the sorrowful mind. But a religion that stands helpless at the sickbed and deathbed, that cannot strengthen the doubting and raise the downcast, is unworthy of the name, Gottschick, _Die Kirchlichkeit_ etc., p. 4 f. The contrast so often drawn between truth and consolation has no place in religion. A truth that contains no consolation and is not connected with man’s religious-ethical life thereby also ceases to be a religious truth. Just as medical science in all its parts is governed by the healing of the sick, so in religion man is concerned with peace and blessedness. But however highly this consolation may be esteemed in religion, and however much it may be considered together with other proofs as a powerful _motivum credibilitatis_, nevertheless, when placed alone and by itself, it is insufficient as proof. For some consolation and satisfaction are found in all religions; experiences of misery and guilt, of doubt and trust, of patience and hope, are present not only among Christians but also, in greater or lesser measure, among Muslims, Buddhists, and others. A religion that offers no consolation and grants no satisfaction to man’s moral needs is certainly false; but conversely, not every religion in which man seeks his consolation and satisfaction is therefore the true one. Further, the needs of heart and conscience that some religion satisfies have either been awakened under the influence of that religion itself, in which case their satisfaction is quite natural and a proof of little force; or they have arisen apart from that religion, under other influences and under the operation of another religion, in which case precisely that peculiar need which this religion presupposes is not present and satisfaction is entirely lacking. Almost nothing of an unconscious aspiration of the soul toward Christianity is found in reality. The history of missions teaches bitterly little of a ripeness of peoples for the gospel. The gospel is not according to man, not according to the needs as man himself conceives them. Apart from revelation man does not know even himself. The frequently repeated assertion that Christianity answers to man’s needs brings with it the serious danger that the truth will be made to fit human nature. The proposition that truth is genuinely human because it is divine so easily turns into its opposite: that it is divine only because it is human. Preaching to suit Jerusalem’s taste instead of addressing its heart is not uncommon even in Christian pulpits. Next, the experience in which the proof for the truth of religion is sought is a very fluid concept. What is there in a historical religion that can actually be the content of experience? Certain religious-ethical emotions of guilt, remorse, forgiveness, gratitude, joy, and so forth are experienced. But everything else that occurs in a historical religion falls outside experience. For not one of the twelve articles of faith can “I believe” be replaced by “I experience.” That God is Creator of heaven and earth, that Christ is God’s only-begotten Son, conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of Mary, and so forth, is by its nature incapable of experience. The truth of historical Christianity can never be constructed on the foundation of religious-ethical experience. The result of elevating experience to the _principium cognoscendi_ is therefore only that the content of faith and dogmatics is increasingly detached from everything historical and restricted to the so-called religious-ethical. This, however, is nothing other than a new form of the separation between idea and fact in Christianity that has already been attempted repeatedly and in vain. The fruit can no longer be plucked when the tree is cut down; and the fresh, clear water flows no longer when the spring is stopped.
