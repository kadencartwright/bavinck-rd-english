## 8.

The widespread belief that the religions are different forms of one essence proceeds from the thought that the essence of religion lies not in the _religio objectiva_ but in the _religio subjectiva_. The former is indifferent; what matters is the latter, the religious disposition or frame of mind. That disposition must therefore be investigated in order to determine the essence of religion. But where and how must that investigation be conducted? Two methods present themselves, the historical and the psychological. The historical method, advocated, for example, by Dr. A. Bruining, _Wĳsbeg. v. d. godsd._, _Theol. Tĳdschr._ 1881 p. 365f., seeks to determine the essence of religion from historical investigation and comparative observation of religious phenomena. But this method is in fact impossible, because every investigation of the religions already presupposes a notion of religion; a comparative investigation of all religions is an impracticable task; and precisely in the religions the religious disposition lies most deeply hidden and escapes almost all observation. What do we still know of the frame of mind and disposition that underlie religious phenomena in the various currents and churches within Christianity! The psychological method, defended among others by Hugenholtz, _Studiën op godsd. en zedek. gebied_ II 83f., and Rauwenhoff, _Wĳsbeg. v. d. godsdienst_ 41f., seeks to explain the essence of religion psychologically and therefore requires that the student of the science of religion be a religious person and, as such, observe and judge religious phenomena. In itself there is nothing to be said against this. To leave one’s own religious consciousness out of account in the study of religions, as Dr. Bruining wishes, would be the same, according to Dr. Hugenholtz’s apt observation, as putting out one’s own eyes for fear of an optical illusion. But then it may surely be required that such an investigator of religions bring with him not a false but a true and pure conception of religion; otherwise he tests all religions only by his own, perhaps very mistaken, conception and by that of his kindred spirits—for example, by the modern conception of religion. If the question is posed in this way, however, then between the orthodox and the modernists there is no longer an issue of method, but only of the truth or untruth of the religious conception from which both proceed. And then the warrant for the truth of the religious conception brought by the orthodox is at least as great as that for the modern conception of religion; for the former is derived from Holy Scripture and accords with the church of all ages, while the latter is of recent origin and is accepted only within a small circle of kindred spirits.
