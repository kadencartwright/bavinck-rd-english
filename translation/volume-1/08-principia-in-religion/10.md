## 10.

Hegel correctly recognized that religion also contains knowledge and that religion and metaphysics are most closely akin. The nature of the case makes this clear as well. Religion is always a relation of man to a divine Power that stands above him. Religion therefore does not and cannot exist without a definite representation of God; and this in turn includes other representations concerning the world and man, the origin and final end of things. For the believer these religious representations have transcendental significance; he is most deeply convinced of their objective reality and truth. As soon as he begins to regard these representations as products of his imagination, as ideals without reality, or to despair of the knowability of the metaphysical, his religion is finished. Skepticism destroys the object of religion and with it religion itself. God must also be served with the understanding; but when the understanding perceives that religious representations correspond to no reality, it ceases to be religious. A religious and a theoretical worldview, theology and science, are not the same, but they cannot possibly conflict with each other. Such a dualism stands in irreconcilable conflict with the unity of the human mind, Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_, II, _Die Religion des Geistes_, 2te Aufl., Leipzig, Friedrich, S. 3–27.

Hegel nevertheless erred in placing religion and philosophy in relation to each other as lower and higher, as representation and concept, and thus conceiving them as successive moments of a single process. Hegelians such as Strauss, _Glaub._ I 12 f., and Biedermann, _Christl. Dogm._, 2e Aufl., I 184 f., themselves recognized the error of this. Content and form are never joined so mechanically and externally that a complete alteration of the latter leaves the former unchanged. The conversion of religious representations into philosophical concepts also affects the religious content itself. The history of Hegelian philosophy soon brought this to light. Almost nothing of the Christian dogmas remained within it; Trinity, incarnation, and satisfaction retained the orthodox names but were interpreted entirely differently. The facts of Christianity were counted as form and deemed worthless. In their place came concepts that no longer had any content.

A second error of Hegel consisted in assigning religion and philosophy the same content and yet regarding the former as a lower form of the latter. Religion was thereby reduced to a relative good that still had value only for the simple and undeveloped. Philosophers were elevated far above it and had enough in philosophy. This rests on a total misunderstanding of the nature of religion. For although religion and science are related, they nevertheless differ as widely as heaven and earth. Even if they often have the same content and object, these nevertheless appear in each under a wholly different point of view. Science is concerned with knowledge; religion, with comfort, peace, and salvation. Religion and philosophy, no more than A. Comte’s _état théologique, métaphysique et positive_, are historically successive states of the human mind; they are different points of view from which the same matter can often be considered.

Even the most profound philosopher therefore does not rise above religion with all his knowledge; he can never satisfy his religious need through science. Science may tell him that God exists and what God is; only through religion does he know that this God is also his God and his Father. Science may teach him that there are sin and grace; only through religion does he partake of the blessedness of forgiveness and of being a child of God. Even if science could know everything and solve every metaphysical problem, it would still provide only theoretical knowledge and no personal participation in the benefits of salvation. Salvation is attached not to knowing but only to believing.

But science and philosophy are far from being capable of attaining even so much. Precisely on the most important questions they fail to supply an answer. The expectation that Renan could cherish of science in 1848 proved to him in 1890 to be nothing but an illusion, Renan, _L’avenir de la science. Pensées de 1848_, 2e éd., Paris, 1890. Science tells us neither what God is nor what man is; it leaves us ignorant of the origin and destiny of things. It observes the phenomena, but the noumenon remains hidden from it. It can never replace religion and can never compensate for its loss. Voigt, _Fundam. dogm._ 120 f.; Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_ II, S. 3–27; H. Siebeck, _Lehrbuch der Religionsphilosophie_, Freiburg, 1893, S. 1–11; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 92–96; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss der christl. Gl. u. Sittenlehre_ § 11.
