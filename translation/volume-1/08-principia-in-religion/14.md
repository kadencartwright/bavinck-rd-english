## 14.

Even within this conception, it is beyond dispute that feeling occupies an important place in religion. Religious representations in themselves and without more are not yet religion; religion arises only when man enters into a real, personal relation with the object of those representations. And such a personal relation to God cannot but affect feeling. It does not leave man cold and indifferent but stirs him to the depths of his soul; it awakens in him a powerful sense of pleasure and displeasure and fosters a whole series of emotions: consciousness of guilt, pain, remorse, regret, sorrow, joy, gladness, trust, peace, rest, and so forth. Religion awakens the deepest and tenderest emotions in the human heart. There is no power that grasps and stirs more deeply, universally, or powerfully. All these emotions awakened by religious representations give religion warmth, inwardness, life, and power, in contrast to the lifelessness of intellectualism and the coldness of moralism. The heart is the center of religion.

But feeling is not therefore the only religious function, nor the only seat and source of religion. Feeling—understood here not as a separate faculty, which it is not, but as the totality of passions and emotions—is by nature passive. It reacts only to what consciousness brings into contact with it and then becomes a feeling of pleasure or displeasure. It has nothing in itself and produces nothing from itself but judges things, the representations that come from without, solely according to whether they are pleasant or unpleasant. In itself, feeling, every emotion, is neither good nor evil, neither true nor false. These categories belong properly not to the emotions but to the representations. They can be applied to the emotions only insofar as these are awakened by true or false representations and accompanied by good or evil orientations of the will. In religion, therefore, not feeling but faith—the religious representation—is primary; yet that faith also acts upon feeling. But when, as in Schleiermacher, feeling is detached from faith, from the religious representation, and made the independent and sole source and seat of religion, then feeling loses its quality, becomes entirely independent of the categories of true and false, good and evil, and in fact every feeling as such is already religious, true, good, and beautiful. And that was the grievous error of the whole Romantic movement.

Naturally, then, the error of confusing and identifying religious feeling with sensuous and aesthetic feeling lies close at hand. From history everyone knows the affinity and transition between religious and sensual love. But the mingling of religious and aesthetic feeling, of religion and art, is equally dangerous. The two are essentially distinct. Religion is life and reality; art is ideal and semblance. Art cannot bridge the chasm between ideal and reality. It does indeed lift us for a moment above reality and cause us to live in the realm of ideals. But this occurs only in imagination. Reality remains unchanged. Art does indeed show us from afar the realm of glory, but it does not bring us into that realm or make us its citizens. It does not atone for our guilt, dry our tears, or comfort us in life and death. It never makes the “there” a “here”—_Dort niemals Hier_. Religion alone does that. It is reality and gives reality. It bestows life and peace. It posits the ideal as true reality and makes us partakers of it. Therefore aesthetic feeling can never replace religious feeling, nor art religion. Yet the two are connected. Religion and art have been closely linked from the beginning; the decline of one brought that of the other with it; the ultimate driving force of art lay in religion. Cf. G. Portig, _Religion und Kunst in ihrem gegenseitigen Verhältniss_, Iserlohn 1879-’80, I 5, 85, 90, 225, etc.; Paulsen, _System der Ethik_, Berlin, Hertz, 1889, S. 434 f. In religion, particularly in the _cultus_, imagination has its right and its value. _Beim religiösen Process ist allerdings auch die Phantasie betheiligt, aber nicht als Erzeugingsprincip, sondern nur als Belebungsprincip. Die Einbildungskraft kan immer nur bereits gegebene Stoffe und Triebe gestalten, aber nie kan sie die Religion selbst erst schaffen_, Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, p. 91. But the _Schaubühne_ is least of all suited to be a _moralische Anstalt_ (Schiller). The theater cannot replace the church, nor Lessing’s _Nathan_ the Bible (Strauss). Ideals and creations of imagination are no compensation for the reality that religion offers. Religious feeling, however inward and deep it may otherwise be, is pure only when it is awakened by true representations, Hartmann, _Religionsphilosophie_ II 27-55; S. Hoekstra, _Godsdienst en Kunst_ 1859; Lamers, _Wetenschap v. d. godsd._ II 164 f.; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, S. 97 f.
