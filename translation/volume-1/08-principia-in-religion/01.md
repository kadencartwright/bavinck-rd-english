# § 8. Principia in Religion

### A. The Nature of Religion

## 1.

Like science, religion too has its _principia_. In order to know these, it is first of all necessary to determine the nature of religion, especially in distinction from science and art. The name _religion_ sheds little light. Cicero, _De nat. deor._ 2, 28, derives the word from _relegere_, to reread, to do over again, to observe accurately, and thereby designates religion as a continual and diligent observance of all that pertains to the worship of the gods; cf. _De invent._ 2, 22 and 53. Lactantius, _Instit. divin._ 4, 28, explains it from _religare_ and thus understands by religion the bond that unites man to God. A third derivation, from _relinquere_, occurs in Gellius, _Noct. Att._ 4, 9, and indicates that everything belonging to religion is separated from the profane on account of its holiness. Augustine, _De civ. Dei_ 10, 4, once connects it with _re-eligere_: in religion we choose back again, as the source of our salvation, the God whom we had lost through sin.

J. C. Leidenroth, _Neue Jahrb. für Philologie und Pädagogik_ von Seebode, Jahn und Klotz, 1834, S. 455, proceeds from the fact that the three verbs _diligere_, _negligere_, and _intelligere_ have a different perfect from _lego_ and its compounds, and assumes a lost stem _ligere_, Sanskrit _lok_, Greek λευσσειν, German _lugen_, English _look_, cf. _lucere_, with the meaning “to see.” _Diligere_ would then mean to regard with love, _negligere_ not to see, and _intelligere_ to see into; and from this would also have come _religere_, to look back, and _religio_, to look about with fear, cf. _respectus_. The derivations from _religare_, _relinquere_, and _re-eligere_ encounter grammatical objections and also fail to explain the peculiar meanings that _religio_ possesses in Latin. The dispute between the derivations of Cicero and Leidenroth has not yet been settled. In substance, however, the two agree in this: _religio_ designates religion as a disposition of timid fear toward the deity and as the anxiously scrupulous observance, flowing from that disposition, of what the cult of the gods requires. Cf. the line in Figulus (Gellius, _Noct. Att._ 4, 9): _religentem esse oportet, religiosum nefas_; H. Voigt, _Fundamentaldogmatik_, Gotha, Perthes, 1874, S. 9 f.; F. A. B. Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, Freiburg, Mohr, 1889, S. 83; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsdienstleer_ I 52 f.

The word is therefore by no means suited to express the full content of the Christian religion. Yet common usage and the derivation of Lactantius, which gained general acceptance, Christianized the word. The Vulgate adopted it at Acts 26:5 and James 1:27. The word passed into all European languages, and in our language too it has acquired and retained full citizenship alongside _godsdienst_ (divine service), _vroomheid_ (piety; from Gothic _fruma_, Latin _primus_, standing in the foreground, virtuous, brave, for example in the States Translation at Gen. 42:11, etc.), _godsvrucht_ (fear of God), and _godzaligheid_ (godliness).
