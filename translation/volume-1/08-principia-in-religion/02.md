## 2.

Holy Scripture gives no definition, nor does it possess a general concept for designating the phenomenon of religion. It has separate words for its objective and subjective sides. The _religio objectiva_ is identical with the revelation of God and consists in the covenant, בְרִית, which God gave to Israel and which may therefore in the full sense be called a divine institution, διαθηκη, Exod. 20:1 f., 34:10 f., 27 f.; Isa. 54:10, etc. The ordinances within that covenant that Israel must observe together constitute the content of the תּוֹרָה—instruction, teaching, law, the lawbook of the Lord—and are designated by many different names. They are called דְּבָרִים, words, Num. 12:6; Ps. 33:4, etc.; מִצוֹת, commandments, Gen. 26:5; Exod. 15:26, etc.; פִקּוּדִים, precepts, Ps. 119:4, 5, 15, etc.; חֻקִּים, statutes, decrees, Exod. 15:26; Lev. 25:18; Ps. 89:32; Job 28:26, etc.; מִשְׁפָּטִים, legal cases, judicial decisions, Num. 36:13; Ps. 19:10, etc.; דְּרָכִים‎, ‏אֳרָחוֹת, ways, paths, Deut. 5:33; Job 21:14; Ps. 25:4, etc.; and מִשְׁמָרוֹת, laws that are to be kept, Gen. 26:5; Lev. 18:30, etc. The many expressions indicate how prominently the objective element, the ordinance of God, stands in Israel’s religion. Corresponding subjectively to this _religio objectiva_ is the יִרְאַת יהוה, the fear of the Lord. This expresses the inward disposition of the pious Israelite toward the holy laws prescribed to him by God for observance. Yet this fear is essentially different from the anxious timidity originally enclosed in the Latin word _religio_. This is evident from the fact that the fear of the Lord passes over into, and is joined with, all kinds of other religious dispositions, such as believing, הֶאֱמִין, Gen. 15:6; Isa. 7:9; Hab. 2:4; trusting, בָּטַח, Ps. 26:1, 37:3, 5; taking refuge, חָסָה, Ps. 5:12, 37:40; leaning, סָמוּךְ; clinging fast, דָּבַק, 2 Kings 18:6; hoping, קִוָּה; waiting, חִכָּה; indeed, even loving God, חָשַׁק, Exod. 20:6; Deut. 6:5; Ps. 91:14. The judgments of the Lord do not remain outside and above the Israelite as an object of dread and fear but become an object of his love. He meditates on them with his understanding and keeps them with his will. They are his delight all day long. In the New Testament we encounter essentially the same conception. But now God gives his revelation not in a series of laws but in the person of Christ. He is the way and the truth, John 14:6. The ὁδος του κυριου, Acts 18:25; 19:9, 23; 22:4; the διδαχη or διδασκαλια, Matt. 7:28; 22:33; John 7:16, 17; Acts 2:42; Rom. 6:17; 1 Tim. 1:10; 4:6, 16; 6:1, 3; 2 Tim. 4:2, 3; Titus 1:9; 2:1, 7, 10; the εὐαγγελιον, Mark 1:1, 14, 15, etc.; and the λογος του θεου, Matt. 13:19; Mark 2:2; 4:14 f.; 2 Cor. 9:19, etc., all center on Christ and are nothing but an explication of his person and work. Accordingly, the subjective disposition changes as well. The ordinary Greek words were not suited to express this disposition in its proper character. Δεισιδαιμονια is used by Festus for the Jewish religion, Acts 25:19, and its adjective is used by Paul for pagan religion, Acts 17:22. Θεοσεβεια occurs only once, 1 Tim. 2:10. Εὐσεβεια denotes holy reverence for God; it is related in meaning to the Latin _pietas_ and thus expresses a disposition like that also present in children toward their parents, and so forth. This word occurs several times in the New Testament, especially in the Pastoral Epistles; what εὐσεβεια properly is and ought to be was first revealed in the gospel, 1 Tim. 3:16. Nor has fear entirely disappeared from the _religio subjectiva_ of the New Testament, Luke 18:2; Acts 9:31; 2 Cor. 5:11; 7:1; Rom. 3:18; Eph. 5:21; Phil. 2:12; 1 Pet. 1:17; 3:2; 3:15. Yet it occurs much less frequently as a description of the religious disposition; in most passages it relates to particular events, for example, God’s judgment, and it is replaced by love, Rom. 8:15; 1 John 4:18. The ordinary word for _religio subjectiva_ in the New Testament is πιστις. Corresponding on man’s part to the glad tidings of forgiveness and salvation in Christ is faith, which is a childlike trust in God’s grace and therefore immediately works love in our hearts. Πιστις and ἀγαπη are the fundamental dispositions of Christian piety. The words λατρεια, Rom. 9:4; 12:1; Heb. 9:1, 6, and θρησκεια, Acts 26:5; Col. 2:18; James 1:27, designate the _cultus_ rendered to God from the principle of faith. J. Köstlin, _Die Religion in N. T._, _Stud. u. Kr._ 1888, S. 7-102; Nitzsch, op. cit., p. 85.
