### B. The Seat of Religion

## 7.

Having investigated the nature of religion, we must determine the place it occupies in man amid his faculties and functions. At the same time, the relation in which religion stands to science, art, and morality will then become clear. Scholasticism already raised the question whether _religio_ was a _virtus intellectualis_ or _moralis_, and Thomas said the latter, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 81, art. 5. But only in modern philosophy, especially after the religions of the peoples became better known, was the essence of religion made an object of psychological and historical investigation. Lessing’s _Erziehung des Menschengeschlechts_, 1780, and Herder’s _Ideen zur Philos. der Gesch. der Menschheit_, 1784, made a beginning with this. Since then the history and philosophy of religions have become favorite fields of study.

In determining the nature of religion, present-day investigation almost always proceeds from the _religio subjectiva_, while the right, power, and value of the _religio objectiva_ are almost universally misunderstood. This results from the philosophical premise that all religions are the same in essence and differ only in form. But this religious indifferentism is untenable because every religion necessarily includes cognition and posits the reality of its object; as soon as this is the case, however, a religion falls under the category of true and false. If religion were nothing but feeling and imagination, it would attach only aesthetic value to its creations. But every religion is convinced of the reality and truth of its objects—gods, prophets, holy places, and so forth—and cannot exist without that faith; cf. Dr. Bruining, _Theol. Tĳdschr._, Nov. 1894, pp. 563 f., 598 f. In fact, everyone accordingly applies the category of true and false to religions. The philosopher of religion does not believe in the truth of the gods of the peoples, even though he may appreciate the religious disposition that is often expressed in their worship.

Further, the various religions themselves are by no means indifferent toward one another; they regard themselves and one another not as related in terms of lower and higher, but in terms of true and false. The philosopher stands virtually alone with his indifferentism. Frederick the Great may say, _in meinem Reiche soll Jeder nach seiner Façon selig werden_, but the religions themselves think quite differently about it. And they cannot do otherwise: what one posits, the other negates. They exclude one another and cannot both be true. If Christ is the One sent by the Father, then Muhammad is not.

Finally, religious indifferentism also rests on the sinful thought that it is immaterial to God how he is served. It deprives him of the right to determine the manner of his service. In any case, it proceeds from the thought that God has not prescribed his service, and thus from the _a priori_ denial of revelation. Reversed, the proposition that religions agree in essence and differ in form is much more correct: they differ in essence but agree in form.

One final observation must be made here: indifferentism in matters of religion can extend more or less far. Syncretism regards what is ecclesiastical as indifferent; Deism, what is Christian; Modernism, the _religio objectiva_; and _morale indépendante_, everything religious. In fact and objectively, however, nothing is indifferent, neither in nature nor in the state, neither in science nor in art. Everything, even the least thing, has its definite place and significance in the whole. Man is indifferent only to what he does not know; what he knows is also of itself assessed and valued by him. God is indifferent to nothing, because he knows everything. Cf. Lamennais, _Essai sur l’indifférence en matière de religion_, 9e éd., Paris, 1835, especially tome premier, whose introduction begins: _le siècle le plus malade n’est pas celui qui se passionne pour l’erreur, mais le siècle qui néglige, qui dédaigne la vérité_.
