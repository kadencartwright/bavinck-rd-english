## 5.

The _religio subjectiva_ is first of all a ἑξις, a _habitus_, a certain disposition in man which, through the operation of the _religio objectiva_, passes over into _actus_ (_cultus internus_ and _externus_). Such a _habitus_ is present in every human being: _semen religionis omnibus inditum est_, Calvin, _Inst._ I, 4, 1. But in fallen man this _habitus_ is corrupted and, when fertilized by an untrue and impure _religio objectiva_, also produces a _cultus_ that is _idololatreia_, ἐθελοθρησκεια. Pure religion therefore requires, first, that the _religio objectiva_ coming to us from without once again make God known to us as he truly is and, second, that the corrupted _habitus religionis_ in man be reborn and renewed. In this sense the _religio subjectiva_ is therefore a _virtus infusa a Spiritu Sancto_, Hoornbeek, _Theol. pract._ II, pp. 207, 213.

Yet this still does not say enough. Man possesses many virtues, both in his understanding and in his will. The distinctive character of the _virtus_ at work in religion must therefore be specified further. Formerly this _virtus_ was defined as _pietas_, _reverentia_, _timor_, _fides_, and so forth, and today likewise as reverence, awe, fear, and the feeling of dependence. Yet none of these definitions is sufficiently specific; we also have all these affections, to a greater or lesser degree, with respect to creatures. There must be an essential distinction between _cultus religiosus_ and _cultus civilis_, between λατρεια and δουλεια, between the affections of fear, reverence, awe, and so forth, that we cherish toward God and those that we cherish toward creatures. That distinction can lie only in the fact that religion takes account of the _absoluta dignitas et potestas Dei_ and our own _absoluta subjectio_, Hoornbeek, _Theol. pract._ II 205 sq. We are dependent on creatures only in part; as creatures we stand on the same level with them. But God is a being on whom we are absolutely dependent and who decides our well-being and woe in every respect. Among the pagans this absoluteness of God is, as it were, divided among many gods, yet each god is invested within his own sphere with such power that man is absolutely dependent on him for his happiness or unhappiness.

It was especially Schleiermacher who, in his _Christ. Gl._ § 4, defined religion as _schlechthiniges Abhängigkeitsgefühl_. Many objections have been raised against this definition, briefly summarized among others by Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsdienstleer_ I 70 f. Indeed, in Schleiermacher this definition has a meaning that cannot be accepted. Dependence is construed by him so pantheistically that objectively it refers only to the totality of the world and subjectively is restricted to feeling. Nevertheless, Schleiermacher’s definition contains an important element of truth. What makes man a religious being and impels him toward religion is the consciousness that he stands to God in a relation specifically different from all the other relations in which he is placed. This relation is so deep and tender, so rich and many-sided, that it can scarcely be expressed by a single concept. But certainly the concept of dependence is the first and foremost to be considered here. For in religion man feels himself related to a personal being who holds his destiny in his hand in every domain of life and for time and eternity.

God is not therefore conceived in religion exclusively as power; for even as Gracious, Merciful, Righteous, Holy, and so forth, God nevertheless always confronts man as Sovereign, as the Absolute, as God. And man always stands before him as a creature; he is that before no one and nothing else, but only before God. This creaturely dependence is therefore not the essence but nevertheless the foundation of religion. Man, however, is not only a creature but also a rational and moral creature; his relation to God is therefore wholly different from that of angels and animals. The absolute dependence in which man stands toward God does not therefore exclude freedom. He is dependent, but in a different way from the other creatures; he is dependent in such a manner and sense that he at the same time remains a rational and moral being, that he is akin to God, his offspring and his image. He is absolutely dependent, so that denial of this dependence never makes him free, and yet acknowledgment of it never debases him into a slave. On the contrary, in the conscious, voluntary acceptance of this dependence man attains the highest freedom. He becomes human to the same degree that he is a child of God.

Holy Scripture does not undertake an investigation into the nature of the _religio subjectiva_ as it is still found among all people and under all religions. That would also be a hopeless undertaking. For the religious disposition differs so greatly among the many religions that at most a very general and vague concept could be given for it. Scripture, however, designates by the name of faith that religious disposition which the Christian feels toward God and his revelation. This is the central concept in the Christian’s _religio subjectiva_. There has at all times been great disagreement in the Christian church concerning the nature of this faith. Religious life is so rich and deep that it can continually be viewed from another side. But faith nevertheless always contains these two elements: first, that before God and his revelation man is wholly receptive and absolutely dependent on God; and second, that precisely by acknowledging this dependence he becomes a partaker by grace of forgiveness, sonship, and salvation.

There is certainly an analogy of this Christian _religio subjectiva_ in other religions as well, but only in the Christian religion is man’s subjective relation to God perfectly normal. Dependence and freedom are here reconciled with one another. The sovereignty of God is here fully maintained, and man’s kinship with God is nevertheless fully acknowledged. Man is the more religious and becomes the more conformed to the image of God in proportion as he more deeply understands and acknowledges his dependence. Therefore all virtues with respect to creatures can be carried to excess, but with respect to God no excess is possible. One can never believe, trust, or love him too much, and faith can never expect too much. Voigt, _Fundamentaldogmatik_, pp. 65 f.; Aug. Dorner, _Stud. u. Kr._, 1883, S. 217 f.; Kahnis, _Die luth. Dogm._, 2e Ausg., 1874, I 81 f.; Abr. des Amorie van der Hoeven Jr., _De godsdienst het wezen van den mensch_, Leeuwarden, 1848, pp. 38 f.
