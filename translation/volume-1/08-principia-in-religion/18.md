## 18.

But apart from these particular objections, there is a serious objection to the very method by which the origin of religion is investigated. What does it mean to say that the origin of religion must be scientifically explained? It cannot mean that the religion of the first man or the first human beings is historically investigated. For here the history of religions leaves us entirely in the lurch; the historical method is unusable. As is increasingly recognized, there is no warrant at all for regarding the religious forms of the lowest, savage peoples as the original ones. The method can therefore be no other than psychological, and by its very nature this can never advance beyond a hypothesis that appears plausible and, for lack of anything better, may for a time enjoy general assent.

But what is the question that this psychological method sets itself in seeking the origin of religion? Surely it can be no other than this: from what causes and powers in man or in humanity did religion proceed? One must therefore search for that point _wo noch nicht religiöse Lebenskräfte des Menschen zu dem Lebenskeim der Religion sich verbinden?_ Holsten, in Rauwenhoff, p. 50. Hypothetically, then, a human being must be assumed who is so undeveloped and barbarous that religion is not yet present in him at all. From this standpoint an original, implanted _semen religionis_ may not be acknowledged. For then this original feeling, or whatever else one wishes to call it, is something scientifically unexplained, a kind of mystical revelation, a presupposition already of the very thing one seeks to explain, Dr. Bruining, _Gids_, June 1884.

The psychological method therefore requires that religion be explained from factors in man which are not themselves religious, but which through a certain combination, under the influence of nature from without, cause religion to arise. But such a religionless human being is a pure _Gedankending_, an abstraction as rarefied and empty as Rousseau’s natural man and that of the adherents of the _contrat social_. In reality he has never existed. Religion itself thus becomes entirely a product of chance, just as morality does in Darwin. A “mere chance,” says Prof. Rauwenhoff, p. 97, cf. pp. 193, 250, 263, could lead man to make some thing or other from nature into a god for himself. A different combination of the factors, and religion would never have existed.

Religion thereby loses its independent place, its universality and necessity; even its value and right can then no longer be maintained. For it is not true, as Zeller, _Vorträge und Abhandl._ II 57 f., claims, that the value of religion is independent of the manner of its origin. If the origin of religion is accidental, it loses the firm foundation on which it must rest. And its right to exist is then also seriously threatened. For if religion is explained in such a way that the existence of God need not be assumed at all, then even if its origin were a psychological necessity, metaphysically it would still be nothing other than an absurdity.

The psychological method in fact attempts to understand religion without the existence of God. Man is a purely natural being, a product of nature. It is he who is led by all manner of circumstances to religion and brings forth the idea of God. God does not create man; man creates God. The _religio subjectiva_ is the source of the _religio objectiva_. Man determines whether and how he wishes to serve God. The psychological method is thereby condemned. In principle it conflicts with the nature of religion and therefore destroys the phenomenon it has to explain. _Kant, Lotze, Albrecht Ritschl, eine kritische Studie_ von L. Stählin, Leipzig, 1888, S. 245 f. Compare further, on the origin of religion, Rauwenhoff, _Theol. Tĳdschr._, 1885, pp. 257 f.; idem, _Wĳsb. v. d. godsd._, pp. 37–109; Saussaye, _Lehrb. der Rel. Gesch._ I 21 f. and the literature cited there; Siebeck, _Lehrb. der Rel. Philos._ 43 f.; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsdienstleer_ I 136 f.
