### C. The Origin of Religion

## 16.

There is no more satisfactory explanation of the origin of religion than there is of the origin of language. The derivation of religion from fear, priestly deception, or ignorance is still current in some atheistic circles but no longer finds scientific defense. Darwin, _De Afstamming des menschen_, trans. Dr. Hartogh Heys van Zouteveen, 3e dr., 1884, I, pp. 127 f., seeks religion in germ, embryonically, already among the animals—for example, in the love that a dog feels for its master, which is accompanied by a feeling of subordination and fear. But for several reasons this analogy does not hold. First, we know little of the inner, psychical life of animals. Further, religion is always connected with worship, _cultus_, and a religious act such as prayer, sacrifice, and so forth, does not occur among animals. Moreover, animals undoubtedly possess certain qualities of loyalty, attachment, and so forth, but these no more constitute religion than the same qualities among human beings in relation to one another are already religion. The proper object of religion, a supersensible power, is entirely unknown to animals. For the present, therefore, the word of Lactantius remains in force: _Instit. div._ 7, 9, _religio est paene sola, quae hominem discernit a brutis_. Cf. Hegel, _Vorlesungen über die Religion_ I 207; Abr. d. A. v. d. Hoeven, _De godsdienst het wezen van den mensch_, 1848, pp. 29 f.; Hartmann, _Religionsphil._ I, S. 3–11; Saussaye, _Lehrb. d. Relig. gesch._ I 10 f.; Lamers, _Wet. v. d. godsd._ II 150 f.; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. godsdienstleer_ I 1 f.

The explanation of religion from Animism, E. B. Tylor, _Primitive Culture_, 1872; from Fetishism, Fr. Schultze, _Der Fetischismus, ein Beitrag zur Anthropologie und Religionsgeschichte_, 1871; and from ancestor worship, H. Spencer, _Ecclesiastical Institutions, being part VI of the principles of sociology_, London, 1886, § 583 etc., proceeds from the wholly arbitrary assumption that the lowest forms of religion are the primitive ones and mistakes phenomena accompanying religion for its essence. Max Müller’s attempt—for example, in his _De oorsprong en de ontwikkeling van den godsdienst nagegaan in den godsdienst van Indië_, Utrecht, 1879—to derive religion from the feeling of the infinite has likewise rightly found little acceptance. For perception of the endless in nature does not of itself and without more lead to the concept of the infinite, and still less to worship of the Infinite One, of God.

Equally unacceptable is the hypothesis that seeks the origin of religion in the impulse of causality, in man’s need for an explanation of world and life; cf. Peschel, _Völkerkunde_, 1881, S. 244 f.; Voigt, _Fundamentaldogm._, 1874, S. 94 f. For despite all their kinship, religion differs essentially from metaphysics and philosophy and answers to wholly different needs in man. Finally, there are now few who, with Cicero, _De nat. deor._ 1,15; 2,5; Sextus Empiricus 9,18; and the optimistic Rationalists of the previous century, cause religion to arise from the childlike poetry, grateful feeling, and naïve joy awakened in innocent natural man by glorious and beneficent nature, which led him to acknowledge and worship a supernatural being who bestowed all this; cf. still Pfleiderer, _Relig. phil._ II 24 f. Present-day science sees in this portrayal of the innocence of natural peoples and of the beauty and beneficence of nature nothing other than a poetic idyll. Reality shows us everywhere a difficult _struggle for life_.

A wholly different conception of the origin of religion therefore prevails today. Nature often confronts man as an enemy. With its storms and tempests, its scorching heat and biting cold, its raging forces and unbridled elements, it threatens man’s existence and life. He sees himself continually compelled to protect his life against it and maintain his existence. But he is weak and powerless. Thus, in the anxious conflict between himself and nature, between his sense of self and his sense of need, he calls to his aid an invisible power that stands above nature and can help him in the struggle. Man wishes to be happy, but he is not and cannot become so by his own strength; in religion, therefore, he seeks to make favorable toward him all those personal powers which, according to his representation, are present in the phenomena of nature.

The distinctive feature of this explanation of religion is that it causes religion to arise not from theoretical but specifically from practical motives; not from representations but from affections of fear, anxiety, sense of need, and so forth. To that extent it is a return to Petronius’s _primus in orbe deos fecit timor_. It is already found in Hume, cf. _Stud. u. Krit._, 1890, 2tes Heft, S. 245, and in the _Système de la nature_, cf. Stöckl, _Gesch. der neueren Philos._, 1883, I 386, but especially in modern times it has been accepted in very wide circles as the best solution to the question of the origin of religion.

Yet it does not occur in the same form among all. Some say that religion arose from the _Selbsterhaltungstrieb_ in general. In religion man wishes to partake of some good, whatever that good may be—a sensible, physical, egoistic good or also a moral, spiritual good. In a word, he desires a happy life, deliverance from physical or also from ethical evil. So W. Bender, _Das Wesen der Religion und die Grundgesetze der Kirchenbildung_, Bonn, 1886; Ed. Zeller, _Ursprung u. Wesen der Religion_ (_Vorträge und Abhandlungen_ II, 1877, S. 1–83); J. Kaftan, _Das Wesen der christl. Religion_, Basel, Bahnmaier, 1881, S. 38 f.; H. Siebeck, _Lehrb. der Religionsphilos._, 1893, S. 58 f.

Others seek the origin of religion specifically in ethical _Selbstbehauptung_, in man’s maintenance of his moral freedom and worth over against the necessity and compulsion of the physical world. In this way Kant already postulated, on the ground of man’s moral nature, the existence of a God who could make the world of necessity subservient to that of freedom, natural law to moral law, _physis_ to _ethos_. Ritschl reasons in the same way, _Die christl. Lehre von der Rechtf. u. Versöhnung_, 2e Aufl., III, 1883, S. 186; W. Herrmann, _Die Religion im Verhältniss zum Welterkennen und zur Sittlichkeit_, Halle, Niemeyer, 1879, S. 267 f.; cf. also Rauwenhoff, _Wĳsbeg. v. d. godsd._, pp. 94 f.

Finally, there are also those who acknowledge in religion not only an ethical but also a mystical element, and who therefore regard it as grounded not more or less in a free act of the will but in human nature. According to them, in religion man indeed seeks ethical _Selbstbehauptung_, but nevertheless also something else and more—namely, communion with and life in God, and precisely thereby also freedom over against the world. Religion is first of all a relation to God, and thereafter to the world. So Pfleiderer, _Religionsphilosophie_, 2e Aufl., Berlin, 1883, II 28 f.; idem, _Grundriss der christl. Glaubens- und Sittenlehre_, 3e Aufl., Berlin, Reimer, 1886, § 10; Lipsius, _Lehrb. der ev. prot. Dogm._, 2e Aufl., 1879, § 18; Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, S. 99 f.
