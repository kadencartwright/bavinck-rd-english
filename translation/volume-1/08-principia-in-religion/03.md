## 3.

Holy Scripture, then, gives no description of the essence of religion as, according to the modern conception, it underlies all religions. Rather, it portrays and describes as religion only that relation between God and man which has been ordered and determined by God himself. Earlier theology likewise always proceeded from the _religio vera_ when determining the nature of religion. This method still commends itself by its correctness. It can be opposed only by those who think that the categories of true and false do not apply to religions, since no knowledge of the supernatural is possible, and that religious representations therefore have absolutely nothing to do with the religious disposition. This indifferentism, however, is untenable, as will appear later; cognition too is an essential element of religion. Assume for the moment that there is knowledge of the supernatural and that a _religio vera_ exists. Then no conceivable objection can be made to deriving from it the definition of the essence of religion and using it as a standard in judging other religions. No religion can object to being tested by the pure concept of religion. The false is known only by the genuine, and the lie is discovered by the truth. In no field is a correct judgment and valuation of things possible without a fixed, positive standard. This is so in law, morality, and aesthetics, and it is so in religion as well. The study of religions presupposes that we know at least to some extent what religion is. Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 184.

Following the example of Holy Scripture, in our inquiry into the nature of religion we must distinguish between its objective and subjective sides. This distinction is obvious and is present in every religion. The _religio objectiva_ comes first. All people find it at birth; they grow up in it just as they do in the family, society, church, state, and so forth. These are all objective institutions and powers that do not arise arbitrarily from and through man, but receive him at birth, form and educate him, and continue to govern him throughout his life, even against his will. It conflicts with both history and psychology to proceed from the _religio subjectiva_ in determining the essence of religion and then to see in the various religions nothing but changing forms and indifferent expressions of its life. In this way everyday experience is ignored, the power of the _religio objectiva_ is denied, and every relation of object and subject is revolutionarily inverted.

The _religio objectiva_ certainly also has an origin, which must be sought and explained. Yet all religions whose origin is known to any degree show us that they arose in connection with what already existed and stand in the closest relation to the whole historical milieu. There is no religion that is the pure expression of the religious disposition and can be explained from that alone. The _religio subjectiva_ modifies and reshapes existing religious representations and practices and often inspires them with new life. But it does not create; always and everywhere it itself arises under the influence and operation of existing religions. There are therefore no founders of religion in the proper sense. The _religio objectiva_ is the hearth of the _religio subjectiva_, Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ I 123.

Moreover, all religions wish to be regarded not as the expression and form of a religious disposition but as the fruit of revelation. They derive their origin not from man but from God. Whether or not one allows this appeal to revelation, the fact is too powerful and too universal not to require explanation. It indicates that the concept of religion is inseparably connected with that of revelation. There is no religion without revelation. Revelation is the foundation and origin of all religion. The scholar is therefore not at liberty to eliminate this concept of revelation _a priori_ and to see in religions nothing but forms of one and the same essence, expressions of one and the same religious disposition.

Holy Scripture too derives the _religio objectiva_ from revelation. God, who at various times and in many ways spoke in time past to the fathers through the prophets, has in these last days spoken to us through the Son, Heb. 1:1. Indeed, it also lies in the nature of the case that revelation and religion are most closely connected. God alone can determine the manner in which he wishes to be served by human beings. _Hominum non est, instituere et formare Dei cultum, sed traditum a Deo recipere et custodire_, _Conf. Helv._ II, art. 19. According to the teaching of Holy Scripture, God has done this partly through nature and partly through his Word. The _religio objectiva_ therefore coincides with the Torah, taken in the broadest sense—that is, with that whole instruction of the Lord which comes to us in law and gospel, word and deed, history and prophecy. It is the principal content of Holy Scripture and the material of dogmatics. The _religio objectiva_ is nothing other than the manner in which God himself has determined that he wishes to be served and honored.

Formally, all religions agree. But the agreement extends still further. All religions are composed of several elements that constantly recur. First, in every religion there is a tradition concerning its divine origin; every religion appeals to revelation. This is the historical, positive element, the element of tradition. Next, in every religion there is a certain doctrine in which God reveals to man the knowledge indispensable for his service; this is designated by the word _dogma_. Further, every religion also contains certain laws that prescribe what man must do and refrain from doing in order to live in communion with God; this is the moral teaching that every religion brings with it. Finally, in every religion there is also a greater or smaller number of ceremonies—that is, solemn forms and practices that outwardly express, accompany, and strengthen the communion of man with God. This is the cultic or liturgical element in religion. In the various religions the mutual relation of these elements differs greatly; in some there is much dogma and little cultus, and conversely; in others there is a rich tradition and a small number of moral precepts, and so forth. But all four elements are present in every religion. The _religio vera_ deposited in Holy Scripture likewise has its history and dogma, its morality and cultus. F. A. B. Nitzsch, _Lehrb. der ev. Dogm._, 1889, S. 101–108; H. Siebeck, _Lehrb. der Religionsphilosophie_, Freiburg, Mohr, 1893, S. 263 f.
