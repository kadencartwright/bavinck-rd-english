## 4.

Corresponding to this _religio objectiva_ in man is the _religio subjectiva_. Formerly religion was very commonly defined as _recta verum Deum cognoscendi et colendi ratio_. This definition already occurs in Lactantius, _Instit. div._ 4,4, and has been retained into the present century. Yet it is not above criticism. It says nothing about the subjective disposition necessary for serving God; it does not indicate the connection between knowing and serving God, but simply places the two alongside one another; and it makes no mention of the other elements contained in religion.

Scholasticism penetrated more deeply into the matter and investigated the subjective disposition from which religion arises in man. Thomas includes it among the virtues. These he divides into three kinds: _virtutes intellectuales_ (_sapientia_, _scientia_, _intellectus_, etc.) and _virtutes morales_ (the four cardinal virtues, _prudentia_, _justitia_, _fortitudo_, and _temperantia_)—these two groups were borrowed from Aristotle—and, added to them as a third class, the three supernatural or theological virtues of faith, hope, and love, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qq. 57, 58, 62.

Now Thomas includes the _religio_ not among the _virtutes theologicae_ but among the _virtutes morales_. For the distinctive character of the theological virtues is that they have God as their direct object; they _ordinant nos ad Deum immediate et directe ut ad objectum_, ibid. II 1, qu. 62, art. 2. But in religion God is not the _objectum_ but the _finis_; the proper object in religion is the _cultus_ rendered to God. It is therefore not a _virtus theologica_, which has God as its object, but a _virtus moralis_, which is concerned _circa ea, quae sunt ad finem_; it _ordinat hominem in Deum, non sicut in objectum, sed sicut in finem_. Yet among the _virtutes morales_ it is the foremost, because it stands in the closest relation to him who is the goal of all virtues—namely, God, ibid. II 2, qu. 81, arts. 5, 6.

More specifically, Thomas reckons _religio_ among the moral virtue called _justitia_. He understands _religio_ as that virtue _per quam homines Deo debitum cultum et reverentiam exhibent_. Although James 1:27 also counts visiting orphans and widows, and so forth, as religion, nevertheless religion in the proper and narrower sense is a relation only to God and never to human beings. And although everything must be done to the glory of God, the honor rendered to God in religion is nevertheless intended in a special sense and, strictly speaking, comprises only what pertains _ad reverentiam Dei_. It is therefore one with _latreia_, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 81, arts. 1–4.

This definition of Thomas, which has been adopted by very many—cf. C. R. Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae -- -- sive Cursus Theologiae_, Tom. IV, 1746, pp. 5 sq.; Petrus Dens, _Theologia ad usum seminariorum_ IV, pp. 9 sq.; P. Collet, _Instit. Theol. moralis_ III, pp. 401 sq., etc.—is, first of all, too narrow, since it defines only the _cultus_ and thus coincides with _latreia_. The _religio subjectiva_ is not merely service or worship, but is first of all a disposition that expresses itself in that service. Further, the distinction between _virtutes morales_ and _virtutes theologicae_ is too supernaturalistic and dualistic. There is naturally some truth in it, namely, that even in fallen man remnants of the image of God and moral virtues still remain; even religion has not been entirely eradicated. But the _virtutes morales_ and the religious disposition too must be renewed and reborn in order to be truly good.

Thomas accordingly acknowledges that the theological virtues—faith, hope, and love—_causant actum religionis, quae operatur quaedam in ordine ad Deum_, _S. Th._ II 2, qu. 81, art. 5 ad 1, but they themselves are nevertheless excluded from religion. And whereas the _virtutes intellectuales_ and _morales_ are _secundum naturam hominis_, the _virtutes theologicae_ are _super naturam_, I 2, qu. 62, art. 2.

The Reformation modified this conception of religion especially in two respects. First, the theologians of the Reformation distinguish more clearly and correctly between _pietas_ as the principle and _cultus_ as the _actio_ of religion. Second, faith, hope, and love are not placed alongside religion as separate theological virtues, but are incorporated into religion itself precisely as the principal acts of the _cultus internus_. Religion, says Zwingli, _Opera_, ed. Schuler et Schulthess, III 155, comprises _pietatem totam Christianorum, fidem, vitam, leges, ritus, sacramenta_. It consists _in ea adhaesio, qua (homo) Deo utpote summo bono inconcusse fidit eoque parentis loco utitur_, ibid. 175; it is _animae deique connubium_, ibid. 180.

In Calvin we encounter three concepts: 1° _notitia_, the knowledge of God, the consciousness of his virtues; 2° this _notitia_ is _idoneus pietatis magister_; it cultivates _pietas_, which consists in _conjuncta cum amore Dei reverentia, quam beneficiorum ejus notitia conciliat_; and 3° it is again this _pietas_, _ex qua religio_, in the sense of _cultus_, _nascitur_, _Inst._ I, 2, 1. Zanchi likewise distinguishes, _Op._ IV 263 sq., between _cultus_, which designates the _actio externa vel interna, qua Deum veneramur_, and _religio_ or _pietas_, which is the _virtus_ from which _cultus_ is born. Polanus says, _Synt. Theol._ 580 A, that _religio_ differs from _cultus Dei, ut causa ab effectu_. _Religio_ or _pietas_ is the _causa interna cultus Dei_.

The _cultus_, the fruit of _pietas_, is then distinguished, as among the scholastics, into _internus_ and _externus_. The principal acts of the former are _fides_, _fiducia_, _spes_, _adoratio_, _dilectio_, _invocatio_, _gratiarum actio_, _sacrificium_, and _obedientia_; and the _cultus externus_ is in turn either _moralis_ (confession, prayer, etc.) or _ceremonialis_ (_sacramenta_, _sacrificia_, _sacra_).
