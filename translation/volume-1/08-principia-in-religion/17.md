## 17.

Yet this explanation of religion, however generally accepted, cannot satisfy. First, the idea of God remains unexplained. It is usually said that undeveloped, childlike natural man makes no distinction between the personal and the impersonal, conceives the forces in nature by analogy with himself, and regards all things as inhabited by souls or spirits. The foundation and presupposition of religion is then the so-called animistic worldview. But, first of all, it is highly improbable that the obvious distinction between personal beings and impersonal things was unknown to the first human beings. Next, belief in souls and spirits that dwell and work in natural phenomena is not in itself religion. These souls or spirits become religious objects only when the concept of the divine is transferred to them. A certain consciousness of deity must be assumed in man before he can make the souls and spirits that he thought present in the forces of nature into objects of his religious worship.

The psychological method indeed attempts to reverse the order. According to it, natural man first placed himself in a religious relation to some force of nature conceived as personal, and later abstracted the concept of the divine from this, Hartmann, _Religionsphil._ I 15. But in any case this is not how matters proceed among historical human beings. All first receive the concept of deity through instruction and education and on that basis develop a religious relation. And there is no proof whatever that it proceeded differently among the first human beings or even could have proceeded differently. A religious relation to any power always already presupposes the idea of God; and this remains unexplained by the method described above.

Moreover, the origin of religion in the subjective sense likewise remains incomprehensible under this psychological analysis. Grant that belief in personal beings in nature is given; but why does—and especially when does—the seeking of some sensible or moral good from those beings become religion? Religion arises only when help is sought not in general, as human beings also seek it from one another and in art and science, but when faith, trust, and a consciousness of dependence with respect to an invisible divine power are awakened in the heart in a wholly distinctive manner. Religion always presupposes a certain distinction between God and world, between the power of a being above and the subordinate powers in nature. That divine power may indeed then be conceived as dwelling within the phenomena of nature, but the object of religious worship is never the natural force in itself, but the divine being who reveals himself and works in it.

The greatest objection to the explanation described above, however, lies in this: under it God becomes nothing other than a helper in distress, a being whose existence has been devised to supply man with some good or other. Religion becomes a means for satisfying man’s sensible or moral, but nevertheless always egoistic, needs. Egoism was the source and origin of religion, Hartmann, _Religionsphil._ I 27. The first form in which this explanation of the origin of religion was presented above states this clearly and without concealment. In the second form, religion is derived not from sensible but from moral needs; deity is not a product of eudaemonistic wishes but a postulate of practical reason. Yet this makes little difference in the essence of the matter. The subjective and egoistic point of departure is not overcome; God remains a servant of man. The third form attempts to avoid this egoism by acknowledging a mystical element in religion and thus making God the direct object of religious worship. But here too this mysticism was added only later; it was not the principle and origin of religion.

Under the explanation described above, in whatever form it is presented, man always stands first in relation to the world; and religion and the idea of God are born from conflict with that world. God thus comes to stand in third place; he becomes a _Wunschwesen_, a product of man’s egoism, an expedient in the struggle against nature. The affinity of all these explanations with that of Feuerbach is immediately apparent. But it may be asked: why is man not satisfied with the powers present in nature? Why does he not seek in science, art, industry, and so forth—in all the factors of culture—the help he needs in the struggle against nature? How can it be explained that man continues to believe in the gods, creations of his wishes, even where they so often disappoint him and abandon him to his own fate? Why does he offer everything, even what is dearest to him, in sacrifice to his gods and consider nothing too precious for their worship? Finally, how does it happen that he continues to serve the gods even as his dominion over nature gradually expands through other means and ways? If the explanation of the origin of religion described above is correct, religion forfeits its independence over against art and science, and its end is reached as soon as man can save himself in another way.
