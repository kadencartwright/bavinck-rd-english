### D. Result

## 19.

The preceding investigation compels us to choose another point of departure and to follow another method. The nature and origin of religion cannot be explained by the historical and psychological method; its right and value cannot thereby be maintained. It will not do to understand religion without God. God is the great presupposition of religion. His existence and revelation are the foundation on which all religion rests. It is indeed called unscientific to go back to God in the explanation of any phenomenon; and he may certainly not serve as an _asylum ignorantiae_. But it is nevertheless an impoverished science that may take no account of God and seeks to explain all things outside and apart from him.

In the scientific explanation of religion this applies in a double sense. For God is here the proper and direct object. Without him all religion is an absurdity. The choice here lies only between these two: either religion is folly because God does not exist or is absolutely unknowable, or it is truth, but then inexorably and in the strict logical and scientific sense requires and presupposes his existence and revelation. Whoever cannot accept the first is compelled to assume the second and to acknowledge God as the _principium essendi_ of all religion. Religion exists only because God exists and wishes to be served by creatures. Only when the existence of God is established can the nature and origin, right and value, of religion be understood.

But religion requires still more. It presupposes not only that God exists but also that he reveals and makes himself known in one way or another. All religions possess this concept of revelation. It is not imposed upon religion from without but flows of itself from religion’s origin and essence. There is no religion without revelation; revelation is the necessary correlate of religion. The essence of religion consists not only subjectively in a religious disposition that expresses itself as it pleases, but also in a _religio objectiva_, in a dogma, morality, and cultus that possess authority for the believer only because, in his conviction, they contain the will and service of God. The origin of religion cannot be demonstrated historically or explained psychologically, but necessarily points to revelation as its objective foundation.

The distinction between religion on the one side and science and art on the other presents us with this same concept of revelation. Nature, the world around us, is the source of our knowledge and _artis magistra_. But in religion that same world comes into consideration from yet another point of view—namely, as revelation of God, as the disclosure of his eternal power and divinity. In religion man is concerned with something wholly different from what concerns him in science and art. In religion he seeks neither an increase of his knowledge nor satisfaction of his imagination, but eternal life in communion with God, a real transformation of his being, a deliverance from sin and misery. In religion he is concerned with God himself, because he understands that only in God can he find rest and peace. Religion therefore requires a different source from science and art; it presupposes a revelation that causes God himself to come to man and brings man into communion with him. In its nature and origin religion is a product of revelation.

Gradually this too is beginning to be acknowledged. The philosophy of religion, which for a time deemed the concept of revelation unworthy of serious discussion and subjected it only to negative criticism, is compelled to reckon with it. The conception of that revelation still differs greatly and is sometimes very incorrect; but the fact is nevertheless significant that many take up this concept once again and attempt to assign it a positive meaning. Cf. Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 13 f.; Rauwenhoff, _Wĳsb._ 46; Hartmann, II 69 f.; Biedermann, _Dogm._ I 264; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 53; Nitzsch, _Dogm._ 127; Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsd._ I 136 f., etc. Holy Scripture proceeds from the existence and revelation of God. God does not leave himself without witness, and therefore on man’s side there is a seeking, in the hope that he might find and feel after him. According to Scripture, revelation existed both before and after the fall. Revelation is the _principium cognoscendi externum_ of religion.
