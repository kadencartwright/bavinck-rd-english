When the nature and origin of religion are thus explained from revelation, this may not be understood in the Socinian sense, as though religion were not grounded in man’s nature but arose from an external communication of doctrine, Fock, _Der Socin._ 291 f. In that case Schelling, _Philos. der Mythol._ I 141, would rightly have observed: _wäre der ursprüngliche Mensch nicht an sich schon Bewusstseyn von Gott, müsste ihm ein Bewusstseyn von Gott erst durch einen besonderen Actus zu Theil werden, so müssten die, welche diess annehmen, selbst einen ursprünglichen Atheïsmus des menschlichen Bewusstseyns behaupten_. Religion would then be a _donum superadditum_, not something essentially proper to human nature.

But man is man because he is the image of God; immediately as man he is a religious being. Religion is not the essence of man, as des Amorie van der Hoeven Jr. expressed himself less accurately, for religion is not a _substantia_ but a _habitus_ or _virtus_. It is nevertheless an essential property of human nature, so naturally given with it and so inseparably connected with it that, though devastated by sin, it could not be eradicated. Religion is therefore also universal and possesses such great power in life and history. Whether one wishes it or not, one always finally encounters a certain religious disposition in man. One may call it by different names—_semen religionis_, _sensus divinitatis_ (Calvin), religious feeling (Schleiermacher, Opzoomer), faith (Hartmann, _Relig. Philos._ II 67), and so forth—but it is always a certain receptivity of human nature for apprehending the divine, to which philosophical investigation into religion must return and at which it must end. It cannot penetrate behind this.

For it must be one of two things. Either religion is essentially proper to human nature and thus given with it from the outset; or man was originally not a religious being and hence also not a human being but an animal, and gradually developed into a religious being—in which case religion is accidental and a passing moment in the process of evolution. The question to which philosophical investigation finally descends is this: was man from the beginning human, the image of God, akin to God, a religious being; or did he gradually develop into this? Was culture or raw, savage nature the first condition of the human race? Was humanity’s beginning absolute or relative? Cf. Schelling, _Vorles. über die Meth. des akad. Studiums_, _Werke_ I 5, S. 286 f.

Whoever wishes to maintain religion in its essence and regards and acknowledges it as an essential property of human nature takes man’s beginning as absolute and causes him from the outset to be akin to God and religiously disposed. Such a person can no longer have any objection to the _status integritatis_ in which Scripture causes the first man to appear. According to Scripture, man was human from the first moment of his existence, created in God’s image, and hence from that same moment also a religious being. Religion was not added later by a separate creation or along the long road of evolution, but is naturally entailed in man’s having been created in God’s image. That original state was indeed corrupted and devastated by sin, but man nevertheless remains akin to God; he is θεου γενος and continues to seek him, in the hope that he might feel after and find him.

Thus corresponding in man to God’s objective revelation is a certain _facultas_, an _aptitudo_ of his nature for apprehending the divine. God does no work by halves. He creates not only the light but also the eye with which to behold that light. The internal answers to the external. The ear is adapted to the world of tones. The _logos_ in creatures corresponds to the _logos_ in man and makes science possible. The beauty in nature finds resonance in his sense of beauty. And so there is not only an external, objective revelation but also an internal, subjective revelation. The former is the _principium cognoscendi externum_ of religion and the latter the _principium cognoscendi internum_. Both _principia_ stand in the closest relation to one another, like light and the eye, thought in the world and reason in man.

The question which of the two came first, external or internal revelation, is entirely superfluous. At the same moment in which God revealed himself to man by creating him in his image, man knew and served God, and conversely. True, genuine religion can exist only in a perfect harmony of internal with external revelation. Truly religious, the image of God, servant of God, child of God, human in the full sense is the one who loves God, as he is and makes himself known through revelation, with all his heart and all his soul and all his strength.
