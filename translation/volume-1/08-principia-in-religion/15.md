## 15.

The result, therefore, is that religion is not restricted to one of man’s faculties but embraces the whole man. The relation to God is total and central. We must love God with our whole understanding, our whole soul, and all our strength, and so forth. Precisely because God is God, he claims us entirely, in soul and body, with all our faculties and in all our relations. There is indeed order in this relation of man to God as well. Here too every faculty in man exists and operates according to its own nature. Knowledge is first; there is no right service of God without right knowledge. _Ignoti nulla cupido._ What is unknown is unloved. Whoever comes to God must believe that he is and that he is a rewarder of those who seek him, Heb. 11:6. Faith comes by hearing, Rom. 10:13, 14. The pagans fell into idolatry and unrighteousness because they did not retain God in their knowledge, Rom. 1:18 f.

But this knowledge of God penetrates the heart and there awakens all manner of affections of fear and hope, sorrow and joy, guilt and forgiveness, misery and deliverance, as they are portrayed for us throughout Scripture and above all in the Psalms. And through the heart it works in turn upon the will; faith reveals itself in love and in works, James 1:27; 1 John 1:5–7; Rom. 2:10, 13; Gal. 5:6; 1 Cor. 13, etc. Head, heart, and hand are equally, though each in its own way, laid claim to by religion; it takes the whole man, soul and body, into its service.

Religion therefore also comes into contact with all the other powers of culture, especially science, morality, and art. Proudhon once said: _il est étonnant, qu’au fond de toutes les choses nous retrouvons la théologie_. But Donoso Cortés rightly replied: _dans ce fait il n’y a rien d’étonnant que l’étonnement de Mr. Proudhon_. Religion, as relation to God, indicates the place in which man stands toward all other creatures. It contains dogma, law, and cultus, and therefore stands in close connection with science, morality, and art. It embraces the whole man in his thinking, feeling, and acting, in his whole life, everywhere and at all times. Nothing falls outside religion. It extends its power over the whole man and all humanity, over family, society, and state. It is the foundation of the true, the good, and the beautiful. It brings unity, coherence, and life into world and history. From it science, morality, and art took their origin; to it they return and find rest. It is the beginning and the end, the soul of everything, the highest and the deepest. What God is for the world, religion is for man. Staudenmaier, _Encyklopädie der theol. Wiss._, 1834, S. 114 f., 146.

And yet religion is distinct from all the powers of culture and preserves its independence over against them all. Religion is central; science, morality, and art are partial. Religion embraces the whole man, but science, morality, and art are rooted in the different faculties of understanding, will, and disposition. Religion intends nothing less than eternal salvation in communion with God; science, morality, and art are limited to creatures and seek to enrich this life through the true, the good, and the beautiful. Religion can therefore be equated with nothing else; in the life and history of humanity it occupies its own independent, unique, and all-governing place.

Its indispensability can even be proved from the fact that, at the very moment man rejects religion as a delusion, he nevertheless makes some creature or other into his God again and seeks in another way compensation for his religious need: in the service of humanity (Comte), in moral idealism (Salter), in the formation of ideals (Lange, Pierson), in Spiritism, Theosophy (Mme. Blavatsky, Mrs. Besant), or in other religions such as Buddhism, Mohammedanism, and so forth. Cf. H. Druskowitz, _Moderne Versuche eines Religionsersatzes_, Heidelberg, 1886; idem, _Zur neuen Lehre_, 1888.
