## 13.

Finally, there are also those who assign religion a place in feeling and sometimes even assume for it a separate faculty in man. Mysticism and Pietism had already prepared the way for this in earlier times. But only through the Romanticism of the previous century did this conception for a time gain dominion. Romanticism was in general a reaction of the free, unbound life of feeling against the objective classicism that bound and regulated everything. The subject elevated itself above the laws imposed upon its spontaneous expression in every sphere of life; imagination reclaimed its rights over against the understanding; the organic conception took the place of the mechanical; the idea of becoming displaced that of making. In every sphere eyes were opened to the free, the natural, and the genial. Becoming, growth, and development were the manner in which things arose. Not the useful but the beautiful, not prose but poetry, not labor but play, not _Machwerk_ but art possessed the highest value. The mannerism of the past turned into a shallow sentimentality.

Such was the movement that found its interpreters in England in Young, Southey, Wordsworth, and S. T. Coleridge. In France it was introduced by Rousseau, who in his _profession de foi du vicaire Savoyard_ (_Émile_, livre 4) builds his entire deistic dogmatics and morality from feeling: _le sentiment est plus que la raison, notre sensibilité est antérieure à notre intelligence_. In Germany Winckelmann opened people’s eyes to the beauty of Greek art; Lessing set the genius of Shakespeare over against the poetry of French classicism; Herder pointed out in history the revelation and operation of eternal, divine nature; Hamann, Claudius, Lavater, and Stilling proceeded from rich subjectivity and set this over against the shallow, mechanical conception of the _Aufklärung_; Kant and Fichte placed the human ego in the foreground. Everywhere there was a breach with objectivity; the subject became the absolute principle.

Under this influence Jacobi appealed to feeling as the _unmittelbare Vernehmen des Göttlichen_. Religion is feeling for the true, the beautiful, and the good; admiration, love, and esteem for the divine. And such a feeling is innate in us as _Grundtrieb der menschlichen Natur_, _Werke_ II 59 f., 194 f., etc. The aesthetic Rationalism of Jacobi was elaborated by Fries and de Wette in such a way that they made a strict separation between the empirical-mathematical worldview of the understanding and the ideal, aesthetic-religious worldview of feeling.

Schleiermacher’s conception of religion must be explained from the same Romantic tendency. In the second address of his _Reden über die Religion_, 1799, he defines religion as the immediate consciousness of the being of all that is finite in and through the infinite. It is neither knowing nor doing, neither metaphysics nor morality, but feeling of the infinite. The object of that feeling is not a personal God with whom man lives in communion, but the universe, the world conceived as a whole and a unity. And the organ for apprehending that infinite is not understanding, reason, or will, but feeling, the direction of the disposition toward and the sense for the infinite. This feeling is not defined more precisely. Still vaguer is the answer to the question when that feeling becomes specifically religious feeling. In the third address Schleiermacher answers only in figurative language: one must open one’s feeling as widely as possible to the world as a whole, contemplate everything in the one and the one in everything, understand every particular as a revelation of the infinite, and so forth. All this says little. It appears that ultimately every feeling is religious which is awakened by the totality of the world and reveals the highest unity to us. In any case, religious feeling is not clearly delimited from aesthetic feeling.

In the _Glaubenslehre_ we encounter essentially the same conception. Here too piety is feeling, § 3, and specifically the feeling of absolute dependence, § 4. Yet there is a twofold difference. In the _Reden_ God was the whole; in the _Glaubenslehre_ he is the absolute causality of the world. Correspondingly, feeling there was a sense for the infinite, and here is immediate self-consciousness and absolute dependence. God here acquires more of an existence of his own, distinct from the world, and religion therefore also acquires a content of its own, distinct from feeling for the world. Thus some approach to Theism can be discerned. Yet the basic thought remains the same insofar as God is conceived not as transcendent above but only as immanent in the world, and the organ for the divine is not reason, conscience, and so forth, but feeling.

This conception of religion has not only been adopted, though with modifications, by some mediation theologians; it is found in principle among all who seek to construct an aesthetic worldview alongside the mechanical and incorporate religion into it or even cause religion to be completely absorbed into it. Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._, S. 830, says that the core of religion consists not in a doctrine concerning God and so forth, but in the elevation of the disposition above reality and in the _Erschaffung einer Heimath der Geister_. Above the world of facts, _die Welt des Seienden_, man constructs by his imagination a _Welt der Werthe_, a _Welt der Dichtung_. Pierson, _Eene levensbeschouwing_, embraces a similar formation of ideals: man’s feeling, stimulated from without, creates from the representations that the understanding obtains from the sensibly perceptible world religious, ethical, and aesthetic ideals which, though not realities existing independently of us, are nevertheless of great value for our life, pp. 113–179.

Opzoomer even assumed a separate religious feeling and saw in it the source of religious representations, _De weg der wetenschap, een handboek der logika_, 1851, § 15; _De Godsdienst_, pp. 126–140; _Het wezen der kennis, leerboek der logica_, § 14. Rauwenhoff indeed considers the essence of religion to lie in faith in a moral world order, but nevertheless ascribes the form of religion—the worship of a personal God—to imagination. Cf. also Guyau, _L’irreligion de l’avenir_, Paris, Alcan, 1887; Dr. R. Koch, _Natur und Menschengeist_, Berlin, 1891, etc.
