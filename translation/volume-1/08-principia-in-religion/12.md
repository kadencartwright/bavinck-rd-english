## 12.

Now there can be no doubt that religion and morality are most intimately connected.
Their affinity is evident first of all from the fact that religion itself is a moral relation.
Religion does indeed rest on a mystical union of God and man, but it is not itself a substantial communion of the two; rather, it is an ethical relation of man to God.
God has no religion, but God’s indwelling in man engenders, on man’s part, that relation to God which we call religion, Hoekstra, _Wĳsg. Godsdienstleer_ I 57 f., 64.
This relation is therefore ethical in nature; it is regulated by the same _lex moralis_ that also determines man’s other relations to his fellow creatures.
All man’s religious acts are moral duties, and the whole of religion is a moral command.
Conversely, the moral life is in turn a service of God.
To visit widows and orphans, James 1:27, is not a religious act in the proper sense, but it can nevertheless be called religion because religion must manifest and confirm itself in it.
Scholasticism therefore distinguished between the _actus eliciti_ and the _actus imperati_ of religion, Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2 qu. 81 art. 1.
Faith without works, without love, is a dead faith.
For us, love for God proves itself in love for our neighbor, Jer. 22:16; Isa. 1:11 f.; 1 John 2:3 f.; James 2:17, etc.
Our entire life ought to be a service of God.
The inscription “I am the Lord your God” also stands above the commandments of the second table.
Love is the one great principle that fulfills the whole law, Rom. 13:13.
The moral law is one organic whole, so that whoever transgresses one commandment violates the entire law, James 2:10.
Our neighbor must be loved for God’s sake, and sin against our neighbor is also sin against God.
This relation between religion and morality already makes clear that morality can never constitute either the content or the foundation of religion.
For despite their intimate affinity, the two are essentially distinct.
Religion is always a relation to God; morality, a relation to human beings.
Religion has πιστις as its principle; morality, ἀγαπη.
Religion manifests itself in those religious acts which together constitute _cultus internus_ and _cultus externus_; morality manifests itself in acts of justice, mercy, honesty, etc., toward one’s neighbor.
Religion is regulated by the first table of the law, morality by the second.
This distinction between religion and _ethos_ cannot be maintained by pantheism, because there God has no independent existence of his own, distinct from the world.
No personal relation between God and man is then possible; love for God can express itself only in love for one’s neighbor.
Deism too, because it denies communion between God and man, cannot foster religion in the proper sense.
There is still belief in God, but no service of God apart from fulfilling the moral commandments.
From the theistic standpoint, however, man stands in relation to the world but also in a distinct relation of his own to God as a personal being.
Religion is therefore essentially different from morality and manifests itself in a series of acts of its own.
If this is so, morality cannot be the foundation of religion; on the contrary, religion must form the basis of morality.
The relation to God is then the primary and central relation that determines all man’s other relations.
Both historically and logically, morality is always grounded in religion.
Religion enjoins moral duties, and morality seeks the sanction of religion.
An autonomous morality is found nowhere in reality.
Everywhere and among all peoples, morality finds its ultimate ground and its ultimate end in religion.
Morality loses the ground beneath its feet when it is deprived of divine authority in conscience.
_Alle Moral hat sich geschichtlich aus der Religion entwickelt, und wenn auch die so entwickelten geistigen Anlagen der Menschheit zur Sittlichkeit Dank ihrer Herkunft eine Zeit lang selbstständig fortbestehen können, wenn sie von ihrem Mutterboden abgelöst werden, so ist doch diese selbstständige Existenzfähigkeit zeitlich sehr begrenzt und schon in der zweiten Generation machen zich deutlich die Symptome des Verfalls der Sittlichkeit bemerkbar_, E. v. Hartmann, _Relig. philos._ II 59.
Materially, of course, by no means all obligations and actions that human beings regard as moral accord with the will of God.
But the formal element—that which makes every duty an unconditional duty and binds man in conscience—is from God.
There is no morality without metaphysics.
Human beings, customs, mores, etc., cannot impose an absolute obligation upon the conscience.
God alone can do so.
Therefore the conscience is sacrosanct, and freedom of conscience is an inexorable demand and an inalienable right.
Commands and prohibitions that are not anchored in the conscience are not felt to be moral.
A law that has no foundation in the conscience of a people is powerless.
In virtue of this connection, religion and morality also exert a reciprocal influence on each other.
For a time they can become separated, both in an individual and in a people, and can even conflict with each other.
But they cannot rest until they are in harmony and equilibrium.
What is approved in religion cannot be condemned in morality, and vice versa.
The relations to God and to human beings must both bear the same moral character and be regulated by the same moral law.
Religion and morality, _cultus_ and culture, must be rooted in the same principle.
This is the case in Christianity.
Love is the fulfillment of the law and the bond of perfection.
