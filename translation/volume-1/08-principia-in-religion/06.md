## 6.

Through the operation of the _religio objectiva_, the _religio subjectiva_ passes from its habitual state into acts. These acts are internal or external, and thus give rise to the distinction between _cultus internus_ and _externus_. _Religio_ and _cultus_ are related as cause and effect. Yet this does not mean that the _cultus_ is a free invention and expression of subjective religion. All ἐθελοθρησκεια is forbidden, Matt. 15:9; Mark 7:7; Col. 2:23. Both antinomian Anabaptism and nomistic Romanism must be avoided here. God alone determines how he wishes to be served. And the regeneration of the _religio subjectiva_ corrupted by sin, of the _habitus religionis_, consists precisely in this: that believers receive a sincere desire to live in perfection according not merely to some but to all the commandments of God. It is their food to do the will of the Father. Jesus never spoke or did anything except what he had received a command from the Father to do. Scripture therefore places such strong emphasis on walking in God’s commandments, keeping his ordinances, and so forth. And God regenerates man in order to bring him, who is averse to his service, once again into conformity in the innermost part of his being with his will and law as deposited in the _religio objectiva_.

The _cultus internus_ comprises the acts of faith, trust, fear, love, prayer, thanksgiving, and so forth, and the _cultus externus_ manifests itself in confession, prayer, song, the ministry of the Word and sacraments, vows, fasting, watching, and so forth. The latter is therefore partly moral and partly ceremonial, and can in turn be _solitarius_ or _socius_. In the latter case it is private or public; common public worship is regulated in church orders. In all these religious acts, the consciousness of absolute dependence is the fundamental religious thought, the animating element. Severed from it, they become service of the letter, lip service, cold and dead formalism. But animated by it, they all receive their specifically religious character.

Creatures too are objects of our faith, our hope, our love, and so forth. What stamps all these acts as religious is that they place us in relation to a Person on whom, together with all things, we are dependent in an absolute sense and yet in a distinctive manner—that is, as rational creatures. For the essence of religion can lie in nothing other than this: that God is glorified and thanked precisely as _God_. Every religion that falls short here detracts from the honor of God and to that same degree also ceases to be genuinely religious. True religion, by contrast, consists in such a disposition of man as, on the one hand, is rooted in the profound consciousness of his absolute dependence on God as Creator, Redeemer, Sanctifier, and so forth, and, on the other hand, strives to walk sincerely according to all God’s ordinances.
