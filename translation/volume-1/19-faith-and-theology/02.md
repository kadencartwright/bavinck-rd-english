## 2.

No one will deny that there is seriousness and truth in these complaints about theology. It has repeatedly overshot its goal and degenerated into battology. All too often it has forgotten that our knowing on earth is knowing in part and seeing through a glass darkly. At times it seemed to proceed from the thought that it could answer every possible question and solve every problem. It has often lacked modesty, tenderness, and simplicity. This was all the worse because theology deals with the deepest problems and comes into contact with the finest stirrings of the human heart. More than any other science the admonition μη ὑπερφρονειν παρ’ ὁ δει φρονειν is fitting for it. An honest _non liquet_ is better than a daring conjecture. _Nescire velle, quae Magister optimus docere non vult, erudita inscitia est._ But this does not yet condemn theology in principle. For if revelation consisted only in the communication of life and religion only in states of mind, there would be no place for theology proper. But revelation is a system of words and deeds of God; it contains a world of thoughts; it has its center in the incarnation of the Logos. And religion is not feeling and emotion alone but is also faith, a conscious life, a serving of God with heart and head together. Therefore that revelation of God can be thought through, so that it may enter all the better into human consciousness. Theology cannot even be blamed for applying itself to clarity in thought, precision in distinction, and exactness in expression. In all sciences such _praecisitas_ is pursued and prized; it is equally in place in theology. The danger that it will thereby degenerate into sophistries and hairsplitting exists equally in other sciences, for example law and letters. But no one will therefore dispute the right of those sciences. Theology too has its periods of flourishing and decline, but it is not permissible to condemn theology itself because of the abuse made of it. _Abusus non tollit usum._ Further, it has already become evident to us that the separation between the Christian religion on the one hand and metaphysics and so forth on the other is neither conceived purely nor practicable. History has repeatedly proved this already and demonstrates it again today. To make such a separation even somewhat possible, all the tendencies named above are compelled to form for themselves a one-sided and incomplete conception of the gospel of Christ. They almost never return to the whole of Scripture, but always to one part: to the New Testament alone, or to the Gospels, or to the Sermon on the Mount, or even to a single text. Francis of Assisi, for example, ordered his whole life according to Matt. 10:9, 10, Reuter, _Gesch. der rel. Aufklärung im M. A._ II 184, 186; Paul Sabatier, _Leben des h. Fr. v. A._, German by M. L., Berlin, 1895, p. 53. Tolstoy finds the kernel of the gospel in Matt. 5:38, 39, _Worin besteht mein Glaube_, Leipzig, Duncker, 1885, p. 12. Drummond seeks the _summum bonum_ in the love of 1 Cor. 13. Ritschl changes the dogmas into religious-ethical value judgments. Harnack, _D. G._ I² 54, agrees with Ritschl in his conception of the original gospel. And many now ask why the Christian churches were not content with the Sermon on the Mount. What the essence of Christianity is, wherein revelation or the Word of God consists, who the person of Christ is—these matters are not decided by the apostles; everyone determines them for himself according to his own insights. The result is that all these tendencies must set not only church, confession, and theology but also the apostles over against Jesus and the original gospel. Harnack, for example, _D. G._ I 53 f., acknowledges that even the apostolic-catholic doctrine in the New Testament no longer reproduces the gospel of Jesus purely. The influence of Judaism, Hellenism, and Greco-Roman _Religionsphilosophie_ is already perceptible there. The apostles themselves, especially Paul and John, already falsified the gospel. Ernst von Bunsen, _Die Reconstruction der kirchl. Autorität_, Leipzig, Brockhaus, 1893, argues that Paul changed the gospel of Jesus into a speculative theology. And when a fact such as the resurrection of Christ nevertheless cannot be removed from the original gospel, it is stripped of its religious value, Harnack, _D. G._ I 74. It follows further from this conception that the history of dogma cannot come into its own. It becomes one great aberration of the human, of the Christian, spirit. The promise of the Spirit who would lead into all truth proves to have been vain. Even the possibility of knowing the truth is taken from the church, since the apostles already led her astray. The doctrines of the Logos, the Trinity, the first and second Adam, and so forth—all dogmas that are supposed to prove the intrusion of Greek philosophy—are found, though not verbally yet substantially, already in Scripture. In a word, the history of dogmas becomes, as with Strauss, the history of their criticism. The church did not overcome the world; the world overcame the church. Finally, this one-sided conception of the original gospel also threatens the danger of losing communion with the church of all ages and thereby with the age in which one lives. This has been the fate of all sects. Cut off from the churches and despising theology, they lost their influence upon their age and their bond with culture. Congregation and world, church and school, religion and science fall dualistically apart. Theology, by contrast, has the glorious calling to keep these two connected with each other: on the one hand, to preserve Christian life from all manner of spiritual illnesses of mysticism and separatism; and on the other, to deliver scientific thought from error and falsehood through the truth of Christ. The right of theology is grounded in the essence of the Christian religion. Revelation addresses the whole man and has the whole world as its object. In every domain it takes up the struggle against falsehood. It offers material to the deepest thought and, in the scientific domain, plants the knowledge of God alongside and in organic connection with the knowledge of man and world. Concerning the dogma-historical views of Harnack and Hatch, consult Pfleiderer, _Entw. der prot. Theol._, p. 369 f.; Kuenen, _Theol. Tijdschr._, 1891, p. 487 f.; Van Rhijn, _Theol. Studiën_, 1891, pp. 365 f., 437 f.; Dr. W. Schmidt, _Der alte Glaube und die Wahrheit des Christ._, Berlin, Wigandt, 1894; Henri Bois, _Le dogme grec_, Paris, Fischbacher, 1892.
