## 3.

Although Christian dogma cannot be explained from Greek philosophy, it nevertheless did not arise without it.
In Scripture there is as yet no dogma and no theology in the proper sense.
As long as revelation itself was still continuing, it could not become the object of scientific reflection.
Inspiration had to be completed before reflection could have its say.
The usage of the terms Mosaic, Pauline, and biblical theology and dogmatics, and so forth, is therefore not to be commended; moreover, the word theology does not occur in Scripture and only gradually acquired its present meaning, Kuyper, _Encycl._ II 117 f.
Theology first arose in the congregation of Christ when childlike naïveté had passed and reflective consciousness had awakened.
Gradually the need arose to think through the ideas of revelation, to connect them with the rest of knowledge, and to defend them against all kinds of attack.
For this philosophy was needed.
Scientific theology arose with its aid.
But this did not happen accidentally.
The church was not the victim of deception.
The church fathers made extensive use of philosophy in the formation and development of the _dogmata_.
But they did so in full consciousness, with clear insight into the dangers connected with it, with a clear understanding of the grounds on which they did so, and with explicit acknowledgment of the word of the apostles as the sole rule of faith and life.
Accordingly, they did not make use of the whole of Greek philosophy; they made a selection.
They used only that philosophy which was most suited to thinking through and defending the truth of God.
They proceeded eclectically and adopted no single philosophical system, whether that of Plato or that of Aristotle, but with the aid of Greek philosophy produced a distinctively Christian philosophy.
Moreover, they used that philosophy only as an aid.
Just as Hagar served Sarah, as the Israelites used the treasures of Egypt to adorn the tabernacle, and as the wise men from the East laid their gifts at the feet of the child in Bethlehem, so in the judgment of the church fathers philosophy was subordinate to theology.
All this clearly shows that the use of philosophy in theology rested not upon a mistake but upon a firm and clear conviction.
The church fathers knew what they were doing.
This admittedly does not exclude the possibility that the influence of philosophy upon them was too strong at certain points.
But then one must immediately distinguish between the theology of the _patres_ and the _dogmata_ of the church.
The church has at all times guarded against the misuse of philosophy; it not only rejected Gnosticism but also condemned Origenism.
And thus far no one has succeeded in explaining the _dogmata_ materially from philosophy; however often this has been attempted, in the end the scriptural character of orthodoxy has always been vindicated.
Cf. Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ IV 143 f.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher usw._ II 572 f.

At first the Reformation adopted a hostile posture toward scholasticism and philosophy.
But it soon abandoned this posture; because it was not and did not wish to be a sect, it could not dispense with theology.
Luther and Melanchthon therefore already returned to the use of philosophy and acknowledged its usefulness; cf. Ritter, _Gesch. der neuern Philos._ I 495 f.; Ueberweg, _Grundriss der Gesch. der Philos._ III, 5te Aufl., 1880, S. 17 f.; and for the later Lutheran theologians, Hase, _Hutterus Rediv._ § 30; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ § 5.
From the outset Calvin took this lofty position, _Inst._ II, 2, 12 sq., and saw in philosophy a _praeclarum Dei donum_, _Opera_, ed. Amst., IX B. 50.
And all Reformed theologians judged likewise: Hyperius, _de theologo seu de ratione studii theol._, 1556, lib. I; Zanchi, _Opera_ III 223 sq., VIII 653 sq.; Alsted, _Praecognita theol._, p. 174 sq.; Voetius, _Disp._ III 741 sq., 751 sq.; Owen, _Theologumena_, p. 509 sq.; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 71 sq.
The question at issue, then, is not whether theology must make use of a particular philosophical system.
Christian theology has never taken over any philosophical system without criticism and certified it as truth.
Neither the philosophy of Plato nor that of Aristotle has been regarded by any theologian as the true philosophy.
That preference was nevertheless given to these two philosophical systems was due to the fact that they lent themselves best to developing and defending the truth.
It also expressed the thought that the Greeks and Romans had received a distinctive calling and gift for the life of culture.
In fact, our entire civilization is still built upon that of Greece and Rome today.
And Christianity did not destroy this inheritance but Christianized and thereby sanctified it.
Nevertheless, theology does not need a particular philosophy.
What it needs is philosophy in general.
In other words, scientific theology comes into being only through thinking.
The proper _principium cognoscendi internum_ of theology is therefore not faith as such but believing thought, the _ratio christiana_.
Faith is conscious and certain of itself.
It rests in revelation.
It includes knowledge, but that knowledge is wholly practical in nature, a _γιγνωσκειν_ in the sense of Holy Scripture.
Theology therefore does not arise from believers as such; it is not a fruit of the church as _ecclesia instituta_; it does not have its origin in the office that Christ bestowed upon his congregation.
But believers have another, richer life than that which comes to expression in the _ecclesia instituta_.
As Christians they also live in the family, the state, and society, and they practice science and art.
Many more gifts than those that work through the office are bestowed upon them, gifts of knowledge and wisdom and prophecy.
There are also among them persons who feel within themselves a strong impulse toward research and knowledge and who have received gifts to think through the truth of God and bring it into a system.
And thus theology arises in the church of Christ; its subject is not the instituted church but the church as organism, as the body of Christ; it is a fruit of the thought of Christendom.
