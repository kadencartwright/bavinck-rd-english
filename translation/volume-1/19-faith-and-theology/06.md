## 6.

In investigating the relation between faith and theology, the question must be stated precisely. It does not ask which truths a person must at minimum know and hold to be true in order to be saved. Let that question be left to Rome, and let Roman Catholic theology decide whether two or four or more articles are needed for this. In the doctrine of the _articuli fundamentales_, Protestant theology did indeed create the appearance that it wished to follow a similar path. But it ended by acknowledging that it did not know the magnitude of God’s mercy and therefore could not determine the measure of knowledge necessarily proper to a sincere faith. Moreover, despite every apparent correspondence, there is an important difference between the doctrine of _fides implicita_ and that of the _articuli fundamentales_. At Rome that doctrine was developed with a view to simple laypeople, the _asinae_ of Job 1:14. But in the theology of the Reformation it had its origin in the fact that various churches appeared alongside one another with confessions differing mutually on many points; it was therefore actually an inquiry into the essence of Christianity. At Rome faith is assent to all manner of revealed truths that can be enumerated article by article and have increased in number over the course of time. But the Reformation conceived faith as _fides specialis_, with a particular central object, the grace of God in Christ; here an enumeration of articles whose knowledge and acceptance were necessary for salvation was no longer possible. Faith is a personal relation of man to Christ; it is organic and has discarded addition and quantity. Rome therefore had to determine a minimum without which salvation could not be spoken of; in the Reformation faith is trust in the grace of God and is therefore no longer susceptible of calculation. Every believer in both the Old and the New Testaments possesses in germ the same knowledge that is developed more deeply and broadly in theology. From this standpoint the relation between faith and theology can also be further illuminated.

First of all, there is a strong correspondence between the two. They have in common the _principium_: the Word of God; the object: the knowledge of God; the goal: the glory of God. Theology as science also stands on the foundation of faith. The place occupied by observation in the other sciences is occupied here by faith. Faith furnishes theology with the material for thought. In mundane science the maxim is _sensus praecedit intellectum, nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in sensu_; in theology the watchword is _fides praecedit intellectum, nihil est in intellectu quod non prius fuerit in fide_, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ §267, II, p. 180 note. Leibniz therefore compared _la foi_ with _l’expérience_, _Discours sur la conformité de la foi avec la raison_, ch. 1. _Begriffe ohne Anschauungen sind leer_, Kant said; and so theology too has no content except from and through faith. As soon as it abandons faith, it itself ceases to exist as theology. Nor does it ever rise through thought above this standpoint of faith. This has indeed been attempted in all manner of ways but has proved vain. Faith is the beginning and also the end of theology; theology never reaches knowing in the proper sense, that is, knowledge on the ground of one’s own observation and insight. Theology is not thereby robbed of its freedom. Faith simply establishes and maintains the relation that ought to exist in this domain between subject and object. It places the theologian not outside, over against, and above, but under and within the truth he has to investigate. It does nothing other than bind theology to its own object, in no other way than every other science is and remains bound to observation and through it stands in relation to its object. Theology is as free and as dependent as every other science. It is free from all bonds that conflict with its nature, but it is wholly determined by the object it seeks to know, and it shares this with all sciences. The more strictly it binds itself to its object, the less danger it runs of degenerating into barren scholasticism and empty rhetoric. Through faith theology remains a science of religion, a _Theologie der Thatsachen_, which thinks not about concepts but about realities and does not lose itself in airy abstractions but stands with both feet in that world of realities which Scripture reveals to us, Vilmar, _Die Theologie der Thatsachen wider die Theologie der Rhetorik_, 4e Aufl., 1876.

On the other hand, however, there is a considerable difference between faith and theology. In earlier times the two could easily be confused because theology and dogmatics together with ethics were virtually identical concepts. But theology has now become the name for an entire cycle of disciplines. The distinction therefore now immediately catches everyone’s eye. Theology today comprises a multitude of sciences that the simple believer does not even know by name. But even if theology is taken in the old sense, the distinction remains great. In every domain there is a difference between ordinary, everyday, empirical knowledge and proper, higher, scientific knowledge. Every person has some empirical knowledge of sun, moon, stars, and so forth, but this knowledge differs vastly from the scientific knowledge of the astronomer. The former knows only the _facta_, the latter the _rationes_. The scientist does not disdain ordinary empirical knowledge; he does not overthrow natural certainty; but he nevertheless has the calling to clarify and expand that ordinary knowledge and, if necessary, also to purify and correct it. It is no different in theology. Faith remains with the facts; theology seeks to penetrate to the idea. Faith is satisfied with the _that_; theology asks after the _why_ and the _how_. Faith is always personal; it always places the object in relation to man himself and has a direct interest in the religious content of the dogmas. Theology in a certain sense makes the object _gegenständlich_; it tries to view the truth as it exists objectively in itself, traces its unity and inner connection, and seeks to arrive at a system. Faith directs itself to the central object; theology extends its investigation to the whole circumference of the circle. But however different, they cannot do without each other. Faith preserves theology from secularization; theology preserves faith from separatism. Church and school (seminary, theological faculty) are therefore indeed two, but they ought nevertheless to stand in close connection. Nor does this detract in any way from the freedom and independence of theology. Every faculty cultivates science not only for its own sake but also trains men for various positions in society. Every science must in fact take account of the demands of life. And so theology too stands not high above but in the midst of actual life, in the life of the congregation. The disproportion that now exists almost everywhere between church and theology is a disaster for both.
