## 7.

If theology thus has its _principium internum_ not in faith as such but in believing thought, the task of reason in theological science must be described more precisely.
Here one must first reject in principle the conception that sees faith and reason as two independent powers struggling with each other to the death.
In this way an antithesis is created that has no place on Christian ground.
Faith is then always _supra_ or even _contra rationem_.
Rationalism threatens on the one side and Supernaturalism on the other.
Faith, the _fides qua creditur_, is not an organ or faculty alongside and above reason but a disposition, a habit, of reason itself.
Reason—or, if one prefers, thought—is certainly not a source of theology, no _principium quo seu per quod aut ex quo seu cur credamus_, Voetius, _Disp._ I, p. 3; reason is a source for no science, or at most only for the formal sciences, logic and _mathesis_.
Yet reason is _subjectum fidei recipiens, fidei capax_; faith is an act of consciousness, of human consciousness; an animal is incapable of believing.
Further, faith is not compulsion but a free act of the human being.
The Christian does not believe on command, from fear, or through force.
Believing has become the natural _habitus_ of his understanding.
Naturally, this does not mean that there is not repeatedly much in his soul that resists that believing.
It does mean, however, that although he often does what he does not will, he nevertheless delights in the law of God according to the inward man.
Believing is the natural breath of the child of God.
His submission to the Word of God is not slavery but freedom.
In this sense faith is no _sacrificium intellectus_ but _sanitas mentis_.
Faith therefore does not release the Christian from investigation and reflection; rather, it incites him to them.
Nature is not destroyed by regeneration but restored.

The believer who wishes to devote himself to theology must therefore first prepare his thinking for the task awaiting him.
There is no entrance into the temple of theology except through the _facultas artium_.
Philosophical, historical, and linguistic propaedeutics are indispensable for the student of theological science.
Philosophy, said Clement of Alexandria, προκατασκευαζει την ὁδον τῃ βασιλικωτατῃ διδασκαλιᾳ.
Emperor Julian knew what he was doing when he deprived Christians of pagan learning; he feared being defeated with his own weapons.
This thinking, thus prepared and trained, then has principally a threefold task in theology.
First of all, it renders its service in finding the material.
Scripture is the principle of theology.
But that Scripture is no lawbook; it is an organic whole.
The material for theology, specifically for dogmatics, lies scattered throughout Scripture.
Just as gold must be mined, so the truth of faith must be excavated from Scripture with the exertion of all the powers of the mind.
A few _loca probantia_ accomplish nothing.
The dogma must be built not on a few isolated texts but on Scripture as a whole; it must arise organically from the _principia_ present for it everywhere in Scripture.
The doctrine of God, humanity, sin, Christ, and so forth is not found only in a few statements but is dispersed throughout Scripture and is contained not merely in certain proof passages but also in all manner of images and parables, ceremonies and historical narratives.
No part of Scripture may be neglected.
Let the whole of Scripture prove the whole system, Hofmann, _Der Schriftbeweis_, 2e Aufl., I, S. 1–32.
Separatism must also be avoided in theology.
It is characteristic of many sects that they take their point of departure in a small portion of Scripture and otherwise neglect it entirely.
The worst and most widespread instance is the rejection or neglect of the Old Testament.
Marcionism has repeatedly returned in the Christian church and also plays a large role in modern theology; cf. Diestel, _Gesch. des Alten Test. in der christl. Kirche_, Jena, 1869; H. Schmidt, _Der Marcionitismus in der neueren Theologie_, _Neue Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol._, 1893; and see below in the doctrine of the covenants.
All this arbitrary use of Holy Scripture leads to one-sidedness and error in theology and to sickness in religious life.
The full, rich image of the truth then fails to come to light.
The person and work of the Father, the Son, or the Holy Spirit are misconceived.
Christ is not given his due in his prophetic, priestly, or royal office.
The Christian religion forfeits its catholic character.
The head, heart, and hand of the Christian are not harmoniously formed and guided by the truth.
Only the full, whole Scripture preserves us from all these one-sided tendencies.
But for that reason thought also has an important task in this discovery of theological material.
Next, the theologian must also process through thought the material obtained in this way.
The _dogmata_ are found in Scripture not _totidem verbis_, κατα ρητον, but κατα διανοιαν; they are _conclusiones fidei_.
The doctrine of the Trinity, of the two natures of Christ, of satisfaction, of the sacraments, and so forth is not based on one single statement of Scripture but is built up from many data dispersed throughout Scripture.
_Dogmata_ are the concise summary in our language of everything Scripture teaches concerning the relevant subjects.
Roman Catholic and Protestant theologians therefore defended the right of dogmatic terminology against all manner of tendencies that wished to stop at the literal expressions of Scripture.
They did so not because they wished to be less scriptural than those tendencies but because they wished to be more and better scriptural.
In their view, Scripture came to its full honor precisely when not one individual text was cited literally but the whole truth comprehended in many texts was summarized and rendered in the dogma.
Theology is therefore not merely a noetic but also a dianoetic science, not an apprehensive but a discursive science.
It reflects, compares, judges, summarizes, deduces other truths from those obtained, and so forth.
Jesus and the apostles also did this, Matt. 22:32, 44 f.; John 10:34 f.; Acts 15:9 f., 18:28; 1 Cor. 15, etc.; and the church fathers, Scholastics, Roman Catholic theologians, and Protestant theologians followed that example.
God did not call us to repeat literally but to think after him what he thought beforehand for us in his revelation.
Finally, thought in theology also has the task of bringing all truth together in one system.
A system is the highest thing that can be desired in science.
Theology too does not rest until it has discovered the unity hidden in revelation.
It may not impose that system from without or press the truth into a philosophical system alien to its nature.
But it continues seeking until the system present in the object itself is reflected in human consciousness.
In all this theology proceeds just as the other sciences do.
It labors in the same manner.
Like them, it is bound to its object.
In its thinking it is subject to the laws that govern thought; theology too cannot sin against logic with impunity, Alsted, _Praecognita_ 186.
For theology too, the highest goal is the unity of truth, the system of the knowledge of God.
However much theology may differ from the other sciences in principle, object, and goal, it agrees with them formally and may rightly lay claim to the name of science.
And because revelation does not conflict with human reason _per se_ but only _per accidens corruptionis et pravae dispositionis_, theology may even in a certain sense be called _naturalis_ and _rationalis_, Voetius, _Disp._ I 3.
The Christian religion is a λογικη λατρεια, Rom. 12:1.
The literature on the use of reason and philosophy in theology is astonishingly rich.

El._, loc. 1, qu. 8–13; Witsius, _Misc. Sacra_ II 584 sq.; and further literature in M. Vitringa’s edition of C. Vitringa, _Doctrina christ. relig._ I, pp. 32–34.
