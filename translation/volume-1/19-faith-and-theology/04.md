## 4.

Faith and theology are therefore indeed distinct. People were also more or less conscious of this at all times. The sharp opposition Gnosticism drew between πιστις and γνωσις was rejected, and the relation the Alexandrian school assumed between the two was not approved and justified in every respect; cf. above, p. 60. Yet the distinction was emphatically maintained, while at the same time the intimate connection between the two was upheld and acknowledged. Augustine elevated the maxim _per fidem ad intellectum_ into a principle of theology; between the two he assumed a relation like that between conception and birth, work and reward. _Fidei fructus est intellectus_, _Tract._ 22 _in Ev. Joh._, n. 2. _Intellectus merces est fidei_, _Tract._ 29 _in Ev. Joh._, n. 6. He exhorts us _ut ea, quae fidei firmitate jam tenes, etiam rationis luce conspicias_. God does not despise reason. _Absit namque, ut hoc in nobis Deus oderit, in quo nos reliquis animantibus excellentiores creavit_, _Ep._ 120 _ad Consent._, nn. 2–4. And this was the principle and fundamental thought of the whole of scholasticism. Faith and theology were distinguished as _habitus_ and _actus_, as _theologia infusa_ and _acquisita_. Faith is assent to, theology is knowledge of, the revealed truths. Faith does include some knowledge concerning God and divine things, but this knowledge concerns more the _existentia_ than the _rationes_ of those truths; the theologian, however, penetrates to the idea, traces the connection of the truths, and by thought derives others from them, Liebermann, _Instit. Theol._, ed. 8, Mainz, 1857, cap. 1, §1. That people were conscious of the distinction between religion and theology is also evident from the doctrine of _fides implicita_. If, according to Rome, faith consisted in assent to revealed truths, sooner or later the question had to arise which and how many of those truths one had at minimum to know and accept in order to partake of salvation. This question could arise with a view to pagans, with a view to believers of the Old Testament, and also with a view to ignorant and uneducated believers in the days of the New Testament. Augustine had already said: _turbam non intelligendi vivacitas, sed credendi simplicitas tutissimam facit_, _C. Epist. fundamenti_, cap. 4. But Lombard first brought this question clearly under discussion in his _Sententiae_, lib. III, dist. 25. From then on it was treated at length in theology; in the seventeenth century it even occasioned a serious controversy in France, which Innocent XI did not resolve but nevertheless ended. The doctrine of _fides implicita_ is usually presented in Roman Catholic theology in this manner. _Fides carbonaria_ is expressly rejected; all Roman Catholic theologians maintain that saving faith includes some knowledge, even if slight; faith therefore can and may never be entirely implicit. But that knowledge differs in the various dispensations of grace. In the days of the Old Testament, from Adam to the fall of Jerusalem, when the proper mysteries of Christianity, such as the Trinity and the incarnation, had not yet been promulgated, only these two articles had to be believed _necessitate medii_: that God exists and that he is a rewarder, Heb. 11:6. This does not say that many believers under the Old Testament had no greater and deeper knowledge of the truth; Adam, for example, also knew the Trinity and the incarnation, Gen. 1:26; 2:23, 24; 3:15. But simple believers could content themselves with those two articles. For then they nevertheless also implicitly believed in the proper mysteries of Christianity, the Trinity and the incarnation. The Trinity is implicitly contained in God’s existence and unity, and the incarnation is implicitly contained in the fact that he is _remunerator_. Now others, such as Wiggers, Daelman, and others, did not see this last point so clearly and therefore said that Old Testament believers had to accept four articles, namely, in addition to the two named above, the immortality of the soul and the corruption of sin (_infectio animarum_), in which the two principal dogmas of Christianity were then implicitly comprehended. The most common view, however, was that the two articles named were sufficient for Old Testament believers. But after the fall of Jerusalem these two principal truths of Christianity were clearly revealed and made known; it is therefore now necessary _necessitate medii_ for everyone who wishes to be saved to accept these four articles: _Deus, isque remunerator, sanctissima trinitas, mediator_. Others add further articles, such as _Deus Creator, Dei gratia ad salutem necessaria, immortalitas_; and some even call acceptance of the twelve articles _quoad substantiam_ necessary. But the advocates of the four articles express the most common opinion. This entire doctrine of _fides implicita_ is then supported by an appeal to Job 1:14: the oxen, that is, the educated believers, were plowing, and the she-asses, that is, the simple laity, were feeding beside them. This exegesis already occurs in Gregory the Great, _Moral._, lib. II, cap. 25, and is then adopted by Lombard, Thomas, Bellarmine, and others. Cf. Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 25; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 2, qu. 2, artt. 5–8; Bellarmine, _De Justific._ I, cap. 7; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae sive Cursus Theol._ VII, p. 46 sq.; Daelman, _Theol. seu Observ. theol. in Summam D. Thomae_ IV, p. 44 sq.; Dens, _Theologia in usum semin._ II 278 sq.; M. Becanus, _Theol. Scholastica_, tom. II, pars II, tract. 1, p. 22 sq.; Jansen, _Praelect. Theol._ I 699 sq.; Albr. Ritschl, _Fides implicita_, Bonn, 1890. The question itself that underlies this doctrine of _fides implicita_ is serious and important enough, for it concerns nothing less than the essence of Christianity itself, the significance of Israel, and the possibility of progression in revelation alongside the absolute character of truth. But the intellectualistic conception of faith led to resolving this question in purely quantitative terms: acceptance of four articles of faith makes the Christian; in practice the legend of _fides carbonaria_ proved to contain only too much truth. In any case, however, the doctrine of _fides implicita_ plainly proves that Roman Catholic theologians distinguish between faith and theology in content and scope.
