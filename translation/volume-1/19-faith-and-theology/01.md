# § 19. Faith and Theology

## 1.

The Christian church has not been satisfied with faith but almost from the beginning has striven for knowledge of religious truth and given rise to a distinct science, theology. Yet it cannot be said that this impulse toward science is contained in faith as such. For faith is certainty and excludes all doubt; it rests in the Word of God and finds it sufficient. _Nobis curiositate opus non est post Christum Jesum, nec inquisitione post evangelium. Cum credimus, nihil desideramus ultra credere_, Tertullian, _De praescr. haer._ 8. _Die Frömmigkeit ist ihrer selbst unmittelbar gewiss_, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_, §7. The right and value of theological science have therefore repeatedly been contested in the Christian church. During the Monophysite and Monothelite controversies there was already a party of Gnosimachi who considered all science unnecessary for Christians. They taught that God requires nothing of the Christian but good works and that it was better _simplici rudique animo institutum suum persequi, quam multam curam in cognoscendis decretis atque sententiis ponere_, John of Damascus, _De haeresibus_, _Opera Omnia_, Basel, 1575, p. 585. Toward the end of the Middle Ages aversion to theology was general. Scholasticism had lost all confidence. In all circles and among all sects there was a strong longing for a simpler, practical Christianity, Harnack, _D. G._ III² 573. Humanism looked down on scholasticism with contempt, Paulsen, _Gesch. des gelehrten Unterrichts_, Leipzig, 1885, p. 1 f. Since then the opposition to scholasticism in the Roman Church has scarcely ever fallen silent. Bajus, Jansenius, Launoy, and many theologians in the previous and present centuries, including Günther, brought all manner of serious accusations against scholastic theology, Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ IV² 133 f.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher von der rel. Erk._ II 566 f.

The Reformation initially adopted the same standpoint. Luther’s judgment concerning Aristotle, scholasticism, and reason is well known, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ I 106 f. In the first edition of his _Loci_, Melanchthon wrote: _hoc est Christum cognoscere, beneficia ejus cognoscere, non quod isti (scholastici) docent, ejus naturas, modos incarnationis intueri_, ed. Augusti, 1821, p. 9. Zwingli said that being a Christian did not consist in _schwatzen von Christo, sunder wandeln wie er gewandlet hat_; cf. my _Ethiek van Zwingli_, p. 119 f. Calvin likewise places strong emphasis on this practical side of faith, _Inst._ I, 2, 2; I, 5, 9; I, 12, 1; _Comm._ on Rom. 1:19. But many went further than the Reformers and rejected all theology. Appealing to Matt. 23:8, Karlstadt condemned all scientific titles and went to live as a peasant among peasants, Herzog² 7, 528. The Anabaptists and Mennonites wished to know nothing of scientific training for the ministry of the Word and gave all believers the right to “exhort.” Menno Simons repeatedly rendered a very severe judgment concerning the church and its ministers, study and scholarship, _Alle de Godtgel. Wercken_, Amsterdam, 1681, pp. 34 f., 59 f., 260, 270, etc. Only later did Mennonite ministers receive scientific training, Herzog² 9, 575; Sepp, _Kerkhist. Studiën_, Leiden, 1885, pp. 84, 85. And when later the scholastic treatment of theology gained ground in the Protestant churches, reaction came from every side. Calixtus and Cocceius, Spener and Zinzendorf, Fox and Wesley, and others, were all driven by the desire for more simplicity and truth in the doctrine of faith. To this end one had to return from doctrine to life, from confession to Scripture, from theology to religion. Even deism and rationalism were akin to this striving; seeking the general and common element that lay at the foundation of all religions and confessions, they appealed from _die christliche Religion_ to _die Religion Christi_, from _die statutarische Religion_ to _die Vernunftreligion_. Through the agnostic tendency in philosophy, Schleiermacher’s theology of feeling, historical criticism of Scripture, and other influences, this striving increased still further. Aversion to dogmatics is now general. Many look longingly for a new word, a new dogma; desire a religion without theology, a life without doctrine; and campaign for a practical, undogmatic Christianity (Dreyer, Egidy, Drummond, Tolstoy, and others). To a certain extent this striving found its scientific defense in the school of Ritschl. It appeared with the demand that theology be wholly delivered from metaphysics, Ritschl, _Theol. u. Metaph._, 1881; cf. also his studies on the doctrine of God, _Jahrb. für deutsche Theol._, 1865, 1868. Harnack in his _Dogmengeschichte_, E. Hatch in his work _The Influence of Greek Ideas and Usages upon the Christ. Church_, London, 1890, German translation by E. Preuschen, _Griechenthum und Christ._, Freiburg, 1882, and also Kaftan, _Die Wahrheit der christl. Religion_, 1889, applied this principle to the history of dogmas and tried to demonstrate that theology is the fruit of the marriage contracted in an evil hour between original Christianity and Greek philosophy. The accusations brought by all these tendencies against theology, and dogmatics in particular, amount to this: that theology falsifies the purity and simplicity of the Christian religion; changes religion into a doctrine that must be intellectually proved and accepted; kills religious life, promotes a cold and barren orthodoxy, and makes _fides implicita_ necessary; and finally also brings religion as doctrine into conflict with science and alienates the educated classes from Christian faith. Cf., e.g., Kaftan, _Glaube und Dogma_, 3e Aufl., 1889.
