## 5.

The Reformation too remained conscious of this difference. Lutheran and Reformed theologians adopted the distinction between the _habitus_ and _actus_ of theology, between _theologia infusa_ and _acquisita_. The former is the portion of all believers; in principle they know _totam theologiam_, they possess the true and pure knowledge of God. But as _acquisita_, theology is the portion only of those who cultivate the knowledge of God scientifically. For then it is a _notitia conclusionum ex principiis theologicis per discursum theologicum collectarum, et certo ordine dispositarum, atque animo impressarum diuturno labore ac exercitio_, Alsted, _Methodus sacrosanctae theologiae_, 1623, pp. 117, 126, 175; Owen, _Theologoumena_, p. 465 sq.; Calovius, _Isagoge ad S. Theol._, pp. 17, 18. _Religio_ and _theologia_ were indeed repeatedly interchanged, cf., for example, Cloppenburg, _Op._ I 699; Mastricht, _Theor.-pract. Theol._ I, cap. 2, § 3, which could happen all the more easily because theology and dogmatics were approximately the same. But even in the flourishing period of orthodoxy the distinction between faith and theology was not entirely lost, as is proved by the doctrine of the _articuli fundamentales et non fundamentales_. The Reformation decisively rejected the Roman doctrine of _fides implicita_, Calvin, _Inst._ III 2, §§ 2–6; Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._, tom. III, lib. 12, cap. 5; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._, p. 592; Maresius, _Synt. Theol._, loc. 11, § 29; etc.; cf. Ritschl, _Fides implicita_, S. 56 f. It was compelled to do so because it did not make saving faith consist in holding as true a number of articles not understood, but in a personal trust in the grace of God in Christ. In its place, however, there soon arose the distinction between fundamental and nonfundamental articles. Calvin had already said that one ought not to separate from the church over less essential points, _Instit._ IV 1, §§ 12, 13; IV 2, § 1. _Non enim unius sunt formae omnia verae doctrinae capita. Sunt quaedam ita necessaria cognitu, ut fixa esse et indubitata omnibus oporteat ceu propria religionis placita, qualia sunt: unum esse Deum, Christum Deum esse ac Dei filium, in Dei misericordia salutem nobis consistere et similia. Sunt alia, quae inter ecclesias controversa, fidei tamen unitatem non dirimant_, IV, cap. 1, § 12. The juxtaposition used in the description of faith—of “a certain knowledge whereby I hold as true all that God has revealed in his Word” and of “a trust that all my sins are forgiven me for Christ’s sake”—could give occasion to seek the center of gravity either in the _fides generalis_ or in the _fides specialis_. The great number of churches that successively arose from the Reformation and differed from one another in various articles furthered the distinction between essential and incidental elements in revelation. The gradually emerging syncretism and indifferentism, which returned from the particular to the common, made it necessary to identify what belonged to the foundations of the Christian religion. Thus arose the doctrine of the _articuli fundamentales_. Nic. Hunnius seems to have been the first to use the expression in his _Διασκεψις de fundamentali dissensu doctrinae Lutheranae et Calvinianae_, 1626. Quenstedt spoke of _articuli primarii et secundarii_. Others followed, in both the Lutheran and Reformed churches; cf. Voetius, _Disp. Sel._ II 511–538; H. Alting, _Theol. probl. nova_ I 9; Spanheim fil., _Opera_ III, col. 1289 sq.; Heidegger, _Theol._ I, § 51 sq.; Turret., _Theol. El._ I, qu. 14; Moor, _Comm. in Marckii Comp._ I 481 sq.; Witsius, _Exercit. in Symb._ II, § 2; Bretschneider, _System. Entw. aller in der Dogm. vork. Begriffe_, 4te Aufl., 1841, S. 103 f.; Tholuck, _Der Geist der luth. Theologen Wittenbergs_, 1852, S. 252 f. Orthodoxy was naturally not inclined to restrict the _articuli fundamentales_ to a small number; nevertheless, a tendency is perceptible to understand these articles centrally and to group them around the person of Christ. The Roman Catholics, however, rejected this distinction, however much apparent similarity it had to _fides implicita_. They caused the Reformed and Lutheran theologians no small perplexity. They asked where God had distinguished in his Word between essential and incidental truths; whence one derived the right to separate the fundamental from the nonfundamental in divine revelation; which truths, then, had to be reckoned among the fundamental; who had to determine this; and how rationalism and indifferentism were to be avoided on such a standpoint. Faith, they maintained, has as its object not only _de divina misericordia_, Council of Trent, sess. VI, can. 12, but _omnia quae Deus revelavit_, Bellarmine, _De Justif._ I, c. 8. According to the Jesuits at the religious colloquy at Regensburg in 1601, it was also an _articulus fidei, quod canis Tobiae caudam moverit_. Cf. Denzinger, _Vier Bücher usw._ II 277 f.; Heinrich, _Dogm._ II 658 f.; Jansen, _Prael. Theol._ I 449 sq.; Lamennais, _Essai sur l’indifférence_ I, chs. 6, 7; etc. Over against all these objections the Protestant theologians did indeed appeal to Scripture, Matt. 16:16; 1 Cor. 2:2; 3:11 f.; Eph. 2:20; Gal. 6:14; 1 Pet. 2:6; etc., and made all manner of distinctions, Spanheim, op. cit., col. 1308 sq. But they did not conceal the difficulties from themselves; they were afraid of erring _in excessu_ and _in defectu_; and they ended by declaring that they could not determine the minimum of knowledge with which a sincere faith had to be accompanied, Voetius, _Disp._ II 537, 781; Spanheim, op. cit., col. 1291; Witsius, _Exerc. in Symb._ II, §§ 2, 15; Hoornbeek, _Conf. Socin._ I, p. 209. Orthodoxy ended in rationalism on the one side and Pietism on the other. Doctrine and life fell ever farther apart. Head and heart contended for priority. Theology and religion came to stand over against one another. We have not yet transcended the antithesis. For years the cry was heard: “Religion is not doctrine but life; what matters is not what you believe but only how you live.” Gradually, however, eyes are also being opened to the one-sidedness of this tendency, and the value of religious conceptions for religious life is being better acknowledged; cf., for example, Dr. Bruining in various writings: _Gids_, June 1884; _Moderne mystiek_, 1885; _Het bestaan van God_, 1892; _Theol. Tijdschr._, Nov. 1894.
