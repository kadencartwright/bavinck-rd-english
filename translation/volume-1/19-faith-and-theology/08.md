## 8.

But although knowledge is attainable in theology, theology does not reach comprehension. There is a great distinction between knowing-that, cognizing, and comprehending. These words are indeed often used interchangeably, but a clear difference can nevertheless be indicated. Knowing-that concerns existence, the _that_; cognizing concerns quality, the _what_; comprehending concerns the inner possibility, the _how_, of a thing. We comprehend very little—properly speaking, only that which lies entirely in our power, which we can make and break. I comprehend a machine when I see how it is put together and how it works, when nothing wondrous remains in it. Comprehension excludes wonder and admiration. I comprehend, or think I comprehend, what, as people say, speaks for itself and is entirely natural. Often comprehension ceases the more deeply one investigates. What spoke for itself proves to be utterly extraordinary and wondrous. The further a science penetrates into its object, the more it approaches mystery. Even if it encountered no other mystery along its path, it would nevertheless always finally come up against the mystery of being. Yet where comprehension ceases, room still remains for cognizing and admiring. So it is also in theology. In revelation the μυστηριον εὐσεβειας, the mystery of God’s grace, is unveiled to us. We see it; it meets us as a reality in history and in our own life; but we do not fathom it. In this sense Christian theology always deals with mysteries that it can indeed know and admire but cannot comprehend and fathom.

Often, however, mystery has been understood in Christian theology in an entirely different sense. The word μυστηριον, from μυστης, μυω—to close or shut, of eyes, lips, wounds—is in ordinary Greek the name for the religious-political secret doctrine that in certain societies of Eleusis, Samothrace, and elsewhere was communicated only to initiates and hidden from all others, Foucart, _Des associations religieuses chez les Grecs_, Paris, 1873; Edwin Hatch, _Griechentum u. Christenthum_, German by Preuschen, 1892, p. 210 f.; Gustav Anrich, _Das antike Mysterienwesen in seinem Einfluss auf das Christ._, Göttingen, 1894. In the New Testament the word always has religious significance and denotes a matter of the kingdom of God that is hidden either because of the obscure, enigmatic form in which it is presented, Matt. 13:11; Mark 4:11; Luke 8:10; Rev. 1:20; 17:5, 7, or also because of its content. In particular, the universal counsel of God concerning redemption in Christ, which also includes the Gentiles, is so called, Rom. 16:25; Eph. 1:9; 3:3; 6:19; Col. 1:26, 27; 2:2; 4:3, together with the manner in which it is carried out, Rom. 11:25; 1 Cor. 15:51; 2 Thess. 2:7; Rev. 10:7. But this _mysterium_ is so called not because it is still hidden now but because it was formerly unknown. Now it has precisely been made manifest through the gospel of Christ; it is proclaimed by the apostles as οἰκονομοι μυστηριων θεου, Rom. 16:25, 26; Col. 1:26; 1 Cor. 2:14; Matt. 13:11; 1 Cor. 4:1; and from now on it also comes increasingly to light in history, 1 Cor. 15:51, 52; 2 Thess. 2:7. The New Testament μυστηριον thus denotes no truth of faith that is uncomprehended and incomprehensible to the thinking understanding, but a matter that was first hidden with God, afterward made known in the gospel, and now understood by believers, Cremer, _Wörterbuch der neutest. Gräcität_, s.v. Ecclesiastical usage, however, soon understood by it a matter that was incomprehensible and also far surpassed the understanding of the believer, such as the incarnation, the _unio mystica_, the sacraments, and so forth, and later all _articuli puri_ that could not be proved by reason, Suicerus, _Thes. Eccles._, s.v. Even so, a great difference still remained between pagan and Christian usage. For there it denoted a secret doctrine that had to be kept hidden from the uninitiated; but in the Christian church there has never been a proper _disciplina arcana_, even though a certain order was observed in communicating the truth. Zezschwitz, art. “Arkandisciplin” in Herzog²; Hatch, _Griechenthum u. Christ._, 217 f.; Kattenbusch, _Vergleichende Konfessionskunde_ I 393 f.; Suicerus, _Thes. Eccl._, s.v. δογμα. Yet the dogmas were uncomprehended and incomprehensible truths of faith, indeed not _contra_ but nevertheless high _supra rationem_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 32, art. 1; _S. c. Gent._ I, c. 3; IV, c. 1; Bellarmine, _De Christo_ I 3; II 6; Heinrich, II 772 f.; Denzinger, _Vier Bücher v. d. rel. Erk._ II 80–150; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ V 164 f. In the condemnation of Erigena, Raimundus Lullus, Hermes, Günther, and Frohschammer, Rome expressed its disapproval of every attempt to prove the mysteries of faith from reason. And the _Conc. Vatic._ declared: _divina mysteria suapte natura intellectum creatum sic excedunt, ut etiam revelatione tradita et fide suscepta, ipsius tamen fidei velamine contecta et quadam quasi caligine obvoluta maneant, quamdiu in hac mortali vita peregrinamur a Domino; per fidem enim ambulamus, et non per speciem_, sess. 3, cap. 4. The Reformation indeed acknowledged the supernatural character of revelation but nevertheless actually introduced a great change. At Rome the mysteries are incomprehensible in the first place because they belong to another, higher, supernatural order that far surpasses man’s understanding as such. Rome must therefore place all emphasis on the incomprehensibility of the mysteries and protect and maintain it. The incomprehensible seems in itself to be a proof of truth. _Credibile est, quia ineptum est. . . . Certum, quia impossibile_, Tertullian, _De carne Chr._ 5. But the Reformation replaced this opposition between natural and supernatural order with that between sin and grace. It sought the essence of mystery not in the fact that it was incomprehensible for man in himself, but for the understanding of the psychical man, Calvin, _Inst._ II, 2, 20; Voetius, _Disp._ I 3. Without doubt this conception agrees much more closely with New Testament usage. Nowhere there does the abstractly supernatural and scientifically incomprehensible character of mystery stand in the foreground. But while it is foolishness in the eyes of the natural man, however wise he may be, it is revealed to believers, who see divine wisdom and grace in it, Matt. 11:25; 13:11; 16:17; Rom. 11:33; 1 Cor. 1:30. Naturally, Scripture does not thereby mean that the believer comprehends and understands these mysteries in the scientific sense. For we walk by faith, know in part, and see through a glass darkly, Rom. 11:34; 1 Cor. 13:12; 2 Cor. 5:7. Yet the believer does know these mysteries; they are no longer foolishness and offense to him; in them he admires God’s wisdom and love. It therefore does not occur to him that they surpass his reason, that they are _supra rationem_; he does not feel them as an oppressive burden but as a liberation for his thought. His faith passes over into admiration, his knowledge ends in adoration, and his confession issues in a song of praise and thanksgiving. Of this nature too is the knowledge of God sought in theology. It is not knowing alone, much less comprehension; but it is better and more glorious—it is a knowledge that is life, eternal life, John 17:3.
