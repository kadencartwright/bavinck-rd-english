## 7.

An even greater change in the form of dogmatics came through the influence of philosophy. This is evident especially in the fact that material dogmatics proper becomes increasingly impoverished while the _pars formalis_ continually gains in size and extent. Until then the situation had been entirely different. The prolegomena were either wholly absent or small in scope and dealt at most with theology and Scripture; all energy was devoted to elaborating and defending the particular _dogmata_. The foundation lay so firmly that it was not investigated at all; all labor was spent on the building erected upon that foundation. This changed through philosophy, through the rights that reason gradually arrogated to itself over against revelation. It was no longer content with the modest role of handmaid but demanded a voice in the deliberations. In the _pars materialis_ this had the consequence that every scholastic treatment of the _dogmata_ was avoided as much as possible. One returned from the confession to Scripture and honored the historical, biblical method. The dogmas were simplified and worn down until they lost what was distinctive about them; all deeper reflection on the dogmas was banished; simplicity became superficiality. But the influence on the formal part of dogmatics was still greater. Here the Reformation’s starting point in faith was abandoned and that of Roman Catholic theology resumed. First, it was thought that human reason, even outside faith, could produce from itself all the truths of _theol. naturalis_. The _theol. naturalis_ preceded the _theol. revelata_ as _praeambula fidei_; reason was emancipated from faith and revelation, and the two stood independently alongside each other. S. van Til treated them separately in his _Theologiae utriusque compendium, cum naturalis tum revelatae_, 1706. Next, reason not only received a domain of its own alongside revelation but extended its power over revelation itself. It was granted the right to investigate the truth of revelation. In _theologia naturalis_ one stood on firm ground, on a purely scientific basis. From this standpoint revelation too was investigated; and once reason had demonstrated the truth of revelation by all kinds of rational and historical proofs, as so many _motiva credibilitatis_, it was surely reasonable to believe that revelation and submit to it. Thus the prolegomena were continually expanded. First, religion is treated as distinct from theology; then _theol. naturalis_ and natural or rational truths; next, revelation, whose possibility, necessity, and actuality are demonstrated at length; and finally, Holy Scripture, whose truth is substantiated by all kinds of historical, critical, and rational proofs. Only after this long road does one arrive at dogmatics proper, which looks as spare and simple as possible. The entire standpoint has changed: the starting point is no longer faith but reason. What wonder that in deism and rationalism reason denies all revelation—a revelation that, after all, gives nothing new and is entirely superfluous. This order of treatment is essentially proper to scholastic and Roman Catholic theology. The Socinians never rose above this rationalistic standpoint, Fock, _Der Socin._ 291 f. And the Remonstrants returned to this method. Limborch, _Theol. Christ._, speaks in cap. 1 _de theologia ac religione_, in cap. 2 _de existentia Dei_, and then in cap. 3 f. about Holy Scripture. In his _Instit. Theol._ Episcopius expands this introductory material still further. He even speaks first about the qualifications of the theologian and then about the practical character of theology, _theol. naturalis_, religion, revelation, and Holy Scripture. Gradually this expansion of the prolegomena also penetrated orthodox dogmatics. The division of dogmatics into two parts, _de fide_ and _de operibus_, leads Amesius and Maestricht, after theology and Scripture, also to discuss the nature of faith at the beginning. Marck and Moor speak about religion in the third chapter; Brakel begins with a chapter on _theol. naturalis_. There is therefore much difference and confusion about what belongs in the introduction. But during the eighteenth century a series of subjects for the introduction gradually begins to become established: theology, natural knowledge of God, revelation, and Scripture—as, for example, in J. A. Turretinus, Werenfels, Osterwald, Buurt, and all theologians of the rationalistic and supranaturalistic currents.
