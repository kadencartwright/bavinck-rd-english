## 10.

The content of dogmatics is the knowledge of God as He has revealed it in Christ through His Word. The distinctive character of the believer’s knowledge consists in this: that he views everything religiously and theologically, that he considers everything in the light of God, *sub specie aeternitatis*. This is the difference between his worldview and a philosophical or scientific worldview. In dogmatics it is always the believer, the Christian, who is speaking. He does not speculate about God; he does not proceed from an abstract, philosophical concept of God; and he does not arrive at the *theologia revelata* by way of the *theologia naturalis*. He does not reason about God as He exists in Himself, for this knowledge is completely inaccessible. He describes only that knowledge of God which has been revealed to him in Christ. Thus even when, in the first part of dogmatics, he treats God, His attributes, and the Trinity, he speaks and thinks as a believer, as a Christian, as a theologian, and not as a philosopher. The heart of religion therefore beats in every dogma. Dogmatics is not a philosophical system; dogmatics is theology. But for precisely this reason, in his system of the knowledge of God the dogmatician does not describe how he came to faith and how, through faith, he subjectively and successively gained insight into the various truths of faith. That would be an analytical method, excellent in a catechism but inappropriate in a dogmatics. Rather, he explicates the content of his faith as God Himself has objectively spread it out before the eye of his faith in revelation. He derives the principle of division and the arrangement of the material not from his own life of faith but from the object itself that he must describe in his dogmatics—not from the believing subject but from the object of faith. Thus, although we agree wholeheartedly that in dogmatics, always and everywhere, from beginning to end, it is the believer who thinks and speaks, this is nevertheless entirely different from his deriving the arrangement of the dogmatic material from his own experience. That would misconstrue the character of dogmatics, turn it into anthropology, and cause it to cease being theological. It remains theological only when the system of dogmatics is derived from its own subject matter and content.

If this starting point is well chosen, only two divisions commend themselves. The first is the Trinitarian division already discussed. It has much that is attractive; hence it has repeatedly found acceptance and has also exercised great influence in philosophy. It commends itself by its purely theological character. God is beginning and end, alpha and omega. Nature and history are subsumed under Him. All things are from God and unto God. The Trinitarian principle guards against uniformity and guarantees life, development, and process. But this at once indicates its danger. It is too speculative; it sacrifices history to the system; it all too readily incorporates cosmogony into God’s Trinitarian life and then becomes theogony. The philosophy of Erigena, Böhme, Baader, Schelling, and Hegel supplies the proof. Therefore preference is due to the division that is theological and at the same time bears a historical-genetic character. It too takes its starting point in God and considers all creatures only in relation to Him. But, proceeding from God, it descends to His works, in order then to ascend through them back to Him and to end in Him. In this division too, therefore, God is the beginning, the middle, and the end. From Him and through Him and unto Him are all things. But here God is not drawn down into the process of history, and history receives its due more fully. A distinction is made between God and His works. In those works He appears as Creator, Restorer, and Perfecter. He is *exemplar effectivum in creatione, refectivum in redemptione, perfectivum in retributione*. Dogmatics is the system of the knowledge of God as He has revealed Himself in Christ; it is the system of the Christian religion. And the essence of the Christian religion consists in this: that the creation of the Father, ruined by sin, is restored in the death of the Son of God and is recreated by the grace of the Holy Spirit into a kingdom of God. Dogmatics shows us how God, the All-Sufficient One in Himself, nevertheless glorifies Himself in His creation, which, even when it is shattered by sin, is gathered together again in Christ, Eph. 1:10. It describes for us God, always God, from beginning to end: God in His being, God in His creation, God over against sin, God in Christ, God through the Holy Spirit breaking all resistance and leading the whole creation to the goal appointed by Him, the glory of His name. Dogmatics, therefore, is no dry science. It is a theodicy, a hymn to all God’s virtues and perfections, a song of adoration and thanksgiving, a δοξα ἐν ὑψιστοις θεῳ.
