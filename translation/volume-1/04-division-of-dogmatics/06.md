## 6.

In the long run a reaction against this scholasticism could not fail to appear. It began in the Lutheran church with Calixtus and in the Reformed church with Coccejus. Calixtus conceived theology as a practical science and followed the analytic division in his _Epitome theologiae_ of 1619. This was not entirely new, since Thomas too, after the doctrine of God, proceeds in the second part to the destiny of man and in the third part describes the way that leads to it in Christ. But Calixtus begins at once with the end, the destiny of man. Part I treats _de fine_, that is, _de immortalitate animae, resurrectione et extremo judicio_. Part II speaks _de subjecto_, that is, about God, angel, man, and sin. Part III treats _de mediis_, that is, about predestination, incarnation, Christ, justification, word, sacrament, etc. These three parts form the _pars communis_, which concerns all believers; but they are followed by a _pars propria_, which deals chiefly with the church and is of particular importance for officebearers. Although there was much that was good in emphasizing the practical character of theology over against its scholastic treatment, this division is nevertheless burdened by many objections. Quite apart from the fact that the doctrine of the church treated in the _pars propria_ is a truth important to all believers, it makes a strange impression for dogmatics to begin with the end—with immortality and so forth. The second part is supposed to deal with the subject of theology, man, but it also contains the whole doctrine of God. The third part corresponds best to its title, but shortchanges soterology in comparison with soteriology. Gass, _Gesch. der prot. Dogm._ I, 304 f. Nevertheless, this analytic method was followed by later Lutheran theologians: Calovius, Quenstedt, König, Baier, and Scherzer. It also gained acceptance among the Reformed. As early as 1603, before Calixtus, Barth. Keckermann of Dantzig had published a _systema S. Theologiae_ in which, appealing to Ursinus in his _Catechesis_, he defined theology not as a _scientia contemplatrix_ but as a _disciplina operatrix_, or, better still, as a _prudentia religiosa ad salutem perveniendi_, cap. 1. He therefore decides firmly for the analytic method, since the synthetic method is proper to the sciences but the analytic method is suited to the _disciplinae operatrices_. Thus he divides his theology into three parts. In the first part he treats the _principia_ of theology—namely, God, the _principium essendi_, and His Word, the _principium cognoscendi_. From these two alone the end and the means leading to it become known to us. In the second part he says in a single word that the end of theology is _vita, salus aeterna_, just as Ursinus places this first in question 1 of his catechism. The means for attaining this end are twofold: knowledge of our misery and deliverance from that misery, lib. II, cap. 1. Book II therefore treats man and his sin, and Book III the _media salutis_: election, Christ, church, justification, and sacraments. It is also noteworthy that Keckermann especially likes to compare theology with medical science and even borrows from it the names for the various parts of his theology. Bonaventura, _Brevil._ Pars I, cap. 1, had already done this before him. Following him and others, Keckermann speaks of a _pars pathologica, therapeutica, diaetetica_ of theology, pp. 214, 295; cf. Kuyper, _Encycl._ I, 40.

In the same way Coccejus exchanged the theological standpoint for the anthropological. What was new in his _Systema doctrinae de foedere et testamento Dei_ of 1648 did not consist in the concept of the covenant as such, which already occurs in Zwingli and Calvin and had been developed by Bullinger, Olevianus, and Cloppenburg. Rather, it consisted in this: first, Coccejus for the first time divided all dogmatic material from the standpoint of this concept, intending thereby to provide a more biblical-theological, anti-scholastic dogmatics; further, in arranging the material, he followed the historical order of the dispensations of the covenant, distinguished those dispensations so sharply that their unity was lost and could be preserved only by arbitrary typological exegesis; and finally, he viewed the entire history of the covenant of grace from beginning to end as an abolition of the covenant of works. Sin, Christ, the New Covenant, bodily death, and resurrection are the five turning points by which the covenant of works is successively deprived of all its force and operation, cap. III § 68. This division is burdened by many objections. It takes its standpoint not theologically in God but in the covenant between God and man, and therefore cannot treat the doctrine of God and man except beforehand in an introduction, by way of presupposition. By its historical course it erases the boundary between the history of revelation and dogmatics and thereby undermines the latter. It lapses into frequent repetitions and naturally comes to exaggerate the analogies on the one hand and the distinctions among the various dispensations of the covenant on the other. Nevertheless, it found many followers, including Momma, Heydanus, Vitringa, Braun, Witsius, and also Lutherans such as W. Jäger, 1702. Even Leydecker, a follower of Voetius and an opponent of Coccejus, attempted to combine the trinitarian division with the federalist one in such a way that the successive economies of grace were related to the three persons and the threefold activity in the Trinity. Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. ref. k._ I 115 f.
