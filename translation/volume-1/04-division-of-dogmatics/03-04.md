## 3.

These divisions were generally adopted by later Roman Catholic theologians, not only in the commentaries produced, for example, by many medieval theologians and later still by Estius and others on Lombard’s *Sententiae*, or by Suarez, Valentia, Vasquez, Billuart, Daelman, and others on Thomas’s *Summa*, but also in independent dogmatic works. Thus Thomas’s division was adopted by Mart. Becanus in his *theologia scholastica*, Mogunt. 1619, and the method of loci used by Theodoret, Damascenus, and Lombard was adopted by Petavius, Opus de theol. Dogm. Paris 1644. Proleg cap. I § 4. But scholasticism exercised yet another influence on the division of the material. Bonaventura had already included the doctrine of Scripture in the *prooemium*; Thomas treated the nature of theology in advance; and the development of scholasticism led to the distinction between *articuli mixti* and *puri* and to the doctrine of the *praeambula fidei* and *motiva credibilitatis*. Thus the material for the prolegomena became increasingly extensive. And when the Reformation soon appeared with the exclusive authority of Scripture, it became necessary for Roman Catholic theologians to give an account of the *principia* of theology as well. Melchior Canus, especially in his *loci theologici* 1563, developed the doctrine of the *loci*, that is, of the sources of theology. This work is not a dogmatics but a *topica* in Cicero’s sense (see above, p. 2). In twelve books he treats ten sources of theology, namely, Scripture, tradition, pope, council, church, church fathers, scholastics, reason, philosophy, and history. The first seven of these can be reduced to two, Scripture and tradition, while the last three are theological only *ministerialiter*. In this way a broad introduction gradually became necessary before dogmatics proper, and through the fundamental contestation of theology by philosophy and other disciplines it grew into an entire fundamental dogmatics. Most Roman Catholic dogmatics therefore treat in a first part the nature or concept of theology, the *praeambula fidei* (*theol. naturalis*), the *motiva credibilitatis* (religion, revelation, prophecy, miracles, the miraculous spread of Christianity, martyrs, and so forth), faith, the sources (Scripture and church), theology and philosophy (faith and reason), and the concept of dogmatics. So it is in Klee, Heinrich, Jansen, Scheeben, Liebermann, Perrone, and others. Perrone’s work, for example, treats *de vera religione* in vol. I and the *loci theologici* in the final two volumes, VIII and IX. These latter two did indeed appear after dogmatics proper, but according to the author’s own declaration, vol. VIII, praef., they belong materially after the first volume. In these *loci*, in deliberate distinction from Canus, Perrone places the church before Scripture. Such too is the arrangement among most recent Roman Catholic dogmaticians. The church presses increasingly to the foreground; it is the *principium fidei*. The course of dogmatics then ordinarily follows the method of loci. One begins with God, considered in Himself as one and triune, and then proceeds to God considered in His relation to the creatures: first as *Deus Creator*, then as *Deus Redemptor*, afterward as *Deus Sanctificator*, and finally as *Deus Consummator*.

## 4.

The dogmatics of the Reformers was originally anti-scholastic and was at first presented in a very simple and practical form. Melanchthon’s *Loci*, published in 1521, arose from lectures on Paul’s epistle to the Romans. They are practical through and through, treat only anthropological and soteriological *capita*, especially those of sin and grace, law and gospel, and leave entirely undiscussed the objective *dogmata* concerning God, Trinity, creation, incarnation, and satisfaction. But the 1535 edition was greatly expanded by Melanchthon and ushered in the *secunda aetas* of the *Loci*. In a preface it was dedicated to Henry VIII; it began with five new *capita* on God, unity, Trinity, creation, and the cause of sin; it was enriched in the middle by several additional chapters; and it expanded the final three ethical *capita* into ten chapters of ethical, ecclesiological, and eschatological content, making thirty-nine *capita* or articles in all. In 1543 the *Loci* entered their *tertia aetas*: a preface *pio lectori* was added; through simplification the number of *loci* fell from thirty-nine to twenty-four; but the content increased still further. The final edition prepared by Melanchthon himself was that of 1559. The successive editions show an ever closer approximation to the synthetic division, which begins with God and descends to His works in nature and grace. Yet it is noteworthy that the prolegomena are wholly absent; that Christology receives too little attention because Christ’s person and work are not treated separately; that the dogmatic and the ethical remain most closely connected; and that the whole concludes not with the eschatological *dogmata* but with several ethical *capita*, Phil. IV 45, do indeed also treat several dogmatic *loci*, but they were soon overshadowed by Calvin’s *Christianae Religionis Institutio*. The first edition appeared in Latin at Basel in March 1536 and contained a *praefatio* dated August 1535 to Francis I, followed by six chapters on the law, faith, prayer, the sacraments, the Roman Catholic sacraments, and freedom. It was expanded three times, in 1539, 1543, and 1559. The final edition was approximately five times larger than the first and contained enlargement and expansion, but no change. In the later editions Melanchthon had become synergistic and crypto-Calvinistic; Calvin remained the same. Melanchthon’s *Loci* became more synthetic in form but retained the character of a series of *loci*; Calvin’s *Institutio* acquired an increasingly systematic form. The edition of 1559 contains four books: on the knowledge of God as Creator, as Redeemer, and as Sanctifier, and a final book on the external means of grace. The division is therefore not purely Trinitarian but is borrowed from the Apostles’ Creed; hence a fourth part is appended, dealing chiefly with church and sacraments. The first book gives much more than its title promises and also treats the sources of the knowledge of God and the doctrine of the Trinity. Cosmology and anthropology are divided between the first and second books. In addition to soteriological chapters, the third book contains many ethical chapters, as well as the doctrine of election and resurrection.
