# § 4. Division of Dogmatics

## 1.

As soon as dogmatics arose, a division of the material treated within it also became necessary. At first this division was exceedingly simple. The three principal works of Clement of Alexandria, namely, *Cohortatio ad gentes*, *Paedagogus*, and *Stromata*, are indeed bound together by the idea of the education of the human race by the Logos, but they contain no proper division; and although the last work develops the true philosophy of Christianity over against paganism and Judaism, Clement himself says at the beginning of its sixth book and at the end of its seventh that he merely wrote whatever came to mind and that one therefore ought not to be surprised at the lack of order. Origen’s work *περι ἀρχων*, *de principiis*, already attempts to bring some order to the material and accordingly has four principal concepts: God, world, freedom, revelation. The first book treats God, the Trinity, and the angels; the second, the world, the God of the Old Testament, good and evil, the incarnation, and the resurrection; the third, freedom of the will, temptations, and the end of the world; and the fourth, Holy Scripture, its inspiration and interpretation. The various *loci* concerning God, angels, humanity, Christ, and so forth already emerge here, but not yet in good order or with precise delimitation, and still incompletely; the doctrine of the sacraments is absent, that of Scripture comes at the end, and no principle of division can yet be discerned. This division of Origen was adopted in improved form by Theodoret in the fifth book of his *Αἱρετικης κακομυθιας ἐπιτομη*, *haereticarum fabularum compendium*, which contains an outline of the orthodox faith. It successively treats God, the world, angels and humanity, Christ, Scripture and sacrament, resurrection and judgment, while several ethical *capita* concerning virginity, penance, and fasting are added at the end. Furthermore, this same schema underlies the *Ἐκδοσις ἀκριβης της ὀρθοδοξου πιστεως* of John of Damascus. Its division into four books was introduced only in the Middle Ages, after the work had been translated into Latin by the jurist Johannes Burgundis of Pisa at the command of Eugenius III and Peter Lombard had set the precedent in his *Sententiae* with a division into four books. Damascene begins with the doctrine of God, the Trinity, and the attributes (book I); then speaks of the world, creation, angels, human beings, and providence (book II); next treats the person and work of Christ (books III and IV, cap. 1-9); and concludes with several soteriological *capita* (faith, baptism, etc.), ethical *capita* (law, Sabbath, etc.), and eschatological *capita* (book IV, cap. 10-28).

Alongside this, the Apostles’ Creed, following the baptismal formula, early provided another division of the dogmatic material. Rufinus’s *Expositio symboli* and Augustine’s book *de fide et symbolo* provided commentaries on it and treated the principal elements of the Christian faith according to the Trinitarian schema that underlay the Apostles’ Creed. Augustine, however, not only served as an example to many later writers in this division, but in his *Enchiridium ad Laurentium* he treats the whole body of dogmatic and ethical material under the three Christian virtues of faith, hope, and love, though in a very uneven manner. After an introduction, cap. 1-8, Augustine sets forth what must be believed from cap. 9 through cap. 113, chiefly according to the Apostles’ Creed; but then devotes only three *capita* (114-116) to hope, taking the Lord’s Prayer as his guide, and the remaining *capita*, 117-122, to love. Faith, prayer, and commandment thus form the schema of dogmatics here, a schema later adopted frequently, especially in catechisms. Isidore of Seville likewise follows essentially this sequence of the Apostles’ Creed in the first book of his *Sententiarum sive de summo bono libri III* and treats in thirty *capita* God, creation, the world, angels and human beings, Christ, the Holy Spirit, the church, Scripture and the sacraments, resurrection and the last things; in the second and third books he then treats exclusively ethical material.

## 2.

The division Lombard chose in the Middle Ages is more reminiscent of that of Origen, Theodoret, and Damascene. His *Sententiae* is divided into four books. The first treats *de mysterio trinitatis*; the second, *de rerum corporalium et spiritualium creatione et formatione*: creation, angels, the hexaemeron, man, the fall, and sin; the third, *de incarnatione verbi*: the person and work of Christ, faith, hope, love, the four cardinal virtues, and other ethical subjects; the fourth book, *de sacramentis*: the seven sacraments, resurrection, judgment, heaven, and hell. Noticeable progress can already be discerned here: the subjects are not only better grouped and delimited, but the whole is also divided into four parts, each with its own distinct object; the ethical material is not appended at the end, as in Theodoret, but incorporated into dogmatics itself; the sacraments, formerly discussed only briefly, are treated at length. On the other hand, the arrangement leaves much to be desired in many respects, and various subjects such as Scripture, the church, and especially the soteriological topics are scarcely discussed at all. Most of the scholastics followed this work of Lombard and wrote commentaries on it. Some made felicitous improvements in the ordering of the material. Thomas, for example, explains beautifully in the prologue to his commentary on the *Sententiae* that first God, and then His works—creation, restoration, and consummation—are the subjects of the four books. But from a formal point of view, a place of honor is also occupied by Bonaventure’s *Breviloquium*. First, Bonaventure prefixes a *prooemium* to his work and in seven paragraphs gives a fine overview of the doctrine of Scripture, especially of its content, which he briefly unfolds according to its length and breadth, depth and height. He then gives us in seven *partes* an outline of the truth of faith: *pars 1* treats *de trinitate*, *pars 2 de creatura mundi*, *pars 3 de corruptela peccati*, *pars 4 de incarnatione verbi*, *pars 5 de gratia Spiritus Sancti*, *pars 6 de medicina sacramentali*, and *pars 7 de statu finalis judicii*. Here there is a fixed, methodical progression, a complete mastery of the material, a precise delimitation of the subjects, and also a consciously chosen principle of division; for in *pars 1 cap. 1* Bonaventure says that, although theology treats all seven subjects, it is nevertheless *scientia una*, because God is not only *rerum primum principium et exemplar effectivum in creatione, sed etiam refectivum in redemptione et perfectivum in retributione*. Quite different is the division of Thomas in his *Summa*. This work has three *partes*; the *partes* are divided into *quaestiones*, and these in turn into *articuli*. *Pars I* treats in 119 *quaestiones* God and His creation before and apart from sin: God as *principium* and *exemplar* of all things. *Pars II* speaks of man as His *imago* and is itself divided into a *prima* and *secunda*. The *prima* comprises 114 *quaestiones*, begins with man’s final end, namely, blessedness, and with that end in view discusses the will, qu. 6-17; human acts in their good and sinful quality, followed by the passions and habits, qu. 18-54; the virtues in general, qu. 55-70; sins in their classification, seat, cause, and consequences, qu. 71-89; and then the external and internal motives that move man toward the good—the law, the gospel, grace, and merit—qu. 90-114. The *secunda* of this second part then treats in 189 *quaestiones* sins and virtues in particular, chiefly according to the order of the three theological and the four cardinal virtues. After man’s final end has thus been defined and his virtues and sins brought to light, *Pars III* describes the way by which we may attain the blessedness of immortal life, namely, Christ and the sacraments. In ninety *quaestiones* it describes the person and work of Christ, and then the concept of sacrament, baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, and *poenitentia*. Here Thomas’s work ends; he left it unfinished at his death. The *supplementum*, added to it from his other writings, contains ninety-nine *quaestiones*, continues the sacraments, and in qu. 69-99 treats the state of souls after death, the resurrection, judgment, blessedness, and damnation. An appendix discusses purgatory in three *quaestiones*. Thomas’s division not only differs from Bonaventure’s but is also inferior to it in many respects. To be sure, *Pars I qu. 1* gives an extensive account of the nature of theology, but by contrast the doctrine of Scripture remains wholly undiscussed. Furthermore, at the beginning of the second part Thomas leaps at once to the final end of human life and is thereby compelled frequently to anticipate what can be treated only later. The Christian virtues, the gospel, the law, and grace, which Thomas includes in this second part, presuppose the person and work of Christ. Thomas’s division is already connected with the Roman Catholic doctrine of the *ordo supernaturalis*, to which man must be elevated. After the first part has treated God, the second speaks of man, who as the image of God is destined for the *gratia supernaturalis* but cannot attain it, and the third then indicates, in the person of Christ and the sacraments, the way by which man can reach this destiny. God, man, and Christ are thus the subjects of the three parts, Werner, *Der h. Thomas v. Aq.*, Regensburg 1858 I, 801 f.; Kleutgen, *Theol. der Vorzeit*, 2e Aufl. V, 60-70; Kling, *Descriptio Summae theol. Th. Aq.*, Bonn 1846. Finally, the form of treatment by questions is not beyond objection either, Pierson, *Studiën over Joh. Calvĳn*, I 228 f. Thomas frames each truth of faith as a question and adduces everything opponents have brought against it. Yet by appealing to one authority or another—Scripture, the church fathers, Aristotle—he demonstrates the truth of the proposition in question and draws the *conclusio*. This is then elucidated and finally defended against the objections raised.
