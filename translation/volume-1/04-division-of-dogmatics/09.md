## 9.

First, as regards the division of dogmatics into a general and a special part, this has become necessary not only because dogmatics has been opposed at the level of principle, but is also useful and good in itself. The need was felt early on to clarify the nature of theology and its _principia_ before treating the dogmas proper. Polanus already distinguished between the _principia_ and the _articuli fidei_. And gradually it has become an urgent need for the believer to know not only what but also why he believes. But this also defines the task of the first, general part of dogmatics. This first part must not involve itself with all kinds of encyclopedic questions, such as the nature, history, and division of theology, as Gretillat, _Exposé de théol. system._ I, 1885, p. 199 s., still does. This was formerly done because theology was identified with dogmatics and because theological encyclopedia was not yet practiced as a discipline of its own. Today, however, the development of the nature of theology must be left to encyclopedia. In dogmatics there belongs only the exposition of the name, concept, method, division, and history of dogmatics itself, as is done in the introduction. But the principial part of dogmatics must also be limited in another way. The method that already arose with scholasticism and later found acceptance among Protestants as well—namely, first to treat natural knowledge of God (_praeambula fidei_) and then all the historical and rational proofs (_motiva credibilitatis_) for revelation—deserves rejection because at the outset and in principle it abandons the standpoint of faith, misconstrues the positive character of dogmatics, moves onto the opponent’s ground, and is thus in fact rationalistic and makes dogmatics dependent on philosophy. To this also belongs the attempt to identify _theologia_ or _religio naturalis_ with the _foedus operum_ and then to treat it as a preparation for the _theologia revelata_, which is materially one with the _foedus gratiae_. Schweizer, _Gl. der. ev. ref. K._ I, 107–115, II 1 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 304 f.; cf. Dr. van Dijk, _Studiën_, VI, 1880, first part, pp. 11 f. Both make natural religion (_foedus operum, status integritatis_), the religion of law (_foedus gratiae ante legem_ and _sub lege_), and the _Erlösungsreligion_ (_foedus gratiae post legem_) three moments in the religious process. Revelation is thereby deprived not only of its supernatural character, but the Reformed division is also used contrary to its intention. The _foedus operum_ before the fall is not a preparation for but forms a contrast with the _foedus gratiae_, which enters history only after the _foedus operum_ has been broken by sin. The distinction between _theol. naturalis_ and _relevata_, on the other hand, is an entirely different one—not a historical distinction, but one that always exists and continues in theology. Over against such a rationalization of religion and theology, their positive character must be maintained with Schleiermacher, Rothe, Frank, Ritschl, and others. The _principia fidei_ too are _articuli fidei_, resting not on human reasonings and proofs but on divine authority. The acknowledgment of revelation, of Scripture as God’s Word, is a fruit and act of faith. In dogmatics it is the believer who speaks from beginning to end, both in the _principia_ and in the _articuli fidei_. He confesses and gives an account of his faith, of both its ground and its content. In the first part, therefore, only the _principia fidei_ are developed. These are twofold: _principium externum_ and _internum_, _objectivum_ and _formale_, Voetius, _Disp._ I 2; Alting, _Theol. Schol. didact._ p. 10, just as _religio objectiva_ and _subjectiva_ must be distinguished. The whole first part of dogmatics is treated under these two.

In arranging the material of the second part, the trinitarian division must be rejected because it naturally cannot include the treatment of the Trinity itself in one of the three economies and must therefore place it beforehand in a chapter by way of presupposition. Furthermore, this division carries the danger that the _opera ad extra_ will be conceived far too much as _opera personalia_ and considered too little as _opera essentialia_, as common works of the three persons; or, on the other hand, that in maintaining the unity, the Trinity will be understood only economically and its ontological character misconstrued. Finally, this division also has the disadvantage that the _loci_ on creation, angel, man, sin, church, and so forth, when distributed among the persons of the Trinity, cannot come into their own. But still more objections exist against the Christological division. However attractive it may be at first glance, it is nevertheless unusable. First, it often rests on the incorrect conception that not Scripture but specifically the person of Christ is the _principium_ and source of knowledge for dogmatics, although we know nothing of Christ except from and through Scripture. Furthermore, Christ is most certainly the center and principal content of Holy Scripture; but precisely because He is the center, He is not the starting point. He presupposes God and man, just as He appeared in history not immediately with the promise but only many centuries later. Next, there is no doubt that Christ has revealed the Father to us, but this speech of God through the Son does not nullify God’s speaking in many ways through the prophets. Not the New Testament alone, nor even the words of Jesus alone, but the whole of Scripture is a Word of God that comes to us through Christ. Finally, it is clear that the Christological division can treat the _loci_ on God, creation, world, and man only by way of presuppositions and postulates and therefore cannot develop them in their rich significance. The other divisions—derived from the three virtues of faith, hope, and love; from faith, prayer, and commandment; from the destiny and final end of man; from the covenant or fellowship of God and man; from the kingdom of God; from the concepts of life, love, spirit, and so forth—may have much to recommend them practically and may be appropriate in a catechism, but they cannot qualify for a dogmatics that is the system of the knowledge of God. They are not sufficiently central and universal for this purpose. Accordingly, they are either introduced from without and do not govern the system, as in Van Oosterzee, or they are strictly maintained as the principle of division but fail to do justice to various _loci_.
