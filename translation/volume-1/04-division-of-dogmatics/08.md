## 8.

Kant did indeed undermine this rationalistic foundation of dogmatics, and Schleiermacher did attempt to save faith and the doctrine of faith by restricting them to feeling and its description. But in fact no change occurred in this ordering of dogmatics. The attacks on the Christian religion in this century are directed first of all against the foundations themselves. In earlier centuries faith was more vigorous, and the question “Why do I believe?” scarcely arose. The foundations seemed so firm that investigating them was entirely unnecessary; all strength was devoted to erecting the building. But now dogmatics is contested precisely in its philosophical presuppositions. It is not one doctrine or another within dogmatics but the possibility of dogmatics that is denied, Pierson, Ter uitvaart 1870. The human faculty of knowledge is restricted to visible things; revelation is deemed impossible; Holy Scripture is deprived of its divine authority by historical criticism; and even the right and value of religion are seriously disputed. Alongside and partly through all this, religious life has greatly diminished. There is indeed much activity in the religious sphere, but there is little genuine religious life. Faith no longer feels secure; even among believers there is much doubt and instability. The childlike and at the same time heroic “I believe” is seldom heard and has given way to critical doubt. One perhaps still believes one’s confession, but one no longer confesses one’s faith (Schweizer). In times of awakened religious life, one speaks as one having authority and not as the scribe; then “I know whom I have believed” sounds from the lips. But in a critical time such as ours there is uncertainty precisely about the *principia*: about the source of knowledge, method, proof, and so forth. The *pars formalis* is therefore still the principal part of dogmatics. An entire apologetics precedes dogmatics, Hoekstra, Godgel. Bĳdr. 1864 p. 2f. Schleiermacher did make dogmatics a positive, historical science and attempted to free it from all apologetics. But he placed philosophical theology before historical theology, to which dogmatics belongs. This philosophical theology had to take its starting point *über dem Christenthum*, in religious communal life in general, and from there had to determine critically the essence of Christianity, Kurze Darstellung des theol. Stud. 2e Ausg. § 32 f. In fact, therefore, he did not free theology from philosophy but made it as dependent on philosophy as possible. This is also evident from the fact that in his *Glaubenslehre* he prefixes an extensive introduction containing all kinds of *Lehnsätze* from ethics, the philosophy of religion, and apologetics. Schleiermacher’s example of encyclopedically placing philosophical theology before historical theology found only a few followers; nevertheless, in connection with him it became customary to begin dogmatics with an apologetic part. Thus Voigt wrote a *Fundamentaldogmatik*; Lange wrote a philosophical dogmatics that preceded the positive dogmatics; Van Oosterzee laid an apologetic foundation; Dorner prefixes a *fundamentaler Theil* or *Apologetik*; Biedermann, a *prinzipieller Theil*; Lipsius and Nitzsch, a *Principienlehre*; and so forth. Liebner’s advice, Jahrb. f. d. Th. 1856 I 196 f., to develop in the introduction only the concept of dogmatics—since otherwise an entire dogmatics comes before dogmatics, and the concepts of religion and revelation already presuppose the doctrine of God and humanity—has found few followers. The subjects treated in this *Principienlehre* are not the same in all authors, but for the most part they concern the following concepts: the nature of our knowledge in religious matters, religion, revelation, Holy Scripture, and church. The chapter on theology, formerly included repeatedly, has moved to the encyclopedia. The division of material dogmatics varies greatly. Some follow the Trinitarian division, such as Martensen, Lange, Kahnis, Ebrard, and Schweizer. Others proceed from Christ and treat the presuppositions, person, and work of Christ, such as Liebner, Thomasius, and Lange. The antithesis of sin and grace, with the antecedent presupposition of the relation between God and the world, underlies the division of Schleiermacher and Rothe, zur Dogm. 29. Hofmann, Philippi, and Lutbardt take the concept of *foedus*, that is, communion with God, as their starting point and treat the origin, disruption, objective restoration, subjective realization, and consummation of that communion. The kingdom of God is chosen as the *principium dividendi* by Van Oosterzee; the concept of love by Schoeberlein; that of life by Oetinger, Reiff, and others; while finally many follow the customary order of theology, anthropology, and so forth, such as Vilmar, Hodge, Shedd, Böhl, and others.
