## 5.

At first, Lutheran and Reformed theologians almost universally followed this synthetic division of Melanchthon and Calvin. For various reasons it was regarded as the best. Not only Reformed theologians such as Hyperius, _Methodus Theol._, 1574, pp. 11–16; Alsted, _Theol. schol. didact._, in the _praefatio_; Ursinus, _Opera_, Heidelb., 1612, I 417; H. Alting, _Oratio inauguralis de methodo loc. comm._, Heidelb., 1613; Leydecker, _de Verit. Rel. Christ._, 1690, p. 77; but also Lutherans, such as Flacius, _Clavis Scripturae Sacrae_, vol. II, tract. I, p. 54, _Declaratio tabulae trium methodorum theologiae_, in Gass, _Gesch. der prot. Dogm._ I 46, gave preference to the synthetic method because in it, as Hyperius says, _a primis principiis per formas ac differentias ad finem usque fit progressus_. This division was therefore preferred because it followed the same historical course that God had marked out in His revelation; because it offered the least occasion for _a priori_ speculation and best preserved the positive character of theology; and because it was analogous to the method of the other sciences, which also begin with the simplest elements or _principia_ and then proceed to what is composite. This division accordingly remained dominant in the Lutheran church until Calixtus; it is found in its main features in Strigel, Selneccer, Heerbrand, Chemnitz, Hutter, Gerhard, and others. In Reformed theology it was followed until Coccejus by Sohnius, Musculus, Hyperius, Ursinus, Martyr, Wollebius, Polanus, Amesius, and others. Nevertheless, it was significantly modified at some points. An introduction was soon prefixed to dogmatics proper, treating the concept of theology, the doctrine of Scripture, and occasionally—as, for example, in Amesius and Maestricht—the nature of faith. Dogmatics was thus divided into two parts, _de principiis theologiae_ and _de articulis fidei_. Polanus, _Syntagma Theol._, p. 133. Within the corpus itself there came to be better distinction and delimitation, as well as a more systematic ordering of the _loci_. Election, treated by Calvin in the third book, was moved forward into the doctrine of the decrees; the doctrines of the world, man, Christ, and so forth each received a place of its own; the conclusion of dogmatics was occupied not by several ethical topics but by the _consummatio seculi_. Ethical material was either discussed within soteriology, or treated in a second part _de operibus_ in distinction from the first _de fide_, or, with Danaeus and Calixtus, separated altogether from dogmatics. During the seventeenth century the treatment of the individual _loci_ became increasingly scholastic; the connection with the life of faith was felt less and less.
