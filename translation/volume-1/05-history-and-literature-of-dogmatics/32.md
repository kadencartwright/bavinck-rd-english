## 32.

But already in the seventeenth century the principles were present that undermined Reformed theology and brought it to decline. In the century of the Reformation there was not only a Lutheran and a Calvinistic Reformation; two other parties also arose alongside them, namely, the Anabaptists and the Socinians, who at all times exercised great influence precisely in the Reformed church and theology in Switzerland, the Netherlands, England, and America. They represent the mystical and the rational element in religion and theology. On the Anabaptists and Mennonites, see the older literature of Luther, Melanchthon, Zwingli, Bullinger, a Lasco, de Bres, Modet, Marnix, Taffin, Caspar Heydanus, Spanheim, Faukelius, Schotanus, Hoornbeek, Cloppenburg, and others in Walch, _Bibl. Theol. sel._ II 13–29, and the more recent literature of Arnold, Schenkel, Erbkam, Hase, Halbertsma, Blaupot ten Cate, Hoekstra, Gorter, Cramer, and others in Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 271–308. Further: L. Keller, _Gesch. der Wiedertäufer u. ihres Reiches in Münster_, 1880; Goebel, _Gesch. des christl. Lebens u. s. w._ I, 1849, S. 134 f.; Ritschl, _Gesch. des Pietismus_ I, 1880, S. 22 f.; J. H. Maronier, _Het inwendig woord_, Amst., 1890; L. Keller, _Die Reformation u. die älteren Reformparteien_, Leipzig, 1885; various essays by Sepp in his _Geschiedk. Nasporingen_, 2 volumes, 1872/73, and _Kerkhist. Studiën_, 1885; A. Brons, _Ursprung, Entw. u. Schicksale der Taufgesinnten oder Mennoniten_, Norden, 1884; arts. “Anabapt.” and “Mennon.” in Herzog². On the Socinians and Unitarians of earlier and later times, compare Fock, _Der Socinianismus_, 1847; Trechsel, _Die protest. Antitrinitarier vor Faustus Socin_, 2 Bde., 1839/44; art. “Socin.” in Herzog²; Harnack, _Dogmengesch._ III 653–691.

Arminianism already had its precursors in the sixteenth century in Coolhaes, Coornhert, Wiggerts, and others, but at the beginning of the seventeenth century it became a power in the church. It resisted the confession of God’s absolute sovereignty on the familiar five points: predestination, satisfaction, human corruption, conversion, and perseverance. See their _Remonstrantie_, presented to the States in 1610; the _Confessio_ and _Apologia pro confessione_, composed by Episcopius, _Opera_ II 69 sq., 95 sq.; a Dutch edition of the confession appeared in 1665 through Uytenbogaert. Arminius, _Opera Theologica_, Francof., 1631; Uytenbogaert, _Onderwĳzing in de christ. religie_, 1640; Episcopius, _Institut. Theol._, _Opera_ I 11 sq.; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._, ed. 5a, 1730; Curcellaeus, _Opera Theol._, Amst., 1675.

In principle, Cartesianism was a complete emancipation from all authority and all objectivity, and a construction of the entire cosmos from the subject, from thought. _Cogito, ergo sum._ The rejection of all tradition and the apparently certain mathematical method by which Descartes inferred the existence of the world, of God, and of spirit appealed to many. Descartes gained many adherents, including among theologians. Renerius and Regius in Utrecht; Raey, Heerebord, Abr. Heydanus in Leiden; and further Roell, Bekker, Joh. van der Waeyen, Hautecour, and Andala adopted Cartesianism and introduced rationalism into the church. The relation of reason and revelation now became the principal question. Reason emancipates itself from revelation and attempts to regain its independence, A. C. Duker, _Schoolgezag en eigen onderzoek_, Leiden, 1861. Added to this was Cocceianism, which was indeed akin to Cartesianism in method. Cocceianism too was a reaction against traditional theology and accordingly soon allied itself with Cartesianism toward the end of the century. What was new in Coccejus, d. 1669, was not his doctrine of the covenant, as is now generally acknowledged, for this is already found in Zwingli, Bullinger, Olevianus, and others and in this country in Snecanus, Gomarus, Trelcatius, Cloppenburg, and others, but his federalist method. Coccejus’s _Summa doctrinae de foedere et testamento_, 1648, was a biblical-historical dogmatics. It made Scripture not only the principle and _norma_ but also the object of dogmatics, and thus set _theologia scripturaria_ over against _theologia traditiva_, covenant over against decree, history over against idea, and the anthropological method over against the theological. The danger of this method consisted in its drawing the eternal and immutable (_substantia foederis_) down into the stream of the temporal and historical (_oeconomia foederis_) and thus transferring the idea of becoming to God Himself. But many followed the Cocceian method: Heydanus, Wittichius, Momma, Burman, Braun, Van der Waeyen, Witsius, Camp. Vitringa, S. van Til, Joh. d’Outrein, F. A. Lampe, and others; Diestel, _Studien zur Foederaltheol._, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ 1865, 2tes Heft. The struggle between Voetians and Cocceians, and afterward between green and dry, flexible and rigid Cocceians, continued well into the eighteenth century. It ended in fact with a victory for Cocceianism and Cartesianism. Scholasticism had had its day; the flourishing of Aristotelian philosophy was over. Most chairs were occupied by Cocceians. Lampe’s arrival at Utrecht in 1720 was a victory for the Cocceians. The dogmatic handbooks that now appeared are mostly Cocceian: Melchior, _Systema_, 1685; C. Vitringa, _Korte grondstellingen der Godg._, 1688; S. van Til, 1704; T. H. v. d. Honert, _Waeragtige wegen Gods_, 1706; Ravestein, 1716; J. v. d. Honert, 1735; and others. In their main substance they are still orthodox, but they are generally small in scope, avoid all scholasticism, and already depart from the older presentation on many points. Doubt arises concerning particular satisfaction, election, the generation of the Son (Roell), the Trinity (P. Maty), the covenant of works (Alting, Vlak, Bekker, and others), and reason and revelation (Roell). The Voetians were pushed back increasingly and withdrew into seclusion. Marck’s _Merch_, 1686, Dutch trans. 1705, and Brakel’s _Redel. Godsd._, 1700, were the last dogmatics in their spirit, but they too already lacked the vigor of the earlier period. Pietistic, Labadist, and antinomian ideas penetrated their circles: Lodenstein, Labadie, Koelman, Lampe, Verschoor, Schortinghuis, Eswĳler, Antoinette de Bourignon, and others.
