## 15.

Neo-scholastic theology arose in Spain and was cultivated there especially at the universities of Salamanca, Alcalá (Complutum), and Coimbra.
Francisco de Vitoria 1480-1566, a native of Vitoria in Cantabria, belonged to the Dominicans and was sent by his order to Paris to devote himself to theology.
There he studied Thomas above all.
After returning to Spain, he became professor at Salamanca and spread the doctrine of Aquinas there; he also wrote a commentary on Aquinas’s *Summa*.
Among his pupils were the most famous theologians of Spain: Melchior Canus † 1560, *Loci theolog.* 1563; Dominicus Soto † 1560, *Comm. in 4ᵐ librum Sent.* 1557-60 and *de natura et gratia libri III* 1547 against the Scotist Catharinus; Barthol. Medina, *Expositio in I 2 Thomae* 1576.
Thomistic theology was cultivated especially by the Dominican order, to which, besides those named above, also belonged: Petrus de Soto, professor at Dillingen † 1563, *Institutiones christ.* 1548, *Methodus confessionis* 1553, *Compendium doctrinae cathol.* 1556, *Defensio cathol. confessionis* 1557 against Brenz; Dom. Bannez, *Comm. in I Thomae*, 2 tomi 1584, ’88; Didacus Alvarez, *de auxiliis gratiae div. et humani arbitrii viribus et libertate etc.* Lugd. 1611, *de incarn. verbi div.* Lugd. 1614; Vincentius Contenson, *Theologia mentis et cordis*, 2 tom. Colon. 1687; J. Baptista Gonetus † 1681, *Clypeus theol. thomist.* 1659-69, *diss. theol. de probabilitate casuistarum*; Natalis Alexander, *Theol. dogm. et mor. sec. ordinem catech. Conc. Trid.* Paris 1703; Billuart † 1757, *Summa S. Thomae hodiernis academiarum moribus accommodata*, 19 tom. Leodii 1747-59, new edition by Palmé in Paris in 8 vol.; and furthermore Fr. de Sylvestris, Joh. Viguerius, Joh. Gonsalez, Martin Ledesma, Joh. Vincentius, Balt. Navarretus, Raphael Ripa, Franz and Dominicus Perez, Gazzaniga † 1799, *Praelect. theol.* Bononiae 1790, et al.
The Carmelites align themselves with the Dominicans; especially famous among them is *Salmanticensis collegii Carmelitarum discalceatorum cursus theol. in D. Thomam*, 10 vol. Lugd. 1679 sq.
But Scotist theology also still found adherents and defenders in the modern period: Ambrosius Catharinus † 1553; Fr. Lychetus, *Comm. in I II III librum Sent. Scoti*, Venet. 1589; Frassenius † 1711, *Scotus Academicus*, 4 tom. Paris 1662-77; Dupasquier † 1718, *Summa theol. scotisticae*; Barth. Durandus, *Clypeus Scoticae doctrinae*; Thomas of Charmes, *Theol. universa*; and further Brancatus, Mastrius, Faber † 1630, Bonaventura Bellutus † 1676, Luke Wadding † 1657, editor of Scotus’s works at Lyon 1639f., et al.
The Franciscan de Rada, bishop of Trani, † 1608, gave in his work already cited above a survey of the controversies between the Thomists and the Scotists.

Scholastic theology, however, was cultivated especially by the Jesuits, who contributed more than any other order to its revival and flourishing.
Methodically and with extraordinary talent, they took up the Counter-Reformation.
Among them the following appeared as polemicists against the doctrine of the Protestants: Possevinus † 1611; Bellarminus † 1621, *Disput. de controv. christ. fidei adv. hujus temporis haereticos*, Ingolstadt 1581. Gretser, *Opera omnia* in 17 tomi, Regensburg 1734 sq. Becanus, *Manuale controversiarum*.
The most famous theologians from the Jesuit order are Petrus Canisius † 1597, *Summa doctrinae et institutionis christ.* 1554, published 400 times in 130 years, and a shorter catechism, *Institutiones christ. pietatis* 1566; Franc. Toletus † 1596, *In Summam S. Thomae tom. 4*, reissued at Rome 1869; Joh. Maldonatus, a pupil of Toletus and Soto at Salamanca, † 1583, a famous exegete and author of many dogmatic treatises, *de sacramentis, de libero arbitrio, de gratia* etc.; Leonh. Lessius, professor at Leuven, † 1623, *Disput. de gratia, decretis divinis, libertate arbitrii et praescientia Dei conditionata* Antw. 1610, *De perfectionibus divinis libri 14*, Antw. 1620, *Theologia* 1651 etc.; Lud. Molina, professor at Evora and later at Madrid, † 1600, *Liberi arbitrii cum gratiae donis, divina praescientia, provid. praedest. et reprob. concordia* 1588, *de justitia et jure*, and a commentary on the first part of Thomas; Greg. de Valentia, professor at Dillingen, Ingolstadt, and Rome, † 1603, *Analysis fidei cathol.* 1585, *Theol. Comment. in Summam S. Thomae Tomi IV* 1602; Mart. Becanus, professor at Mainz, † 1624, *Manuale controv.* and *Theol. scholastica*, 3 partes 1612-’22; Roderich Arriaga, professor at Valladolid, Salamanca, and Prague, † 1667, *Disputationes theol.* 8 tomi 1643 sq.; Franz. Suarez † 1617, *Commentaria et disputationes in Thomam*, tomi V, and many treatises *de gratia* etc.; a short excerpt from his theology is *Theologiae R. P. Fr. Suarez S. J., Summa seu Compendium auctore T. Noel S. J. Tomi II* Paris, Migne 1858; Gabr. Vasquez † 1604, *Comm. in Summam S. Thomae*, Ingolstadt 7 vol. 1609 sq.; Didacus Ruyz de Montoya † 1632, *Comment.* on various *loci* of Thomas, *Theol. scholastica* 1630, *Clavis Theol.* 1634; Antoine, *Theologia universa speculativa et dogmatica*, Paris 1713; Dion. Petavius † 1652, *De theol. dogmatibus*, 5 tomi, unfinished, Paris 1644; and furthermore Melch. de Castro, Lusitanus, Zunniga, Tannez, Hurtado, Ripalda † 1648, Mendoza, Lugo † 1660, Arriago, Gotti † 1742, Zaccaria † 1795, et al.
Special mention should also be made of the *Theologia Wirceburgensis, dogm. polem. schol. et mor.*, written by the Würzburg professors S. J., 14 vol. Wirceb. 1766-1771, new edition in 10 vol. Paris 1880.

The Jesuits generally followed Thomas but, as a consequence of their Pelagianism, departed from him in the doctrines of sin, free will, and grace.
This became the cause of a protracted conflict.
It began with Baius, professor at Leuven, † 1589, who presented the doctrine of Augustine concerning sin and the bondage of the will and also rejected the immaculate conception of Mary.
Already in 1560 various theses of Baius were rejected by the Sorbonne, and Pope Pius V condemned 79 propositions of Baius in the bull *Ex omnibus afflictionibus* 1567.
Baius recanted.
But that did not end the conflict.
It flared up again in 1588 through Molina’s work *Liberi arbitrii cum gratiae donis ... concordia*.
The Thomists, mostly Dominicans, attacked this work, especially through one of their most famous representatives, Bannez † 1604, who taught a physical predetermination.
Thomists (Bannez, Sylvester, Alvarez, Lesmos, Reginaldi, etc.), Augustinians (Noris † 1704, Laur. Berti † 1766, Bertieri), and Jesuits—Molinists or Congruists (Bellarminus)—stood opposed to one another for years.
A multitude of polemical writings on sin, free will, and grace appeared, Walch, *Bibl. theol. sel.* I 179 sq.
The commission appointed in Rome was dissolved in 1607 without reaching a decision; and the pope said that he would deliver a judgment later and that in the meantime neither party was permitted to condemn the other as heretical.
In 1640, when the work *Augustinus* by Cornelius Jansen, bishop of Ypres, appeared, the spark was once again cast into the powder.
The conflict continued into the eighteenth century.
The men of Port-Royal sided with Jansenius: Arnauld † 1694, Pascal † 1662, Nicole † 1695, Sacy † 1684, Tillemont † 1698, Quesnell † 1719.
But learning and eloquence were of no avail.
In various bulls of 1653, 1656, 1664, 1705, and 1713, Jansenism—and in it Augustine, and even Paul—was condemned, and later the bull *Unigenitus* of 1713 was confirmed several more times.
And just as Pelagianism triumphed *in dogmaticis*, so Probabilism triumphed *in ethicis*, and Curialism, or the papal system, *in ecclesiasticis*.
