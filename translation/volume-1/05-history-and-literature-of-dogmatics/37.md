## 37.

An overview of theology in England and Scotland is given by Pfleiderer, *Die Entw. der prot. Theol. in Deutschl. seit Kant u. in Grossbritt. seit 1825*, Freiburg 1891, S. 386f. Ad. Zahn, *Abriss einer Gesch. der ev. Kirche im britischen Weltreich im 19 Jahrh.*, Stuttgart 1891. Dr. Carl Clemen, *Der gegenwärtige Stand des relig. Denkens in Grossbrittannie*, *Stud. u. Krit.* 1892, 3tes Heft S. 513-548. G. Elliott, *Bilder aus dem kirchl. Leben Englands*, Leipzig, Akad. Buchhandlung. G. d’Alviella, *L’évolution relig. chez les Anglais, les Americains et les Hindous* 1884. Walker, *Present theological drifts in Scotland*, *Presb. and Ref. Rev.* Jan. 1893. Religious England, as we know it today, is a product of Methodism. John Wesley 1703-1791 was not a scholarly theologian but a powerful preacher who individualized the gospel and made it a vital question for every human being; cf. J. Wedgwood, *John Wesley and the evangelical reaction of the eighteenth century* 1870. The Methodism that arose through him and George Whitefield 1714-1771 was not primarily a departure from one or another of the 39 Articles, but it concentrated the whole truth of religion around two centers: first, the sudden, conscious experience of guilt and grace, that is, personal conversion; and second, the manifestation of that new life in an entirely new form, consisting in setting out to convert others, abstaining from various *adiaphora*, and holding Christian perfection to be attainable already in this life. This one-sidedness gradually led to various dogmas being contested, modified, or regarded as of subordinate value; on Methodism, cf. Schoell’s art. in Herzog². Saussaye, *Godsd. Bewegingen* 109f. Möhler, *Symbolik* § 75-76. Schneckenburger, *Vorles. über die Lehrbegriffe der kleineren prot. Kirchenparteien* 1863 S. 103-151. Kolde, *der Methodismus u. seine Bekämpfung*, Erlangen 1886. Id. *Die Heilsarmee* ib. 1885, Dutch trans. by Dr. A. W. Bronsveld. Methodism exercised an incalculable influence on Independents, Baptists, Presbyterians, and Anglicans; Wesley was the re-creator of English and American Protestantism, revivals have never since departed from the Protestant churches, and he was the *Archbishop of the Slums*, the father of the home mission, and the founder of Christian socialism. Methodism, however, was confined mainly to the common people. In the higher circles—in politics, philosophy, and literature—cold liberalism remained dominant. A reaction against it came through Romanticism (Walter Scott, Robert Southey, S. T. Coleridge † 1834), which was transplanted into the ecclesiastical and theological sphere in the Oxford Movement. This movement considerably strengthened the High Church party, which had existed since the sixteenth century, and vigorously promoted Roman Catholic doctrine and ritual in the Anglican Church. Cf. art. Traktarianismus in Herzog². The authors of *Lux Mundi* 1890—Canon Holland, Moore, Illingworth, Talbot, C. Gore, and others—also attempted to renew Puseyism and to make the Christian dogmas plausible by placing them in the light of the modern age. Alongside the High Church party arose the Broad Church party, which had already been prepared in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries by Latitudinarianism and in this century gained outstanding representatives in Thomas Arnold † 1842, Hampden † 1868, F. D. Maurice † 1872, Charles Kingsley † 1874, Whately † 1863, F. W. Robertson † 1853, and A. P. Stanley † 1881. Averse to the Methodism of the Low Church party, they all strove earnestly and nobly for a reconciliation of Christianity and culture. In their conviction, the church had to become a school for the education of the people; religion had to reconcile itself with all that was true, good, and beautiful, wherever it might be found; and Christianity had to reveal its power above all in the moral renewal of society. This broad, tolerant standpoint found increasing assent. The rise and advance of ritualism, the many sects and currents, the distress of the lower strata of society, and especially the Higher Criticism, which had already entered the English world with Bishop Colenso and was then propagated by W. Robertson Smith, T. K. Cheyne, and S. R. Driver, gave the Broad Church tendency the upper hand. One of its principal spokesmen at present is F. W. Farrar, dean of Westminster Abbey. But this broad standpoint is now adopted by many not only within but also far beyond the Established Church. Among its principal spokesmen today are the Baptist J. Clifford; the Congregationalists E. W. Dale, *The old Evangelicalism and the new* 1889, Dr. Joseph Parker in London, and Dr. Fairbairn of Mansfield College, *The place of Christ in modern theology*, cf. *Theol. Stud.* by Dr. Daubanton, May, June 1894; the Presbyterians Prof. Henry Drummond, *The natural law in the spiritual world*, *Summum bonum*, and so forth; Marcus Dods; A. B. Bruce, *Apologetics, or Christianity defensively stated*, Edinburgh, Clark 1892; James Lindsay, *The progressiveness of modern Christian thought*, Edinb., Blackwood 1892; Horton, *Inspiration and the Bible*; Rev. J. B. Heard, *Alexandrinian and Carthaginian Theology*, Edinb., Clark, Hulsean lectures; Prof. Edw. Caird, *The evolution of religion*, 2 vols.; James Orr, *The Christian view of God and the world, as centring in the incarnation*, Edinb., Elliot 1893, cf. my essay, *Theol. Stud.* by Dr. Daubanton 1894. And furthermore, there are Flint, Milligan, and Gloag in the Established Church of Scotland; Brown, Rainy, Davidson, Salmond, Laidlaw, and G. A. Smith in the Free Church; Cairns, Muir, Thomson, and J. Smith in the United Presbyterian Church; and others. Besides its Christological point of departure, which concentrates everything around the incarnation, this movement is also characterized by its drive toward union. Ecclesiastical differences are erased or presented as having little significance; in practice people work together with men of every church and tendency; and in theory many even strive for the union of all Protestant churches, as does J. Clifford, for example. W. T. Stead surely goes furthest when he hopes for a “church of the future” that will lead the whole of social reform, incorporate all of culture, even the theaters, and embrace all people. Often this is also accompanied by the hope of a restoration of all things beyond the grave; from the abundant literature, see only *The wider Hope, Essays and Strictures on the doctrine and literature of future punishment by numerous writers, lay and clerical*, London, Fisher Unwin 1890. Strict Calvinism is losing ground by the day. Here and there it is still maintained—in the Scottish Highlands, in Wales, and elsewhere—but there is no longer any question of its scholarly cultivation and defense. By contrast, the doctrine of evolution is expanding in England by the day. After deism had already subjected miracles, prophecies, and revelation to formidable criticism, this process of religious dissolution continued still further under the influence of Darwin’s theory of descent, Spencer’s agnosticism, Stuart Mill’s empiricism, and the materialism of Tyndall and Huxley, *Evolution and ethics* 1893, cf. *Tĳdspiegel*, April 1894. The critique of the Christian dogmas received unexpected, powerful support from Edwin Hatch, *The organisation of the early christ. churches* 1881, *The influence of Greek ideas and usages upon the christ. church*, Hibbert lectures 1888, who, like Hampden before him in his *The scholastic philosophy in relation to christ. theology* 1832, explained the dogmas from the union of pagan philosophy with original Christianity. Unitarianism, which established its first congregation in 1773 and now possesses an outstanding spokesman in James Martineau, *A study of religion, its sources and contents* 1888, *The seat of authority in religion* 1890, is gradually gaining influence. Many have even broken completely with Christianity—George Eliot, Charles Bradlaugh, Annie Besant, W. St. Ross, Morley, J. C. Morison; cf. Ch. Bradlaugh, A. Besant and Ch. Watts, *The freethinkers textbook* 1876—and seek their religious satisfaction in Buddhist theosophy (Madame Blavatsky, Annie Besant), in morality (M. Arnold, *Literature and dogma* 1873, *God and the Bible* 1875, who defined religion as *morality touched with emotion* and God as *the eternal power, not ourselves, which makes for righteousness*), or in a society for ethical culture (founded in 1876 in America and in 1886 in England; W. M. Salter, *Die Religion der Moral*, deutsch von G. von Gizycki, Leipzig 1885), or even in Mohammedanism, for which a mosque was opened in Liverpool in 1891.
