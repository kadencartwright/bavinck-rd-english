# § 5. History and Literature of Dogmatics

### A. Rise of Dogmatics, Second–Fourth Centuries

## 1.

Holy Scripture is not a dogmatic system. It contains all the knowledge of God that we need, but it has not formulated this knowledge dogmatically. There the truth is laid down as the fruit of revelation and inspiration, in a language that is the immediate expression of life and therefore always remains fresh and original. But that truth has not yet become an object of reflection and has not yet passed through the thinking consciousness. Here and there, for example in the Epistle to the Romans, there may be a beginning of dogmatic development; but it is no more than a beginning. The period of revelation had to be closed before that of dogmatic reproduction could begin. Scripture is the gold mine, but it is the church that digs out the gold, stamps it, and converts it into current coin, Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* II 20 f. This dogmatic processing of the content of Holy Scripture, however, is not the work of one individual theologian, or of a particular church or school, but of the entire church through all the centuries, of the whole new humanity reborn through Christ. Just as there is unity and continuity in the development of every science, the same is true of theology and dogmatics. These too have a history. Not only in the sense that the material and content were gradually revealed by God over the course of centuries; nor merely in the sense that the various truths were gradually formulated more clearly by the church and applied in life; but also in the sense that scientific consciousness acquired an increasingly clear insight into the organism and system of that revealed truth. The scientific character of dogmatics and theology has likewise undergone a historical development.

The Apostolic Fathers, edited among others by Hefele-Funk, Tübingen 1878-1881; Gebhardt, Harnack, Zahn, *Patrum apost. opera.* Lips. 1875-’77, *ed minor repetita.* Lips. 1894; Hilgenfeld, *Nov. Test. extra canonem receptum, ed sec.* Lips. 1884, translated by Duker and Van Manen, *Oud-Christ. letterkunde.* Amst. 1871, still stand entirely at the standpoint of a naive, childlike faith. Christianity was not the fruit of human investigation but of revelation and therefore demanded faith in the first place. They still simply echo Holy Scripture. The biblical concepts—such as Christ as Lord, His death, faith, conversion, congregation, watchfulness, prayer, almsgiving, resurrection, life, immortality, etc.—are indeed adopted, but without reflection awakening and without their content being thought through and analyzed. Christianity, moreover, found acceptance chiefly among the simple and uneducated. All the more, therefore, every effort is directed toward translating Christian truth into life and bringing it to practical dominion also in worship and organization; the emphasis is placed not on *gnosis* but on a holy life, on the practice of the Christian virtues of love, meekness, peace, unity, etc. The circle of ideas within which they move is therefore still narrow; many biblical concepts are altogether absent, and others are weakened or modified; the works-righteous conception of Christianity is being prepared. In general, the writings of the Apostolic Fathers are ethically more powerful and richer than they are dogmatically. Despite the same epistolary form and, in part, the same congregations to which they are addressed, the difference and distance between the Apostles and the Apostolic Fathers stand out very sharply. Väter*, Wien 1880. Lechler, *Das apost. u. nachapost. Zeitalter*, 3e Ausg. 586-610. Zöckler, *Handbuch der theol. Wiss.* Supplementband 1889 S. 18 f. Dorner, *Entw. Gesch. der Lehre v. d. Person Christi*, 2e Aufl. I 130 f. Harnack, *Dogmengesch.* I 125-186.
