### E. Dogmatics in the Reformed Church

## 29.

Despite all their agreement, extending even to the confession of predestination, there was nevertheless from the beginning an important difference between the German and Swiss Reformations. The differences in land and people among whom Luther and Zwingli appeared, and the differences in origin, upbringing, character, and life experience, contributed to driving the two apart. It was only a short time before it came to light that the two Reformers were of a different spirit. In 1529 peace was indeed signed at Marburg, but only on paper. And when Zwingli passed away and Calvin, despite his high esteem for Luther and his rapprochement in the doctrine of the Lord’s Supper, nevertheless chose Zwingli’s side in principle, the breach between Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism grew ever wider and became a fact that could no longer be undone. Historical investigations in this century into the characteristic difference between the two have clearly demonstrated that a difference of principle underlies it. In former times the dogmatic differences were simply enumerated without being reduced to a common principle, Hoornbeek, Summa Controv. 1653 p. 618. But Max Goebel, Die relig. Eigenthümlichkeit der luth. u. ref. K. 1837, Dutch trans., Groningen, Smit 1841, offered the first attempt at a historical and principial explanation of the difference between the two. Since then various men have continued that investigation, including Ullmann, Semisch, Hagenbach, Ebrard, Herzog, Schweizer, Baur, Schneckenburger, Guder, Schenkel, Schoeberlein, Stahl, and Hundeshagen, whose works are cited and evaluated by Voigt, Fundamentaldogmatik, 1874 S. 397-480, Scholten, Leer der Herv. Kerk, 4th ed. II 309f., and others. The difference still appears to be best expressed by saying that the Reformed is theological and the Lutheran anthropological. The Reformed does not remain within history but ascends to the idea, to the eternal decree of God; the Lutheran takes his standpoint in the midst of the history of salvation and feels no need to penetrate more deeply into the counsel of God. For the Reformed, therefore, election is the *cor ecclesiae*; for the Lutheran, justification is the *articulus stantis et cadentis ecclesiae*. In the former the first and principal question is, “How does God receive His glory?” In the latter, by contrast, it is, “How does the human being attain salvation?” In the former the struggle is directed chiefly against paganism and idolatry; in the latter, against Judaism and works-righteousness. The Reformed has no rest until, looking backward, he has traced everything to the decree of God and discovered the διοτι of things and, looking forward, has made everything subservient to the glory of God. The Lutheran is content with the ὁτι and enjoys the salvation of which he partakes through faith. From this difference in principle, the dogmatic controversies in the doctrine of the image of God, original sin, the person of Christ, the order of salvation, the sacraments, church government, ethics, and other topics can readily be explained.

The history of Reformed dogmatics is much more difficult to describe than that of Lutheran dogmatics. The Reformed church is not confined to one land and people but has spread through different countries and among different peoples. The Reformed type is not deposited in a single confession but has found expression in numerous confessions. Dogmatic development—for example, in the doctrine of election, justification, regeneration, the sacraments, and other topics—has been much richer and more varied in the Reformed church than in the Lutheran. Finally, the history of Reformed dogmatics has received much less study than that of the other churches; here a field still lies open for research. The principal works that treat it are C. M.
