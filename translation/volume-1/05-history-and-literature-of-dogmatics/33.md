## 33.

And such was the course of theology in all the Reformed churches. In France the academy of Saumur became the center of all kinds of startling propositions. John Cameron † 1625 not only aligned himself with Piscator in Herborn in denying the imputation of Christ’s *obedientia activa* but also taught that the will always follows the intellect and that the bending of the will in regeneration was therefore not a physical but an ethical act. Moïse Amyraut † 1664, *Traité de la prédestination*, made the common doctrine of the *voluntas signi*, of the earnest and well-meant offer of grace, into a separate decree that preceded the decree of election. In doing so, he laid a Remonstrant foundation beneath the Calvinistic edifice and ran the risk of reducing human inability to believe to a merely moral inability. Louis Cappel † 1658 maintained in his work *Arcanum punctationis revelatum*, published anonymously in Leiden by Erpenius in 1624, that the Hebrew points *quoad figuram* had been invented later by Jewish scholars and inserted into the text, and he provoked a rebuttal from Buxtorf in 1648. In his *Critica sacra* 1650 he taught that the Hebrew text had not remained uncorrupted, and in his *Diatribe de veris et antiquis Hebraeorum literis* 1645 that the Samaritan script was older than the Hebrew square script. Josué de la Place, *de statu hominis lapsi ante gratiam* 1640, denied the immediate imputation of Adam’s sin. Claude Pajon † 1685 denied the necessity of the *gratia interna* and was opposed by Pierre Jurieu in his *Traité de la nature et de la grâce* 1687. Thus the theological controversy in France centered especially on the nature of subjective grace. Cameron restricted it to the enlightenment of the intellect, Amyraut made objective grace universal, and Pajon taught that a special subjective grace was superfluous. In this way deism and rationalism were prepared.

Among the Nonconformists in England there was great diversity. After the Westminster Assembly the Presbyterians declined both in number and in influence and had to yield their place to Independency, which had already been embraced in the sixteenth century by Robert Browne, Johnson, Ainsworth, and John Robinson † 1625, *Works*, 3 vols., London 1851; cf. *Ned. Archief v. K. Gesch. v. Kist en Royaards* 1848 VIII p. 369-407, and which increased in power and standing during the Civil War. At the Westminster Assembly the Presbyterians still had the majority, and the Independents still commanded only a few votes: Thomas Goodwin † 1680, Philip Nye † 1672, Jeremiah Burroughs † 1646, William Bridge † 1670, William Carter † 1658, Sidrach Simpson † 1658, Joseph Caryl † 1673, and others. But by October 1658 a meeting in London was already attended by delegates from more than a hundred Independent congregations. There the Savoy Declaration was drawn up; Hoornbeek translated it into Latin in 1659 and printed it after his *Epistola ad Duraeum de Independentismo*, Schaff, *Creeds* I 820-840 III 707. Their principal theologian was Dr. John Owen 1616-1683, *Works*, 21 vols., London 1826; Milton’s *Doctrina Christiana* was published in Brunswick in 1827, *Stud. u. Kr.* 1879, issue 4. See further Jos. Fletcher, *History of independency in England since the reformation*, 4 vols., London 1847-49, and other literature in Schaff, *Creeds* I 820. The Baptist movement, too, already appeared sporadically in England in the sixteenth century, but it did not begin to form its own congregations until 1633. In 1644 it numbered 7 congregations in London and 47 outside it. In 1677 the Baptists issued a *Confession of faith* that differed from the Westminster Confession and the Savoy Declaration only in church government and baptism. On the basis of this confession William Collins composed a catechism in 1693 that was generally adopted. The General, Arminian, or Free-will Baptists are to be distinguished from the Calvinistic Baptists. The Baptist movement later spread especially in America through Roger Williams † 1683. It contributed little to dogmatics, but in John Bunyan † 1688, Robert Hall, John Foster, and others it produced powerful preachers, Schaff, *Creeds* I 845-859, III 738-756. J. M. Cramp, *Baptist History from the foundation of the Christ. Church to the close of the 18 century*, Philad. 1868. The period of the Civil War in England was a time of the greatest confusion in the religious and theological sphere. All kinds of ideas and currents churned together. Formerly these were regarded as so many separate sects, but they are much better regarded, with Weingarten, as variations within the one great party of the Saints. Arminian, Baptist, chiliastic, antinomian, and even libertine sentiments gained acceptance. A religious individualism prevailed. In Quakerism this reached its culmination. Emancipation from tradition, the confession, and ecclesiastical organization is completed in it: every believer is placed on his own, detached even from Scripture, and possesses within himself—in the Spirit, in the inner light—the source of his religious life and knowledge. Everything objective—Scripture, Christ, church, office, sacrament—is set aside; the believers live by a principle of their own and distinguish themselves in society as well by their own manners, customs, clothing, and so forth. George Fox 1624-1640, *Works*, 3 vols., London 1694-1706, was the founder of this sect; Robert Barclay 1648-1690, *Apologia theologiae vere christianae*, Amst. 1675, was its theologian; and William Penn 1644-1718 was its statesman. William Sewel, *History of the rise, increase and progress of the christian people called Quaker*, London 1725. Th. Evans, *An exposition of the faith of the religious society of friends*, Philad. 1828. Schneckenburger, *Vorles. über die Lehrbegriffe der kleineren prot. Kirchenparteien* 69f. Weingarten, *Rev. Kirchen Englands* 364f. Möhler, *Symbolik* 492f. Schaff, *Creeds* I 859-873. Herzog² art. Quäker.

All these individualistic currents paved the way for deism. The realism of the English national character, the nominalism of Duns Scotus, Roger Bacon, and William of Ockham, and the empiricist philosophy of Francis Bacon † 1626 had already prepared it. And when the confusion in religious convictions was added to this in the seventeenth century, and all England was divided into parties and sects, many came to think that the essence of religion could lie only in what was common to all. Latitudinarianism gained ground and ended in deism. The line of deists was opened by Herbert of Cherbury † 1648, who in his works *de veritate* 1624 and *de religione gentilium* 1645 reduced the essence of religion to five truths: the existence of God, the worship of God, virtue, repentance, and retribution; but this original, true, and pure religion had been corrupted in all kinds of ways by the priests. This was the program of deism. From this position the attack on revelation was then undertaken. Locke † 1704, *The reasonableness of christianity* 1695, entrusted to reason the decision concerning revelation. John Toland † 1722, *Christianity not mysterious* 1696, declared that Christianity contained nothing contrary to reason and nothing above reason. Anthony Collins † 1729, *Discourse on freethinking* 1713, commended free—that is, unbelieving—thinking. Thomas Woolston † 1731 wrote *Discourses on the miracles of our saviour* 1727-30 and attempted to explain the miracles by allegory. Matthew Tindal † 1733, *Christianity as old as creation* 1730, set aside revelation altogether. Deism ended in skepticism with Henry Dodwell, *Christianity not founded on argument* 1742. And this skepticism was brought to completion in philosophy by David Hume † 1776.
