### C. Dogmatics in the Latin, Roman Catholic Church

## 8.

From the beginning, church and theology in the West bear a distinctive character. This is already clearly apparent in Tertullian, Cyprian, and Irenaeus. In the East the dominant thought in dogmatics is that through sin man is subjected to φθορα and is now delivered from death by God Himself in Christ and made a partaker of life, immortality, and the divine nature. The ideas of substance, being, and nature stand in the foreground here and foster stasis and repose in both doctrine and life. In the West, by contrast, the emphasis falls on the relation in which man stands to God. This relation is that of a guilty person before a righteous God whose commandments he has transgressed. Through His work, however, Christ has obtained the grace of God, the forgiveness of sins, and the power to keep the law. And this impels one toward an active life, toward obedience and submission. In the East they follow chiefly John; in the West, Paul. There the center of gravity lies in the incarnation; here, in the death of Christ. There the person of Christ stands in the foreground; here, His work. In the East the primary concern is the divine-human nature, the unity of both natures in Christ; in the West, by contrast, the distinction between the two natures and the mediatorial position Christ occupies between God and man. There the mystical and liturgical element prevails; here, the juridical and political. Kattenbusch, _Confessionskunde_ I 103 f. This difference was present from the beginning. The schism was a question of time. With the rise of Constantinople the open conflict began. Constantinople could not point to an apostolic origin and derived all its importance from politics, from the imperial court. It wished to be a second Rome. According to canon 3 of the council of 381, the bishop of Constantinople received τα πρεσβεια της τιμης after the bishop of Rome, δια το εἰναι αὐτην νεαν Ρωμην. It was satisfied with this, with a place alongside Rome. The East desired one church, certainly, but in two halves, with two emperors, two capitals, and two bishops of equal rank. The Greek church calls itself Orthodox; it considers itself in full possession of the truth; it rests and enjoys. But it also calls itself Anatolian; it binds itself to a particular land and is content with this. The situation with Rome was entirely different. Rome maintained itself not as a political city alongside Constantinople but placed itself, as _sedes apostolica_, high above Constantinople. Rome represented and defended a religious interest. It soon based its claims and rights on Matt. 16:18 and demanded a universal, a catholic place. The Western church therefore contains an aggressive, world-conquering tendency. These two different directions drove East and West apart. When differences in customs and rites, and especially in the confession of the _filioque_, were added, the schism was increasingly prepared. It was formally established in 1054.

Nevertheless, in many respects the West was dependent on the East. There, after all, the church had first been founded. There the Apostolic Fathers and the Apologists appeared. There the mighty struggle against Gnosticism and Manichaeism was waged. There the theological and Christological dogmas were established at the councils. Synods first arose in Asia Minor in the second century. The ecumenical councils from 325 until the middle of the ninth century were all held in the East, in Asia Minor or Constantinople, and all through the council of 879 are also recognized by the Western church. The objective foundations of church doctrine are the same in East and West. From the second half of the second century, Eastern theology also penetrated the West. Victorinus Rhetor, Hilary, Ambrose, Jerome, and Rufinus transferred the theological world of thought from East to West. Old Testament exegesis, Platonic theology, monasticism, and the ideal of virginity entered the West and there married the Western spirit. Ambrose, d. 397, studied the works of Clement, Origen, Didymus, and especially Basil, and brought into the West Old Testament exegesis (_Hexaemeron_, _de Paradiso_, _de Cain et Abel_, etc., following Basil); the ideal of virginity in the sense of the mystical marriage of the soul with Christ (_de virginitate_, _liber de Isaac et anima_, following Origen and Methodius); and also the Cappadocians’ doctrine of the Trinity and Christology (_Libri V de fide_, _Libri III de Spiritu S°_, _liber de incarnationis dominicæ sacramento_). During his exile in Asia Minor from 356 to 359, Hilary of Pictavium, d. 368, informed the bishops of Gaul about the Christological controversy in the East in his work _de Synodis seu de fide Orientalium_, defended this doctrine in his twelve-book work _de Trinitate_, and in his exegetical works on Matthew and several psalms made generous use of typological and allegorical exegesis. Victorinus Rhetor, highly praised by Augustine, _Conf._ 8, 2, introduced Neoplatonic philosophy into theology in his writings _Liber ad Justinum Manichæum contra duo principia Manichæorum et de vera carne Christi_, in his _liber de generatione divina_, and in his controversial work _Adversus Arium Libri IV_, and thereby exercised the greatest influence on Augustine. Rufinus, d. 410, spent many years in Egypt and Palestine and associated with the hermits, with Jerome, with Didymus in Alexandria, and with John in Jerusalem. He was important above all because he rendered many Greek works of Josephus, Eusebius, Origen, Basil, Gregory Naz., and others into Latin. He also wrote a _Historia monachorum_, biographies of thirty-three saints in the Nitrian desert, and _Peregrinationes ad loca sancta_; various commentaries on books of the Old Testament; and finally an _Expositio symboli apostolici_, whose value is greater for history than for dogmatics. Finally, Jerome, d. 420, must be mentioned here. Although educated in Rome, he spent the greater part of his life in Syria and Palestine. His merit lies chiefly in his many studies of Scripture. In theology he is little independent and very anxious about his orthodoxy; in his exegesis he often honors the allegorical method of Philo and the Alexandrian theologians; above all he is a eulogist of asceticism. He defended the virginity of Mary against Helvidius, the meritoriousness of fasting and celibacy against Jovinian, and the veneration of the martyrs and their relics against Vigilantius; and, in Luther’s words in the _Table Talk_, he always speaks of fasting, food, virginity, and almost never of faith and hope and love.
