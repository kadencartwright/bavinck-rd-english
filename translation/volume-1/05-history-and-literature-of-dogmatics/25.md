## 25.

This, after all, had also been Hegel’s endeavor. Hegel is the most consistent idealist; the Cartesian starting point is here carried to its final consequences. Thought produces being, and therefore all that exists is logical and rational. But he combines this thought with that of becoming, of evolution. The realization of thought in being takes place gradually, from stage to stage. In the human being this thought first comes to consciousness. But at the lowest stage, as a natural, finite spirit, the human being feels himself separated from God. In religion, especially in the Christian religion, however, he sees that separation abolished: God and the human being are one. This unity of God and the human being is the essence of all religion, but it is not adequately expressed in religion; there it is clothed in forms of representation and feeling. Only in philosophy does the idea attain its adequate concept. Philosophy is absolute knowledge: humanity’s knowledge of God, God’s knowledge of Himself. Hegel attempted to explain all Christian *dogmata*—Trinity, incarnation, reconciliation, and others—according to these philosophical ideas. Many believed in the Hegelian reconciliation of theology and philosophy. The so-called Right—Marheinecke, Daub, Göschel, and Rosenkranz—treated the *dogmata* in his spirit and attempted to unite Hegelian speculation with orthodox theology. But gradually the danger hidden in Hegelian philosophy for the Christian *dogmata* became evident. Feuerbach, Strauss, Vatke, and Bruno Bauer drew the consequences and abandoned representation, the garment of religion, in order to retain only the abstract concept. Strauss saw in the Gospel narratives unconscious symbolic fictions of the ideal truth that the Infinite pours itself out into the finite, though not in a single human being but in humanity; humanity is the true Son of God, the ideal Christ. In his Glaubenslehre 1840 he attempts to show that the history of every dogma is at the same time its critique and dissolution. Religion and philosophy differ in form, and therefore also in content; philosophy replaces religion. The neo-Hegelians, Biedermann and Pfleiderer, have indeed become more cautious, but they have nevertheless adopted from Hegel the view that thought stands above religious consciousness and therefore has the task of stripping religious representation of its temporal, sensible form, presenting it in its ideal core, and bringing it into agreement with our entire worldview.
