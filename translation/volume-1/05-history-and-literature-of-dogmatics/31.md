## 31.

Already toward the end of the sixteenth century the scholastic method arose in Reformed theology. The simple treatment of the *dogmata* that we encounter in Calvin, Hyperius, and Sohnius could not satisfy in the long run. In Martyr, Sadeel, and Junius we already find familiarity with the questions that were treated by the scholastics in the Middle Ages. Zanchius † 1590 in particular, in his works De tribus Elohim, de natura Dei, de operibus Dei, de incarnatione (Opera Omnia in 8 tomi, Geneve 1619), and Polanus of Polansdorf † 1610, in his Syntagma Theologiae, were exceptionally well acquainted with the theology of the church fathers and the scholastics.
Dogmatics was then treated in a scholastic manner in the Reformed churches during the seventeenth century: in the Netherlands by Trelcatius Jr., Scholastica et methodica locorum omnium S. Scr. institutio 1604; Nerdenus, Systema theol. 1611; Maccovius, Collegia theologica 1623, ed. 3a 1641. Loci Comm. Theol. 1626; Fr. Gomarus, Opera theol. omnia Amstel. 1664; Gisb. Voetius, Disputationes sel. 5 partes, Ultraj. 1648-59; and elsewhere especially by J. H. Alsted, professor at Herborn and Weissenburg † 1638, Theol. scholastica didactica, exhibens locos communes theol. methodo scholastica 1618. The scholastic method, however, found by no means universal approval. At the Synod of Dort, Maccovius received the admonition *ut cum Spiritu Sancto loquatur, non cum Bellarmino ant Suarezio*, Heringa, De twistzaak van Maccovius, Archief voor Kerk. Gesch. III 1831 p. 505 f. The dispute between Maccovius and Amesius—see De twist van Maccovius en Amesius, ib. p. 643 f., and Van der Tuuk, Joh. Bogerman p. 229 f.—and that between Maresius and Voetius had their basis in the same scholastic method. Maresius called Voetius a *theologus paradoxus*, enumerated no fewer than 600 *paradoxa* in his theology, and accused him above all of seeking to *lacum asphaltidem scholasticorum derivare in fontem Siloe*; see his Theologus paradoxus retectus et refutatus 1649 and, in reply, Voetius, Disput. Sel. V. 572-716.
But even where theologians guarded against philosophical terminology, scholastic distinctions, and idle scholastic questions, and presented the truth in a simpler form, the seventeenth century was nevertheless the century of objectivity. The material lay ready; it needed only to be ordered. Tradition became a power. Not only Scripture but also the confession—indeed, even the dogmatic treatment itself—acquired an inviolable authority and caused Camero to complain that in doctrine one could not depart *ἀπο των δοκουντων εἰναι στυλοι* without being persecuted, Schweizer, Centraldogmen II 237.
The principal theologians in the Netherlands were Polyander, Walaeus, Thysius, and Rivetus, authors of the Synopsis Purioris theologiae, as well as Trigland and Hoornbeek at Leiden; Maccovius, Acronius, Amesius, Schotanus, Bogerman, Cloppenburg, and Arnoldus at Franeker; Ravensperger, Gomarus, H. Alting, and Maresius at Groningen; Voetius, Essenius, Mastricht, and Leydecker at Utrecht; furthermore Bucanus at Lausanne and Wollebius at Basel; Danaeus, Franc. Turretinus, and B. Pictet at Geneva; J. H. Heidegger and J. H. Hottinger at Zurich; Chamier, Bérault, and Garissoles at Montauban; Tilenus, Dumoulin, and Beaulieu at Sedan; and further Benj. Basnage, David Blondel, Sam. Bochartus, Jean Mestrezat, Charles Drelincourt, and Jean Daillé; and especially the theologians at Saumur: Camero, Amyraldus, Cappellus, and Placaeus. Cf. Félice, Histoire des Protestants de France 7e ed. 1880.
In England during the seventeenth century the High Church and Arminian tendency gained ground. It found support among the Stuarts, the archbishops, and the nobility, and was promoted by Bancroft, Whitgift’s successor from 1604 to 1610, who in a sermon in 1589 defended episcopacy as necessary; Buckingham 1625-28; Archbishop Laud 1628-45; and Lord Clarendon † 1674. By contrast, there were still many theologians in the Anglican Church who defended episcopacy yet remained faithful to Calvinism. Such were Whitgift, archbishop of Canterbury, 1583-1604, adviser to Elizabeth; Archbishop Abbot 1604-1623, who fell into disgrace in 1622; the delegates to the Synod of Dort, Carlton, Hall, Davenant, professor at Cambridge and later bishop of Salisbury, Determinationes quaestionum quarundam theologicarum, Cambr. 1634; Ward, Goad, and Balcanqual; and further men such as Burton, Warton, Prynne, Rouse, Preston, Usher, Corpus theologiae, Dublin 1638, Dutch translation by Ruytingius, ’t Lichaam der Godd. leer Amst. 1656; Morton; John Prideaux, Lectiones theologicae, Scholasticae theologiae syntagma 1651; Saunderson; Hammond, Opera Omnia Lond. 1684; Westfield; Stillingfleet, Op. Omn. Lond. 1709; Tillotson, archbishop of Canterbury, Works London 1704; John Pearson, Exposition of the Creed 1659, Lectiones de Deo et ejus attributis; Burnet † 1715, professor at Glasgow and later bishop of Salisbury, An exposition of the 39 articles; Roger Boyle, Summa theologiae christ. Dubl. 1687; John Forbes of Corse, professor at Aberdeen, Instructiones histor. theol. de doctrina christ. 1699; Thomas Pierce, Pacificatorium orthodoxae ecclesiae corpusculum 1685; Foggius, Theol. speculativae schema 1712; W. Beveridge, Thesaurus theol. or a complete system of divinity Lond. 1710-11; and Th. Bennet, Instructions for studying 1 a general system or body of divinity, 2 the 39 articles of religion, Lond. 1715. Among the Puritans, the best known from this period are Bradshaw, Raynolds, Baynes, Byfield, Rogers, Hooker, White, Archer, Hildersham, Davenport, Lightfoot, Seldenus, Twissus, Calamy, Gataker, Baxter, Bates, Mead, Owen, etc.
Arminianism had great influence in England among both dissenters and Anglicans. Alongside it, Amyraldism was also brought into England from France. The two frequently flowed together and found their union in the neonomian theory, which gave rise to an important and protracted controversy. The neonomians placed the ground of justification in faith, as did, for example, the Arminian John Goodwin, the friend of Milton, in his The banner of justification displayed, Imputatio fidei 1642; Richard Baxter, Justifying Righteousness; Dr. Dan. Williams, Works, 1750; and Benj. Woodbridge, The method of grace in the justification of sinners 1656. Opposed to them were others who were wrongly called antinomians but should properly have been called anti-neonomians and who placed the ground of justification solely in the imputed righteousness of Christ, such as Dr. Crisp; Dr. Tully, Justificatio paulina sine operibus 1677; Isaac Chauncy, Neonomianism unmasked 1692. Id. Alexipharmacon, a fresh antidote against neonomian bane 1700; John Eaton, The honeycombe of free justification by Christ alone 1642; William Eyre, Vindiciae justificationis gratuitae 1654, etc. Cf. Witsius, Misc. Sacra II p. 753 Sq. James Buchanan, The doctrine of justification, Edinburgh Clark, 1867 p. 176, 464.
Taken as a whole, however, the center of gravity of English theology lay not in dogmatic studies but in biblical, church-historical, patristic, archaeological, and practical studies. The political and ecclesiastical circumstances naturally gave occasion for this, Gass, Gesch. der prot. Theol. III 297 f. Ypey, Syst. Godg. II 268 f.; and further Weingarten, Die Revolutionskirchen Englands 1868. Neal, Historie der Puriteinen, Rott. 1752 f. Marsden, History of the early and later Puritans from the reformation to the ejection of the nonconf. clergy in 1662, 2 vol. London 1852. Dr. Stoughton, History of religion in England from the opening of the long Parliament to the end of the 18th century, 8 vol. 1881. Dr. Tulloch, Rational theology and Christ. theology in England in the 17th century, 2 vol. 1872.
By comparison, dogmatic life in Scotland was richer and more vigorous. There Calvinism had found congenial soil and was developed further in a strict, positive spirit. The principal theologians in this period were Rollock, principal of the University of Edinburgh from 1583, author of commentaries on the epistles of Paul, the Psalms, and Daniel, and especially of a treatise on effectual calling; John Welsh of Ayr, who wrote against Romanism; John Sharp, who published a harmony of the prophets and the apostles; the Simpson brothers—Patrick, who produced a church history; William, who wrote on the Hebrew accents; and Archibald, who produced an exposition of the seven penitential psalms; Boyd of Trochrigg, professor at Saumur and, in 1614, principal of the University of Glasgow, renowned for his commentary on the Epistle to the Ephesians, which not only provides an exposition but is a true *thesaurus* and contains all kinds of dogmatic and theological excursuses on the Trinity, predestination, incarnation, sin, baptism, etc.; David Calderwood, who spent time in the Netherlands and wrote his Altare damascenum against the Anglican episcopate; Samuel Rutherford, professor at St. Andrews, known not only for his Brieven but also for many other works, Exercitationes apol. pro divina gratia 1637, de Providentia, Examen Arminianismi, The spiritual Antichrist, etc.; George Gillespie, author of Nihil respondes, Male audis, Aaron’s Rod blossoming, Miscellanies; and further Baillie, Dickson, Durham, Dr. Strang, James Wood, Patrick Gillespie, Hugh Binning, and others. Cf. David Calderwood, The history of the Kirk of Scotland, 7 vol. Edinb. 1842. Buckle, History of civilization in England, 5 vol. Leipzig 1865, ch. 17-20. James Walker, The theology and theologians of Scotland, Edinb. Clark 1872.
This positive development of Reformed dogmatics in a certain sense reached both its high point and its endpoint in the Canons of Dort, 1618/19; in the Westminster Confession and Catechism, 1646; in the *Consensus Helveticus*, 1675; and in the Walcheren Articles, 1693.
