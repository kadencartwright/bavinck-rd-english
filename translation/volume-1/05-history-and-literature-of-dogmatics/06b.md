No history yet exists of theology and dogmatics in the Eastern church after Damascene. The patristic age, in which the great formation of dogma took place, ended approximately with Justinian I, 527–565, or else with Photius, ca. 860. This whole period from the sixth to the ninth century is a time of transition. The icon controversy of 726–842 is its characteristic event, Harnack, _D. G._ II 452 f.; K. Schwarzlose, _Der Bilderstreit, ein Kampf der gr. K. um ihre Eigenart und um ihre Freiheit_, 1890. Relics and images had already been in use before the fifth century, but Christological dogma came to their support. What was distinctive about Christianity seemed to consist in its making the Divine sensuously and bodily present. The image soon changed from a symbol into a bearer and organ of the holy. Paganism returned within the Christian church. Yet the defense of images was connected precisely with the defense of the church’s freedom and with the religious interests then present. Over against it stood the imperial party, which opposed images but thereby also wished to subject the church to the state, wished to leave the determination of ecclesiastical dogma to the emperor, and by opposing images wished to accommodate Jews and Muslims. The whole of orthodoxy concentrated itself in the veneration of images. In the sensible people wished to possess and enjoy the Divine. John Damascene was one of the most forceful defenders of the veneration of images in his _de imaginibus orationes III_. He brings it into the closest connection with the incarnation of God in Christ and sees Judaism and Manichaeism in its rejection. The dogmatic justification of image veneration was the final labor of the church in the East. The Byzantine period, from the ninth century until the capture of Constantinople by the Turks in 1453, now begins. It is a time of repose, of powerlessness in production. The Greek church is the church of orthodoxy; it only preserves; Christological dogma is the dogma _par excellence_. Nevertheless, there was vigorous scholarly life until 1453. In Migne’s _Cursus patrologiae graecae_, the writings of the Byzantine theologians, from Damascene down to those who experienced the capture of Constantinople, form volumes 94–161. After Damascene, whose dogmatics remains a _norma_ to the present day, special mention from the Byzantine period is due above all to Photius, patriarch of Constantinople, 891. His principal work, Μυριοβιβλος or _Bibliotheca_, contains learned excerpts from all kinds of writers, and he appeared as a dogmatician in his Μυσταγωγια του ἁγιου πνευματος, ed. Hergenröther, Ratisb., 1857; cf. Gass in Herzog²; Hergenröther, _Photius, Patriarch v. K._, Regensburg, 1867–69. Next there is Euthymius Zigabenus in the twelfth century, who at the command of Emperor Alexius I wrote a Πανοπλια δογματικη της ὀρθοδοξου πιστεως ἠτοι ὁπλοθηκη δογματων; and Nicetas Choniates, ca. 1220, who supplemented the work of Euthymius in his Θησαυρος ὀρθοδοξιας, published in part; cf. Ullmann, _Nic. v. Meth., Euth. Zigab. und Nicetas Chon. oder die dogm. Entw. der gr. K. im 12 Jahrh._, _Stud. u. Kr._ 1883, 4tes Heft. Furthermore, the work of Nicolaus Kabasilas, περι της ἐν Χριστῳ ζωης λογοι ἑπτα, was published by Gass in 1849, and a treatise of Demetrius Kydonius, περι του καταφρονειν τον θανατον, by Kuinoel, Lips., 1786.
