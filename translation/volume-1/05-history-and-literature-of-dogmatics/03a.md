## 3.

The beginnings of theology among the Apologists, however, were not only weak but were also, in many respects, marred by one-sidedness and error. The problems that presented themselves were many and formidable; the relation of theology and philosophy, the doctrine of the Logos in His relation to God, the significance of Christ and His death, etc., were far too profound for correct insight to be attained all at once. Differences of opinion frequently emerged. With the rise of theology came differences of tendency and school. Two dogmatic tendencies soon became distinguishable.
On the one hand there was the tendency represented by Tertullianus (ed. by Oehler, Leipzig 1853; by Gersdorf, Bibl. patr. eccles. lat. vol. 4-7. Migne, ser. lat. t. 1-2), Cyprianus (ed. Gersdorf. vol. 2-3. Hartel, Vienna 3 vol. 1868-71. Migne ser. lat. t. 4), and Lactantius (ed. Gersdorf. vol. 10-11. Migne t. 6), and also by Irenaeus, of whom only the principal work *Ἐλεγχος και ἀνατροπη της ψευδωνυμου γνωσεως*, usually cited as *Adversus haereses libr. V*, has survived (ed. Stieren, Leipzig 1853. Migne ser. gr. t. 7), and by his pupil Hippolytus of Rome, probably the author of the *φιλοσοφουμενα ἠ κατα πασων αἱρεσεων ἐλεγχος*, which was first attributed to Origenes (ed. Duncker u. Schneidewin Gött. 1859. Migne, ser. gr. t. 10). All of these stand in an antithetical relation to philosophy. Irenaeus warns against it with great earnestness, adv. haer. II 25-28, and Tertullianus banishes it altogether and as sharply as possible, de praescr. 7, in the familiar passage: what have Athens and Jerusalem, the academy and the church, Christians and heretics in common, etc.? Yet they all make the most naive use of it in turn.
Tertullianus is therefore so important for theology because he introduced a number of *termini* for Trinitarian and Christological dogma, such as *trias, trinitas, satisfacere, meritum, sacramentum, una substantia* and *tres personae, duae substantiae in una persona*, etc. Yet in doing so they stand on the foundation of the church’s faith; their theology is historical, positive, and realistic, and between faith and theology, *πιστις* and *γνωσις*, they make no qualitative distinction, but at most a quantitative one, Iren. adv. haer. I 10, 3.
To be sure, there is as yet no dogmatic system among these men; the *dogmata* stand loosely beside one another, no definite principle can be found, and even in Christology Gnosticism was not wholly overcome by Tertullianus and Hippolytus. Nevertheless, the theology of these men, especially that of Irenaeus, became the theology of succeeding centuries. All the later *dogmata* can be found in him. The unity of God; the unity of essence between Father and Son and between the God of creation and the God of re-creation; the unity of the God of the Old Covenant and the God of the New Covenant; the creation of the world out of nothing; the unity of the human race; the origin of sin in free will; the two natures of Christ; the absolute revelation of God in Christ; the resurrection of all human beings, etc.—all these were clearly articulated and maintained by him over against Gnosticism.
With Irenaeus, Christianity first developed an independent theological science of its own, Harnack, Dogm. Gesch.
