## 12.

Scholasticism passes through three periods, an *aetas vetus, media*, and *nova*.
It begins with Anselm.
He still lives in the naive confidence that faith can be elevated to knowledge, and he attempts this for the existence of God in his *Monologium*, and for the incarnation and satisfaction in his *Cur Deus homo*.
He does not yet do so in the Aristotelian-scholastic form, but more in the manner of Plato’s dialogues; nevertheless, scholastic speculation begins with him.
In his *Sententiarum libri IV* Peter Lombard gave not individual treatises, as Anselm did, but a complete dogmatic and ethical handbook.
He supplied the text for scholastic theology and himself already made extensive use of philosophy to clarify and defend the truth.
Alexander of Hales wrote a *Summa universae theologiae*, which is actually already a commentary on the work of Lombard; but whereas Lombard reasons continuously through a subject to the end, Alexander clothes his thoughts in a strictly dialectical, syllogistic form.
With that, the scholastic method was permanently established.
This did not happen entirely without conflict.
Many objected to the use of Aristotle in theology.
There remained at all times Platonists who considered Plato much more in accord with the doctrine of the church.
John of Salisbury, Gerhoch, Walter of St. Victor, Peter the Chanter, Alan of Lille, William of Auvergne, et al., pointed to the dangers of philosophy; and Abelard seemed a deterrent example.
But the scholastic method, championed by renowned men, gained ground.
Before long the philosophy of Aristotle, though modified here and there, was considered the best defense of the doctrine of the church.
Dogmatics was developed most fully according to this method by Albert the Great † 1280, Thomas Aquinas † 1274, and Bonaventure † 1274.
All three wrote a commentary on the work of Lombard and were later followed in this by many.
Fleury already counted 244 in his day.
In addition, Albert wrote a *Summa theologiae* (unfinished) and a *Summa de creaturis*; Thomas a *Summa theologiae* (unfinished), and *Summa de veritate cath. fidei contra gentiles*; Bonaventure a *Breviloquium*.
All three brought scholasticism into general esteem, secured for theology a place of honor among the sciences, and treated the deepest problems with extraordinary intellectual power.
But scholasticism was unable to maintain itself at that height.
With Duns Scotus † 1308, confidence is already shaken.
He rejects nominalism but nevertheless contests Thomas everywhere he dares and is able.
The Franciscan de Rada † 1608 later counted no fewer than 86 points of dispute in his *Controversiae theol. inter Thomam et Scotum*, Colon. 1620.
The principal ones concerned the knowability of God, the distinction among the divine attributes, original sin, the merits of Christ, etc., and especially the immaculate conception of Mary.
Scotus is still a realist, but he is also skeptical and places theology and philosophy alongside each other, Werner, *Joh. Duns Scotus*, Wien 1881.
Philosophy does not reach God; theology rests solely on authority, on revelation.
But nominalism in particular contributed to the decline of scholasticism; it had already appeared with Roscelin and Berengar, but gained ground especially in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.
Peter Auriol † 1321, the author of a commentary on Lombard and of *Quodlibeta*, said that the *universalia* did not exist objectively in things but were only thoughts; the real is always individual.
William Durand of St. Pourçain † 1332 denied the *universalia* and denied the scientific character of theology: it is not a unity, it cannot demonstrate the truth of the *dogmata* or refute the grounds brought against them.
William of Ockham † 1349, who denied the pope all power over temporal princes, supported them in their resistance to the pope but in turn also asked them for protection, attacked both the school of Thomas and that of Scotus.
He took pleasure in demonstrating the uncertainty of theology; the existence, unity, and omnipotence of God, the finitude of the world, the immateriality of the soul, the necessity of revelation, etc.—all are indemonstrable.
Everything is only because God wills it so.
There are no truths of reason.
God could become man, but He could also become a stone.
Platonism and Augustinianism disappear from theology.
Everything becomes arbitrary.
Theology is swallowed up in skepticism.
Although realism generally remained dominant in the schools, it no longer possessed creative power; the form became more rigid, the language more barbarous, the method more subtle; subtlety replaced thoroughness, ostentation took the place of scientific seriousness, and dogmatics degenerated into endless dispute.
In addition, the nominalism of Ockham gained adherents: Adam Goddam, Armand de Beauvoir, Robert Holcot—from whom the saying reportedly originated that something can be true in theology and false in philosophy—John Buridan, Peter of Ailly, and Gabriel Biel † 1495, the last scholastic.
