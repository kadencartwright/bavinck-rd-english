## 19.

Once the Lutheran dogma had thus been completed, it was treated and developed in scholastic fashion in the seventeenth century. Heerbrand † 1600, Compendium theologiae 1573, considerably enlarged in 1578, and Hafenreffer, Loci Theologici certa methodo ac ratione in libros tres tributi 1603, had already made a beginning with this. Scholastic treatment was then continued by Leonhard Hutter † 1616, Compendium locorum theol. ex Scriptura sacra et libro Concordiae collectum 1610, Joh. Gerhard † 1637, Loci Communes theologici, 9 tomi 1610-22, best edition by Cotta 1762-87, reprinted Berlin-Leipzig 1864-75, and reached its culmination in Dannhauer † 1666, Hodosophia christiana; Hülsemann † 1635, Breviarium theologiae 1640; Calovius † 1686, Systema loc. theol. 1655-77; Quenstedt † 1688, Theologia didact-polem. 1685; Hollaz † 1713, Examen theol. acroamaticum 1707; König † 1664, Theol. positiva acroamatica 1664. The strength of this dogmatics lay in its objectivity. The *dogmata* lie ready to hand; the subject submits to them without criticism. They are only elaborated and applied exegetically, in terms of the history of dogma, polemically, scholastically, and practically. But already in the seventeenth century a reaction arose against this method. Philippism had not been overcome by the Formula Concordiae; it continued to have adherents, especially in Altdorf and Helmstadt. Georg Calixtus, professor at Helmstadt † 1656, came through his study of Aristotle, his acquaintance with Roman Catholic and Reformed theologians, and his aversion to scholastic orthodoxy to a moderate, irenic theology. In his works de praecipuis religionis christianae capitibus 1613, epitome theologiae 1619, de immortalitate animae et resurr. mort. 1627, he advocated a sharper separation of philosophy and theology and returned to the original Christianity of the first four centuries in order to seek, in what was common to all Christian confessions, a union of Lutherans, Reformed, and Roman Catholics.

## 20.

Calixtus naturally encountered much opposition because of his syncretism. But the age of objectivity passed away. In the eighteenth century the subject asserts itself, reclaims its rights, and rises in opposition to the power of the objective. In the Pietism of 1700 to 1730, subjective piety becomes the starting point, and the center of gravity is shifted from the object to the subject. Spener, the father of Pietism, 1635-1705, exercised an enormous influence through his person and through his works, including Pia desideria 1678, Allgemeine Gottesgelahrtheit aller gläubigen Christen und rechtschaffenen Theologen 1680, Tabulae Catecheticae 1683, Theolog. Bedenken 1712, and others. In Halle the principal Pietists were Francke † 1727, Breithaupt † 1732, Instit. theol. 1695. Freylinghausen † 1739, Grundlegung der Theologie 1703. Joachim Lange † 1744, Antibarbarus orthodoxiae 1709. Mosaisches Licht u. Recht, Rambach † 1735, Der wohlinformirte Katechet 1722. In Württemberg, with Hedinger † 1704, Bengel † 1752, and Oetinger † 1782, Pietism united with a biblical realism and with apocalyptic expectations. Orthodoxy naturally opposed this Pietism with great hostility, for example through Löscher in Dresden † 1749. But the time for orthodoxy had passed. In the years 1730-60, in Buddeus † 1729, Instit. theol. dogm. et mor.; Weismann † 1760; Crusius † 1775; J. G. Walch, Einleitung in die dogm. Gottesgelahrheit 1749; and his son Ch. W. F. Walch, Breviarium theol. dogm. 1775, it enters into an alliance with Pietism and passes over into an inwardly devout tendency that emphasizes the practice of faith, is averse to scholastic subtlety, is moderate in polemics, and devotes its energies chiefly to learned historical research. Herrnhutism is related to Pietism and was likewise a reaction of feeling against intellectual orthodoxy. Von Zinzendorf’s father was a Spenerian. But whereas Pietism seeks to lead to conversion through a *Busskampf*, Herrnhutism attempts to achieve this through the preaching of the dear Savior. It wishes to know nothing of law, but only of Gospel. Grace pushes nature so completely aside here that Jesus even replaces the Father: Jesus is the Creator, the Ruler, the Father, the Jehovah of the Old Testament. And under Roman Catholic influence, Zinzendorf conceived His person and suffering so pathologically that the affinity of mysticism and sensuality, especially in the first romantic period, 1743-1750, came very clearly to light. Rationalism runs parallel with Pietism. Both, each in its own way, impaired the authority of orthodoxy; both shift the center of gravity to the subject. Rationalism arose through the philosophy of Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz † 1716, and Wolff † 1754. They made clarity—mathematical clarity—the norm of truth. Carpovius, professor of *mathesis* at Weimar, attempted in his Oeconomia salutis N. T. seu theologia revelata, dogmatica methodo scientifica adornata 1737-65 to demonstrate church doctrine according to the mathematical method. Canz, Reusch, Schubert, Reinbeck, especially S. J. Baumgarten, Halle † 1757, Evangel. Glaubenslehre, edited by Semler 1759-60, and J. L. von Mosheim at Göttingen † 1755 belong to this Wolffian tendency. In the main these men were still orthodox, but the religious significance of truth is no longer felt; the doctrine of faith becomes an object of historical learning and rational demonstration. No wonder that other Wolffians, such as Töllner, Syst. der dogm. Theol.; Heilmann, Compendium theol. dogm. 1761; J. P. Miller, Instit. theol. dogm. 1767; and Seiler, Theol. dogm. polem. 1774, already adopt a freer attitude toward the church’s doctrine.
