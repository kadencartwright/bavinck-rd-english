Quite different was the attitude adopted by the Alexandrian theologians toward philosophy and Gnosticism. In various places, especially in Alexandria, toward the end of the second and the beginning of the third century, the endeavor arose to treat the Christian material scientifically and thus *mit dem Zeitbewusstsein zu vermitteln*, Harnack, *D. G.* I 549. The origin and founding of the catechetical school in Alexandria are unknown to us, Guericke, *De schola quae Alexandriae floruit catechetica* 1824. Vacherot, *Hist. crit. de l’école d’Alex.* 1846-51. Herzog² I 290-292; but it already existed around 190 and soon increased in esteem and influence. The first teacher known to us through his surviving writings is Clemens Alexandrinus. But he is overshadowed by Origenes, the most influential theologian of the early centuries. Their endeavor was to transform the church's doctrine of faith into a speculative science. They did indeed maintain the faith and, in distinction from the Gnostics, took their starting point in the positive doctrine of the church. Clemens even called faith a γνωσις συντομος and esteemed it more highly than pagan wisdom; Christianity is a way of salvation for all people and can be appropriated only by faith; the content of that faith has been summarized by the church in its confession, and the sole source of the knowledge of truth is revelation, Holy Scripture. All of this is maintained just as strongly by Clemens and Origenes as by Irenaeus and Tertullianus. But the difference begins here: the Alexandrians assumed a qualitative distinction between faith and science. Faith may be good and necessary for the simple; the educated do not find it sufficient. Theology must strive to develop the content of faith into a science that rests not on authority but finds its warrant and confirmation within itself. The *pistis* must be elevated to *gnosis*. Here *gnosis* is no longer a means for warding off and combating heresy but becomes the goal. The *pistis* leads only to a χριστιανισμος σωματικος, but theology must develop from Holy Scripture the χριστιανισμος πνευματικος. What Philo had attempted for the Jews, Clemens and Origenes undertook for the Christians; they continued the work of Justinus Martyr. To attain this goal, they naturally had to possess knowledge of and make use of philosophy; they did not attach themselves to one particular system, but made use of the whole of Greek philosophy since Socrates, especially that of Plato and the Stoa. And with its aid Origenes produced a system that undoubtedly testifies to an inspired vision and profound power of thought, but that also repeatedly runs the risk of allowing theology to be swallowed up in philosophy. The subordination of the Son, the eternity of creation, the preexistence of souls, the dualism of spirit and matter, earthly purification, and the restoration of all things are so many elements in Origenes's system that brought it into conflict with the faith of the church and later caused his condemnation. All of this was reconciled with Scripture by means of a pneumatic, allegorical exegesis. But in fact, in this theology of Origenes, the Christian religion is dissolved into ideas. It seeks an accommodation between church and world, faith and science, theology and philosophy, a compromise between the foolishness of the cross and the wisdom of the world, and is thus the finest and richest type of the *Vermittelungstheologie* that repeatedly arises in the church, Bigg, *The Christian Platonists of Alexandria.* Oxford 1886.
