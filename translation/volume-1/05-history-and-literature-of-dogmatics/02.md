## 2.

Theology, however, could not remain at this simple repetition and practical application of scriptural truth. The opposition that Christianity gradually encountered from pagan culture compelled reflection and defense. At first pagan power confined itself to persecution, Uhlhorn, der Kampf des Christ. mit dem Heid. 3e Aufl. Stuttgart 1879, and to the hatred, contempt, and mockery expressed by Tacitus, Ann. 15:44, and Lucianus in his Peregrinus Proteus, Th. Keim, Rom und das Christenthum, herausg. v. H. Ziegler 1881. Stud. u. Kr. 1851, S. 826 f. Herzog² 8, 772. But gradually the pagan world had to reckon with Christianity and began to attack it scientifically. In his Hellenismus und Christenthum, Kölln 1866, Heinrich Kellner expressly described this geistige Reaktion des antiken Heidenthums gegen das Christenthum and finally, S. 431 f., also pointed out its affinity with the attack on Christianity in the present age. The principal scientific opponents were Celsus (W. Th. Keim, Celsus’ wahres Wort. Zürich 1873. Origenes, contra Celsum. Bindemann, Zeits. f. d. histor. Theol. 1842, 2tes Heft S. 58 f. Baur, Kirch. Gesch. der 3 ersten Jahrh. 383 f. Herzog² 11, 100 f. Vigouroux, Les livres saints et la critique rationaliste, 3e ed. I 133 s.); Porphyrius (Baur, ib. 420 f. Jahrb. f. d. Theol. 1878. 2tes Heft S. 269 f. Herzog² 10, 524. Vigouroux, ib. I 156 s.); Fronto, the friend of Aurelius (as appears from Minucius Felix, Octavius 9, 31. Boissier, Les polémiques religieuses au second siècle, Revue des Deux Mondes 1 Jan. 1879, Herzog² 8, 772); and later still Julianus, who, as appears from the refutation of Cyrillus, contra Julianum, wrote a book against the Christians (C. J. Neumann, Juliani imperatoris librorum contra Christianos quae supersunt Lips. Teubner 1880, also translated into German, ib.). All the arguments later brought against Christianity throughout the centuries are already found in them: for example, against the authenticity and truth of many books of the Bible—the Pentateuch, Daniel, and the Gospels; against revelation and miracles; against various *dogmata*, such as the incarnation, satisfaction, forgiveness, resurrection, and the eternity of punishment; and against Christian morality, such as asceticism, contempt for the world, and lack of culture—not to mention the slanderous accusations of worshiping an ass’s head, infanticide, adultery, and so forth. Yet this scientific attack did not overcome Christianity either. The pagans therefore found themselves compelled either to revive the old religion, as Neopythagoreanism and Neoplatonism attempted, Zeller, Philos. der Griechen 3e Aufl. V 79 f. 419 f., or to mix and combine Christianity with paganism, as occurred in Gnosticism and Manichaeism. Gnosticism in particular was a powerful attempt to absorb Christianity into a combination and fusion of all kinds of pagan elements—Neoplatonic philosophy, Syrian and Phoenician mythology, Chaldean astrology, Persian dualism, and so forth—and thus to divest it of its absolute character. The principal question was how the human spirit had come into the bonds of matter and could now be freed from them. In Gnosticism, God is usually the abstract, undifferentiated unity. Matter, the cause of evil, cannot be explained from Him. It is derived from a lower god, the demiurge, who stands between the supreme God and the sensible world and is identified with the God of the Old Testament. To redeem the spirits bound in matter, various aeons proceed from God; they represent the different religions and attain their culmination in the aeon Christ. Christianity is therefore not the only religion but the highest. Christ, however, is understood docetically, for what matters is not fact and history but the idea. Consequently, the highest blessedness also consists in knowing; knowledge together with asceticism saves. The *pistis*, theology, may be good for the undeveloped; the *gnosis*, philosophy, is the highest and is the possession of the πνευματικοι. Through allegorical exegesis these ideas were brought into agreement with Scripture and were presented in forms and images borrowed from mythology and embellished by the imagination. They changed Christianity into a kind of *Religionsphilosophie*, a speculative philosophy that has exercised its influence through all the centuries, even into the systems of Hegel and Schelling, cf. Jacobi in Herzog² 5, 204 f. Harnack Dogm. I 186 f. Vigouroux, loc. cit., I 106 s., and the literature cited there.

Defense was necessary against these various attacks. Christians were compelled to reflect on the content of revelation and to set a true, Christian *gnosis* over against the false one. Revealed truth became the object of methodical, scientific thought. Theology arose, not from and for the church and for the training of her ministers, but in response to and for the purpose of repelling the attacks directed against Christianity. Naturally, knowledge of pagan philosophy was necessary for such intellectual activity; theology in fact arose with the aid of and in connection with philosophy. The Gnostics had already attempted this as well, but there was an essential difference between the manner in which the Gnostics and the manner in which the Apologists sought this connection. That difference does not consist in the former undertaking an *acute* and the latter an *allmählige Hellenisirung* of Christianity, Harnack, Dogmengesch. I 168 f. 413 f. Rather, among the Gnostics the positive, absolute content of the Christian religion was lost, whereas among the Apologists it was preserved; among the former the use of philosophy was material, among the latter chiefly formal; the former were therefore repudiated by the church, the latter acknowledged; the former presented the various philosophies as a religious process into which Christianity too was incorporated, while the latter attempted to demonstrate the Christian religion, which they acknowledged and accepted, as the highest truth, as the true philosophy that unites in itself all elements of truth found elsewhere. This last is in fact the fundamental thought of the Apologists, and they develop it as follows. God is one, ineffable, spiritual, and so forth, but through the Logos He is also creator of the world, the ultimate cause of all that exists, and the principle of all moral good. Gnostic dualism is overcome here; the world everywhere bears the stamp of the divine Logos, and matter too is good and created by God. The human being was originally created good, received reason and freedom, and was destined for ἀθανασια; he had to and could attain it by the way of free obedience. But he allowed himself to be led astray by the demons and has now come under the dominion of sensuality and fallen into error and death. Here too dualism is avoided, and the cause of sin is sought in the human will. But for that reason new means are now needed to bring the human being back from the way of falsehood and death and to lead him to immortality. From the earliest times God revealed Himself through the Logos and imparted knowledge of the truth even to some pagans, but above all to the prophets in Israel, and finally in His Son Jesus Christ. In Him all earlier truth is confirmed and completed. Through Him as teacher of the truth, the human being is brought back to his destination. The Apologists are therefore intellectualistic and moralistic. Yet in Justinus, for example, the effort is not absent to understand Christ also as Reconciler and Redeemer, through whose blood we receive forgiveness of sin.
