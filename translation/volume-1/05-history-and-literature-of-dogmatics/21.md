## 21.

When this rationalistic current was further nourished and strengthened from without by English Deism and French unbelief, the _Aufklärung_ arose in Germany after 1760 and sought to establish the common sense of the individual human being as ruler over all objective truth. Everywhere the positive, the traditional, and what had come to be historically had to yield to the rational, to what was clear to the understanding. Wolff and his associates had still found revelation reasonable. But the _Aufklärung_ was deistic and rationalistic. Frederick the Great was its king, Berlin its center, and the _Allgemeine Deutsche Bibliotheek_, 1765–1805, its organ. Dogmatics was treated in this rationalistic spirit by W. A. Teller, _Lehrbuch des christl. Glaubens_, 1764, _Religion der Vollkommneren_, 1792; Henke, _Lineamenta instit. fidei christ._, 1793; Eckermann, _Compend. theol._, 1791; and especially Wegscheider, _Instit. theol. christ. dogm._, 1815, 8e Aufl., 1844. Over against this Rationalism, orthodoxy assumed the weakened form of Supranaturalism. It no longer dared to take its position positively and thetically in faith, but shared with its opponents the foundation of reason. From there alone it still attempted to arrive at revelation, whose necessity, possibility, and actuality it defended. The content of revelation, however, gradually contracted under its treatment. The dogmas were freed as much as possible from everything offensive and made acceptable to reason by means of so-called biblical presentation. This standpoint was adopted by Doederlein, _Instit. theol._, 1780; Morus, _Epitome Theol. christ._, 1789; Knapp, _Vorles. über die christ. Glaub._, 1827; especially Reinhard, _Vorles. über die Dogmatik_, 1801; Storr, _Doctrinae christ. pars theoret._, 1793; and others. A reconciliation of Rationalism and Supranaturalism was attempted by Tzschirner, von Ammon, Schott, and especially Bretschneider, _Dogm. der ev. luth. Kirche_, 1814.
