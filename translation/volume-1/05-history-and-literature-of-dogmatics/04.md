## 4.

At the beginning of the third century the foundations of Christian theology had been laid. Over against paganism and Judaism, Gnosticism and Ebionitism, the church had consciously taken a firm position and preserved the independence of Christianity. But now all kinds of internal disputes arose in the third century. The great controversy of that century concerned the relation of the Logos (and the Spirit) to the Father, and the heresy that had to be opposed was Monarchianism in both its forms, dynamic and modalistic Monarchianism. The former—such as the Alogi, Theodotus and his party, Artemas and his associates, and especially in the East Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch from 260—sought to maintain the unity of God in such a way that they regarded the Son and the Spirit not as persons but as attributes and denied the deity of Jesus. Jesus was a man equipped in a special measure by the divine Logos and anointed with God’s Spirit. The modalistic Monarchians, however, taught that the Godhead itself had become flesh in Christ. They therefore acknowledged the deity of Christ but identified Father and Son, and so arrived at Patripassianism. This view was very widespread in the third century and found many adherents; it was defended and promoted by Noetus, Epigonus, Cleomenes, Aeschines, Praxeas, Victorinus, Zephyrinus, Callistus, and especially Sabellius. The Monarchians and antitrinitarians were opposed by Hippolytus in _Contra haeresin Noeti_ and _Philosophumena_, and also in the so-called _Parvus Labyrinthus_ (in Euseb., _h. e._ 5, 28); by Tertullian, _adv. Praxeam_; Novatian, _de trinitate_; Dionysius Alex., _adv. Sabellium_; Eusebius, _contra Marcellum_, _de eccles. theologia_, and _de fide ad Sabellium_. See Harnack, _D. G._ I 604–709, and art. “Monarch.” in Herzog² 10, 178; Dorner, _Gesch. d. Lehre v. d. P. Christi_, 2e Aufl., I 497 f.; Lange, _Gesch. u. Entw. der Systeme der Unitariër vor der nic. Synode_, 1831; Hagemann, _Die römische Kirche in den 3 ersten Jahrh._, 1864. By the end of the third century the dogma of Christ’s deity and of His distinction from the Father stood firm. There were three hypostases in the divine being: Father, Son, and Spirit. This was the faith in both East and West, Harnack, _D. G._ I 667, 709. The concepts with which thought would occupy itself in the following century—such as μονας, τριας, οὐσια, ὑποστασις, προσωπον, and so forth—already existed, but only later would they acquire their definite character and fixed value. The foundation had been laid, and the boundaries marked out within which Christian speculation would test its strength.
