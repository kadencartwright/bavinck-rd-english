## 23.

Another reaction against the _Aufklärung_ came from the side of feeling. Sentimentality was a characteristic of the second half of the eighteenth century. Just as in England Shaftesbury appeared against Hume and in France Rousseau against Voltaire, so too in Germany feeling asserted its rights over against cold Rationalism in men such as Hamann, Claudius, Lavater, Stilling, Herder, and Jacobi. They all proceed from immediate experience, from the power and inwardness of the heart. That is the deepest essence, the human in man; on it religion rests; from it faith springs; here man perceives the Divine. And from here he sees and discovers the Divine around him in nature, in history, and above all in Christ, the most human of all human beings. The content of faith differed among these men, all the more because they were averse to a system; but the ground of faith is the same in all. Feeling, faith, _Vernunft_, inspiration, _Begeisterung_, experience—or whatever one may call it—is for all of them the ground and norm of truth. Over against advancing criticism Jacobi pointed to the immediate sense in which another, higher objectivity opened up to man. He thus arrived at a consistent dualism of head and heart, philosophy and faith, the sensible and spiritual worlds. In dogmatics this dualism was applied by de Wette, _Bibl. Dogmatik_, 3e Aufl., 1831; _Dogm. der prot. Kirche_, 3e Aufl., 1840; _Ueber Religion u. Theol._, 2e Aufl., 1821; _Das Wezen des christ. Glaubens vom Standpunkt des Glaubens_, 1840; and after him by Hase, _Evang. Dogm._, 1826, 6e Aufl., 1870. Common to them all is the separation of believing and knowing, of the aesthetic (ethical, religious) and intellectual (empirical) worldviews, of the ideal and sensible worlds. The truth demanded by religious feeling or consciousness is invulnerable to the intellect; it belongs to another, imperceptible world.
