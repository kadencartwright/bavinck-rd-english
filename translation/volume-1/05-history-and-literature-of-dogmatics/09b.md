But philosophy is nevertheless insufficient. This is so not only because of reason's inability to find the way to truth, but especially because *superbia* stands in its way. And *humilitas* alone is the way to life. There is therefore another way to truth, namely, *auctoritas*, *fides*. Faith presupposes, on the one hand, some knowledge, but, on the other hand, seeks knowledge and strives after understanding. Augustinus attempted to prove from nature, and especially from man himself, not only the existence of God and the immortality of the soul but also the Trinity. But for him God is not the abstract, predicate-less being, but the living God, the highest truth and the highest good, the highest blessedness, and therefore the sole and full satisfaction of the human heart. All of Augustinus's thinking is religious, theological; he sees everything in God. In that light he also views the world; on the one hand it is a nonbeing, an image, perishable, but on the other hand, as God's creation, it is a work of art, created according to the ideas in God's consciousness, gradually, step by step and degree by degree, realizing those ideas and forming a unity that contains the richest diversity within itself; things differ from one another in their measure of being and thus of truth and goodness. It is a cosmos resting on idea and number, order and measure, held together by one will, one reason, an *amplissima inmensaque respublica*; within it miracles are only *contra quam est nota natura*, while sin is only a *privatio*, is compensated for by punishment, and also contributes to the beauty and harmony of the whole. In the *pulcherrimum carmen* of creation this antithesis too is necessary; sin is like the contrasts in a speech, the barbarisms in language, and the shadow in a painting. Augustinus attempts to incorporate evil into the order of the whole. But in doing so he does not excuse sin. He locates the end of things not in the ethically good, but in this: that creation is, and increasingly becomes, a harmonious revelation of all God's virtues and perfections. And sin too is made subservient to this by God's will. Furthermore, it is well known how deeply and seriously Augustinus conceived of sin. *Nondum considerasti, quanti ponderis sit peccatum.* He saw it around him and felt it: man seeks God and needs Him, and yet he cannot and will not come to Him. The only good still remaining in man is that he exists. Humanity is a *massa perditionis*. Sin is above all pride, *superbia* in the soul and *concupiscentia* in the body. In Adam sin was the deed of us all and therefore became the fate of us all. It is *carentia dei, privatio boni*, not only an act but a condition, *natura vitiata*, a *defectus, inopia, corruptio*, a *non posse non peccare*. The virtues of the pagans are *splendida vitia*. Rescue from that condition is possible only through the *gratia*, which already takes its beginning in the *praedestinatio*, which manifests itself objectively in the person and work of Christ, *certum propriumque fidei catholicae fundamentum*, but which must also come into us subjectively as *gratia interna* and must infuse faith and love into us. But in Augustinus this *gratia* works only within the bounds of the visible church. For him the church is an institution of salvation, dispenser of grace, seat of authority, guarantor of Scripture, dwelling place of love, foundation of the Spirit, indeed the *regnum Dei* itself. Augustinus deeply felt the importance of community for religion; the church is the mother of believers. The doctrine of *praedestinatio* and *gratia* cannot be reconciled with this concept of church and sacrament. *Multi qui foris videntur intus sunt et multi qui intus videntur foris sunt.* There are sheep outside and wolves inside the fold. Augustinus did indeed also teach the *perseverantia sanctorum*, but he did not dare affirm the subjective assurance of it. And precisely because of this conception of church and sacrament, faith and forgiveness could not come into their own in Augustinus's theology. Faith and love, forgiveness and sanctification, are not clearly distinguished. It is as though faith and forgiveness are merely provisional; Augustinus proceeds immediately from them to love, sanctification, and good works. Communion with God, the *religio*, thereby becomes the result of a process that faith, love, good works, etc., gradually bring about. Blessedness, eternal life, the *visio* and *fruitio Dei*, nevertheless become once again a fruit of merit, and asceticism is one of the means that enable man to attain this end.
