## 13.

A distinctive form of scholasticism was mysticism, which was formerly indeed regarded as hostile to scholasticism but is now better understood in its nature and character. The mystics never opposed scholastic theology; men such as Hugh and Richard of St. Victor treated various parts of theology in their treatises according to the same method as Peter Lombard, and conversely scholastics such as Alexander of Hales, Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, and Bonaventure also left behind many mystical writings. Mysticism was even incorporated into scholastic theology, Thomas S. Th. II, 2 qu. 179 sq. There is therefore no question of conflict and antagonism.
Added to this is the fact that the church and theology have at all times distinguished between true and false mysticism. Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, Eriugena, Amalric of Bena, Eckhart, Molinos, Böhme, etc., have always been condemned, but the writings of Pseudo-Dionysius, Albert the Great, Bonaventure, etc., have always been praised and approved by the Roman Catholic Church. There must therefore be a distinction between orthodox and pantheistic mysticism. The former, then, did not stand in hostility to scholasticism. But it was indeed distinct from it.
First, in method: scholasticism followed the analytical method of Aristotle and sought by reasoning to ascend from finite things to God; mysticism followed the synthetic method of Plato and sought, from the higher contemplation attained by the soul through grace, to gain insight into the truths of faith. Second, in origin: scholasticism arose especially as the writings of Aristotle became known and had as its object the *sententiae* of Peter Lombard; mysticism arose especially as the works of Pseudo-Dionysius became known, and these gained currency in the West through the translation of Eriugena. Third, in essence: scholasticism is the attempt to obtain, with the aid of philosophy, scientific knowledge of revealed truth; mystical theology had as its object the mystical communion with God granted to a privileged few by special grace, and described how and by what path the soul could attain it and what light that communion with God could shed upon the truths of faith.
Mysticism in this sense has had its representatives in the Christian church at all times and occurs to a greater or lesser degree in all the church fathers; it is most closely connected with the monastic ideal. It proceeds from the assumption that there is not only a knowledge of God through the intellect, but also an experience, inward apprehension, and communion with God through the heart. In the Middle Ages it aligned itself especially with Augustine, who had first plumbed the depth of the life of the soul and rendered it in inimitable language, and with Pseudo-Dionysius, who had marked out the stages and milestones along which the soul could ascend from finitude to the infinite God. Through practical exercises such as asceticism, purification, self-mortification, flight from the world, etc., or also through theoretical reflection such as *auditio, lectio, oratio, cogitatio, consideratio, meditatio*, the soul can already here on earth attain a state of beholding or enjoying God. Mysticism is conceived and described in this way in various works of Bernard of Clairvaux, Hugh and Richard of St. Victor, Bonaventure, Thomas Aquinas, Gerson, and Thomas à Kempis.
But it was natural that mysticism, by thus placing the emphasis on contemplation, came to disparage knowledge; in the enjoyment of the heart, clarity of consciousness and the value of knowledge were lost.
