### B. Dogmatics in the Eastern Church

## 5.

In the East, the period from the fourth to the eighth century is occupied entirely by the Christological controversies. The _homoousia_ with the Father of the Son who became man in Christ was the dogma _par excellence_. The religious interest at stake was that God Himself had to become man so that we human beings might be delivered from death, led to immortality and the vision of God, and made partakers of the divine nature. The deity of Christ is the essence of Christianity. No one understood this better than Athanasius. His history is the history of his century. For him the whole of Christianity is concentrated in deliverance unto eternal life through the true Son of God. In this he maintains the distinctive character of the Christian religion, frees the doctrine of the Trinity from the cosmological speculations still connected with it in Origen and Tertullian, and preserves Christianity from secularization, Harnack II 21–27, 204 f. Athanasius is a Christologist. He feels deeply the religious importance of the deity of Christ. Christ had to be God in order to be our Savior. Because of this wholly unique significance of Christological dogma, no dogmatic systems proper appear. There are, however, a number of important dogmatic treatises. Arianism is opposed by Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, in his _Epistolae de Ariana haeresi deque Arii depositione_; by Athanasius in all his writings, especially in his _Orationes contra Arianos_; by Basil in his _Libri V adv. Eunomium_ and his _Liber de Spiritu Sancto_; by Gregory Naz. in his _Orationes V de theologia_; by Gregory Nyss., _Libri s. orationes XII c. Eunomium_; by Cyril, Hilary, Ambrose, Fulgentius, and others; and it was condemned at the synods of Nicaea and Constantinople. The deity and _homoousia_ of the Holy Spirit were defended against Macedonius of Constantinople by Athanasius in his _Epistolae ad Serapionem_; by Basil in his work against Eunomius and in his treatise _de Spiritu Sancto_; by Greg. Naz. in several of his _Orationes theologicae_; by Greg. Nyss. in his _adv. pneumatomachos Macedonianos_; and especially by Didymus in his works _de Trinitate libri tres_ and _de Spiritu Sancto_; and they were established at the Synod of Constantinople in 381. Even during the question concerning the _homoousia_ of the Son, a second question arose concerning His human nature and its union with the divine nature. Apollinaris acknowledged that the Savior had to be God, but He could not be a complete man, for then there would be two beings and two persons and no unity would result. The Logos therefore assumed animate σαρξ and Himself formed within it the πνευμα, the ego, the principle of self-consciousness and self-determination. But he was opposed by Athanasius, _de incarnatione domini nostri J. C. contra Apollinarem_ and _de salutari adventu J. C._; by Greg. Naz. in his _Epistolae ad Cledonium_; and by Greg. Nyss. in his _Antirrheticus adv. Apollinarem_; and he was condemned at the Synod of Rome in 377 and at Constantinople in 381. Once the two natures had been established, disagreement arose concerning the nature of their union. Not substantial and essential but moral and relative, said Nestorius; there are two persons, ὑποστασεις, in Christ. But he found a vigorous opponent in Cyril of Alexandria, who attacked him in various works, _de incarnatione Unigeniti_, _adversus Nestorii blasphemias contradictionum Libri V_, and others; and he was condemned at the Synod of Ephesus in 431. The diametrically opposite opinion of Eutyches was opposed by Theodoret in his _Ερανιστης ἠ πολυμορφος_ and by Leo the Great in his _Epistola ad Flavianum_, and it was condemned at the Synod of Chalcedon in 451.
