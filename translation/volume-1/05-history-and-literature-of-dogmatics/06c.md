After the capture of Constantinople by the Turks, the Greek church in the East would have been wholly destroyed or reduced to a sect had it not found support in the Russian Empire, which was Christianized in the tenth century and adopted the Greek Orthodox faith in its entirety and without criticism. We know even less about the theological literature after that time than about that of the earlier period. Migne’s edition goes no further and fails us here. A survey of names and works is given in _Νεοελλενικη φιλολογια, συγγραμμα Κωνσταντινου Σαθα_, Athens, 1868. The attempts at union with Rome at the councils of Lyon in 1274 and Florence in 1439 are known to us from the acts. The correspondence of the Tübingen theologians in 1576 with Patriarch Jeremias II was published at Wittenberg in 1584, Gass, _Symb. der gr. K._ 45 f.; concerning that of Cyrillus Lucaris with many Protestant theologians and statesmen, see Gass, art. “Lucaris” in Herzog² 9, 5 f.; Kattenbusch 141 f. The history of this literature cannot be summed up in a single word such as petrification, orthodoxism, and so forth. The center of gravity of the Greek church has shifted to Russia, and Russia is still young, has as yet no past, and is only now emerging. Its literary and scientific life has only just begun.
