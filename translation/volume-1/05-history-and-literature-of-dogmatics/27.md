## 27.

It was natural that ecclesiastical orthodoxy should resist this manifold mingling of theology and philosophy. A theology of separation inevitably had to follow the theology of mediation. The study of the confessions and the historical dogmatics of de Wette, Bretschneider, Hase, Schmid, and Schneckenburger benefited confessional consciousness. _Altlutheraner_ such as Guericke and Rudelbach; Neo-Lutherans such as Harms, Hengstenberg, Keil, Philippi, Vilmar, Kliefoth, Höfling, Thomasius, and Hofmann; men of the positive Union such as Kahnis, Luthardt, Zöckler, H. Schmidt, Frank, Grau, and others labored for the restoration of the old Lutheran theology. Yet they too were children of their age. Pietism continued to exert influence in men such as Hengstenberg and Tholuck. Vilmar, Löhe, Münchmeyer, and others were not free from Roman Catholic exaggeration in their conception of church, sacrament, and office. The South German theologians at Erlangen, Thomasius, Hofmann, and also the present positive theologians stand more freely over against the confession and depart on important points—Christology, satisfaction, Scripture—from old Lutheran dogmatics. Even so orthodox a Lutheran as Philippi, like Hofmann and Frank, proceeds from subjective faith-consciousness and has thus borrowed his starting point from Schleiermacher. Alongside these confessional theologians stand the biblical theologians, foremost among them Beck, who wish to build the dogmatic system not from believing consciousness or even from the confession, but from Scripture alone. But Beck conceived Scripture in the sense of that mystical theosophy practiced in Swabia since Oetinger and Michael Hahn: as a system of heavenly truths that were impressions of heavenly powers and that implanted these powers into the human soul through the operation of the Holy Spirit. He thereby frequently arrived at a rather arbitrary exegesis and at a system that, though profound and original, could not be acquitted of one-sided asceticism and individualism.
