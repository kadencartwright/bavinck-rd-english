## 17.

In the nineteenth century too, rationalism at first continued to prevail in many Roman Catholic circles. In Germany there was a large party that wished to bring the organization and doctrine of the Roman Catholic Church into accord with the demands of the modern age: Dalberg † 1817, Wessenberg † 1860, Werkmeister, and others. Hermes, professor at Bonn † 1831, Einleitung in die Christkath. Theologie 1819, ’29, Christkath. Dogm. 1834, ’36, attempted to ground revelation and authority on rational foundations and, in judging what revelation is, granted reason the same rights that Wolffian philosophy had granted it. At first Hermes had many followers: Achterfeldt, who published Hermes’s dogmatics; Braun; von Droste-Hülfshof, professor of law at Bonn; and Spiegel, archbishop of Cologne. But after Pope Gregory XVI condemned Hermesianism on 26 Sept. 1835, his influence declined.
Anton Günther † 1863 in Vienna, Vorschule zur specul. Theol. 1828. Peregrius Gastmahl 1830 u. s. w., aligned himself with Hegel’s proposition that philosophy and speculative theology are actually one. There are not two kinds of truth and certainty. Rather, faith is the beginning and presupposition of all knowledge, and every faith, including faith in revelation, can pass over into knowledge and attain evident certainty. Günther too had many followers—Pabst, Merten, Veith, Gaugauf, Baltzer, Knoodt—but he was condemned in 1857.
Franz von Baader † 1841, Vorlesungen über specul. Dogmatik, Sämmtliche schriften, Leipzig 1850-57, in 15 volumes, under the influence of Böhme and Schelling, sought salvation not in a return to the old but in its new development, in the renewal of the *dogmata*, and wished by a theosophical path to raise faith to knowledge. His followers were Schaden, Lutterbek, Hoffmann, Hamberger, Sengler, and Schlüter, but Baader was censured because of his opposition to the primacy. J. Frohschammer, Einleitung in die Philosophie und Grundriss der Metaphysik 1858, etc., rejected both scholastic and idealistic philosophy and attempted to build metaphysics and theology not upon abstract reason but upon the concrete, universal, historical fact of God-consciousness in humanity. For him, theology and philosophy therefore coincide in content; they differ only in method. The natural and the supernatural cannot be strictly separated. Pius IX condemned this philosophy in a letter to the archbishop of Munich on 11 Dec. 1862. Frohschammer’s works were placed on the Index, and he himself was suspended in 1863. He did not submit, however, and continued in numerous writings to contend for the freedom of scholarship and against the claims of the pope. Thus Rome on the one hand opposed the independence of scholarship, but on the other hand nevertheless acknowledged its relative freedom.
After the Revolution, traditionalism arose in France, namely, the doctrine that the higher metaphysical truths cannot be found by reason but are obtained only from revelation, which from the first human being onward has been propagated in humanity by tradition and is preserved in language. This theory was ably defended by de Bonald, Recherches philos. sur les premiers objets des connaissances morales, Paris 1817; Lamennais, Essai sur l’indifférence en matière de religion, Paris 1817; and Bautain, de l’enseignement de la philos. en France au 19e siècle, 1833, Philos. du Christianisme, 1835. But it could find no favor in Rome. In 1840 Bautain signed six theses that were put before him and recanted his doctrine. Likewise rejected was the ontologism of Gerdil † 1802, Gioberti † 1852, Rosmini † 1855, Gratry † 1872, Ubaghs, and others, which returned to the idealism of Malebranche and derived all higher truth from the immediate intuition of God and the ideas.

All these condemnations prove that, after the rationalism of the preceding century, Rome became increasingly self-conscious and awakened. After the Revolution there was also a revival of the Roman Catholic Church and theology. Romanticism worked to Rome’s advantage and made many converts: Winckelmann, Stolberg, Schlegel, Adam Müller, Zacharias Werner, Schlosser, Haller, etc. In France a reaction against the Revolution and unbelief arose through Chateaubriand, Génie du christianisme; Joseph de Maistre † 1821; Bonald; and Lamennais. Puseyism or Tractarianism, which began under Pusey and Newman at Oxford in 1833, led many into the Roman Catholic Church and strengthened the High Church, ritualistic, and Romanizing tendency in the Episcopal Church. The believing theology that arose in Germany at first aligned itself with Schleiermacher on many points. His doctrines of Scripture, regeneration, justification, and the church contained many elements that Roman Catholics could use to their advantage. And this was indeed done with talent and zeal by Görres, Baader, Phillips, and Döllinger in Munich; Klee in Bonn; Möhler, Hirscher, and Drey in Munich and Tübingen; Staudenmaier and Kuhn in Giessen; and others. The men of this tendency were not yet entirely to the liking of Rome and Jesuitism. They all still strove for mediation; they sought a reconciliation of faith and knowledge; they attempted by the speculative method to prove the *dogmata*; and they were far too liberal toward Protestants and occasionally made important concessions. But they had outstanding spokesmen and contributed greatly to the revival of Roman Catholic theology.

Yet in the long run this conciliatory and mediating tendency did not prove satisfactory. Gradually the neo-scholastic tendency arose. In 1814 the Jesuit order was restored, and its influence on the papacy increased more and more; its power spread through all countries. It opposed “freedom of scholarship” with all its might. All the condemnations mentioned above culminated in the famous encyclical of 8 Dec. 1864 and in the *Vaticanum* of 1870, in which the infallibility of the pope was declared. In Italy this neo-scholastic tendency was advocated especially by the philosopher Sanseverino, Philosophia christiana, 7 vol. ed. nov. Neap. 1878, and by the theologian J. Perrone, Praelectiones theologiae, 9 vol. 1838-43; in Germany by J. Kleutgen, Theol. der Vorzeit, 5 Th. 2e Aufl., Münster 1867, and Philos. der Vorzeit, 2 Th., Innsbr. 1878, and by A. Stöckl in various philosophical works. The present pope set his seal upon it on 4 Aug. 1879 by recommending the study of Thomas Aquinas in his encyclical *Aeterni Patris*. Since then there has been a powerful and general effort to restore the authority of Thomas Aquinas in every field of scholarship. Political and legal theory, psychology and ethics, theology and philosophy are studied in his spirit. In that same spirit dogmatics was treated by Franzelin, Scheeben, Heinrich, Bautz, etc.; and in the Netherlands by G. M. Jansen, professor at Rijsenburg, Praelect. theol. fundam. Ultraj. 1875-76, Theol. dogm. spec. 1877-79. See further literature in Kihn, Enc. u. Meth. der Theol., Freiburg 1892 S. 412 f.
