## 26.

While Hegel’s philosophy thus led to the rejection of Christianity, it became known especially after 1830 that Schelling had gradually abandoned the system of identity and was now developing a positive philosophy in which not necessity but freedom reigned and in which will and deed took the place of the logical process. Just as Hamann, Lavater, and others had in the previous century, so Schelling gradually came to the conviction that there is another, deeper being and life than that of the logical understanding, namely, that of will, deed, and freedom. But all life is becoming, an interaction of opposites. So it is in God, and so it is in the world. In God there is first the dark ground of nature, then the understanding, and from both the will is born. In the world there is first chaos, then spirit, and thereafter the cosmos. And so in religion there is first the natural impulse in paganism, then the Word or the Light in Christ, and finally the being of God in all. Under the influence of Böhme and Oetinger, Schelling connected all kinds of theosophical speculations with this. Theosophy is always occupied with two problems: the transition between God and world and that between soul and body. The first problem is solved by seeking the ground of the world in the nature of God and by identifying, more or less—or at least placing in parallel—the theogony, the Trinitarian process in God, with the cosmogony. God Himself first comes to the full unfolding of His being in and through the world-process. The second problem is solved in the same way by the idea of *Geistleiblichkeit*. The spiritual—God, the soul—is not incorporeal in the strict sense; it is indeed not material, but *übermateriell*, and so the spirit in turn has the task of *vergeistigen* and *vergeistlichen* the body, the world, whose coarse materiality is a consequence of sin. This theosophical speculation of Schelling found acceptance in theology: Baader, Görres, Windischmann, J. F. von Meyer, Steffens, Wagner, Stahl, Rothe, Hamberger, Fr. Hoffmann, Keerl, Osiander, Lange, Delitzsch, Bähr, Kurtz, and Splittgerber all underwent its influence to a greater or lesser degree.
