## 24.

Closely related to this current is the theology of Schleiermacher. In his _Reden über die Religion_, 1799, and _Monologen_, 1800, he stands wholly under the influence of Romanticism. Religion is feeling: feeling for the universe, a sense for the infinite, the orientation of the heart toward the eternal, like Spinoza’s _cognitio Dei intuitiva_. Thus Schleiermacher too takes his standpoint in the subject, but not in the intellect or the will—in feeling. For him God is the unity of the world, not an object of thought, for thought always moves in oppositions, but something to be enjoyed only in feeling. And this enjoyment of God in feeling is religion. But Schleiermacher immediately connects with this the idea of community. From the essence of religion springs the longing for community, for the exchange of what has been enjoyed. Religions differ according to how the infinite differently determines feeling and differently forms the community. In his _Glaubenslehre_ the fundamental philosophical ideas are the same, but here feeling is defined more precisely as absolute dependence, God is conceived as absolute causality, and Christianity is defined as an ethical religion in which everything stands in relation to redemption through Christ. _Dogmata_ therefore are and remain descriptions of subjective states of the heart, but nevertheless of such states as are determined by the Christian community and thus by the person of Christ. Through these three ideas—immediate self-consciousness as the source of religion, community as the necessary form of religion’s existence, and the person of Christ as the center of Christianity—Schleiermacher became an influence beyond calculation. All theology after him is dependent on him. No one adopted his dogmatics, and yet he made his influence felt in every current—liberal, mediating, and confessional—and in every church—Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed. Most closely related to him are the so-called _Vermittelungstheologen_: Nitzsch, Twesten, Neander, J. Müller, Rothe, Dorner, Martensen, Schenkel, and others. What is distinctive and common to all these men is their subjective starting point. They all proceed not from an external authority but from the heart, from religious consciousness. At first they accept truth as the content of subjective Christian experience. But they do not stop there. They wish to advance beyond Schleiermacher and to this end combine his subjective religious starting point with Hegelian speculation. The agnostic elements in Schleiermacher, which he shared with Kant, were exchanged for the Hegelian doctrine of the knowability of the Absolute. Truth, first accepted as the content of religious experience, then had to be comprehended by way of speculation as necessary to thought and legitimated before the tribunal of philosophy. Thus faith and science, the natural and the positive, church and culture, scriptural authority and criticism, ancient and modern worldviews would be reconciled and brought into harmony.
