## 10.

Augustinianism was the object of fierce controversy for more than a century and kept minds divided. It met opposition not only from Pelagius, Coelestius, Julianus, and the Pelagians proper, but also from many monks in Gaul, among whom the following are especially named: John Cassian; Vincent of Lérins, who in his Commonitorium not only identifies the marks of tradition but at the end also takes a stand against strict Augustinianism; Eucherius of Lyon; Hilary of Arles; Salvian of Marseille; Faustus of Riez; and Gennadius of Massilia, author of de fide sua ceu de dogmatibus ecclesiasticis in 88 capita. On Augustine’s side stood, besides Possidius of Calama in Numidia, Orosius of Braga in Spain, Marius Mercator in Constantinople, and others, especially Prosper of Aquitaine, Vigilius of Thapsus in Numidia, Fulgentius of Ruspe, author of de fide ad Petrum seu de regula verae fidei, a brief outline of the principal truths of faith, Caesarius of Arles, Avitus of Vienne, and others. The Synod at Orange in 529 did reach a certain decision in favor of Augustine, but it did not prevent semi-Pelagian ideas from gaining increasing acceptance. The *gratia praeveniens* was accepted, but the *gratia irresistibilis* and particular predestination were nevertheless not decisively adopted, Wiggers. Versuch einer pragm. Darstellung des August. u. Pelag. Hamburg, Perthes, 2 Theile. 1833. Id. Zeits. f. d. hist. Theol. 1854-1859 treats the same controversy from Gregory to Gottschalk. Harnack, D. G. III 219 f. Subsequently little remained of Augustinianism. Pope Gregory the Great † 604, who alongside Augustine, Jerome, and Ambrose is indeed called the fourth great teacher of the church, produced nothing new but appropriated the ideas of the earlier church teachers and applied them to life in many ways. His tendency is practical and at the same time mystical-allegorical. He formed no system but simply preserved what had been attained, made the *dogmata* serviceable for clergy and laity, and above all systematized the various mediators and means—angels, saints, Christ, alms, masses for souls, purgatory, and penance—that make it possible for the weakened human will to be freed from the punishments of sin. Through all this he labored with faithful care in the education of the new peoples and the formation of the clergy. He sanctioned the external, legal religion of the Roman church and gave medieval Catholicism its distinctive type. He is the keystone of the old world and the foundation stone of the new. Through his liturgical writings and his ecclesiastical chant he introduced Roman worship among the Germanic peoples. By popularizing the *dogmata* of the church fathers, he made the church’s doctrine practically usable for the uncultured, pagan Germanic peoples and promoted superstition, asceticism, and works-righteousness. Together with Boethius and Cassiodorus, he exercised great influence on the formation and rise of learning among the Germanic peoples. Cassiodorus † ± 565 wrote a Liber de artibus ac disciplinis liberalium litterarum, in which he discussed the significance of each of the seven liberal arts, and in his work de institutione divinarum litterarum gave a methodology of theological study. Through his translations and explanations of the logic of Aristotle and the Isagoge of Porphyry, Boethius introduced the knowledge and use of Greek philosophy among the Germanic peoples. And Gregory transferred theology in the church to the Germanic peoples, Harnack, D. G. III 233-244. Lau, Gregor. I nach seinem Leben und seiner Lehre geschildert, Leipzig 1845, art. in Herzog². Alzog, Patrologie 500.

Given the complete lack of culture and the turmoil of the migrations, there could at first be no question of a scientific life among the Germanic peoples. The first traces are found in the Bible translation and the Arian confession of faith of Ulfilas † 383. By the end of the fifth century the Ostrogoths and Visigoths, Vandals, Suebi, Burgundians, Heruli, Lombards, and others had already been Christianized in the Arian sense. But Clovis 481-511, together with his Frankish kingdom, adopted Roman Christianity. Patrick, author of Confessiones, is the apostle of the Irish † 465. Scotland was Christianized by Columba † 597. The Anglo-Saxons were converted by Augustine with forty monks, sent there by Pope Gregory I in 596. Fridolin, Columbanus † 615, and others labored in France and Italy; the latter left important letters and also a regula coenobialis. Alemannia, Bavaria, Thuringia, Friesland, and other lands were Christianized in the sixth and seventh centuries. Boniface is the apostle of the Germans in the eighth century † 755, J. P. Muller, Bonif. eene kerkhist. studie, Amst. 1869. The Saxons were brought in last, under Charlemagne through the wars of 772-804, and the North chiefly through Ansgar † 865. One of the first dogmaticians is Isidore of Seville † 636. His writings have grammatical, historical, archaeological, dogmatic, moral, and ascetic contents and encompass all that could be known at that time. He transmits classical and patristic learning to his people. He is not original but gives extracts from pagan and Christian works. In his Originum sive etymologiarum libri XX, in book 6 he speaks of Scripture; in book 7, of God, the angels, prophets, apostles, clerics, and believers; in book 8, of the church; and in book 9, of the peoples. His Libri III Sententiarum s. de summo bono is excerpted chiefly from Augustine and Gregory and served as a model for the medieval collectors of sentences. Book I treats God, creation, time, world, sin, angel, the human being, soul, Christ, Holy Spirit, church, heresy, law, Scripture, Old and New Covenants, prayer, baptism, martyrdom, miracles, antichrist, and the end of the world; books II and III have ethical contents. It is a compendium that transmits the theological capital of the preceding centuries to the Germanic people. But it did not attain an independent elaboration. Charlemagne did attempt to introduce the ancient culture into the Frankish kingdom by force. And indeed, the Carolingian period did not lack men of great learning, but diligent collection and dependent reproduction nevertheless remain the characteristic features of the period that begins with the seventh century and ends only with the Crusades. Augustine and Gregory were the authorities. Chief among these Carolingian theologians was Alcuin † 804, who opposed the adoptionism of Elipandus of Toledo and Felix of Urgel in Liber contra haeresin Felicis, Libri VII contra Felicem, and Libri IV adv. Elipandum, and furthermore wrote de fide sanctae et individuae Trinitatis Libri III, de Trinitate ad Fredegisum quaestiones, and Libellus de processione Sp. Sⁱ. In all these works Alcuin displays his familiarity with the works of the church fathers; he refutes the Adoptionist errors with the same arguments formerly used against Nestorianism, for example, by Cyril. The study of Augustine led Gottschalk in the ninth century to confess the *gemina praedestinatio*. He found support from Prudentius of Troyes, Remigius of Lyon, Ratramnus, Lupus of Ferrières, and others, but was at the same time fiercely opposed by Rabanus, Hincmar, and Eriugena. The *filioque* came from Spain into the Frankish kingdom and was included in the creed. Already at the Synod at Gentilly 767 there was the conviction that it was creedal. It was ably defended by Charlemagne’s theologians, Alcuinus de processione Sp. Sⁱ., Theodulf of Orléans de Spiritu S°. The Synod at Aachen 809 decided that the *filioque* belonged in the creed. The veneration of images met opposition in the Frankish kingdom. The Seventh Ecumenical Synod, which required *servitium* and *adoratio* of the images, was not recognized, but after the ninth century the opposition gradually fell silent. Finally, in the Carolingian period the Mass was also developed further, especially by Paschasius Radbertus, liber de corpore et sanguine domini 831, who was opposed by Rabanus and Ratramnus. Radbertus is also known as the author of a compendium de fide, spe et caritate, which gathers truths of faith into a certain whole. Especially deserving of mention in this period is John Scotus Eriugena † ± 891, although he belongs more properly to philosophy than to theology. He is not the father of scholasticism but of speculative theology. He follows the *gnosis* of Origen and the mysticism of Pseudo-Dionysius. His fundamental idea is the Neoplatonic doctrine of emanation. In his work de divisione naturae he first says that theology and philosophy are in fact one. The *recta ratio* and the *vera auctoritas* do not conflict. The *fides* has its truth, as *theologia καταφατικη, affirmativa*, in Scripture and tradition, but reason, as *theologia negativa, ἀποφατικη*, strips this truth of its coverings and traces the idea within it. Thus he changes dogmatic truth into a cosmic and theogonic process. He comprehends all that exists under one concept, *natura*, which manifests itself in 4 Stufen of being, through the Logos, in the phenomenal world and returns again to God.
