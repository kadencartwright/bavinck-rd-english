## 38.

For the history of dogmatics in America one may consult, among other works, _Encyclopedia of living divines and Christian Workers of all denominations in Europe and America. Being a supplement to Schaff-Herzog, Encyclopedia of religious knowledge. Edited by Rev. Ph. Schaff and Rev. S. M. Jackson_, New York, 1887; Adolf Zahn, _Abriss einer Gesch. der Evang. Kirche in Amerika im 19 Jahrh._, Stuttgart, 1889; Fr. Nippold, _Amerik. Kirchengeschichte seit der Unabhängigkeitserklärung der Ver. Staaten_, Berlin, 1892. All the churches were successively transplanted to America and Canada from England and other European countries. The Episcopal Church is the oldest and wealthiest and dates already from the immigration into Virginia in 1607. The Dutch Reformed Church was established there after the discovery of the Hudson and Manhattan Island in 1609. The Independents or Congregationalists first landed at Plymouth in 1620. The Quakers were led to Pennsylvania by William Penn in 1680. Through Roger Williams the Baptists gained a foothold in America on Rhode Island in 1639. The Methodists gained entrance through Wesley in 1735 and Whitefield in 1738. The German churches, both Lutheran and Reformed, were established there from the middle of the previous century. The Presbyterian churches are divided there into various groups. Prof. Schaff gives an outline of Presbyterian theology in his _Theological Propaedeutic_, 2 parts, New York, 1892/93, II, pp. 374–405, and in _The Independent_, New York, vol. 45, nos. 2321, 2324, 2329, and 2330. For Congregationalism see _Congregationalists in America_, ed. by Dr. Albert E. Dunning, New York, Hill and Co., 1894. Almost all these churches and currents within churches were Calvinistic in origin. Of all religious movements, Calvinism was the most powerful in America. It was not limited to one church or another, but under all kinds of modifications was the animating element in Congregational, Baptist, Presbyterian, Dutch and German Reformed churches, and others. It was brought to America from every side—from England, Scotland, France, Holland, and Germany. It formed the character of New England during the colonial period, 1620–1776. A distinction must, however, be made between Puritan Calvinism, which came chiefly from England and gained entrance into New England, and Presbyterian Calvinism, which was introduced from Scotland into the Southern, Middle, and Western States. Both forms of Calvinism had the Westminster Confession of 1647 as their basis, but in both a struggle soon arose between an old and a new school. The first and foremost theologian of New England was Jonathan Edwards, 1703–1758; art. in Herzog²; biography by Prof. A. V. G. Allen, published by Houghton, Miffin and Comp., New York, first volume of _American religious leaders_. His works were published in four volumes at New York by Carter and Brothers in 1881. He combined profound metaphysical power of thought with earnest piety. In 1734, even before Wesley’s arrival in America, a remarkable awakening took place in his congregation at Northampton; and later, with his friend Whitefield, he repeatedly led and defended similar revivals. Theologically he waged battle chiefly against Arminianism, which penetrated New England through the writings of Daniel Whitby and John Taylor. Through his metaphysical and ethical speculations he attempted to strengthen Calvinism, but in fact weakened it through his distinction between natural and moral inability. He became the father of the Edwardeans, New-Theology Men, or New-Lights, as they are called, who indeed maintained the Calvinistic doctrine of God’s sovereignty and election but combined this with the rejection of original guilt and the universality of the atonement, just as the Saumur theologians had done in France. In the doctrine of satisfaction, his son Jonathan Edwards, 1745–1801, presented in substance the theory of Grotius. Samuel Hopkins, a pupil of Edwards, 1721–1803, whose works were published at Boston by Prof. Park of Andover in 1852, wrote a system of theology in which he reproduced Edwards’s system and especially developed disinterested love for God in the sense of Fénelon and Madame Guyon. Nathaniel Emmons, 1745–1840, _Works_, Boston, 1842, was one of the principal defenders of Hopkinsianism. In Timothy Dwight, 1752–1817, and Nathaniel W. Taylor, 1786–1858, the system of Edwards was modified in a Pelagian direction. And in the present day, at Andover under the leadership of Dr. Egbert C. Smyth, professor of church history, it led to the defense of a progressive orthodoxy and to the doctrine of _the future probation_. The Old School in New England theology was represented especially by Dr. Bennet Tyler, 1783–1858, and Dr. Leonard Woods, 1774–1854, who defended the old Calvinism. Puritanism, however, increasingly abandoned the standards of Dort and Westminster. At the synod of the Congregational churches in America, held at St. Louis in 1880, a new confession of twelve articles was prepared in which the characteristically Reformed doctrines were omitted.
