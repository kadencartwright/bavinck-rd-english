## 39.

Theology in the Presbyterian churches in America followed a parallel course. Here too division arose not only among theologians, between the Old Lights and the New Lights, but also in the churches, between the synods of Philadelphia and New York, 1741–1758. One of the first theologians was John Dickinson, 1688–1747, whose principal work is a defense of the five articles against the Remonstrants. The Old School found support especially at Princeton College, founded in 1812, and was represented there by Dr. Archibald Alexander, 1772–1851; Dr. Charles Hodge, 1797–1878, _Systematic Theology_, London and Edinb., Nelson, 1873, 3 vol.; and the latter’s son and successor, Archibald Alexander Hodge, 1823–1886, _Outlines of Theology_, ed. by W. H. Goold, London, Nelson, 1866; _Evangelical Theology_, ib., 1890. So-called Princeton Theology is in its main substance a reproduction of the Calvinism of the seventeenth century as deposited in the Westminster Confession and the _Consensus Helveticus_ and especially as elaborated by F. Turretinus in his _Theol. Elenctica_. The same system is also advocated by the Southern theologians James H. Thornwell, 1812–1862; Robert J. Breckinridge, 1800–1871; and Robert L. Dabney. One of the youngest representatives of the Old School is W. G. T. Shedd, since 1890 professor emeritus at Union Seminary in New York, _Dogmatic Theology_, 2 vol., New York, Scribner, 1888. There is, however, a noteworthy difference between Hodge and Shedd. The former is a federalist and creationist; the latter, a realist and traducianist. Yet the two agree again in understanding election very broadly and including under it all children who die in infancy. The New Lights departed from the Old School not only concerning the authority of the General Assembly, revivals, union with the Congregationalists, and so forth, but especially concerning original guilt and particular satisfaction; later the inspiration of Holy Scripture and eschatology were added. Representatives of this new current were Albert Barnes, 1798–1870; Lyman Beecher, 1775–1863; and Thomas H. Skinner, 1791–1871, none of whom, however, left a theological system. Barnes and Beecher were charged with heresy but acquitted. Nevertheless, a new schism arose in the churches in 1837, when the Old School gained the majority in the General Assembly and severed four synods from fellowship. In 1869, however, they were reunited, chiefly through the influence of Union Theological Seminary in New York, founded in 1836. Dogmatics was taught there by Dr. Henry B. Smith, 1815–1877, _System of Christian Theology_, ed. by W. S. Karr, 4 ed., New York, Armstrong, 1890, who sought a mediation between Old and New Schools in the Christocentric standpoint. One of his pupils, Lewis French Stearns, d. 1892, wrote _Present day Theology_ and in it advocates all the newer views concerning inspiration, providence, kenosis, predestination, and salvation. Another professor at Union Seminary, Dr. Charles Briggs, was charged with heterodoxy in 1892 because he regarded reason as a source, accepted errors in Scripture, and acknowledged Higher Criticism—_The Bible, the Church and the reason_; _Messianic prophecy_; _Inspiration and inerrancy_, and so forth—and was condemned by the General Assembly in 1893. But the crisis did not end with this. The Reformed churches in America are living through a difficult time. The dogmas of the infallibility of Holy Scripture, the Trinity, the fall and man’s inability, particular satisfaction, election and reprobation, and so forth are secretly denied or openly contested. The revision question is indeed temporarily off the agenda in the Presbyterian Church but will probably return. The present does not seem favorable to the flourishing of Reformed theology; cf. my essay _The future of Calvinism_, _Presb. and Ref. Review_, Jan. 1894.
