### D. Dogmatics in the Lutheran Church

## 18.

The following may serve as resources for the history of Lutheran dogmatics: Walch, _Bibl. theol. selecta_ I 35 sq.; Pfaff, _Introductio in historiam theol. litterariam_, 1724, p. 204 sq.; G. Frank, _Gesch. der prot. Theol._, 3 Theile, 1862–75; Dorner, _Gesch. d. prot. Th._, 1867; Gass, _Gesch. der prot. Dogm._, 4 Theile, 1854–67; Tholuck, _Das kirchl. Leben im 17 Jahrh._, 1861–62; idem, _Das akad. Leben des 17 Jahrh._, 1853–54, together forming the _Vorgesch. des Ration._; idem, _Gesch. des Ration._, Ier Theil, 1865; idem, _Der Geist der luth. Theologen Wittenbergs im 17 Jahrh._, 1852; Kahnis, _Der innere Gang des deutschen Protest._, 2 Th., Leipzig, 1874; Ritschl, _Gesch. des Pietismus_, 3 Th., 1880–86; Harnack, _Dogmengesch._ III 691 f.; Zöckler, _Handbuch der theol. Wiss._, Supplement 144 f. Luther was not systematic by nature; he left no dogmatics behind. All the more was he an original, creative spirit. He rediscovered the Christianity of Paul and Augustine, once again understood the gospel as a glorious message of grace and forgiveness, and restored religion within religion. He thereby became fruitful for the whole of theology and the whole of dogmatics. Even the ancient _dogmata_ were indeed adopted by him, but animated with a new religious life. Th. Harnack, _Luthers Theologie_, 2 Th., Erlangen, 1862–66; J. Köstlin, _Luthers Theologie_, 2e Ausg., Stuttgart, 1883; Lommatzsch, _Luthers Lehre vom eth. relig. Standp._, Berlin, 1879. Before the Lutheran Reformation had a confession, it already had a dogmatics in Melanchthon’s _Loci_ of 1521, republished by Augusti in 1821, Plitt in 1864, and Bindseil in _Corpus Reform._ XXI p. 62. Arising from an exposition of the Epistle to the Romans, this work was practical, simple, soteriological, and free of all scholasticism—really much more a confession than a dogmatics. For a time the German Reformation found its unity in this work. But already in 1526 Melanchthon retreated somewhat from the confession of strict predestination, and soon he began to depart from Luther on other points as well, especially concerning the doctrine of the Lord’s Supper. This disagreement with Luther first appears clearly in the new editions of the _Loci_ of 1535 and 1543, then in the alteration of the _Augustana_ in 1540 and 1542, and finally in the Leipzig Interim and the adiaphoristic controversy it occasioned. Two parties now came to stand over against each other. On the one side were the adherents of Melanchthon, the Philippists, especially at the universities of Wittenberg and Leipzig, such as G. Major, Paul Eber, Joh. Pfeffinger, Victor Strigel, d. 1569, whose _Loci Theologici_ arose from lectures on Melanchthon’s _Loci_ and were published by Pezel in four parts, 1582–85; Christ. Pezel, d. 1604, author of _Argumenta et objectiones de praecipuis articulis doctrinae christ._, Neost., 1580–89; Sohnius, _Opera_, Herb., 1609; and others. Cf. H. Heppe, _Dogm. des deutschen Protest. im 16 Jahrh._, 3 Bde., Gotha, 1857. On the other side stood the Gnesio-Lutherans, especially in Weimar and Jena, such as Nic. von Amsdorf, d. 1565; Matth. Flacius, d. 1575, author of the _Solida Confutatio et condemnatio praecipuarum sectarum_ and many other polemical writings; Joh. Wigand, d. 1587; Joh. Marbach, d. 1581; Joachim Westphal, d. 1574, who opposed especially Calvin’s doctrine of the Lord’s Supper; Tileman Heshusius, d. 1588; and others. The many dogmatic controversies that arose among Lutheran theologians in this first period—concerning the law with Agricola; justification with Osiander; Christ’s descent into hell with Aepinus; the _obedientia activa_ with Parsimonius; the _adiaphora_, synergism, and crypto-Calvinism with Melanchthon and his associates; good works with Major; and original sin with Flacius—finally led to and were settled in the _Formula Concordiae_ of 1580, Frank, _Die Theologie der Konkordienformel_, 2 Th., Erl. Deichert, 1858–61. It was chiefly the work of Jakob Andreae, d. 1590, and Mart. Chemnitz, d. 1586, the foremost Lutheran theologian of this century and author of the _Examen Concilii Tridentini_, 4 tomi, 1565–73, republished by Preuss in 1861; a treatise _de duabus naturis in Christo, etc._, 1571, enlarged in 1578; and _Loci Theol._, published after his death by Leyser in 1592.
