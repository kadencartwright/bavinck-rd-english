## 6.

Chalcedonianism, however, brought no peace; confusion increased, and monophysitism was too strong in the East. Chalcedonianism did find a vigorous defender in Leontius of Byzantium 485-543, called the first scholastic by Harnack II 383, and was also recognized at the Fifth Synod at Constantinople 551. But the Monophysites were not won over, not even through the efforts of Justinianus I. The monenergist and monothelite controversy that arose in the seventh century ended with the affirmation of two wills in Christ at the Sixth Synod at Constantinople 680. To the present day there are monophysite Christians in Syria, art. Jacobiten in Herzog², Dr. H. G. Kleĳn, Jakob Baradeus. Leiden 1881. They affirm one nature in Christ, *ex.* rather than *in duabus naturis*; reject Chalcedon and recognize the so-called Robber Synod at Ephesus; use leavened bread at the Lord’s Supper; make the sign of the cross with one finger; have adopted the veneration of images and saints from the Greek and Roman churches; and stand under the “Patriarch of Antioch,” who, however, ordinarily lives in Diarbekr. Of dogmatic importance is the confession of faith of Baradeus in Kleĳn p. 110f. There are also Monophysites in Egypt, called Copts, living under a patriarch in Kaïro, Herzog² 1, 178 f.; in Abyssinia, under an Abbûna appointed by the patriarch in Kaïro and residing in Gondar, Herzog² 1, 69 f.; and in Armenia, under a Katholikos in Etschmiadsin, a monastery near Erivan in Armenia. See Hofmann, Symboliek § 62-68, with the literature listed there and in Herzog; Kattenbusch, Confessionskunde I 205-234.

For dogmatics, however, not only the christological writings are important; other treatises also come into consideration. The doctrine of God, His names, attributes, and providence was treated in continuity with the Apologists, who had upheld the Christian concept of God against Gnosticism. Most began with the natural knowledge of God, with God as a simple, immutable being whose existence could be proved psychologically, cosmologically, and teleologically, who was indeed unknowable in His essence but was revealed in Scripture as triune, Chrysostomus, Homiliae 12 contra Anomoeos seu de incomprehensibili Dei natura, Pseudodiomysius de divinis nominibus, Chrysostomus de providentia L. III. Theodoretus de providentia orationes X, Harnack II 116-122, Münscher-v. Coelln I 124 f. Cosmology and anthropology were treated especially in connection with Genesis 1-3 and in such a way that Origenism was avoided. God had created the world through the Logos after the pattern of a superterrestrial spiritual world; sin arose through free will and is counterbalanced by punishment and redemption, Basilius, Homiliae IX in hexaemeron. Greg. Nyss. Explicatio apologetica in hexaemeron and de hominis opificio, Ambrosius L. VI in hexaemeron. Augustinus, de Genesi contra Manich. L. II, De Genesi ad litteram liber imperfectus. Johannes Philoponus, de æternitate mundi c. Proclum, and de mundi creatione l. VII, Anastasius Sinaita, Anagogicæ contemplationes in hexaemeron, libri XII. Harnack D. G. II 122-129. Münscher-v. Coelln I 141 f. Schwane D. G. II. Furthermore, very many treatises were written on virginity, monasticism, perfection, the priesthood, the resurrection, and so forth, as by Ephraem Syrus, Greg. Naz., Chrysost., Greg. Nyss., Chrysost., together with many apologies against Jews, pagans, and heretics. Most noteworthy for the history of dogmatics are the Ὑποτυπωσεις, Institutiones theologicæ libr. VII, preserved in fragments in Athanasius and collected in Gallandii Bibl. III 662-663. Routh Reliq. Sacr. III and Migne ser. gr. 18. The first three books treat God the Father and Creator, the Son, and the Spirit; the fourth treats angels and demons; the fifth and sixth the incarnation; and the seventh creation. Next are the Κατηχησεις of Cyrillus: eighteen lectures for φωτιζομενοι on the truths of faith and five for νεοφωτιστοι on the mysteries—baptism, anointing, eucharist, and liturgy—Plitt, de Cyrilli Hierosol. orationibus, quae exstant catecheticis. Heidelb. 1855. Gregory of Nyssa, Oratio catechetica in 40 cap., contains a philosophical demonstration of the principal truths of Christianity: God’s existence and essence, Trinity, creation and fall, redemption, sacraments—especially penance and eucharist—and eschatology. Chrysostomus’ Catecheses duo are chiefly moral addresses to the catechumens. Theodoretus gave a compendium of the Christian faith in the fifth book of his Αἱρετικης κακομυθιας ἐπιτομη Haereticarum fabularum compendium. Maximus Confessor treated the truths of the church’s faith in short chapters, Κεφαλαια: 200 on the doctrine of God, 300 on the incarnation and sin, and 500 on love. Also of great significance were the five writings περι θειων ὀνοματων, περι της οὐρανιας ἱεραρχιας, περι της ἐκκλησιαστικης ἱεραρχιας, περι μυστικης θεολογιας, together with ten letters, which came to light in the fifth century and for a long time passed as writings of Dionysius Areopagita. They used Neoplatonic philosophy and pantheistic mysticism to elucidate and develop Christian doctrine and were soon highly esteemed, commented on by Maximus Confessor, Pachymeres, and others, used by theologians, mystics, and ascetics especially in the Middle Ages, and placed almost on a level with Scripture. Finally, all the elements of dogmatic development were brought together and united by Joh. Damascenus in his Πηγη γνωσεως. This work consists of three parts. In part 1, Κεφαλαια φιλοσοφικα, he gives an outline of philosophy as the handmaid and instrument of theology, especially of logic according to Aristoteles and Porphyrius. Part 2 is historical, Περι αἱρεσεων, and gives a survey of the heresies down to Mohammed. Part 3, Ἐκδοσις ἀκριβης της ὀρθοδοξου πιστεως, is the dogmatic part proper in 100 capita; he himself acknowledges that in it he gives nothing but what the fathers taught and accordingly cites the Greek fathers and Pope Leo repeatedly, J. Langen, Johannes van Damaskus, Gotha 1879. Grundlehner, Joh. Dam. Utrecht 1876.
