## 28.

From yet another side opposition arose against the mingling of philosophy and theology. Liebmann, in his work _Kant und die Epigonen_, Stuttgart, 1865, and F. A. Lange, in his _Geschichte des Materialismus_, 1866, gave philosophy the watchword: back to Kant. The speculation of Hegel and Schelling had led to nothing; the intellect had once again to become conscious of its finitude and limitation and to arrogate to itself no knowledge of the supersensible. Alongside this there might remain room for faith and imagination, but reason is limited to what is sensibly perceptible. This Neo-Kantian standpoint was adopted in the field of dogmatics by Lipsius and Ritschl, though with no insignificant difference. Lipsius acknowledges the mystical element in religion; Ritschl denies it. Lipsius regards religion as a distinct, independent power; Ritschl allows it to be absorbed almost entirely into the ethical. Lipsius considers religion primarily a matter of the individual; Ritschl, of the community. Lipsius at least still attempts to bring religious conceptions into accord with the results of science; Ritschl separates theology and science completely. For Lipsius the grace of God in Christ is the principal content of revelation; for Ritschl it is the kingdom of God founded by Christ, and so forth. But both nevertheless join Kant in his aversion to metaphysics and in his doctrine of the limitation of the human faculty of knowledge. Ritschl therefore desires a complete separation of metaphysics (philosophy, science) and religion (theology). Religion and theology express not judgments of being but judgments of value. They say nothing about the unknowable essence of things, but express only the value and significance things possess for us. Through such a separation Ritschl attempts to secure for religion and theology a place of their own that science cannot assail. Religion does not rest on science but is grounded in a principle of its own, in the moral nature of man. It has a content of its own: not judgments of being but judgments of value, that is, purely religious-ethical statements. It has an end of its own: to make man independent of the world in an ethical sense. Ritschl’s theology now exercises great influence not only within but far beyond Germany. Various causes explain its rise. The apparent reconciliation of believing and knowing; the religious-ethical conception of religion; the attachment to the revelation of God in Christ, to Holy Scripture, and to the theology of Luther and Melanchthon; the rejection of all _theol. naturalis_ and scholastic dogmatics; and so forth have brought this theology sudden diffusion and extraordinary success. A host of men—Herrmann, Kaftan, Häring, Harnack, Schürer, Gottschick, Kattenbusch, Stade, Wendt, Schultz, Lobstein, and others—have joined it and apply its principles to the whole field of theological science. Nevertheless, the Ritschlian doctrine of the faculty of knowledge, the complete separation of theology and metaphysics, the moralistic conception of religion, the restriction of religion to judgments of value, and so forth are unsatisfactory. In the long run this current too will be able to satisfy neither head nor heart; cf. my essay on the _Theol. van Ritschl_ in the _Theol. Stud._ of Dr. Daubanton and others, 1888.
