## 36.

In Switzerland and France a revival came through the Réveil, which was transplanted there from Scotland. The Réveil (cf. Wagenaar, _Het reveil en de afscheiding_, which gives literature on p. 40; H. von der Goltz, _Die reform. Kirche Genfs im 19. Jahrh._, Basel and Geneva, 1862; Léon Maury, _Le réveil religieux dans l’église réformée à Genève et en France_, Paris, Fischbacher, 2 vol., 1892; W. van Oosterwijk Bruijn, _Het Reveil in Nederland_, Utrecht, 1890; Pierson, _Oudere Tĳdgenooten_, 1888) was a powerful spiritual movement, but by origin it was non-ecclesiastical and anti-confessional. It stood on a broadly Christian foundation and was further characterized by its individualistic, aristocratic, Methodist, and philanthropic character. In Switzerland it laid particular emphasis on two dogmas: election (César Malan, d. 1864; biography by Malan Jr., Amst., Höveker, 1874) and inspiration (Merle d’Aubigné, d. 1872; Gaussen, d. 1863). But Alexander Vinet, 1797–1847, who came under the influence of the Réveil in 1822/23, proceeded theologically from a different thought. The vital principle of his faith and theology was the harmony of Christianity and conscience. This led him to insist so strongly on the connection between dogma and morality, on the ethical side of truth; it led him to his synergism and to the rejection of election, and so forth. Vinet nevertheless held fast to the principal truths of Christianity to the end, Dr. J. Cramer, _Alex. Vinet als christ. moralist en apologeet geteekend en gewaardeerd_, Leiden, Brill, 1883, and the literature cited there on p. 6. His pupil E. Scherer, however, went much further. At first strictly orthodox, he gradually broke with his past and ended his life in complete unbelief. Since then a liberal Protestantism arose in France and Switzerland: Pécaut, _Le Christ et la conscience_, Paris, 1859; Martin-Paschoud, Réville, Cougnard, Buisson. Two currents, a liberal and an evangelical, thus stood over against each other. But within the latter the principles of Vinet continued to operate, later supported further by the influence of Ritschl. Three currents can now be distinguished: a liberal or left wing, Bouvier in Geneva; a mediating current, Pressensé, Astié, Sécrétan, Sabatier, Leopold Monod, Chapuis, Dandiran, Lobstein; and an evangelical, moderately orthodox current, Godet father and son, Porret, Berthoud, Martin, Doumergue, Bertrand, H. Bois, Gretillat. The present controversy between these currents concerns chiefly two questions. The one concerns authority in matters of religion—whether it lies in Scripture, in Christ, or in reason and conscience. The second concerns the person of Christ—whether He is truly God, or emptied Himself by kenosis, or is merely a man differing from us in degree; cf. Gretillat in _The Presb. and Ref. Review_, July 1892 and July 1893.
