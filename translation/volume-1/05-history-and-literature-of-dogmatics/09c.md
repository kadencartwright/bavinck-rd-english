Thus Augustine became of the greatest significance for later dogmatics. He dominates the centuries that follow. Every reformation returns to him and to Paul. In every dogma he found a formula that has been adopted and repeated by all. His influence extends to every church, movement, and sect. Rome appeals to him for its doctrine of church, sacrament, and authority; the Reformation felt an affinity with him in the doctrine of _praedestinatio_ and _gratia_; scholasticism built further on the subtlety of his observation, the sharpness of his intellect, and the power of his speculation—Thomas was called _optimus interpres S. Augustini_; mysticism found material in his Neoplatonism and religious enthusiasm; Roman Catholic and Protestant piety strengthens itself through his writings; asceticism and Pietism find nourishment and support in him. Augustine belongs not to one church but to all the churches together. He is _Doctor universalis_. Even philosophy can neglect him only to its own harm. And through his beautiful, enchanting style, through his subtle, precise, highly individual and yet universally human expression, he remains more enjoyable today than any other church father. He is the most Christian and the most modern of all the church fathers; of them all, he stands closest to us. He replaced the aesthetic worldview with the ethical, the classical with the Christian. We owe our best, deepest, and richest thoughts in dogmatics to him. Augustine was _the_ dogmatician of the Christian church. Cf. Harnack, _Dogm._ III² 3–215.
