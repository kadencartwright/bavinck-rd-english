## 30.

Reformed dogmatics begins with Zwingli. In him the foundational ideas are already present: the theological point of departure, the absolute dependence of man, predestination, the human nature of Christ, the spiritual conception of church and sacrament, and the ethical and political tendency of the Reformation. But Zwingli’s theology still has many deficiencies. Because of his humanism, he understands sin and reconciliation too superficially; because of his spiritualism, he places God and man, *divina* and *humana justitia*, sign and thing signified in the sacrament, etc., over against each other in an abstractly dualistic fashion. His clarity and lucidity of thought cannot compensate for his lack of depth, and he does not arrive at anything like a rounded and coherent system. Zwingli merely sketched the general outlines within which the various currents in the Reformed churches later moved. Only Calvin’s organizing and systematic spirit gave the Swiss Reformation its clearly defined doctrine and firm organization. His theology was already established with the first edition of his *Institutes* in 1536. There is expansion and development, but no change. Calvin differs from Zwingli in banishing all philosophical and humanistic ideas and adhering as strictly as possible to Scripture. Furthermore, he more successfully maintains the objectivity of the Christian religion, of the covenant of God, of the person and work of Christ, and of Scripture, church, and sacrament, and therefore stands more firmly against the Anabaptists. He also overcomes both Luther’s opposition between the spiritual and the worldly and Zwingli’s opposition between flesh and spirit, and is therefore rigorist but in no respect an ascetic. Finally, he brings unity and system into his thought, something neither Luther nor Zwingli was able to do, while never forgetting its connection with the Christian life. Calvin gradually won all Switzerland to his side, also with respect to the doctrine of the Lord’s Supper (Consensus Tigurinus 1549) and predestination (Consensus Genev. 1552, Tweede Helv. Conf. 1564), Hundeshagen, Die Conflikte des Zwinglianismus, Lutherthums und Calvinismus in der Bernischen Landeskirche von 1532-1558, Bern 1842. Calvin’s *Institutes* was soon studied everywhere. The Bernese later appealed to Calvin’s authority just as much as did those of Geneva, Zurich, Basel, and Schaffhausen. Dogmatics was treated wholly in Calvin’s spirit in Switzerland during the sixteenth century by Beza, Tractationes theol. 1570; Petrus Martyr Vermiglius, Loci Communes 1576; Musculus, Loci Comm. 1560, 1567; and Aretius, Theol. problemata 1579.

From Switzerland, Calvin’s theology spread to France. In 1536 he dedicated his *Institutes* to Francis I in a preface. He became the soul of the French Reformation. His doctrine was generally accepted; his works were translated into French and disseminated; people sought counsel and comfort from him, and many went to Geneva to be trained for the ministry of the Word. The principal theologians in France in this century were Chandieu † 1591, who under the pseudonyms Sadeel and Zamariel wrote various theological treatises, *de verbo Dei*, *de Christi sacerdotio*, *de remissione peccatorum*, etc.; Marlorat † 1562, author of Thesaurus S. Scripturae in locos comm. rerum et dogmatum, published in 1574 by Feugueraeus; and du Plessis Mornay † 1623, known for his Traité de l’Eglise 1578, Traité de la vérité de la religion chrétienne 1581, and above all for his Le mystère d’iniquité c’est à dire l’histoire de la papauté 1611, which is highly inaccurate in its citations.

By way of the exiles who had gone to East Frisia, the Palatinate, and Cleves, and also from the South, Calvinism made its way into the Netherlands. Calvin’s *Institutes* was translated into Dutch as early as 1560. Dathenus, de Brès, Modet, Marnix, Caspar Heydanus, et al., were strict Calvinists. Many sought their education in Geneva and Heidelberg. But the academy at Leiden was founded as early as 1575, and the one at Franeker in 1585. Feugueraeus, Danaeus, Saravia, Trelcatius Sr., Bastingius, and Junius were at Leiden; Lubbertus, Lydius, and Nerdenus were the most famous professors at Franeker in this century. Theological work consisted chiefly in polemics against Rome and against the Anabaptists. Nevertheless, several dogmatic manuals had already appeared: by Gellius Snecanus, Methodica descriptio et fundamentum trium locorum communium S. Scr. 1584; by Bastingius, the first exposition of the Catechism, 1590; by Feugueraeus, Propheticae et apostolicae, i. e. totius divinae et canonicae scripturae thesaurus 1574; by Trelcatius Sr., Loci Communes 1587; and by Junius, Theses Theologicae (Opera Omnia I 1592 sq.).

Calvinism also took root in England and Scotland. There it came into conflict not only with Rome but also with the Reformation undertaken from above by Henry VIII and Elizabeth. Those sympathetic to the Reformation who fled to the Continent under Mary became acquainted there with the teaching of Calvin, Bullinger, Beza, Martyr, et al., and upon their return were soon offended by the half-heartedness of the English Reformation. The difference at first concerned the ceremonies. In doctrine, the Puritans and Anglicans originally agreed. English theology retained a decidedly Calvinistic character into the beginning of the seventeenth century. Calvin’s *Institutes* was taught at the universities. Even the episcopal form of government was defended by Cranmer, Jewel, Hooker, et al., not as the only true form, but solely in the interest of the church’s well-being, Schaff, Creeds of Christendom, 3 vol. New-York 1881 I 602 etc. But when, from 1567 onward, the nonconformists—among whom Pilkington, Whittingham, Thomas Sampson, and Humphrey of Oxford were the principal figures—seceded, the conflict expanded to encompass the entire question of church government. The most vigorous advocate of the presbyterian form of church government was Thomas Cartwright, a professor at Cambridge who was removed from office in 1570 and died in 1603. Toward the end of the century a doctrinal difference arose as well. William Perkins † 1602, Alle de werken, 3 vol. Amst. 1659f., and William Whitaker † 1595, Opera, Genev. 1610 2 vol., professors at Cambridge, still sought to uphold predestination in the 9 Lambeth Articles, Schaff, Creeds I 658 III 523, which they presented to Elizabeth’s adviser Whitgift, but the High Church and Pelagian opinions increasingly gained the upper hand. In Scotland, however, Calvinism was vigorously introduced by John Knox † 1572, John Craig † 1600, et al., and finally was also recognized by the king in 1581.

In Germany the Reformed church and theology were less dependent on Calvin. The Heidelberg Catechism and the theology of Pareus, Ursinus, Olevianus, Hyperius, and Boquinus, as well as that of à Lasco, display in many respects a distinctive character. Hofstede de Groot, Ebrard, and Heppe explained this distinctive character as deriving from Melanchthon, but this account is unhistorical and has been sufficiently refuted. Prof. Gooszen, in two studies on the Heid. Catech. 1890 and 1893, and Dr. Van ’t Hooft, De Theol. van Heinrich Bullinger 1888, derive it much more satisfactorily from Zwingli’s successor in Zurich. Yet there is no substantive difference at all between the theology of Calvin and that of Bullinger, but only a formal and methodological one. It is the distinction between supralapsarianism and infralapsarianism, between the strictly theological and the federal point of departure—a distinction that has always existed in the Reformed churches, with each side recognized as Reformed; cf. my article Calvin. en Geref. in De Vrĳe Kerk, Febr. 1893, and Prof. Gooszen’s reply, Geloof en Vrĳheid, Dec. 1894. Alongside Ursinus and Olevianus, therefore, the strictly Calvinist Zanchius also worked in Heidelberg.
