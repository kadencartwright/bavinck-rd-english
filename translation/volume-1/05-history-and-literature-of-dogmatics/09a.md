## 9.

This entire dogmatic development of East and West culminates in Augustinus. The doctrine of the Trinity and the Christology of the Eastern theologians; the doctrine of man, sin, grace, faith, satisfaction, and merit of Tertullianus and Ambrosius; the Neoplatonism of Victorinus; Cyprianus’s doctrine of the church and the sacrament; the monastic ideal of Hieronymus and Hilarius—all this was adopted by Augustinus and, through his rich experience of life, made into his own spiritual possession. Augustinus did not produce a theological, dogmatic system. The material that flowed to him from every side—from Scripture, tradition, and philosophy—and that he expanded and augmented through his rich personality could not be surveyed and systematized all at once.
The most important work Augustinus produced in this respect is his *Enchiridion de fide, spe et caritate*, an exposition of the principal truths of faith according to the Apostles’ Creed. But his doctrine is not without contradictions, especially the contradiction between his doctrine of the church and his doctrine of grace. Reuter demonstrated that these contradictions cannot be reconciled and that the thoughts of Augustinus cannot be brought together into a system. And yet there has been no church father who pondered all theological problems so deeply and struggled so intensely to attain unity, Harnack III 87. He was the first who attempted to give a clear account of all those theological questions that would later be treated in the *prolegomena* of dogmatics, and who penetrated to the ultimate psychological and noetic problems.
The fixed point from which he proceeds is man—his self-consciousness and his ineradicable longing for and need of truth, happiness, and the good, which are all one. This starting point is certain and reliable (against the skeptics), since doubting itself still presupposes faith in truth and self-consciousness is the ultimate ground of truth. Augustinus himself was consumed by such a burning love of truth. Now Augustinus does indeed assume two organs of knowledge, *sensus* and *intellectus*. But the knowledge obtained by the latter far surpasses that obtained by the former. The sensible is not truth itself; it is only an image of it. Eternal, immutable truth can be found only through thought. Augustinus certainly does not deny that we can also ascend through the visible to the invisible, but he usually seeks the way to truth not outside us through nature, but through man’s own spirit.
There, in his own reason and in the reason common to all, he finds eternal, immutable truths that in turn point back to God and are summed up in Him: the highest truth, the highest being, the one good, *aeterna ratio, principium universorum*. Because God is truth in its fullness, being itself, goodness itself, and beauty itself, in Him alone is there rest for man, for his thought and will. Self-knowledge and the knowledge of God are the two poles between which all his thought moves. The study of nature is not despised, but it is nevertheless assigned a subordinate place. *Deum et animam scire cupio! Noverim me noverim te!* God is the sun of spirits. We see and know no truth except in and through His light.
