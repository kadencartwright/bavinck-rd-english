## 16.

But by the beginning of the seventeenth century the flourishing age of neo-scholasticism had passed. The rationalistic spirit arose throughout Europe. The philosophy of Bacon and Descartes displaced that of Aristotle. Even theologians who accepted the scholastic dogmas believed that another method was needed. Such were Bossuet, d. 1704, the famous defender of the Gallican system and opponent of the Protestants, _Exposition de la doctrine de l’église cath. sur les matières de controverse_, 1671, _Histoire des variations des églises protest._, 2 vol., Paris, 1688; Fénélon, d. 1715; Thomassinus, d. 1695, _Dogmata theol._ Historical and critical studies, practiced especially in France, pushed theology proper into the background. Many learned Maurists and Oratorians even lapsed into unbelief. Scholasticism did indeed continue to prevail in the schools well into the eighteenth century. The various currents also continued alongside one another: Thomists, for example Peri, _Quaest. theol._, 5 vol., 1719–32; Scotists, for example Krisper, _Theol. scolae scotisticae_, 4 Tom., 1728–48; Molinists, for example Anton Erber, _Theol. specul. tractatus octo_, 1787; and Augustinians, for example Amort, _Theol. eclectica moralis et scholastica_, 23 tomi, Augsburg, 1752 sq. Nevertheless, scholasticism withdrew increasingly into the schools. Other deistic and naturalistic parties arose, gained the upper hand, and exercised influence on Roman Catholic theology. Scholastic philosophy was set aside in France by Descartes and in Germany by Leibniz-Wolff. In Austria, Peripatetic doctrine was prohibited in 1752, and in 1759 the direction of theological and philosophical studies was taken away from the Jesuits. The order was suppressed by Clement XIV in 1773. The Gallican system was defended in 1763 by Nicholas von Hontheim, auxiliary bishop of Trier, in a work _de statu ecclesiae et legitima potestate Romani Pontificis_, and was introduced as ecclesiastical law by Joseph II in 1781. Confessional differences were forgotten; in place of the struggle against Protestants came the struggle against freethinkers, unbelievers, and others in Klüpfel, Fahrman, Stattler, Storchenau, and Burkhauser. Theology came entirely under the influence of changing philosophies. The _Aufklärung_ had its representative in Adam Weishaupt, professor at Ingolstadt and founder of the Order of Illuminati. Kant’s influence is evident in Ildefons Schwarz, Peutinger, and Zimmer. Jacobi’s philosophy found adherents in J. Salat and Cajetan Weiller. Thanner stood under the influence of Schelling. The principal theologian who stood firm against every error in the eighteenth century was Alphonsus Liguori, d. 1787, whom Pius IX enrolled among the _doctores ecclesiae_ in 1871.
