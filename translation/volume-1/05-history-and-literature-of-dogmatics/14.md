## 14.

The Middle Ages were not, however, without protests against the direction in which church and theology were developing. Various sects arose—the Cathars, Albigensians, the followers of Amalric of Bena, David of Dinant, Ortlieb, the sect of the Free Spirit, and others—and revived the old Manichaean and Gnostic errors, Reuter, Gesch. der relig. Aufklärung im M. A. Berlin 1875-’77. L. Flathe, Gesch. der Ketzer im M. A. 3 Bde. Stuttgart 1845. Kurtz, Lehrb. der Kirch. gesch. § 108. Döllinger, Beiträge zur Sektengesch. des M. A. 2 Th. München, Beck, 1890. The Waldensians came into conflict with Rome through their doctrine of the freedom of preaching, Comba, art. in Herzog², Haupt, Waldenzerthum und Inquisition im südöstl. Deutschl. Freiburg, Mohr, 1890. In many circles there was a return to Augustine and Paul. Thomas Bradwardine † 1349 appeared in his work de causa Dei contra Pelagium as a courageous defender of the grace of God, Lechler, De Th. Bradwardina commentatio, Lips. 1863 and art. in Herzog². Wycliffe † 1384 was dependent on him, just as Hus † 1451 was in turn dependent on Wycliffe. Wycliffe’s works have been published since 1882 by the Wiclif Society in London. His doctrine is best known from his Summa in twelve books, abridged and systematically summarized in his Trialogus, edited by Lechler; from his treatise de Christo et suo adversario Antichristo, edited by Buddensieg; and from his tract de ecclesia, edited by Loserth. That Hus is wholly dependent on Wycliffe was demonstrated by Loserth, Hus und Wiclif, Prag. u. Leipz. 1884. Even within the church many arose who desired a reformation in capite et membris. Pierre d’Ailly † 1425, Gerson † 1429, Nicholas of Clémanges † 1414, Nicholas of Cusa † 1464, and others defended the episcopal system; and the reforming councils of Pisa 1409, Constance 1414, and Basel 1431 declared themselves in that spirit. At its fourth and fifth sessions the Council of Constance declared that an ecumenical council had its authority immediately from Christ and that the pope too was subject to it. But all these reformations had little success. They were critiques of what existed from the standpoint of the same principle, Harnack D. G. III 408 f. 570 f. And when the Protestant Reformation arose in the sixteenth century, the Roman Catholic Church soon took its position against it. Before the Council of Trent, the principal theologians were Cajetan † 1534, Dr. Eck † 1543, Cochlaeus † 1552, Sadoletus † 1547, and others. They are still distinguished by the frankness with which they acknowledge the defects of the church. Their writings are mostly polemical against the Reformers. The most noteworthy is Dr. Eck’s Enchiridion locorum omnium adv. Lutherum et alios hostes ecclesiae 1525, which was reprinted forty-six times through 1576, Hugo Lämmer, Die vortrident. kath. Theol. des Reformationszeitalters, Berlin 1858. Theology had fallen into disrepute, especially through the mockery of the Humanists; the Middle Ages were regarded as an age of Gothic barbarism; and the devout too desired more simplicity and truth, more practical Christianity. Roman Catholic theologians needed some courage, and also some time, to collect themselves and take up the thread of scholasticism again. The Council of Trent did adopt various decrees of reform, but it took the strongest possible stand against the Protestant Reformation. The *dogmata* disputed with the Reformation—such as the doctrine of tradition, sin, free will, justification, and the sacraments—were formulated forcefully and clearly in a Roman Catholic sense, while internal differences were left untouched. The question of pope and council, of Thomism and Scotism, and others was either not discussed or treated as cautiously as possible, Harnack, D. G. III 588 f. Herzog² 16, 4 f., and the literature cited there. When Trent was deliberating about holding lectures on Holy Scripture in all monasteries, a Benedictine abbot proposed adding a prohibition of scholasticism. But the Dominican Soto took the floor, refuted the objections, praised the usefulness of scholasticism, and found general agreement, Kleutgen, Theol. d. Vorzeit IV 80. Gradually scholasticism was restored to honor, but with modifications. Melchior Cano † 1560 devotes an entire book in his work de locis theologicis to the defense of scholasticism, lib. VIII: de auctoritate scholasticorum doctorum, and concedes that many scholastics had made themselves guilty of various faults. He disapproves of those defects but resolutely defends the scholastic method. It was simplified and stripped of excess, but otherwise retained. A second change consisted in Lombard’s increasingly giving way to Thomas. In the Middle Ages Lombard’s Sententiae had been the dogmatic handbook; and even after the Reformation, Soto † 1560, Maldonatus † 1583, Estius † 1608, and others still wrote commentaries on that work. But the famous Cajetan produced a commentary on Thomas’s Summa. And Francisco de Vitoria † 1566, Hieronymus Perez † 1556, Bartholomew of Torres † 1558, and others followed his example. By the end of the sixteenth century Lombard had been replaced by Thomas in most schools; Thomas was purer, more comprehensive, more methodical, and penetrated more deeply into the *dogmata* than Lombard. Furthermore, another difference between the old and new scholasticism was that the latter connected itself much more closely with positive theology. In the Middle Ages positive dogmatics—that is, the proof of truth from Scripture and tradition—was almost entirely neglected; but now it was incorporated into dogmatics and developed with great learning. Cano wrote a separate work on the sources of proof, which he calls *loci theologici*; all the neo-scholastics—Gregory of Valencia, Suarez, Báñez, Diego Ruiz, and others—diligently cultivated the study of Scripture and tradition. Exegesis, church history, patristics, archaeology, and other fields became distinct, independent sciences. Theology is still something other and more than dogmatics. And finally, because the new scholastics did not enjoy the repose of the theologians of the Middle Ages but were attacked from every side and had to defend Roman Catholic doctrine on every point, there was no longer any place or time for subtle questions and ingenious distinctions. Form, method, language, and expression became simpler. The works of Canisius, Cano, Petavius, Bellarmine, and others are written in pure Latin and in an agreeable style, and in this respect compare favorably with the works of the medieval theologians. Yet whatever was changed and improved, the spirit remained the same. Rome did not deny itself and learned nothing through the Reformation. Indeed, the distinctive character of Roman Catholic doctrine came to light even more clearly after and through the Reformation. Pelagianism and Curialism developed further after the Council of Trent and achieved a complete victory. For the history of Roman Catholic theology after Trent, compare: Walch, Bibl. theol. sel.
