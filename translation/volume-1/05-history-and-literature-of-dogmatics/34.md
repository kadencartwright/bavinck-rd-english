## 34.

Around 1750 the decline of Reformed theology began everywhere. The disintegrating elements already present in the preceding century continued their work and undermined dogmatics. After Cocceianism had gained the victory in the Netherlands, the period of the Tolerants came, from 1740 to 1770. The power of the truth was denied; people withdrew from the confession to Scripture; distinctively Reformed doctrines—original guilt, the covenant of works, particular satisfaction, and so forth—were abandoned; under an attractive form and a biblical name, all kinds of Remonstrant and Socinian errors arose. The confessors of the Reformed religion at most acquiesced in what had been handed down, but they no longer lived in it or spoke from it. The old dogmatics became an object of historical study. Prof. Bernh. de Moor wrote a *Commentarius perpetuus in Marckii Compendium*, 6 vols., Lugd. B. 1761-71, and Rev. Martinus Vitringa provided a commentary on the *Doctrina Christ. religionis* of his father, Campegius Vitringa, under the title *Doctrina Christ. relig. per aphorismos summatim descripta, ed. sexta. Cui nunc accedit ὑποτυπωσις theologiae elencticae in usum scholarum domesticarum Campegii Vitringae, curante Martino Vitringa, qui praefationem, prolegomena et adnotationes adjecit, nec non analysin v. cl. Theodori Scheltingae*, 9 partes, Amst. 1761. Among the few who held fast with heart and soul to the old Reformed doctrine and ably defended and further developed it, the foremost place belongs to Alex. Comrie † 1774, *A. B. C. des geloofs* 1739, *Eigengesch. des zaligm. geloofs* 1744, *Verklaring van den Catech.* 1753, *Brief over de rechtvaardigmaking* 1761, cf. Dr. A. G. Honig, *Alex. Comrie*, 1892; Nic. Holtius † 1773, *Verhandeling over de rechtv. door het geloof* 1750; and J. J. Brahé, *Aanmerkingen wegens de vĳf Walch. art.* 1758. The first two took up the struggle against the Tolerants in their *Examen van het ontwerp van Tolerantie*, in 10 *Samenspraken*, Amst. 1753-59; among the Tolerants, professors J. van den Honert, J. J. Schultens, and Alberti were especially targeted. Alongside them, J. C. Appelius also deserves mention; he is known for his controversy over the Lord’s Supper: *Zedig en vrĳmoedig onderzoek, enz.* 1763. *Over het avondmaal* 1764. *Aanmerkingen over het rechte gebruik van het Evangelie*. *Vervolg van de Aanmerkingen, enz.* *De Hervormde leer* 1769.

But from 1770 onward, so-called neology gained ever more influence. English deism, French unbelief, and German rationalism found fertile soil here. The Revolution brought a total reversal in people’s conceptions. Orthodoxy was carried over into the nineteenth century in the form of a Supranaturalism that was not rationalistic but rational, moderate, and biblical, and as such had its principal representatives in P. Chevallier, *Schema Institutionum theol.* 1773-75; Br. Broes, *Institut. Theol. theor.* 1788; J. van Nuys Klinkenberg, *Onderwĳs in den godsdienst*, 12 volumes, 1780 f.; Samuel van Emdre, *Katechismus der H. Godg.* 1780; W. E. de Perponcher, *Beschouw. Godg.* 1790, and so forth; and especially H. Muntinghe, *Pars theologiae christ. theoretica* 1800. Matters proceeded similarly in other countries. In the eighteenth century France no longer had a Reformed theology of its own. The revocation of the Edict of Nantes drove its best minds out of the country. In the eighteenth century Paul Rabaut † 1794 and Antoine Court † 1760 earned the honorary title of restorers of the Reformed church in France. Most French ministers received their training in Lausanne, where a separate seminary had been established for French students according to Antoine Court’s plan. In Switzerland the *Consensus Helvetius* 1675 could not check rationalism. J. E. Wettstein and his son in Basel, J. C. Suicerus and his son Henricus in Zurich, and Mestrezat and Louis Tronchin in Geneva raised objections to it, Schweizer, *Centraldogmen* II 663 f.; attempts to set it aside were already made in 1685, and in the eighteenth century these were crowned with success in Geneva, Basel, Appenzell, Zurich, Bern, and elsewhere. J. F. Osterwald, minister at Neuchâtel † 1747, forms the transition from seventeenth-century orthodoxy to eighteenth-century rationalism. In his *Traité des sources de la corruption, qui règne aujourd’hui parmi les Chrétiens* 1700, *Catéchisme* 1702, and *Compendium theol. Christ.* 1739, he laments dead orthodoxy and the finely spun *dogmata*, passes over many doctrines in silence, e.g., election, and seeks a revival of morality. Together with him J. A. Turretinus † 1737, *Opera*, 3 vols., and S. Werenfels † 1740, *Opuscula*, 2nd ed., 1739, formed the Swiss triumvirate. The moderate orthodoxy represented by them soon passed into decided heterodoxy in J. J. Zimmermann † 1757, *Opuscula* 1751-59; J. J. Lavater † 1759, art. Genève in the *Dictionn. Encycl.* of Diderot and d’Alembert; and J. Vernet, *Instruction Chrétienne* 1754. Wolff’s mathematical method was applied to theology by D. Wyttenbach, *Tentamen theol. dogm. methodo scientifica pertractata*, 3 tomi, 1747; J. F. Stapfer, *Institut. theol....... ordine scientifico dispositae*, 5 vols., 1743, *Grundlegung zur wahren Religion*, Zurich 1751-52; and Bernsau, later professor at Franeker, *Theol. dogmatica, methodo scientifica pertractata* 1745-47; and in Germany by Ferdinand Stosch † 1780, *Summa paedagogiae scholasticae ad praelectiones academicas in theologiam revel. dogm.* 1770; Sam. Endemann, professor at Marburg, *Instit. theol. dogm.* 1777; and Sam. Mursinna, professor at Halle, *Compendium theol. dogm.* 1777. In England dogmatics was almost entirely taken up with the questions of prophecy, miracle, and revelation that deism had placed on the agenda. Although apologetics was often rationalistically colored, it nonetheless had many representatives, including some excellent ones: Samuel Clarke † 1729, Nathan Lardner † 1768, Joseph Butler † 1752, Richard Bentley, William Whiston, Arthur Ashley Sykes, Thomas Sherlock † 1761, Daniel Waterland † 1742, John Conybeare, John Leland, James Foster, William Warburton † 1779, Richard Watson † 1816, and William Paley † 1805, *Evidences of Christianity* 1794, *Natural Theology* 1802, and so forth. Among the dogmatic works published in this period, the most important are those of Hutchinson, an extract from which is given in *A letter to a bishop concerning some important discoveries in philosophy and theology* 1735; of Stackhouse † 1752, *A complete body of speculative and practical divinity* 1709, Dutch trans. 1758; of Isaac Watts † 1748, known not only for his hymns and logic but also for his catechism 1728; and of Philip Doddridge † 1751, *Rise and progress of religion in the soul* 1745, and others. Among the Scottish theologians of the eighteenth century, the most prominent were Thomas Boston † 1732, *A complete body of divinity*, 3 vols., 1773, *Fourfold State*, Dutch trans. 1742, *A view of the covenant of grace*, Dutch trans. by Comrie 1741; Adam Gib; and the first five Seceders—Fisher, Wilson, Moncrieff, and the brothers Ralph and Ebenezer Erskine—whose works were also translated into Dutch, new ed., Amst. 1856. Of importance was the so-called Marrow controversy, which began in 1717 when the work of the Independent Edward Fisher, *The marrow of modern divinity*, first published in 1647, was republished in Scotland. The neonomian controversy waged in England in the preceding century was thereby transplanted to Scotland. The book was attacked by Principal Hadow of St. Andrews in a work entitled *The antinomianism of the marrow detected* 1721. The “Marrow divines,” chief among whom was Boston, were accused of antinomianism, but their accusers were themselves not free of neonomianism. The General Assembly condemned some propositions in the Marrow book as errors; this also helped occasion the separation of the Erskines, who had sided with Boston. Neonomianism prepared the way for the rationalism that gradually penetrated the theology and church of Scotland as well and was already clearly evident in men such as Simpson, Maclaurin, and others, Walker, *The Theology and theol. of Scotland* p. 25 f. 39 f. 53 f. James Buchanan, *The doctrine of justification* 1867 p. 182-188. Merle d’Aubigné, *Duitschland, Engeland en Schotland*, Rotterdam, 1849.
