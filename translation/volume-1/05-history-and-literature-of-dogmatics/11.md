## 11.

After the tenth century, the _saeculum obscurum_, a new life awakens everywhere. From the monastery at Cluny proceeds a religious reformation that continues in the mendicant orders of the twelfth century. Piety is conceived as following and imitating the life of Jesus, especially in His final week of suffering. The Crusades bring new ideas and broaden the horizon. The power of the popes increases and sets nothing less than world dominion as its ideal. Scholarship receives its own nursery in the universities and appears within theology as scholasticism. _Theologia scholastica_, in distinction from _theologia positiva_, which presents the dogmas in a simple, thetic form, indicates that dogmatic material is processed according to a scientific method customary in the schools. In itself, scholasticism is nothing other than scientific theology. It begins where _theologia positiva_ ends. The latter is satisfied when it has stated and proved the dogmas. But _theologia scholastica_ proceeds from those dogmas as its _principia_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 2 and 8, and seeks by reasoning from them to trace the coherence of the dogmas, to penetrate more deeply into the knowledge of revealed truth, and to defend it against every attack. In the Middle Ages, however, scholasticism acquired through various circumstances a definite character that brought it into disrepute. First, source study was in a very sorry state during the Middle Ages. In theory, observation was not denied as a _principium_ of knowledge, but in fact people simply acquiesced in tradition and believed that earlier generations had already applied observation adequately and deposited it fully in books. Physics, medicine, psychology, and so forth—everything was studied from books. In theology the material lay fully before one in Scripture, but especially in tradition, in the church fathers, councils, and so forth. Scholasticism stood before this material not critically and skeptically but with childlike faith. Faith was the starting point of scholasticism. The dogmatic material was sought in Scripture and tradition and accepted without any criticism. Scripture often received little justice in the process. Hebrew and Greek were unknown. Grammatical and historical sense was almost entirely lacking. The material was drawn chiefly from the church fathers: from Augustine, Hilary, Ambrose, Jerome, Gregory, Isidore, Pseudo-Dionysius, Damascene, and Boethius. In the second place, thought was gradually trained by the logical writings of Aristotle to process this dogmatic material dialectically and systematically. At first only Aristotle’s two logical writings, _de categoriis_ and _de interpretatione_, in Latin translation, were known, together with Porphyry’s introduction to the categories and various commentaries, especially those of Boethius. Of Plato there was only part of the _Timaeus_ in translation and, further, quotations in Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, and others. Scholarship outside theology was divided into the _trivium_ and _quadrivium_ and was contained in the encyclopedic work of Cassiodorus. Only from the middle of the twelfth century did Aristotle’s _Organon_ become completely known, and at the beginning of the thirteenth century his other works on metaphysics, physics, psychology, and ethics as well. From this philosophy people borrowed the dialectical method, but also all kinds of problems and questions concerning the relation of faith and reason, theology and philosophy; the reality of universal concepts; the attributes of God; miracles; creation from eternity; the soul; and so forth. Aristotle became _praecursor Christi in naturalibus_, just as John the Baptist was _in gratuitis_. All kinds of cosmological, scientific, psychological, and philosophical material were thereby incorporated into dogmatics. Dogmatics was no longer a doctrine of faith but became a system of philosophy, an encyclopedia of scholarship in which all kinds of philosophical material were included but in which religion frequently received too little attention. Finally, this entire scholastic system was presented in a form that increasingly gave occasion for serious objections. Not only was the material treated so dialectically, dissected with such hair-splitting subtlety, and handled so juridically that its connection with the religious life of the congregation was completely broken; people also occupied themselves by preference with all kinds of ingenious questions about angels and devils, heaven and hell, and so forth. But the interrogatory form into which everything was cast promoted doubt and frequently allowed _auctoritas_ and _ratio_ to diverge completely and stand in hostility over against each other. Many times the case for a dogma appears entirely lost, but a single appeal to a text or a church father sets everything right again. The impression remains, however, that the situation of the dogma is hopeless. In the long run the language was not suited to giving satisfaction with the content of the system. The barbarous Latin that was written was indeed, according to Paulsen’s observation, evidence that people thought independently and freely formed the words they needed for their ideas, but it could nevertheless no longer satisfy as soon as a sense for classical, simple beauty began to reawaken in some measure.
