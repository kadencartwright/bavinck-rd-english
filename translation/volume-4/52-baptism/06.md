## 6.

This water becomes a sacrament through the word of institution. In Scripture baptism is described at one time as baptism into the name of Christ, Acts 2:38; 8:16; 10:48; 19:5; cf. Rom. 6:3; 1 Cor. 1:13–15; 6:11; Gal. 3:27, and at another as baptism into the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, Matt. 28:19. These expressions are not intended to provide a formula that must be pronounced at baptism, but they describe the essence of Christian baptism. It must be a baptism into the name of Christ and thus into the name of the triune God. That they are not intended as a formula is evident from the fact that no such formula is mentioned with circumcision and Passover, with John’s baptism, or with the Supper. But certainly from the beginning one thing or another was spoken when baptism was administered and received. Confession of sins, Matt. 3:6, and of faith in Christ, Acts 8:37, was made; cf. 1 Tim. 6:12. By the nature of the case, a fixed formula derived from the words of institution in Matt. 28:19 soon came into use for this. The *Didache* speaks of Christians as βαπτισθεντες εἰς ὀνομα κυριου, 9:5, but already knows the trinitarian formula, 7, 1, 3; cf. Justin, *Apol.* I 61. Although in the first period a baptism into the name of Christ, or with the confession that Jesus Christ is the Son of God, Acts 8:37, was entirely sufficient, later, when all kinds of heresies arose, the trinitarian formula increasingly had to be regarded as necessary precisely to maintain the Christian character of baptism; cf. Cyprian, *Ep.* 73, 16–18, and other church fathers in Suicerus, s.v. βαπτισμος. But even this trinitarian formula does not read the same in the various churches. The Greek church uses the words βαπτιζεται ὁ δουλος του θεου ὁ δεινα εἰς το ὀνομα του πατρος—ἀμην, και του υἱου—ἀμην, και του ἁγιου πνευματος—ἀμην, νυν και εἰς τους αἰωνας των αἰωνων. Although the Latin church recognizes baptism administered in this way, it itself nevertheless uses the formula *ego te baptizo in nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti*, *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qus. 10, 11, and the Protestant churches generally adopted this. The Syrian and Armenian churches again have a formula that differs from both the Greek and Latin, Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* I 44. Everything proves that the validity of baptism in itself does not depend upon the literal words spoken by the minister. The trinitarian formula became necessary only to ward off heresy, to give assurance that the baptism administered is true Christian baptism, and to bring desirable fixity into liturgical usage. It is further important to observe that the trinitarian baptismal formula possesses no magical power to change the water into Christ’s blood. Not only do the Reformed deny this, but the Greek, Roman, and Lutheran churches also speak differently at baptism than at the Supper. In the latter sacrament the emphasis falls upon the *recitatio* of the words of institution, their consecratory power, and the trans- or consubstantiation brought about thereby. But although at baptism one also speaks of a *divina virtus* communicated to the water, of *aqua vivida, sancta, divina*, of a *regeneratio per aquam in verbo*, even the *Catech. Rom.* nevertheless says that the words of institution must be pronounced clearly and distinctly for the instruction of the people, II 2, 10, and denies that a transubstantiation, a change of the water into Christ’s blood, takes place at baptism, II 4, 9. Thus the *unio sacramentalis* here differs from that at the Supper. Certainly a difference still remains even then. Roman Catholics and Lutherans conceive the operation of the Holy Spirit at baptism as proceeding *per aquam*. The Reformed reject this local, physical union and assume in its place a connection like that with the Word. Just as the Holy Spirit indeed works *cum verbo* but does not confine his power and operation within the Word, so it is also with the water of baptism. In Eph. 5:26 the words ἐν ρηματι are not, as the Lutherans maintain, a further qualification of λουτρῳ or ὑδατος, for then they would have required the article before them, τῳ or του ἐν ρηματι. Rather, they belong with ἁγιασῃ: Christ sanctified his congregation through the Word of the gospel while cleansing her through the washing of water. Here Paul precisely distinguishes Christ’s operation through the Word from that through the water, as also happens in Heb. 10:22 and 1 Pet. 3:21. Not the minister and not the water but Christ sanctifies and gives the thing signified, Matt. 3:11; 1 Cor. 6:11; Heb. 9:14; 1 John 1:7. If the water of baptism effected regeneration, Paul could not have said in 1 Cor. 1:14 that Christ did not send him to baptize but to proclaim the gospel. But however much disagreement there is about the manner in which sign and signified thing are connected in baptism, there is agreement concerning the reality of that connection. The Reformed church too confesses that in baptism Christ promises and assures everyone who receives it in faith that he is as surely washed by his blood and Spirit from the uncleanness of the soul as he is outwardly washed with the water that customarily removes the impurity of the body, *Heid. Catech.* 69.

There is also agreement in the main concerning the benefits bestowed in baptism upon adult believers. They are all comprehended in the communion with the triune God into which the believer is incorporated through baptism, Matt. 28:19. In baptism the Father testifies to us that he establishes with us an eternal covenant of grace and adopts us as his children and heirs, Gen. 17:7, 10; Acts 2:39. The Son seals to us that he washes us in his blood and incorporates us into the communion of his death and resurrection, Rom. 6:3; Gal. 3:27. The Holy Spirit seals to us that he dwells in us and sanctifies us as members of Christ, 1 Cor. 6:11; 12:13; Titus 3:5. Elaborated more fully, these benefits are: 1º justification or forgiveness of sins, Mark 1:4; Acts 2:38; 22:16; 1 Pet. 3:21; Heb. 10:22. Doedes believes that this benefit comes into consideration not at baptism but only at the Supper, since baptism is called a baptism of repentance *unto* forgiveness of sins, *Leer der Zaligheid*, 326. But this conception is clearly contradicted by Acts 22:16; 1 Pet. 3:21; Heb. 10:22. Repentance is indeed the way along which the forgiveness acquired by Christ comes into our possession and enjoyment, but baptism is precisely proof and pledge of that forgiveness obtained in the way of repentance; for confession of sins and justifying faith precede baptism. In baptism, therefore, all sins with all their guilt and penalty are also forgiven—not only past but also present and future sins—for justification is a juridical act, a change of state, and is therefore brought about at once, completely, and permanently; cf. III 548. 2º Regeneration, conversion, the dying of the old and resurrection of the new human being through communion with Christ’s death and resurrection, Mark 1:4; Rom. 6:2–10; 1 Cor. 6:11; Eph. 5:26; Col. 2:12. According to Rome the same grace is restored in baptism which Adam received as a *donum superadditum* but lost through sin. Just as *concupiscentia* dwelt in Adam as *homo naturalis* even before the fall and was restrained by the *donum superadditum*, so it is with the baptized person. The *concupiscentia* remains in him, but it is not itself sin and is proper by nature to the human being as consisting of flesh and spirit. It can only easily become an occasion for sin if the human being, instead of allowing himself to be led by supernatural grace, listens to it and consents to it. But apart from this danger, which always continues to threaten the baptized person, through the grace he receives in baptism he is freed not only from all guilt but also from every stain of sin. Over against this the *Ned. Geloofsbel.*, art. 15, expressed: original sin is not entirely annulled or wholly eradicated even by baptism (originally in the Walloon text of 1561: *et n’est pas aboli mesme par le baptesme*, enlarged by the synod of 1566 with the words *ou desraciné du tout*). Although many, with Doedes, *Ned. Gel.*, 173, disapprove of these words, they are nevertheless entirely correct and in agreement with Holy Scripture. For in the passages cited above it teaches very clearly that baptism—understood, of course, as sign and seal—regenerates and renews the human being, breaks the power of original sin in him, and causes him to walk in newness of life, yet in such a way that sin still always dwells in his flesh and against his will takes him captive under its law. Thus the stain of original sin is indeed partly and in principle, but not entirely, destroyed by baptism as sacrament. Although it no longer condemns the believer, it nevertheless remains within him unto death an unblessed fountain of all kinds of sin. 3º Communion not only with Christ himself but also with the congregation, which is his body. The baptized person is saved from the crooked generation, set apart from the world, Acts 2:40, 41; made a disciple of Jesus, Matt. 28:19; John 4:1; incorporated into his congregation, 1 Cor. 12:13; and thus also obligated to walk in integrity, Gen. 17:1, and in newness of life, Rom. 6; to confess God’s name; and to keep Jesus’ commandments, Matt. 28:19. All these benefits have already been granted to the baptized person before baptism in the Word of the gospel; on his part they have been accepted through faith; but now in baptism they are further signified and sealed to him. It may therefore not be represented as if only some, or in any case not all, benefits were granted in faith before baptism, and the missing ones were then still conferred in baptism. For the Word contains all the promises, and faith accepts them all. There is no single grace that is not distributed through the Word and only through the sacrament. Incorporation into the body of Christ also occurs through faith and receives its sign and seal in baptism. Baptismal grace consists, and according to Scripture and the Reformed confession can nowhere else consist, than in *declaratio* and *confirmatio*, *Heid. Catech.* 66, 69. Cf. further Calvin, *Inst.* IV 15; Martyr, *Loci Comm.*, p. 435; Polanus, *Synt.*, p. 495; Bullinger, *Huysboek*, 1612, fol. 254; Ursinus, *Explic. Cat.*, qu. 69 sq.; etc.
