## 3.

In the ancient Christian church a settled doctrine concerning baptism and a universally valid rite for it are not yet encountered. Nevertheless, *Didache* 7, 1 already knows the trinitarian formula, while Hermas, *Vis.* III 7, still speaks of baptism in the name of the Lord, or in *Sim.* IX 16 calls it a seal of the Son of God that gives life. The operations of baptism are especially forgiveness of former sins and a new, supernatural, eternal life through the Holy Spirit: Justin, *Apol.* I 61; Tertullian, *de baptismo* 4, 5; Cyprian, *de grat.* 3, 4; Gregory Nazianzen, *Or.* 40, 3 sq. Although it is clearly said that the water in baptism retains its nature, the connection between sign and signified thing is mystically conceived: εἰ τις ἐστιν ἐν τῳ ὑδατι χαρις, οὐκ ἐκ της φυσεως ἐστι του ὑδατος, ἀλλ’ ἐκ της του πνευματος παρουσιας, Basil, *de spir. sancto*, ch. 15; Δι’ ἐκεινου του ὑδατος ἡ θεια χαρις την αἰωνιον δωρειται ζωην, Theodoret, qu. 26 on Genesis, in Suicerus, s.v. βαπτισμα; cf. Tertullian, *de bapt.* 4. Baptism is therefore also designated by all kinds of names borrowed from the mysteries, φωτισμος, μυστηριον τελετη, τελειωσις, μυησις, μυσταγωγια, and is regarded as ὀχημα προς οὐρανον, ὀχημα προς θεον, κλεις οὐρανων βασιλειας: Schwane, *D. G.* II 735; Hatch, *Griech. u. Christ.*, 219; Suicerus, s.v. And when, from the second century onward, the worship service fell apart into a public and a private portion, the administration of baptism and the Supper increasingly assumed a mysterious character intelligible only to the initiated. Preceded by the catechumenate, baptism itself was surrounded by all kinds of symbolic acts, such as the presentation of the person to be baptized by sponsors, the making of confession, blowing upon the face and marking with the cross, placing consecrated salt in the mouth of the person to be baptized, exorcism, threefold immersion or sprinkling, anointing with chrism, giving a new name, clothing with a white garment, presentation of a burning candle, reception into the congregation, the brotherly kiss, and sometimes the celebration of the Supper immediately thereafter: Suicerus, s.v.; Moeller-von Schubert, *Kirchengesch.* I² 339; cf. *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 45 sq.; Bellarminus, *de bapt.*, chs. 24–27. Thus, while in the apostolic age baptism immediately followed conversion and was administered in the simplest manner, Acts 2:38, 41; 8:12, 36; 10:47; etc., from the second century onward it was enveloped in an ever-expanding ritual and changed into a magical and mystical means of grace. Even Augustine promoted the development of the doctrine of baptism in this spirit, although in adults he required prior faith and repentance for a salutary operation of baptism. For first, he says that baptism gives forgiveness of sins and regeneration only within the church. The sacrament is a sacrament of Christ and was given by him to his church. Heretics and schismatics can indeed carry it away outside the church, but it is then a stolen and unlawfully possessed good and therefore exercises no salutary operation but serves *ad perniciem*, *de unit. eccl.* 68; *de bapt.* 3, 13; 5, 7 sq. Second, in the case of children he appears to teach a salutary operation of baptism *ex opere operato*. Children dying unbaptized are lost, *de anima* I 9; III 12; *de pecc. mer.* I 20; *de nat. et gr.* 8. But in those who are baptized, baptism itself or the intercession of the church or the faith of the parents replaces the faith they themselves cannot yet exercise, *de pecc. mer.* I 19, 34 sq. Third, Augustine in any case ascribes to baptism the operation of a *character indelebilis*, by which the baptized belong *rechtens* to Christ and his church and, if necessary, may be brought back under her care by coercion, *de bapt.* V 21; VI 1; *c. epist. Parmen.* II 16; cf. Dorner, *Augustinus*, 248f.; Schwane, *D. G.* II 744f.; Harnack, *D. G.* III 143f. At first scholasticism still stood with Augustine and acknowledged that in adults baptism presupposed faith and was also not absolutely necessary to salvation. But it nevertheless moved increasingly in the direction of having the sacrament work *ex opere operato* and causing the subjective requirements continually to lose significance: Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dists. 3–6; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qus. 66–71; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VI 7; Schwane, *D. G.* III 605–622; Harnack, *D. G.* III 478f. Thus Rome’s doctrine of baptism was prepared, which in brief amounts to this: baptism is the first sacrament, the door to spiritual life, the entrance into the church. It gives the first supernatural grace, which is presupposed and increased by the other sacraments, and is therefore absolutely necessary to salvation, apart from a few cases in which it can be replaced by a *baptismus sanguinis* or *flaminis (voti)*. It must therefore also be administered as soon as possible and, in a case of necessity, by laity or non-Christians. For through this baptism are communicated: 1º the *character indelebilis* that brings a person under the jurisdiction of the church; 2º the forgiveness of all sins, both original and actual sins committed before baptism, and remission of all eternal and also all temporal penalties insofar as they are *opera satisfactionis*, but not insofar as they are natural penalties of sins; 3º the spiritual renewal and sanctification of the human being through the infusion of sanctifying grace and the supernatural virtues of faith, hope, and love, so that the stain of original sin is entirely destroyed and there remains only the *concupiscentia* proper by nature to the human being as a physical being, which is not itself sin but can indeed become an occasion for sinning; 4º incorporation into the communion of saints and into the visible church of believers. Baptism exercises these operations because, with the pronouncement of the familiar formula, the Word of God or the power of the Holy Spirit unites with the water in a mysterious way and makes it an *aqua viva et efficax*, a *uterus maternus* of the new human being. In fact, through the sacrament of baptism are regenerated not only all children but also all adults who have fulfilled the seven *praeparationes* and place no *obex* in the way. Cf. *Conc. Flor.* in Denzinger, no. 591; *Trid.* VI 4; VII, *de bapt.*; XIV, *de poenit.* 2; *Cat. Rom.* II 2; Bellarminus, *de sacr. bapt.*, chs. 1–27; Oswald, *Die dogm. Lehre v. d. h. Sakr.* I² 141f.; etc.; and for the doctrine of the Greek church, Damasc., *de fide orthod.* IV 14; *Conf. orth.*, qus. 102, 103; *Conf. Dosith.*, decr. 16; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Conf.* I 400f.
