## 5.

Today most churches know baptism almost only as infant baptism. Apart from the mission field and Baptist societies, the baptism of adults occurs only as an exception. Yet in Scripture the opposite is the case. It nowhere speaks of infant baptism in so many words; it always proceeds from adult baptism. And the Christian confessions and theologians have always followed it in this respect, that they took their starting point in the baptism of adults and only afterward passed over to infant baptism. Now this baptism was instituted at God’s command by John and thereafter by Jesus because the whole world was condemnable before God. This applied not only to the Gentiles but also to the Jews, who sought to establish their own righteousness from works of the law and therefore did not attain to the law of righteousness, Rom. 9:31. For already the prophets proclaimed that in the future God, who is faithful and remembers his covenant, would give Israel repentance and life, a new heart and a new spirit, forgive them all their sins, pour out his Spirit upon them, sprinkle clean water upon them, and cleanse them from all impurities, Hos. 6:2; Joel 2:28, 29; Mic. 7:18–20; Isa. 1:16; 4:4; Jer. 31:31–34; 33:8; Ezek. 11:17–20; 36:25–28; 37:1–14; 39:29; Zech. 13:1; etc. Regeneration, repentance, and faith were necessary for Israel as well as the Gentiles in order to enter the kingdom of heaven and partake of its goods. John and Jesus appeared with that preaching, and those who accepted it were baptized. Thus the offering and acceptance of the Word of the gospel preceded baptism. Scripture leaves not the least doubt that baptism was instituted exclusively for believers. No persons other than those who confess their sins and give evidence of repentance and faith are baptized, Matt. 3:2, 6; Acts 2:37, 38; 8:12, 37; 18:8. Baptism is therefore called a baptism of repentance so that along that way one might obtain forgiveness of sins, Mark 1:4; Acts 13:24. In Matt. 28:19 the two participles βαπτιζοντες and διδασκοντες do indeed indicate the way in which the μαθητευειν παντα τα ἐθνη must be accomplished, but baptism into the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit presupposes precisely the preceding preaching of and faith in that name, as is also clearly expressed in Mark 16:15, 16, and in John 4:1 the making of disciples precedes baptism. It is children of God through faith in Christ Jesus who have put on Christ through baptism, Gal. 3:26, 27. As long as the baptism of adults is under discussion, there is no disagreement about this among the Christian churches. No church baptizes an adult without prior instruction in the truth and a prior profession of faith. Even Rome acknowledges that in an adult the seven *praeparationes* must precede baptism, and makes not the objective validity but nevertheless the subjective operation dependent upon an *intentio virtualis* as *conditio sine qua non* in the recipient, *Trid.* VI, chs. 5, 7; *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qus. 30, 44. But Rome increasingly weakened these subjective conditions in the recipient and shifted the center of gravity from the Word and faith to the sacrament. For this sacrament works *ex opere operato* without requiring anything in the recipient other than a negative *obicem non ponere*. Like sins, the benefits of grace are endlessly divided by Rome, distributed in pieces and portions here and hereafter. It is always the same idea of hierarchy that makes its influence felt here in the doctrine of grace as everywhere else. Therefore Rome also teaches that preaching and faith have merely preparatory significance. Proper, sanctifying, supernatural grace is communicated only through the sacrament of baptism, which therefore, apart from a few cases in which it is replaced by the *baptismus sanguinis* or *flaminis*, is absolutely necessary to salvation for all human beings, adults and children. Over against this the Reformation set the scriptural principle that the sacrament communicates and can communicate no single benefit that the believer does not already possess through his trust in the Word of God. Faith alone, considered apart from every sacrament, places one in possession and enjoyment of all the benefits of salvation. If baptism now presupposes this faith, no single benefit remains that could still be communicated to the believer through baptism. Baptism can only signify and seal the benefits received through faith and thereby strengthen faith, *Ned. Gel.*, arts. 33, 34; *Heid. Cat.*, qu. 69. The Lutherans too agree with this for the baptism of adults, who were previously regenerated and made profession of their faith. Just as faith and the gift of the Holy Spirit are increased in the regenerate through the preaching of the Word, *ita quoque idem fit per baptismum, quin et baptismus donum regenerationis in illis efficaciter obsignat*, Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 123; Quenstedt IV 145; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.*, 400, 407. A Protestant principle is at stake here. Whoever ascribes to baptism a communication of grace that cannot be obtained through the Word and faith opens the door to the Roman doctrine of the sacraments.

The *forma* of baptism consists in a connection established by God between a visible sign and an invisible spiritual good. Water serves as the sign, Matt. 3:6; Acts 8:36, chosen not accidentally or arbitrarily but because of its striking correspondence with the thing signified. What unclean, defiling, and suffocating matter is for the body, sin is for the soul; and just as water washes away the uncleanness of the body, so the blood of Christ cleanses from all sins. Among almost all peoples and in all religions, therefore, water has a rich symbolic significance. Serving in all kinds of washings, it foreshadowed the spiritual cleansing every human being needs in order to live in communion with God. In Old Testament worship water occupied a broad place, Ex. 30:18–20; 40:30; Lev. 6:28; 8:6; 11:32; 15:12; Num. 8:7; 19:7f.; etc.; and the prophets represented the spiritual cleansing of the people as a sprinkling with water, Ezek. 36:25; 37:23; Zech. 13:1. Of itself and by nature—that is, by virtue of the nature God gave it at creation—water is therefore excellently suited to depict and assure in baptism the washing away of sins and spiritual renewal. Therefore it is also unnecessary, as Rome maintains, that the baptismal water be consecrated beforehand on Easter or Pentecost Saturday and mixed with oil, *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 47. Much less may the use of water be omitted with the Paulicians because Christ is the living water, or baptism be replaced with other sects by branding a mark or by scourging until blood is drawn, Moor V 409–411. It is even superfluous to concede with Beza and others that when water is lacking another liquid may be used, for such a case is virtually impossible, M. Vitringa VII 14. In the first period the act of baptizing consisted in the person being immersed in the water and, after a moment, raised from it again. The Greek word βαπτιζω already points to this, for it literally means to dip, immerse, John 13:26, and even when it is used in a broader sense for washing, Matt. 15:2; Mark 7:4; Luke 11:38; Heb. 9:10, or figuratively, Matt. 3:11; 20:22; Acts 1:5; etc., it denotes an act in which the person or thing baptized is entirely immersed and cleansed. Further, the cases Scripture relates clearly demonstrate that in the apostolic age baptism took place by immersion, Matt. 3:6; John 3:23; Acts 8:38. Finally, the *phraseologia sacramentalis* is entirely built upon this manner of administering baptism, Rom. 6:3, 4; Gal. 3:27; Col. 2:12. For centuries *immersio* accordingly remained in use in the Christian church; the Greek church still adheres to it. Sprinkling (*adspersio*), or rather pouring (*infusio*), occurred in ancient times only when there was not enough water, *Didache*, ch. 7, or when the sick had to be baptized upon their bed (*baptismus clinicorum*). Cyprian, *Ep.* 69, 12, defended *adspersio* or *perfusio* in the latter case by appeal to Ezek. 36:25, but otherwise the church fathers always speak of baptism as an immersion in water, Suicerus, s.v. ἀναδυω. In 754 Pope Stephen II permitted baptism *per infusionem* in a case of necessity for children and the sick, but a council of the year 816 still prescribed to priests, *ut non effundant aquam super capita infantium sed semper mergantur in lavacro*. Thomas said, *tutius est baptizare per immersionem, quia hoc habet usus communis*, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 66, art. 7. The Council of Ravenna in 1311 left the choice between *immersio* and *superfusio* free. Thus until the 13th century immersion still occurs alongside sprinkling in the West, but then the latter increasingly becomes general. When in Christianized Europe adult baptism became the exception and infant baptism the rule, the manner of administering baptism also changed, not from dogmatic but from hygienic considerations. Children were in a certain sense all *in infirmitate positi*. The Reformers aligned themselves with this usage. Luther preferred immersion; Calvin regarded the question as an *adiaphoron*; but the Anabaptists made it a principle and therefore returned to *immersio*. And this alone is what needs to be contested. There is no doubt that immersion was generally in use in ancient times, is still permissible, and brings out the rich significance of baptism better than sprinkling. But it is not to be made into a principle. For 1º the water is not the blood of Christ itself and does not itself work the washing away of sins, but is a sign and seal of it. Thus in baptism it cannot depend upon the quantity of water poured upon the person to be baptized or in which he is immersed. 2º The spiritual benefit depicted through baptism is called not only a washing away of sins but also a sprinkling with clean water and with the blood of Christ, Ezek. 36:25; Heb. 12:24; 1 Pet. 1:2; cf. Ex. 24:6; 29:16, 20. 3º Although immersion remained in use for centuries, from the earliest times sprinkling was nevertheless regarded as permissible in cases of necessity. The Christian church never thought of regarding baptism as invalid merely because it was administered by sprinkling, and in practice advocates of immersion usually themselves recoil from this consequence. 4º Although, despite the threefold immersion customary from ancient times, *Didache*, ch. 7, it must be maintained with Gregory the Great that *utrum unica an trina ablutio fiat, nihil referre existimandum est*, *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 14, sprinkling may nevertheless not take place to so slight a degree that every idea of washing is lost. Just as the Supper, however reduced, must remain a meal, so also in sprinkling with baptismal water the symbolism of washing must be retained. Cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 15, 19; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 683–694; Moor V 413–421; M. Vitringa VII 16–30; Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* I 46–60; De Hoop Scheffer, *Overzicht der gesch. van den doop bij onderdompeling*, Amsterdam, 1882.
