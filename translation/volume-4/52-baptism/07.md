## 7.

To this point there is agreement in the main among the Christian churches in the doctrine of baptism. But all kinds of disagreement appear as soon as infant baptism comes under discussion. From the beginning of its introduction to the present day it has been rejected by a considerable part of Christendom, especially on these two grounds: that it does not occur in Scripture and, according to its original institution, always presupposes faith and repentance, which do not occur in children or in any case cannot be revealed and recognized in them. Cf. William Wall, *The History of Infant Baptism*, 4 vols., new ed., Oxford, 1836; A. H. Newman, *A History of Antipaedobaptism from the Rise of Paedobaptism to A.D. 1609*, Philadelphia: American Baptist Publication Society, 1897; Strong, *Syst. Theol.*, 534–538. Indeed, until the time of Tertullian all direct and positive testimony that baptism was administered to children of believers is lacking. Yet too much may not be inferred from this silence. It goes without saying that in the first and second centuries, when the Christian church was rapidly expanding in the world, proselyte baptism drew far more attention than infant baptism. First adult baptism was the ordinary, repeatedly occurring baptism; alongside it infant baptism then gradually arose; and finally, when the church was established and one people after another had been Christianized, infant baptism became the rule and proselyte baptism, apart from pagan countries, the exception. Accordingly, when Tertullian first mentions infant baptism, *de bapt.* 18, he does indeed oppose it, but not on the ground that it is an innovation and was not customary in the apostolic age, but because his conviction in general is that *cunctatio baptismi utilior est. Si qui pondus intelligant baptismi, magis timebunt consecutionem quam dilationem*. Tertullian did not stand alone in this conviction. As long as Christianity could still expand among pagans in the villages, cities, and countries where it was established, and transitions thus still continually took place, many held that one could do no better than postpone baptism as long as possible, because otherwise one ran the danger of later falling again into sins and losing the grace received in baptism. But Tertullian was the only one who wished to have this view apply also to the children of believers. The church, including in Africa, nevertheless paid no heed to this opposition and continued with infant baptism. Origen testifies that in his days infant baptism was in general use and derived from the apostles. And Cyprian, in agreement with the council held at Carthage in 256, defends the administration of infant baptism not only on the eighth but already on the second or third day after birth. Cf. Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* I 104f. As soon as infant baptism became the rule and adult baptism the exception, its significance naturally had to be brought more fully to light and its legitimacy defended over against all kinds of opponents. This happened in different ways. 1º When Augustine defended original sin over against the Pelagians and thus also the necessity of baptism for children, he had to give an account of the right children had to baptism. Confessing that baptism was instituted only for believers and nevertheless acknowledging that children could not themselves believe, he therefore appealed to the faith of the parents who presented the child for baptism and answered in its place. *Pie recteque creditur, prodesse parvulo eorum fidem, a quibus consecrandus offertur*, *de lib. arb.* III 23. Children of believers must themselves be reckoned among believers, for they believe *fide parentum*. *Credit in altero, qui peccavit in altero*, *de verbis apost.*, *sermo de bapt. parv. c. Pelag.*, ch. 14. And not only the parents’ faith but that of the whole church benefits them: *offeruntur quippe parvuli ad percipiendum spiritalem gratiam, non tam ab eis quorum gestantur manibus, quamvis et ab ipsis, si et ipsi boni fideles sunt, quam ab universa societate sanctorum atque fidelium*, *ad Bonif.*, ep. 25. Upon this ground, according to Augustine, the children of believers have a right to baptism, and in that baptism they themselves partake of the forgiveness of sins and regeneration, yet with the qualification that *quod baptizatur parvulus, si ad rationales annos veniens non crediderit, nec se ab illicitis concupiscentiis abstinuerit, nihil ei proderit quod parvus accepit*, *de pecc. mer. et rem.* I 20. Cf. *Bibl. studii theol.*, Chrispin, 1565, pp. 115–128; and further the same conception among theologians of all kinds of orientations: Lombard et al. on *Sent.* IV, dist. 4; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 68, art. 9; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VI 7; *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qus. 27, 30; Bellarminus, *de bapt.* I 10, 11; Luther in Köstlin I 236, 352; II 88; Calvin, *C. R.* VIII 483, 493; Beza, *Tract. theol.* III 345; and many others—Oecolampadius, Zanchius, Perkins, Bucanus, Marlorat, Rivetus, Venema, Hartmann; cf. M. Vitringa VII 136; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 148; C. Vitringa, *Observ. Sacrae* II, ch. 6; Kalchreuter, “Der stellvertretende Glaube und die Kindertaufe,” *Jahrb. f. d. Th.*, 1866, pp. 523–544. 2º Such a *fides aliena*, however, cannot compensate for the lack of personal faith in the child and therefore imperceptibly leads to the doctrine of a regeneration through baptism. The faith of the parents or the church may give the child the right to be baptized, but in the child itself nothing is required other than, at most, a naturally present *capacitas passiva*, a negative *obicem non ponere*. Therefore the child, who is as it were commended to God with prayers by the whole church, receives in baptism itself the grace it needs. But the grace the child receives is again described very differently. Some scholastics said that at baptism no virtues were infused into the children *actu* or *habitu* or *radice*, but that these were communicated to them later when they grew up or, if they died, granted at the separation of the soul from the body. Others believed that at baptism the children received the virtues either *secundum radicem* or *secundum habitum*: commentaries on *Sent.* IV, dist. 4; for example, Bonaventure, ibid., pars 2, art. 2, qu. 2; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 69, art. 6. Trent established that the sacraments of the New Testament contain grace within themselves and communicate it to all who place no obstacle in the way, so that children too receive grace and the virtues in baptism *ex opere operato* and are not believers beforehand but become believers through baptism, *Trid.* VII, cans. 6–8; *de bapt.*, chs. 13, 14; cf. Bellarminus, *de bapt.* I 10, 11. The Lutherans disputed both that children had faith before baptism and that they were baptized *in aliena fide*, but taught that they received faith in baptism, and indeed not merely *habitu* or *potentia*, but even *actu*. *Per baptismum et in baptismo Spiritus S. fidem veram, salvificam, vivificam et actualem accendit in infantibus, unde et infantes baptizati vere credunt*, Quenstedt IV 147. Some Reformed theologians too—Pareus, Baronius, Forbesius à Corse, Davenant, Ward, de Brais in Saumur, and others—taught that in baptism a certain grace of forgiveness and regeneration was bestowed upon all children, which, when they died young, was sufficient for salvation, but otherwise had to be accepted and confirmed on their part through personal faith. Cf. Witsius, *de efficacia baptismi in infantibus*, *Misc. Sacra* II 618; Voetius, II *Disp.*, 409; M. Vitringa VII 72. And with this agrees the High Churchmen’s doctrine of a *baptismal regeneration*. 3º But this doctrine is burdened by many difficulties. The *fides aliena*, which at first was still maintained by Augustine and others as a reminder of the faith required for baptism according to Scripture, becomes wholly superfluous if baptism communicates grace *ex opere operato* and presupposes nothing in the child other than a *capacitas passiva*. If children can already receive the grace of baptism when they place no *obex* in the way, it is advisable to baptize as many children as possible, including pagan children, for they are all passive and thus all capable of receiving grace. The Scotist school accordingly defended this over against the Thomist school and determined the practice of the Roman church, Schwane, *D. G.* III 621. Second, baptism is robbed of its scriptural character, since it is detached from faith and the Word, ceases to be a sign and seal of God’s promises, becomes an autonomous, independent means of grace working *ex opere operato*, and even occupies the first and principal place among the means of grace. Finally, the benefits communicated by baptism are exaggerated on the one hand and weakened on the other. For in view of the facts supplied by Scripture and experience, no one can maintain that all baptized children later prove to be believers and are saved. One must then assume that the benefits bestowed in baptism can be lost and must later be accepted through personal faith. They are sufficient for salvation for children dying young and insufficient for children who grow up, and place the latter in a doubtful condition between believers and unbelievers: *Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol.* VI 282–287; VII 493–495; Moor V 489; Witsius, loc. cit. The Reformed therefore returned to Scripture and in defending infant baptism unanimously took their standpoint in the covenant of grace, which according to God’s promise embraced not only believers but also their seed. Not regeneration, faith, or repentance, and much less our conjecture concerning them, but only the covenant of grace gave both adults and children the right to baptism: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 16, 23, 24; cf. Kramer, *Het verband van doop en wedergeb.*, 122. This covenant was the firm, scriptural, objective ground upon which all the Reformed, jointly and without distinction, caused the right of infant baptism to rest; they had no other, deeper, firmer ground. But the Anabaptists always adduced a second proof against infant baptism. They maintained not only that infant baptism did not occur in Scripture but also that children could neither have nor display faith and repentance and therefore might not be baptized. Over against this the Reformed argued that children could indeed possess not, as the Lutherans held, the *actus fidei*, but certainly the *habitus fidei*. They expressed themselves very differently. People spoke of *fides in semine, in radice, in inclinatione, in potentia, in habitu, in principio, in virtute interna Spiritus*; of *semen regenerationis*; and so forth; cf. M. Vitringa VII 134. But in the matter itself there was complete agreement. Upon the basis of Scripture, Jer. 1:5; Luke 1:15, and in conformity with the catholicity of the Christian religion, all the Reformed maintained over against the Anabaptists that little children, just as much as adults, could be accepted in grace by God, regenerated by his Spirit, and endowed with the seed of faith. And with this they had enough over against the Anabaptists. The mutual differences that arose as soon as they began to elaborate and apply their principles—above, p. 226—receded into the background in comparison with this common conviction.
