## 8.

The right of infant baptism depends exclusively upon how Scripture regards the children of believers and therefore wills that we should regard them. If Scripture speaks of such children in the same way as it speaks of adult believers, then the right, and with it also the duty, of infant baptism is established; for we may not withhold from children what we grant to adults. Thus in the baptism of children it is impermissible to require less, but just as impermissible to require more, than in the baptism of adults. In the latter case, according to Scripture, we are and must be satisfied that someone professes his faith. We are never entirely certain that someone is not a hypocrite and thus receives the sacrament without authorization, but the judgment concerning this does not belong to us; *de intimis non judicat ecclesia*. So it is also with infant baptism. Whoever wants absolute certainty can never distribute any sacrament. The question is only whether the certainty that in the children of believers we are dealing with believers is the same as the certainty we possess concerning those who profess their faith at an adult age. We do not need and may not require another, stronger certainty. When the question is posed in this way, Scripture gives a clear answer. 1º First of all, the surprise must be removed that the New Testament nowhere speaks of infant baptism in so many words. This fact is explained by adult baptism being the rule in the days of the New Testament and infant baptism, if it occurred at all, the exception. It was the time in which the Christian church was founded and expanded through transition from Judaism and paganism. And it was precisely that transition which was clearly depicted in baptism. Adult baptism is therefore the original baptism; infant baptism is derivative. The former must not be conformed to the latter, but the latter to the former. This does not invalidate the right of infant baptism, nor for its maintenance does it need tradition, as Rome maintains; for what is deduced from Scripture by lawful inference is just as binding as what is expressly mentioned in it. The church acts in this way at every moment in the ministry of the Word, in the practice of life, and in the development of doctrine. She never remains with the letter but under the guidance of the Holy Spirit draws from the data of Scripture inferences and applications that make her life and development possible and promote them. And she acts in this way too when she passes from adult baptism to infant baptism. Scripture indicates the general rule when baptism may and must be applied, and the church applies this rule concretely in life. Scripture nowhere needs to say that children may be baptized; it says enough when it regards children in the same way as adults who have come to profession of faith and never mentions the administration of baptism to such adults as were born of Christian parents. 2º In the Old Testament circumcision was administered to male children on the eighth day after their birth. According to Col. 2:11, 12, this circumcision was replaced by baptism. For the Colossians, although Christians from the Gentiles, are circumcised just as much as the Jews. But they are circumcised not with a carnal circumcision performed by hands, which consists in the putting off of the σωμα της σαρκος, of the whole carnal, sinful nature. And it took place in Christ, by means and by virtue of the circumcision Christ himself underwent with respect to sin in his death, at the moment when in baptism they were buried and raised with Christ. Through Christ’s death, which was a complete putting off and conquest of sin and thus fully realized the idea of circumcision, that circumcision became obsolete and came to its antitypical fulfillment in baptism. Thus baptism is more than circumcision, not in essence but in degree. Circumcision pointed forward to Christ’s death; baptism points back to it. The former ends, the latter begins, with that death. If, however, that circumcision might and had to be administered to children already as a sign of the covenant, this applies *a fortiori* to baptism, which is not poorer but much richer in grace. This is also evident in the fact that the sacrament of the Old Covenant was administered only to male persons, but that of the New Covenant also to female persons; and even the opponents of infant baptism acknowledge in this respect the richer grace of baptism. Sin, in fact, bears among human beings the character of σαρξ. It reveals itself especially in the organs of reproduction and there demonstrates its power. Circumcision brings this to light, as does also the woman’s uncleanness after giving birth. But through his death, which is the true circumcision, Christ removed all sin, including that which adheres to reproduction. He placed the woman in an independent relation to himself; he causes her to share in his grace just as much as the man. In him there is no male or female; and therefore both are buried with Christ in baptism and raised to a new life. Finally, the richer grace of the sacrament of the New Covenant is further evident in the fact that circumcision might be performed only on the eighth day after birth, for until then the children still shared in the mother’s uncleanness; but now, in the days of the New Testament, children have the right to baptism from their birth onward, since from the first moment of their existence they share in the grace of Christ. 3º Circumcision is by no means the only proof that the Old Testament regards children as participants in the covenant. The whole idea of the covenant entails this conception. For the covenant is distinguished from election in that it indicates how the latter realizes itself along an organic and historical way. It is never concluded with one individual person alone but in that individual immediately also with his seed. It never embraces only the person of the believer in the abstract, but that person concretely, as he historically exists and lives; thus not only him but also all that is his; not only him as a person but also him as father or mother, with his household, with his money and property, with his influence and power, and so forth; vol. III, 226. Specifically, the children are reckoned in him. There is a communion of parents and children in sin and misery, ibid., 129f. But over against this God also established a communion of parents and children in grace and blessing. Children are a blessing and an inheritance from the Lord, Ps. 127:3. They are always reckoned with the parents and taken together with them. It goes well with them together, Ex. 20:6; Deut. 1:36, 39; 4:40; 5:29; 12:25, 28. They serve the Lord together, Deut. 6:2; 30:2; 31:12, 13; Josh. 24:15; Jer. 32:39; Ezek. 37:25; Zech. 10:9. The deeds and ordinances of God must be handed down by the parents to the children, Ex. 10:2; 12:24, 26; Deut. 4:9, 10, 40; 6:7; 11:19; 29:29; Josh. 4:6, 21; 22:24–27. God’s covenant with its benefits and blessings continues from child to child and from generation to generation, Gen. 9:12; 17:7, 9; Ex. 3:15; 12:17; 16:32; Deut. 7:9; Ps. 105:8; etc. Grace is no inherited possession, but as a rule it is nevertheless distributed along the line of generations. *Piorum infantibus primus ad salutem aditus est ipsa ex piis parentibus propagatio*, Beza, *Resp. ad coll. Mompelg.* II 103, in Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 211. 4º This conception passes over into the New Testament. Jesus, like John, appears with the preaching: repent and believe the gospel. He adopts John’s baptism and in it proclaims that despite their circumcision the Jews need repentance and forgiveness. The opposition gradually becomes so strong that Jesus no longer has any expectation from his people, and the people reject him and hang him upon the cross. Yet despite this he continues to regard the children as children of the covenant, Matt. 18:2f.; 19:13f.; 21:15f.; Mark 10:13f.; Luke 9:48; 18:15. He calls them to himself, embraces them, lays hands upon them, blesses them, says that the kingdom of heaven belongs to them, sets them before adults as an example, warns the latter not to cause them to stumble, says that their angels watch over them, and sees in their cry of Hosanna a fulfillment of the prophecy that God has made the speech of children into a power by which those who hate him are silenced and has prepared praise, αἰνον according to the LXX, from their mouth. 5º The apostles proceed from the same thought. The covenant of grace established with Israel was indeed changed with respect to its dispensation but remained the same in essence, vol. III, 216f. The ἐκκλησια took the place of Old Testament Israel; she is God’s people and God is her God and Father, Matt. 1:21; Luke 1:17; Acts 3:25; Rom. 9:25, 26; 11:16–21; 2 Cor. 6:16–18; Gal. 3:14–29; Eph. 2:12, 13; Titus 2:14; Heb. 8:8–10; 1 Pet. 2:9; Rev. 21:3. As in the Old Testament, the children of believers are also included among that people of God. For the congregation of the New Testament is no group of individuals but an organism, a body, a temple, and as such, as a people, took the place of Israel. She is like a wild olive tree grafted upon the trunk of the cultivated olive tree, while some branches were cut off, and thus came to partake of its root and fatness, Rom. 11:16, 17. Therefore whole households sometimes pass over to Christianity. The household itself is an institution of God, an organic whole that shares in a common blessing or curse. Jesus’ disciples bring peace to the house they enter, Luke 10:5, and he himself says that when Zacchaeus believes, salvation has come to his house, Luke 19:9. The apostles teach not only in the temple but also repeatedly proclaim the gospel of Christ in houses, Acts 5:42; 20:20. With the head of the household the whole household is saved, Acts 11:14; 16:31, and entire households believe and are baptized, Acts 16:15, 34; 18:8; 1 Cor. 1:16. From this it cannot indeed be proved that infant baptism was already practiced by the apostles, but neither can the opposite be inferred from the silence. From the early introduction of infant baptism, the general recognition it immediately found, and the testimony of Origen follows the possibility and even probability that it was already an apostolic practice. Further, Peter says that the promise of the Old Covenant, that God would be the God of believers and of their seed, passes over into the dispensation of the New Testament, Acts 2:39. This does indeed apply first of all to the Jews, and the Gentiles are mentioned only in the words “and all who are far off.” But this does not alter the fact that the Jews who convert to Christ receive the promise of the covenant not only for themselves but also for their children; and the Gentiles who come to faith share in the same privileges and according to the whole New Testament are in no respect inferior to believers from the Jews. According to Paul, 1 Cor. 7:14, even children from a household in which one of the two parents has become a believer are holy. When such a case occurred, the believing spouse must not suppose that he might not continue married life with the other spouse. On the contrary, through the faith of the one spouse the whole marriage, and also the other spouse, is sanctified, ἡγιασται. And Paul proves this by the fact that the children from such a marriage are not ἀκαθαρτα but ἁγια. This was therefore established, generally accepted, and could consequently serve as an argument. Children in a household whose father or mother is a believer are reckoned according to the believing spouse, even if this is the wife of the household. The Christian confession sets the tone in such a house; it is the standard according to which the whole family must be judged; faith is the higher that dominates the lower. The holiness of which Paul speaks here must be understood not as subjective and inward but as objective and theocratic, for otherwise the children and the husband would be holy not through the believing mother and wife but through themselves. Nor is Paul here thinking at all of infant baptism, or of anything intended to serve as a ground for it. Rather, he is concerned only to demonstrate that Christian faith does not break but confirms and sanctifies the natural ordinances of life; cf. vv. 18–24. Yet this passage is important for infant baptism insofar as it teaches that a whole household is reckoned according to the confession of the believing spouse. The believer has the calling to serve the Lord not only by himself but with all that is his and with his whole household. Therefore the children of believers are also admonished by the apostles as Christian children in the Lord, Acts 26:22; Eph. 6:1; Col. 3:20; 1 John 2:13; 2 Tim. 3:15. Little ones too know the Lord, Heb. 8:11; Rev. 11:18; 19:5, and are set before the throne, Rev. 20:12. Holy Scripture knows nothing of a neutral upbringing that wishes to leave children completely free and independent to choose at a mature age, *Trid.*, *de bapt.*, ch. 14. The children of believers are not pagans, nor are they children of the devil who must still, as Roman Catholics and Lutherans teach, be exorcised at baptism; rather, they are children of the covenant, to whom the promise belongs just as much as to adults. They are included in the covenant and are holy *non natura*, Job 14:4; Ps. 51:7; John 3:6; Eph. 2:3, *sed foederis privilegio*, *Heid. Cat.* 74; *Can. Dordr.* I 17. 6º All this presses the more because grace, especially in the dispensation of the New Testament, is much more abundant than sin, Rom. 5:12–21. If the rejection of infant baptism arose solely from its not being commanded in literal words in Scripture, it would have to be judged with indulgence. But ordinarily it is connected with wholly different considerations and flows from a restriction of grace and a failure to recognize the catholicity of Christianity. For Anabaptism—unless it denies original sin and considers regeneration unnecessary for children—places a boundary upon grace in childhood, in not yet having attained years of discretion, and thus in laws and ordinances established by God himself in nature at creation. Grace, however, knows no such boundaries. Under the Old Testament it may in a certain sense have been confined within the people of Israel; in the midst of that people it was as broad as possible. And in the New Testament every boundary of people and land, sex and age, is completely erased. In Christ there is no male or female, no Jew or Greek, no child or old person, but only a new creature. The Father loved the world; Christ is a propitiation for the whole world and also shed his blood for children; and the Holy Spirit, who caused Jesus to be conceived in Mary’s womb and was granted to a Jeremiah and John already from the first moment of their existence, has access to every heart and is hindered by no age or youth. Therefore, just as without their knowledge children partake of condemnation in Adam, in the same way they can again be accepted into grace in Christ. Although they cannot believe *actu*, they can nevertheless be regenerated and in that regeneration at the same time receive the capacity for faith. 7º Through all this the right and thus also the duty of infant baptism have been abundantly demonstrated. For if children of believers are to be regarded as Scripture teaches us, then according to the divine institution of baptism they have a claim to this sacrament in the same and even a stronger measure than adults who make profession. For absolute certainty is obtainable in neither case. We can judge the heart no more in adults than in children. For us, who are bound to the external, only a judgment of charity is ever possible. According to that judgment we regard those who make profession as believers and distribute the sacraments to them; and according to that same judgment we reckon the children of believers among believers themselves, because they are included with their parents in the covenant of grace. The probability that the baptized are true believers is even greater with children than with adults. For not only do the weakening of the significance of baptism, the neglect of discipline, and the deadening power of custom enter a Baptist church just as readily as a church that practices infant baptism, but almost half of human beings die before attaining years of discretion. For all of these, insofar as they are included in the covenant of grace, there is in Scripture a promise of the Lord which they cannot consciously and voluntarily reject. If they die before the time they can do so, godly parents may not doubt their election and salvation, *Can. Dordr.* I 17; cf. Voetius, *Disp.* II 408, 417; M. Vitringa II 51. And even in the case of children who grow up, according to the judgment of charity that must govern in Christ’s church, their salvation may and must be believed as long as the opposite does not become clearly evident. For from the children of believers the congregation, the assembly of true Christian believers, is continually built. 8º Yet it may never be forgotten here that with both adults and children this is a judgment of charity. It is no infallible pronouncement that establishes the salvation of every baptized person, but only a rule according to which Scripture commands us to act in the practice of ecclesiastical life. The ground for baptism is not the presumption that someone is regenerate, nor even that regeneration itself, but only God’s covenant. The subjective opinion of the minister of the Word concerning the spiritual state of the person to be baptized is altogether irrelevant. Whether or not he is personally convinced of the sincerity of faith in the person to be baptized, he must not take account of that but must act according to the revealed will of God and the rule of his Word. Moreover, it does no good to close one’s eyes to the fact that baptism is frequently administered to those who later prove not to walk in the way of the covenant. Scripture and experience both teach that not all is Israel that is called Israel, that there is chaff among the wheat, that in God’s house there are not only vessels of gold and silver but also earthen vessels. By no means all, therefore, were regenerated when they received baptism. It cannot even be proved that the elect are always regenerated by the Holy Spirit in their youth, before baptism, or even before birth. God is free in the distribution of his grace and can cause the fruit of baptism to be enjoyed at a much later age. Therefore in the Christian congregation there also remains room for the preaching of the gospel, of regeneration, faith, and repentance. The prophets, John the Baptist, and Jesus appeared with this in the midst of their people, who nevertheless were the possession of the Lord. And the apostles too ministered the Word not merely to bring hidden life to manifestation, but also preached it as a seed of regeneration and as a means for working faith. 9º Yet the essence of baptism may not therefore be made dependent upon its effect in life. Just as sincere faith remains what it is according to the description of the *Heid. Cat.* 21, even though reality exhibits all kinds of deviations and deformities from it, so baptism too is, and may be nothing other than, what Scripture teaches of it. Genuine, essential, Christian baptism is that administered to believers. Although baptism, like the external calling, still produces many a blessing even for unbelievers, vol. III, 492, its genuine fruit and full power are nevertheless enjoyed only by believers. Objectively baptism, like the Word, remains the same. Whoever receives the Word in faith, and likewise whoever receives baptism in faith, actually partakes of the promises God connected with it. God remains faithful to himself and grants salvation to everyone who believes. But faith does not belong to all. Ultimately the fruit of baptism is enjoyed only by those who are elect and therefore in the Lord’s time also come to faith. All must acquiesce in that outcome, whether Roman Catholic or Protestant, Lutheran or Reformed. *Sacramenta in solis electis efficiunt quod figurant*, said Augustine, and scholasticism repeated it after him; cf. Lombard, Thomas, Bonaventure on *Sent.* IV, dist. 4; and further Calvin on Eph. 5:26; *Inst.* IV 14, 9, 10; *C. R.* VII 694; Beza, *Tract.* III 124; Voetius, *Disp.* II 408; *Westm. Conf.* 28, 6; M. Vitringa VI 90; VII 378. The elect have obtained it, but the others were hardened. The children of the promise are reckoned as the seed. 10º The benefits of baptism are the same for children as for adults—namely, forgiveness of sin, regeneration, and incorporation into the congregation of Christ. And these are not first granted in baptism but are already through faith the portion of the one who receives baptism according to God’s will. Baptism grants no single benefit that was not already promised in the Word and accepted through faith, but it grants the same benefits as the Word, only in another way and in another form, so that faith, according to the measure God granted to each, is thereby confirmed and strengthened. This rule also applies to children. For just as they can be regenerated unconsciously by the Holy Spirit and endowed with the capacity for faith, so they can also by that same Holy Spirit, without their knowledge, be strengthened in that capacity for faith. Here, as in so many domains, there is a mysterious *Wechselwirkung*. Just as light and the eye presuppose and support each other, so faith enjoys the sacrament the more as it is stronger and is at the same time sealed and ratified by it in that same measure. Therefore, as the believer grows, the sacraments do not gradually diminish in significance for him but continually gain in value. They display ever more beautifully and gloriously before the eye of faith the riches of God’s grace. For every believer and for the whole church they are a proof of grace received, a sign of God’s faithfulness, a ground of pleading for prayer, a pillar for faith, an admonition to new obedience.
