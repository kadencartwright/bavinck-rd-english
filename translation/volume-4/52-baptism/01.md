# § 52. Baptism

## 1.

Baptism in the New Testament was prepared in the days of the Old Covenant by circumcision, which God expressly prescribed to Abraham, Gen. 17:10f. According to Herodotus II 36, 104, circumcision also occurred among the Egyptians, Phoenicians, and Syrians. The Reformed, for example Witsius, *Aegypt.* III 6, 11, 12; Marck, *Med. Theol.* 29, 8, sought either to refute this testimony or to demonstrate that these peoples had adopted it from Israel. But this opinion is untenable. Among the Egyptians it was already in use in very ancient times, at least for the priests. And more recent ethnological investigations have established beyond all doubt that circumcision is a ceremony found among numerous peoples in Asia, America, Africa, and even Australia: Delitzsch on Gen. 17; article “Beschneidung” in Herzog³; Glassberg, *Die Beschneidung in ihrer geschichtl. ethnogr. relig. u. medic. Bedeutung*, Berlin: Boas, 1896. Just as at the institution of temple and priesthood, sacrifice and altar, and laws and ordinances among Israel God connects with existing customs among other peoples, so he also does with circumcision. He takes it over, as it were, but gives it a different, sacramental significance. For physical circumcision did indeed occur among the peoples, but there it by no means bore the character of a sacrament. There too it was frequently performed, as among the Egyptians, only upon certain persons, and ordinarily not in the first days of life but at a later age. When God institutes circumcision with Abraham, however, he commands that every male be circumcised, both the servant and the son of the household; that this circumcision take place on the eighth day; and that it serve as a sign of the covenant, so that whoever does not receive it is a covenant breaker and must be cut off from the midst of his people. Although circumcision may therefore also be a sanitary measure, its purpose among Israel is nevertheless not found in that; here it serves as a sign and confirmation of the covenant of grace, whose one great, all-embracing promise is: I will be your God and the God of your seed, Gen. 17:7. Specifically, it is a seal of two benefits of that covenant: of the righteousness of faith, Rom. 4:11, and of the circumcision of the heart, Deut. 10:16; 30:6; Jer. 4:4; Rom. 2:28, 29; Col. 2:11, that is, of justification or the forgiveness of sins and of regeneration or sanctification. Not that it grants these benefits mechanically, for outward circumcision without that of the heart is without value, Acts 7:51; Rom. 2:28, 29; 3:21, 30; 1 Cor. 7:19. It is a seal of the righteousness of faith and therefore presupposes faith. When the Jews increasingly sought to establish their own righteousness from the law, they became condemnable before God just as much as the Gentiles and despite their outward circumcision, Rom. 3:21. Therefore, even before the public appearance of Jesus, God instituted water baptism through John. This baptism too was not something new, any more than circumcision was in ancient times. All antiquity ascribed a symbolic significance to water in religion. The water of the Euphrates, Indus, and Ganges had a reconciling, sanctifying power. Among Greeks and Romans washings were prescribed on all kinds of occasions, for example at initiation into the mysteries: Pfanner, *Syst. Theol. gent.*, 346. Among Israel too, all kinds of washings were already in use long before the divine institution of baptism, Schürer, *Gesch. des jüd. Volkes*³ II 481, and for proselytes, besides circumcision and sacrifice, baptism was also necessary in order to be received into the congregation, ibid., III 129. But this baptism became a sacrament, sign, and seal of grace only through God’s institution. The New Testament therefore also teaches expressly that a ρημα θεου went out to John to baptize, Luke 3:2, 3; that God sent him for this purpose, John 1:33; that his baptism was not ἐξ ἀνθρωπων but ἐξ οὐρανου, Matt. 21:25; and that the tax collectors who were baptized justified God, while the Pharisees and teachers of the law, refusing John’s baptism, rejected the counsel of God, Luke 7:29, 30. With that baptism John preached to the Jews of his day that although they were circumcised and baptized proselytes, they themselves were guilty and unclean and needed baptism in order to enter the kingdom of heaven. John’s baptism was therefore an indictment against and a condemnation of the Jews, a proclamation of their condemnability—but, let it not be forgotten, it was also something more. It was the irrefutable proof that God remembered his covenant and fulfilled his promise. Although the Jews were guilty and unclean, there was nevertheless forgiveness with God; and this would now reveal itself still more richly than in the days of the Old Testament. John the Baptist is therefore not to be regarded as the last of the Old Testament prophets, for all the prophets and the law prophesied until John, ἑως Ιωαννου, Matt. 11:13, but as the herald of the coming kingdom of God, Matt. 3:2; as the preacher of the approaching gospel, Luke 3:18; as the forerunner of Christ, Mark 1:2; as the witness to the dawning light, John 1:7, 29, 34, 36; cf. Matt. 3:11; Mark 1:7; Luke 3:16; Acts 19:4, who presently makes way for the greater one and directs his disciples to him, John 1:35f.; 3:27f. His baptism corresponds to this content of his preaching. It was a βαπτισμα μετανοιας εἰς ἀφεσιν ἁμαρτιων, Mark 1:4, which does not mean that John’s baptism merely prepared for the forgiveness later to be received through Christ (Meyer), but quite specifically that it granted forgiveness in the way of repentance. For it is said in the same way in Acts 2:38 of Christian baptism that it occurred εἰς ἀφεσιν ἁμαρτιων, because through baptism as sign and seal one obtains forgiveness. Furthermore, Jesus himself was baptized with John’s baptism; he makes no distinction whatever between the baptism administered by his disciples and that of John, John 3:22, 23; 4:1; simply takes over the disciples baptized by John without rebaptizing them, John 1:37; Acts 18:25; and in Matt. 28:19 does not institute another or new baptism but only extends it to all nations. On these grounds the essential identity of Johannine and Christian baptism, notwithstanding a gradual difference, was maintained by the Reformed—Calvin, *Inst.* IV 15, 7, 8; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 4, 17; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XIX 16; M. Vitringa VII 52—and the Lutherans—Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 15 sq., 43 sq. But this was disputed by the Roman Catholics—Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 66, art. 4; *Trid.*, sess. VII, can. 1; Bellarmine, *de bapt.* I 5—by the Anabaptists, Socinians, Arminians, and many more recent theologians—Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.* § 136, 1; Steitz in Herzog¹ 15, 468; Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* I 26f.; Van Oosterzee, *Dogm.* § 87, 2; etc. There are indeed weighty objections that can be brought against the identity. First, from Matt. 3:11; Mark 1:8; Luke 3:16 the objection is derived that the baptism of John and Christian baptism stand over against each other as water baptism and as baptism with the Spirit and fire. But Acts 1:5 clearly teaches that John here sets not his baptism over against Christian baptism but the baptism of the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost, metaphorically so called. Proper Christian baptism, after all, is also a baptism with water, signifying the washing away of sins; and John’s baptism was likewise a baptism with water, but one that at the same time sealed repentance and forgiveness. Both baptisms therefore agree entirely in sign and thing signified. Otherwise the absurd conclusion would also follow that not only John’s baptism but also the baptism Jesus himself had his disciples administer before the day of Pentecost was nothing but a baptism with water. And even the opponents of the identity of the baptism of John and that of Christ do not dare maintain this. They ordinarily say that Christian baptism was instituted either at Jesus’ baptism by John or when Jesus himself baptized through his disciples. But both baptisms, that of Jesus as well as that of John, were distinct from that baptism of the Spirit that would take place on the day of Pentecost, even though both also signified and sealed the same benefits of repentance and the forgiveness of sins. But now—and this is the second objection against the identity intended above—the baptism of the Spirit on the day of Pentecost is nevertheless in turn connected with Christian water baptism. According to Acts 19:1–7, Paul found some disciples at Ephesus who had been baptized εἰς το Ιωαννου βαπτισμα, who were called μαθηται and πιστευσαντες, and yet had not received the Holy Spirit and did not even know εἰ πνευμα ἁγιον ἐστιν. Better instructed by Paul concerning John’s preaching, they allowed themselves to be baptized εἰς το ὀνομα του κυριου Ιησου and to have hands laid upon them, and thus received the Holy Spirit and began to speak in tongues and prophesy. From the latter it is evident that by πνευμα ἁγιον here, just as in 8:15; 10:44; 11:15; 15:8, the gift of the Spirit consisting in glossolalia and prophecy must be understood. The disciples in Ephesus had not received these and had not even heard of them. Baptism did not always impart that gift, not only John’s baptism but also that of Jesus. For in Acts 8:15 we read that the believers in Samaria had indeed been baptized εἰς το ὀνομα του κυριου Ιησου, but that not one of them had yet received the Holy Spirit and that they obtained him only through the laying on of the apostles’ hands. And likewise Acts 19:6 makes this gift a consequence not of baptism but of the laying on of hands. But the strange thing in Acts 19 is that the disciples in Ephesus were baptized in the name of the Lord Jesus before this laying on of hands. Paul therefore cannot have recognized the baptism they had received as a true, genuine baptism. They had been baptized εἰς το Ιωαννου βαπτισμα. John’s baptism was indeed good, for he baptized the baptism of repentance unto faith in Christ. But among John’s disciples who had remained with him and had not gone over to Jesus, all kinds of error had crept in, also concerning baptism; and so the disciples in Ephesus had to be baptized in Jesus’ name not again but for the first time, for their baptism into the name of John was not a true baptism, not genuine Christian baptism and also not the genuine, original Johannine baptism. Cf. Baldensperger, *Der Prolog des vierten Evang.*, Freiburg: Mohr, 1898, who thinks that the whole prologue of John 1 was written against these Baptists or followers of John the Baptist.
