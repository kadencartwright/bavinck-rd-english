## 2.

The divine institution of baptism thus already occurs with John, but Jesus, after himself undergoing it, took it over, had it administered by his disciples, John 3:22; 4:1, and in Matt. 28:19 made it obligatory for all believers from all nations. The last passage is regarded by many as inauthentic, because in the apostolic period baptism still took place in the name of Jesus and the trinitarian formula dates only from later; there are even those who claim that Jesus did not institute baptism at all for his congregation. Various objections, however, stand against this. It cannot be denied that Jesus himself allowed John’s baptism to be administered to him and thereby acknowledged it. Where he speaks expressly about it, he derives it from a command of God, Matt. 21:25. Nor is there any ground for denying that Jesus took over baptism and administered it, if not himself, nevertheless through his disciples, John 3:22, 26; 4:1, 2, for Jesus appeared with the same preaching as John, namely, that the kingdom of heaven was near, and set the same requirements for entrance into it, namely, faith and repentance, Mark 1:15. It was therefore natural that, like John, he had the baptism of repentance administered to everyone who wished to belong to the narrower circle of his disciples. John 3:5 does not speak of baptism, vol. III 501, but the passage nevertheless proves that the communication of the Spirit in the congregation was regarded as having its symbol in water. As the opposition between the Jewish people and him and his disciples grew, an act of separation on the one hand and of admission into the congregation of Jesus on the other became all the more necessary. Baptism as incorporation into the Christian congregation must also have been willed and intended by Jesus himself, since otherwise it would be inexplicable that it was immediately introduced and practiced without any controversy in all Christian congregations, both those from the Jews and those from the Gentiles, Acts 2:38, 41; 8:12, 13, 16, 38; 9:18; and so forth; Rom. 6:3–5; 1 Cor. 1:13–17; Gal. 3:27; Eph. 5:26; and so forth. In 1 Cor. 1:17 Paul does indeed say that Christ sent him not to baptize but to proclaim the gospel. But this does not prove at all that Paul esteems baptism lightly or considers it unnecessary; Rom. 6 and other passages certainly teach otherwise. Jesus himself, however, also did not administer baptism but had it administered. And so too Paul occupied himself principally with preaching the gospel and left baptizing and other activities in the planting and building up of the congregations to his fellow workers.

Now in the earliest period baptism is indeed called a baptism ἐν τῳ ὀνοματι or εἰς το ὀνομα Ιησου, Rom. 6:3; 1 Cor. 1:13; Gal. 3:27; Acts 2:38; 8:16; 10:48; 19:5, but this by no means says that baptism was administered with that specific formula. For Paul says in 1 Cor. 10:2 that the Israelites were baptized εἰς τον Μωυσην; in 1 Cor. 1:13 that the believers at Corinth were not baptized εἰς το ὀνομα Παυλου; in 1 Cor. 12:13 that they were baptized εἰς ἑν σωμα; and in Acts 19:3 the disciples at Ephesus said that they had been baptized εἰς το Ιωαννου βαπτισμα. In none of these cases does anyone think of a formula pronounced at baptism. The expression “in the name of Jesus” is not intended as a formula but is a description of the character of Christian baptism, Zahn, *Einl. in das N. T.* II 309. When departing from Egypt, the Israelites allowed themselves to be baptized in the cloud and in the sea εἰς τον Μωυσην, in relation to Moses, so that they acknowledged him as their rescuer and deliverer, placed their trust in him, and allowed themselves to be led by him. The disciples at Ephesus had been baptized εἰς το Ιωαννου βαπτισμα and had thereby joined themselves to John. And so too Christian baptism is, and is called, a baptism in or into the name of Jesus because it places believers in communion with him and directs all their trust to him alone. The same thing is intended when Jesus says in Matt. 28:19 that his disciples must be baptized εἰς το ὀνομα του πατρος και του υἱου και του ἁγιου πνευματος. Here he does not prescribe to the apostles what they must _say_ when administering baptism but what they must _do_. Christian baptism is and must be an incorporation into communion with that God who has revealed himself as Father, Son, and Spirit. The name designates God in his revelation, and the highest revelation of God consists in this, that he makes himself known and allows himself to be named as Father, Son, and Spirit, vol. II 63f. Being baptized into that name thus does not merely signify that one is baptized by God’s charge or command or for the confession of his name. For the expression “into the name” can be interchanged with the person himself, just as Paul also speaks of being baptized εἰς Χριστον, Rom. 6:3; Gal. 3:27. Rather, it signifies that the one baptized is placed in relation to and in communion with that God who has revealed himself as Father, Son, and Spirit, and on that basis is now also obligated to confess and glorify that name. Cf. Julius Böhmer, *Das biblische „im Namen”. Eine sprachwiss. Untersuchung über das hebr. בְּשֵׁם und seine griech. Aequivalente*, Giessen: Ricker, 1898. Although Jesus after his resurrection had described baptism as an incorporation into communion with Father, Son, and Spirit, it was natural that in the earliest period it was connected chiefly with the person of Christ. At entrance into the congregation, what mattered first of all was repentance and faith in Christ, in order to obtain forgiveness of sins in that way, and baptism was the sign and proof of this. Therefore baptism is still called in Acts, just as with John, a βαπτισμα μετανοιας εἰς ἀφεσιν ἁμαρτιων, Acts 2:38; 22:16. But in the earliest period something else was added. John and also Jesus himself had set this baptism of repentance over against the baptism with the Spirit that would take place on the day of Pentecost. This baptism with the Spirit was by no means bound to water baptism, the baptism of repentance unto forgiveness of sins, for in Acts 2:33 all the disciples receive that Spirit without baptism; in Acts 9:17; 10:44, the gifts of the Spirit are already granted to Paul, Cornelius, and others before baptism; cf. 11:15–17; in Acts 8:17; 9:17; 19:6, glossolalia and prophecy are granted not through baptism but through the laying on of hands. Yet for those who stood outside, the baptism of repentance was the ordinary way along which they could also receive the gifts of the Spirit, Acts 2:38; 19:5, 6. This connection, however, was temporary. Glossolalia and prophecy were not the proper benefits of baptism; Christian baptism remained essentially a baptism of repentance and faith in Christ unto the forgiveness of sins. Thus it is also understood and described everywhere in the New Testament. Peter says in 1 Pet. 3:21 that, just as Noah and his family were saved from death through the water that bore the ark, so believers are rescued from destruction through baptism. But that baptism must then be understood not as σαρκος ἀποθεσις ρυπου, not as it outwardly causes us to put off the uncleanness of the flesh, but as συνειδησεως ἀγαθης ἐπερωτημα εἰς θεον, δι’ ἀναστασεως Ιησου Χριστου, that is, probably, the petition to God for a good conscience freed from guilt, which baptism is and can be only through the resurrection of Jesus Christ as proof of our justification, Rom. 4:25. The same conception recurs in the epistle to the Hebrews. It does indeed reckon the διδαχη βαπτισμων—that is, not the doctrine of Christian baptism but of washings in general, a right understanding of which was urgently necessary for Jewish Christians, cf. 9:10—among the elementary principles of Christianity, but distinguishes two elements in Christian baptism: the washing of the body with pure water and the cleansing of the heart from an evil, accusing conscience, 10:22, 23. Baptism is considered by Paul from another side. He connects it not so much with justification as with sanctification. As a descending into and rising from the water, baptism is a portrayal and pledge of entering into communion with Christ, with his death and with his resurrection, Rom. 6:3–6; Col. 2:12. As many, then, as have been baptized into Christ, into his communion, have put on Christ, appropriated Christ to themselves, so that they are now in Christ and belong to him, Gal. 3:27–29; walk in newness of life, Rom. 6:4, 6f.; Eph. 5:26; live to God, Rom. 6:11, 13; indeed bear the life of Christ himself within them, Gal. 2:20. And just as through baptism they entered into communion with Christ, so too they entered into communion with his congregation, which is his body. They were all baptized by one Spirit into one body, 1 Cor. 12:13; Rom. 12:5. For Paul, water baptism is at the same time Spirit baptism, but not a baptism with the spiritual gifts of glossolalia and prophecy; rather, it is a baptism with the Spirit as the principle of the new life. Baptized human beings are new, spiritual human beings, πνευματικοι. But this renewal of the human being by the Holy Spirit in baptism does not stand loosely alongside justification from faith or come to it by chance. They coincide: at the same moment the Corinthians were washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit of our God, 1 Cor. 6:11; cf. vol. III 531. In baptism all these benefits are joined together and granted to believers. This does not take away the fact, however, that despite their baptism the Corinthians are still called σαρκικοι, νηπιοι ἐν Χριστῳ by Paul and are seriously warned against possible apostasy, 1 Cor. 3:1, 3; 10:1–12.
