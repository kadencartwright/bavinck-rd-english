## 4.

The conflict the Reformation undertook against Rome’s doctrine of the sacraments centered not upon baptism but upon the Supper. The German Reformers even believed that baptism had been preserved virtually intact in the papacy and therefore adopted it with little modification. Many ceremonies that had gradually been added to baptism also remained in use among the Lutherans, such as naming, the sign of the cross, exorcism, sponsorship, laying on of hands, white clothing, blessing, and so forth. Further, Luther taught in both of his *Catechismi* and in the Smalcald Articles that the word of institution made the water of baptism into a *divina, coelestis, sancta et salutifera aqua*. He did indeed reject the view of Thomas and the Dominicans, who failed to recognize the word of institution and had God communicate a *virtus spiritualis* to the water, but he nevertheless assumed an objective, real union of the Word and water. Baptism is *verbum Dei cum mersione in aquam, aqua divino mandato comprehensa et verbo Dei obsignata*. The water in baptism is, as Luther expressed it elsewhere in his *Sermon von der Taufe*, *durch die göttliche Majestät ganz durchgöttet*, just as iron is heated by fire. Later dogmaticians elaborated this and taught that through the word of institution the *materia coelestis*—that is, *tota trinitas* or *sanguis Christi* or *Spiritus sanctus*—so united itself with the *materia terrestris*, that is, the water, that God worked regeneration *in, cum*, and *per aquam baptismi, non seorsim et actione peculiari sed conjunctim cum aqua baptismi et per eam, una atque indivisa actione*. Finally, in the first period Luther did always have the salutary operation of baptism depend upon the faith with which the benefits of baptism were accepted, but later he increasingly emphasized the objective character of baptism and no longer said that children were or could be believers, but grounded infant baptism solely upon God’s command. The Lutherans therefore later taught that in adults the salutary operation of baptism indeed depends upon faith, or at least upon a *passiva capacitas*, and thus, if faith is present, consists in *obsignatio* and *confirmatio*; but in children baptism works regeneration, is *medium ordinarium regenerationis et mundationis a peccatis*, yet always in such a way that the guilt and power, but not the whole stain, of sin is removed; the *radix aut fomes peccati* remains. Cf. *Symb. B.*, ed. Müller, 30, 40, 163, 320, 361, 384, 485, 768, 780; Luther in Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 507f., and Harnack, *D. G.* III 748; Melanchthon, *Loc. de baptismo*; Gerhard, *Loc.* XX; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 106–176; Hollaz, *Ex. theol.*, 1077–1103; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.*, § 54. The Reformed, however, not only rejected most of the ceremonies gradually connected with baptism and returned to the simplicity of Holy Scripture. They also proceeded from and attempted to maintain the thought that baptism was instituted for believers and thus did not work but strengthened faith. They thereby encountered a twofold difficulty with infant baptism. First, chiefly over against the Anabaptists but then also over against Roman Catholics and Lutherans, they had to demonstrate that the children of believers were already to be regarded as believers before baptism and as such ought to be baptized. Second, they were obliged to answer the question in what the gracious operation of baptism consisted in children, since, not yet having come to the use of reason and thus not yet possessing *fides actualis*, they could also hardly be strengthened and confirmed in this faith. The latter question, however, ordinarily received little attention. In general people confined themselves to saying that baptism was a proof to the parents that their seed had been received into God’s covenant; that later, as the children grew up, it was a rich comfort and blessing to them; and that already in their unconscious state it gave them a right to the goods of the covenant of grace, Witsius, *Misc. Sacra* II 648–667. But the first question was answered very differently from the beginning. For the right of infant baptism appeal was made unanimously to Holy Scripture, specifically to its doctrine concerning the covenant of grace. Children and also adults had to be judged according to the rule of that covenant. Faith and repentance give no right to baptism; only the covenant does. Children born of believing parents were not pagan children, did not lie under God’s wrath, did not live under Satan’s power so that an exorcism first had to take place with them. Rather, they were already children of the covenant before baptism; baptism was therefore also not absolutely necessary to salvation, and there was no need for emergency baptism. But as soon as people reflected upon what this inclusion of the children in the covenant of grace entailed, they diverged. There were those who wished to maintain the unity of election and covenant as long and as closely as possible. They therefore maintained that all children born of believing parents had to be regarded as regenerate according to the judgment of charity until they clearly revealed the opposite in doctrine or life; or that at least the elect little children were as a rule already regenerated by God’s Spirit before baptism or even before birth: a Lasco, Ursinus, Acronius, Voetius, Witsius, and others. But others, attending to the difficulties of practice, which so frequently teaches that baptized children grow up without showing any sign of spiritual life, did not dare make this regeneration before baptism a rule. Without exception they all acknowledged that God’s grace is not bound to the means and can also work regeneration in the hearts of young children, but they left undecided whether that regeneration in elect little children takes place before, during, or also—sometimes even many years—after baptism: Calvin, Beza, Zanchius, Bucanus, Walaeus, Amesius, Heidegger, Turretinus, and others. This conception gained the upper hand when the church fell into decay through neglect of discipline. Election and church, the inward and outward sides of the covenant, formerly joined together as much as possible but increasingly distinguished since Gomarus, fell ever further apart. Within the *ecclesia* an *ecclesiola* formed. Baptism was therefore gradually detached entirely from regeneration and, because people nevertheless wished to maintain it for children, was conceived and justified as a sacrament of the church and pledge for the seed of believers in general; or as confirmation of the objective, conditional promise of the gospel; or as proof of communion in the outward covenant of grace; or as guarantee of a regeneration that can be lost, is not indissolubly connected with salvation, and must later be confirmed by personal faith; or as a means of education that exhorts the baptized to sincere conversion at a later age. Cf. Voetius, *Disp.* II 408 sq.; Witsius, *Misc. Sacra* II 611–618; G. Kramer, *Het verband van doop en wedergeboorte*, Breukelen, 1897. In this the conflict repeatedly flared up at the point of the form for baptism. Some understood the expression “sanctified in Christ” of inward renewal by the Holy Spirit and therefore objected to putting this question from the form for baptism to parents who still presented their child for baptism but were otherwise concerned with neither God nor his command. Under pietistic influence they increasingly attached less value to the external act of baptism, insisted upon personal conversion, and withdrew into the narrow circle of the conventicles: Lodenstein, Gentman, Koelman, Brakonier, van de Putt, Kelderman, Vos, and others in Proost, *Jod. van Lodenstein*, 1880, pp. 160, 229; Ypey and Dermout, *Gesch. der N. H. Kerk* III 261–263; Ypey, *Gesch. der Chr. Kerk in de achttiende Eeuw* VI 164; M. Vitringa VII 108, 115 sq.; Moor V 489. Others understood the expression in an objective, covenantal sense, saw in baptism nothing more than a sign of the outward covenant, to which historical faith and an inoffensive life gave sufficient right: Ostervald, *Comp. Theol.* II 6, 4, 4; Vernet, *Christ. Onderw.*, 300; and especially Janssonius and van Eerde against Appelius; cf. M. Vitringa VI 426 sq., 498 sq.; VII 125 sq.

Thus in the Reformed churches themselves baptism was almost entirely robbed of its value, and the doctrine of baptism actually introduced that had already been professed in the century of the Reformation by Socinians and Anabaptists and later by Remonstrants and Rationalists. For despite all their mutual differences, these agree that baptism has value not as a seal of grace on God’s part but primarily as an act of confession on the human being’s part. Baptism works nothing and gives nothing, but is only a symbol of the transition from Judaism and paganism to Christianity, a sign of faith and repentance, a promise of obedience, and therefore either was not at all instituted by Christ as a permanent sacrament or, in any case, is at most permissible and useful for children but not necessary and commanded. The Quakers even went so far as to reject water baptism entirely and acknowledge only the baptism of the Spirit, and the Rationalists disputed whether baptism, which was no more than a solemn symbol, might not better be abolished; cf. M. Vitringa VII 297–415; Strauss, *Dogm.* II 549–558; Wegscheider, *Instit.*, §§ 171, 172; Kant, *Religion*, ed. Rosenkranz, 233. Modern Protestantism still stands at this standpoint and makes baptism optional, Scholten, *Initia*, 247; Ehlers, *Das N. T. und die Taufe*, Giessen, 1890. And among many others the low estimation of the sacrament continues to work in the transfer of the center of gravity from baptism to the subsequent, ever more solemnly arranged reception and confirmation of members. But over against this, in this century an attempt was again made from different sides to uphold the objective character of baptism. Schleiermacher indeed saw in baptism first of all an act of the church by which she receives the believer into her communion, but then subsequently saw in it at the same time reception into the communion of life with Christ, *Chr. Gl.*, §§ 136–138; cf. Schweizer, *Chr. Gl.*, § 171; Lipsius, *Dogm.*, § 846. Others again placed God’s act of grace in the sacrament in the foreground and taught that baptism does not presuppose regeneration but nevertheless grants the power of regeneration or regeneration itself, is an establishment of the bond of love from Christ’s side, and lays the foundation for all later benefits, which then, however, are obtainable only in the way of faith: Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 2, 83f.; Kahnis, *Luth. Dogm.* II 333; Dorner, *Chr. Gl.* II 832; Frank, *Chr. Wahrheit* II 266f.; Althaus, *Die Heilsbedeutung der Taufe im N. T.*, Gütersloh, 1898; H. Cremer, *Wesen und Wirkung der Taufgnade*, ibid., 1899; W. Schmidt, *Dogm.* II 461; Oosterzee, *Dogm.*, § 138, 9f. Many Lutherans even returned to the old doctrine that the Holy Spirit works regeneration in and through the water of baptism and had it consist not only in a spiritual renewal but also in the implantation of a heavenly corporeality: Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 233; Martensen, *Dogm.*, 398f.; Höfling, *Das Sakrament der Taufe* I 17f.; Thomasius, *Christi Person u. Werk* II 297f. In England Tractarianism appeared with the doctrine of a *baptismal regeneration* consisting in this, that through baptism children were so renewed that they could later accept grace independently through faith: Newman, *Lectures on Justification*, 1838; Waterland, *Works*, Oxford, 1843, IV 425–458; cf. Hodge, *Syst. Theol.* III 591–604; and Cunningham, *Historical Theol.* II 133–142; Ryle, *Knots Untied*, 11th ed., London: Hunt, 1886, pp. 105–196. In this country Dr. Kuyper attempted to uphold the objective character of baptism by ascribing to it a particular grace. This does not consist in regeneration, which is presupposed at baptism and therefore no longer needs to be granted, but in a particular benefit not otherwise obtainable—namely, incorporation into the body of Christ, or rather the implantation into our faith of the disposition or impulse not to stand by ourselves but to feel ourselves one with the whole body of Christ, *De Heraut* 646f.
