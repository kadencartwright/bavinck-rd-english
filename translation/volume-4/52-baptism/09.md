## 9.

Christ is the administrator of this baptism. And only when he baptizes and grants the thing signified along with the sign is someone truly baptized. But in the administration of baptism Christ makes use of human beings to whom he entrusts the distribution of the mysteries of God. Under the Old Testament circumcision was bound to no office; every Israelite was permitted to perform it. Ordinarily the father of the household did so, Gen. 17:23; in an emergency the mother also, Ex. 4:25; 1 Macc. 1:60; later usually the physician; and today generally a *mohel* specially appointed for the purpose. But in the New Testament baptism is administered only by those who have been placed in office: John, Mark 1:4; Jesus’s disciples, John 4:2; the apostles, Acts 2:38, to whom it had been expressly entrusted by Christ in Matt. 28:19; Philip, who was a deacon in Jerusalem but later acted as an evangelist, Acts 21:8, and as such administered baptism, Acts 8:38; Ananias, who laid his hands upon Paul and probably also baptized him, Acts 9:17, 18; and Paul, who sometimes baptized personally but otherwise left baptizing to his fellow workers, since as apostle to the Gentiles he was called in the first place to proclaim the gospel, 1 Cor. 1:14–17; cf. Acts 10:48. From this it is clear that the administration of the sacraments is subordinate to the preaching of the Word but has nevertheless been joined with it at all times. The sacrament follows the Word, and therefore the right to administer the sacraments passed naturally from the apostles and evangelists to the teachers, to those presbyters who labored in the Word and doctrine. When these teachers were later regarded as bishops who were distinguished in office from the presbyters and elevated above them, the administration of baptism was considered a right of the bishop, Tertullian, *De bapt.*, ch. 17. But the expansion of the congregations and also the increasingly accepted conception that baptism was absolutely necessary for salvation led to baptism being permitted also to be administered by presbyters, deacons, *parochi*, and in an emergency even by every human being who possesses the use of reason. The Roman church recognizes baptism administered by a heretic, indeed even baptism administered by an unbeliever, by a Jew or heathen, although the required *intentio faciendi quod facit ecclesia* is difficult to demonstrate here, and thereby reserves to itself the right to apply the *cogite intrare* to all the baptized. The only thing it does not recognize is *se ipsum baptizare*. To be safe, it has even introduced conditional baptism if there is any doubt whether baptism was administered or rightly administered, in which the minister says: *si non baptizatus es, ego te baptizo*, and so forth; cf. Suicerus, s.v. βαπτισμος; commentaries on *Sent.* IV, dist. 5; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 67; *Trid.* VII, *De bapt.* 4; *Cat. Rom.* II, qus. 18. 23. 42; Bellarmine, *De bapt.*, ch. 7. Other churches too, such as the Greek, the Lutheran, and so forth, teach the necessity of baptism and therefore in an emergency permitted its administration by laypeople. But in the end none of them dares to accept the consequence that someone would be lost solely because, entirely through no fault of his own, he died unbaptized. All allow exceptions in which a *baptismus sanguinis* or *flaminis* suffices. *Conversio cordis potest inesse non percepto baptismo, sed contempto baptismo non potest*, Lombardus, *Sent.* IV 4, 4. Article 9 of the *Conf. Aug.* does indeed say of baptism in the Latin text *quod sit necessarius ad salutem*, but in the German text teaches only *dass sie nöthig sei*. Lutheran theologians do not deny salvation to children who died unbaptized through no fault of their parents, Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 164. And all who have a special grace communicated through baptism acknowledge that this can also be granted by God in another way, *Heraut* 651. The Reformed therefore judged differently as well. Baptism, after all, was not a cause but a sign and seal of regeneration, which God grants before and without the sacrament. No single benefit was bestowed through baptism that had not been granted through the Word and received by faith. Baptism thus could not be absolutely necessary for salvation; not the *privatio baptismi* in itself but the *contemptus baptismi* makes one guilty before God. In Mark 16:16 baptism is therefore omitted in the second clause. And in John 3:5, a passage that on the other side is generally understood of baptism, *Trid.* VII, *De bapt.*, ch. 2; *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 31, Calvin and others hold that baptism is not spoken of, although baptism may perhaps be in view, for water occurs here, like fire in Matt. 3:11, as a symbol of the activity of the Holy Spirit and is no longer mentioned at all in vv. 6 and 8. Therefore there is also no reason to depart from apostolic usage and permit the administration of baptism in emergencies to persons other than the teachers of the congregation, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 15, 20; Bucanus, *Inst.*, 613; Perkins, *Werken* I 461; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 631; M. Vitringa VII 75. 163; Moor V 435. The Reformed therefore also insisted that baptism always be administered in the midst of the congregation. Although in the New Testament baptism was administered wherever there was water, Matt. 3:6; John 3:23; Acts 8:36, it nevertheless soon became customary, when believers obtained their own meeting places, to have it take place there. Yet in emergencies, in wintertime, in cases of illness, and for princes and prominent persons, an exception was made and the administration of baptism in private homes was permitted. This is certainly contrary to the general rule that must prevail in the church. Even though cases are conceivable in which baptism may be administered in homes, they cannot and may not be other than rare exceptions; their assessment belongs not to the minister of the Word alone but to the whole church council, and even then they require that the administration take place only in the presence of the church council. For in the distribution of the sacrament it is not the building that matters but the assembly of the congregation. The sacrament is a component of public worship, a good Christ has granted to his church, and must therefore be publicly administered with the Word in the congregation. For the sacrament is always united with the Word; Christ himself joined the administration of baptism with that of the Word, Matt. 28:19. In the planting of the church among a non-Christian population, baptism naturally cannot immediately take place in the midst of the assembly of believers. But as soon as that assembly exists, the ministry of Word and sacrament must be transferred into it, for they are a component of public worship and a good belonging to the congregation. Thus in the apostolic period the Supper was celebrated in the midst of the congregation, 1 Cor. 11:20. And this ought no less to be done with baptism, which portrays precisely incorporation into Christ and his congregation, 1 Cor. 12:13, and is therefore most fittingly administered in the public assembly of believers, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 15, 16; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 726–730; Moor V 510–512; M. Vitringa VII 171; Synod of Dort, sess. 163. 175; *K. O.*, art. 56.

There was no small disagreement in the church about the time at which baptism should be administered. Circumcision was performed on the eighth day; baptism in the New Testament was ordinarily administered immediately when someone believed and made confession, Matt. 3:6; Acts 2:41; 8:12, 36; 9:18; 10:47; 16:15, 33; 18:8. But when subsequently all kinds of persons wished to join the church who were altogether unfamiliar with her doctrine and life, the catechumenate already arose in the second century, was afterward increasingly regulated, and according to the Synod of Elvira around 300 had to last two years. At its conclusion the catechumens, preferably on one of the great feast days, were solemnly baptized and incorporated into the congregation. Led by the thought that baptism forgave only past sins, many even delayed baptism as long as possible, until their deathbed. But the increasingly common practice of infant baptism and the doctrine of the absolute necessity of baptism nevertheless drove the church in another direction. It became customary not to delay baptism as long as possible but to administer it as soon as possible after birth. At first many still argued that baptism should be administered in the third or thirtieth year of life, while others preferred to see it administered on the eighth or the fortieth day after birth. The Synod of Carthage in 252 under the presidency of Cyprian already determined that children had to be baptized as soon as possible, on the second or third day after their birth, Cyprian, *Ep.* 59; and this soon became general practice and was regarded as an apostolic custom; cf. Suicerus, s.vv. βαπτισμος and κλινικος; Schwane, *D. G.* I 378. II 755; Moeller–Schubert, *Kirchengesch.* I 338. The Greek church has no provision concerning the time but ordinarily postpones baptism no longer than the eighth day and in an emergency administers it even earlier, immediately after birth. The Roman church urges that the child be baptized as soon as possible after birth, *Trid.* VII, *De bapt.*, ch. 12; *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 28. The Lutherans, Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 245, and the Reformed too agree with this general rule. The provincial Synod of Dort in 1574, art. 57, even declared that the affection of parents who desire to postpone the baptism of their children until the mothers themselves present their children, or to wait a long time for godparents, is no lawful cause for postponing baptism. But no other synod adopted this declaration. Although every needless delay is disapproved and the speedy presentation of the child for baptism is repeatedly urged, it is by no means the intention of the church orders, when they speak only of fathers, to exclude mothers, but to oppose the system of witnesses and not allow them to take the place of fathers; cf. Bucanus, *Inst.*, 634; Bullinger, *Huysboek*, 1612 f. 249 f.; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 44, 52; Voetius, *Pol. eccl.* I 724; Moor V 512; M. Vitringa VII 176. The system of baptismal witnesses—ἀναδοχοι, *sponsores, fidejussores, susceptores, compatres, commatres, patrini, matrinae, patres et matres spirituales*—arose when infant baptism became general practice and is already mentioned by Tertullian, *De bapt.* 18. Persons were now needed who made confession in the child’s place and answered the customary questions; who acted as sureties, *sponsores*, as it were, for the child; and who promised later to give it a Christian upbringing on the foundation of its baptism. They were the representatives of the church, which properly as a whole presents the child for baptism and bears it with her prayer. It was therefore natural to have the parents act as such *sponsores* at their child’s baptism. And in the earliest period this is what happened. But gradually parenthood and godparenthood were placed alongside each other, like birth and regeneration, natural and spiritual kinship. Parents were already naturally obligated to give their child a Christian upbringing and seemed unable to take a special vow to this effect upon themselves. They were the natural parents of the child, but godparenthood was an altogether different spiritual kinship and therefore gradually became an *impedimentum matrimonii* between the *susceptores* on the one hand and the one baptized and his parents on the other, and also among the *susceptores* themselves. The Council of Mainz in 813 therefore already forbade: *nullus proprium filium vel filiam de fonte baptismatis suscipiat*; cf. *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qus. 20–24. But this same catechism already had to complain that this service had been so neglected in the church *ut nudum tantum hujus functionis nomen relictum sit*. Lutherans and Reformed considered this system of baptismal witnesses by no means necessary, since it was not prescribed or mentioned in Scripture, but ordinarily regarded it nevertheless as an *adiaphoron* that could sometimes be of some use, Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 267; Buddeus, *Inst.*, p. 1071; Dort Church Order 57; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 44, 54; Bucanus, *Inst. theol.*, 640; Voetius, *Pol. eccl.* I 704; Moor V 509; M. Vitringa VII 159. The Reformed, however, placed particular emphasis upon the _parents_ answering the questions at baptism in the first place and acting as *susceptores* and *sponsores* for their child, and required that if witnesses were used, they be of pure confession and conduct. Calvin, *Op.*, ed. Amsterdam, IX 142; Hooyer, *Oude Kerkenordeningen*, pp. 7. 11. 17. 46. 69. 105. 153. 205. 265. 314. 344. 456. To this is added that in the Roman church godfathers and godmothers must serve to instruct the child in the doctrine of the faith because, as the *Catech. Rom.* II 2, qu. 20 says, the pastors have no time for it. But following Calvin’s example, the Reformed church introduced the catechesis of baptized youth and entrusted it to the teacher. Thus in a well-ordered Reformed church, apart from a few special cases, the system of witnesses, which otherwise quickly degenerated into a *nudum nomen*, became superfluous and unnecessary and in practice has also virtually disappeared. Cf. Suicerus, s.v. ἀναδοχοι; Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* II 4–20; Steitz in Herzog² 15, 247.

This whole doctrine of baptism as developed by the Reformed brings to light how closely they adhered to Holy Scripture. All the more noteworthy is the fact that they nevertheless—or rather, precisely for that reason—knew how to avoid all sectarianism in their recognition and administration of baptism and preserved a truly Christian breadth of heart and breadth of conception. In agreement with the catholic church in its struggle against the African churches, the Reformed too unanimously taught that the baptism of heretics, provided it was administered in the name of the triune God, had to be recognized. But because they did not understand the baptismal formula magically and did not detach baptism from church and office, they added the further qualification that it had to have been administered by a minister recognized in a Christian congregation. Cf. Dr. G. van Goor, *De strijd over den Ketterdoop*, Utrecht, 1872; Herzog², art. “Ketzertaufe”; Calvin in Herzog, *Das Leben Joh. Calvins* II 486; Voetius, *Pol. eccl.* I 631–645; Turretinus, *Th. El.* XIX, qu. 15; Moor V 443–453; Synod of Dort, 1574, qu. 10; Middelburg, 1578, qu. 29; Dort, 1618, sess. 162. And from baptism they did indeed exclude all things and objects, all dead, deceased, or only partially born persons, all *monstra*, and also all children of heathen parents who had been taken captive, Synod of Dort, 1618, sess. 18. 19. But they admitted to baptism all children who after their parents’ death or as foundlings had been taken into Christian families, who had been born from an unlawful marriage or from *excommunicati*, schismatics, or heretics, provided only that there was some ground for the supposition that the line of the covenant had not been completely broken. Voetius I 645–670; Moor V 500–509; M. Vitringa VII 95. 142–159. The Reformed are more open to the charge of recognizing and administering baptism too broadly than too narrowly. But thereby they maintained in an outstanding manner the unity and catholicity of the church of Christ on earth. All Christian churches still recognize one another’s baptism and thereby declare in fact that enough truth is still present in them all that the possibility of being saved is not excluded. There is one confession upon which they are all built, one faith in which they all share. Despite all difference and conflict, all nevertheless acknowledge one Lord, one faith, one baptism.
