## 5.

The modification the Reformation introduced into the Roman Catholic concept of the church also had practical consequences. Uniformity made way for pluriformity once and for all. Different churches appeared after and alongside one another and gave religion and church an entirely different form. The Reformation tried still to keep invisible and visible church in a proper relation, but history proved how difficult this was. And in churches other than the Reformed and Lutheran the connection was frequently broken entirely, and either the invisible church sacrificed to the visible or the latter to the former. Socinianism still accepted the distinction, but nevertheless spoke almost only of the visible church because it conceived the Christian religion as a doctrine that could readily be accepted by practically everyone, Fock, *Der Socin.*, 690 f. Remonstrantism not only walked in the same track but also deprived the church of all independence and left it nothing but the right of preaching and admonition, *Conf.* and *Apol. conf.* 21, 22; Limborch, *Theol. Christ.* VII. In rationalism the church became an association of people for the practice of religion and the improvement of morals: Wegscheider, *Instit.*, § 188; Bretschneider, *Syst. Entw.*, 760; Doederlein, *Instit. theol. christ.*, 1787, p. 716. With the names invisible and visible church, Kant designated the people of God according to its idea and according to its empirical appearance. The latter—that is, the church with its statutory faith—is destined to pass over more and more into rational and moral religion, into a kingdom of God on earth, *Religion*, ed. Rosenkranz, 119 f., 146 f. With Hegel the church likewise had only a temporal, transitory significance, for the state is the true realization of the moral idea, the *vernünftig-sittliche Substanz*. The church has a right to exist only as long as the state does not yet fully answer to its idea, *Philos. der Rel.*, *Werke* XII 279. From the standpoint of rationalism no place remains for the church as an institution of Christ. Proceeding from a different principle, mysticism arrived at the same result. Anabaptism proceeded from the absolute antithesis between creation and re-creation, nature and grace, world and kingdom of God, and therefore regarded believers as people who in regeneration had become something entirely different and hence had to live separated from the world. Its program was not reformation but separation; it wanted a separated church, Menno Simons, *Werken*, 262. For centuries there had been no church but only Babylon, and Babylon had to be left and shunned, ib. 33f., 289f., 295, 409f. In Münster it was said that for 1400 years there had been no true Christian, Goebel, *Gesch. d. christl. Lebens* I 179. The true church was a church of saints who had been baptized after personal confession and distinguished themselves from others by abstention from oath, war, governmental office, and all kinds of other worldly practices in food and drink, clothing and social intercourse: De Bres, *De wortel, den oorspronck ende het fundament der Wederd.*, 1589, pp. 39–45; Cloppenburg, *Op.* II 233; Goebel, loc. cit., 134 f. The same dualistic principle lies at the foundation of all kinds of sects that later appeared within the circle of Protestantism. In Middelburg in 1666, as earlier in Geneva and Amiens, Labadie called conventicles into being, to which he gave the name prophecies, and in 1669 founded an “evangelical congregation” that might consist only of true believers and later at Herford distinguished itself by a domestic family life, a questionable marriage practice, and community of goods: Goebel, loc. cit., II 181–273; Ritschl, *Gesch. d. Piet.* I 194–268. Pietism, both in the Netherlands and in Germany, concentrated the whole of life into the narrow circle of religion, became indifferent to church and office, sacrament and formulary, gathered believers into separate societies, and promoted separatism, Ritschl, ib. II 135 f. On August 12, 1729, Zinzendorf organized an apostolic congregation which in all sorts of features agreed with Labadie’s congregation, Goebel, ib. II 271. In England, under Anabaptist influence, Independency arose with Robert Browne and John Robinson and had the church arise entirely from the joining together of individual believers, Weingarten, *Die Revolutionskirchen Englands*, 24 f. Tempered in its enthusiasm after Cromwell’s revolution, it passed over, as Anabaptism into Mennonitism, into the religion of the Quakers, who formed a congregation separated from the world and distinguished by all sorts of externals. Detached from everything historical and objective, the church among them became the communion of all who were partakers of the illumination of the Holy Spirit, and further the name for those who gathered together in one place, formed as it were one family, and by virtue of the inward light also agreed outwardly in confession and life: Barclay, *Verantwoording*, 1757, p. 212f.; Weingarten, ib. 185, 186. Methodism too is governed by the same antithesis. Wesley did at first try to reform the church itself, but in 1784 he nevertheless proceeded to separation from the state church. He ordained preachers, placed them under the protection of the Toleration Act, and joined the converted into societies that met daily for prayer, from time to time held love feasts, fast days, watch nights, prayer meetings, and so forth, and received as their principal task to labor for the conversion of others, Schneckenburger, *Kleinere prot. Kirchenparteien*, 1863, p. 103 f. General Booth’s Salvation Army is the consequence of this Methodism. The converted no longer form a church but a standing army of Christ, a corps of evangelists under an officer, separated from the world by distinctive clothing and way of life, Kolde, *Die Heilsarmee*, 1885, p. 49 f. No wonder that amid all such phenomena John Darby came to reject openly and decisively every church and church form. According to his opinion, the New Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace was indeed at first endowed by God with church and office, but these had already in the apostolic period degenerated through human unfaithfulness and been rejected by God. Therefore all churches since that time are nothing but Babylon, preparations for the antichrist, wholly corrupt, to be completely rejected by believers. These must now do nothing other than withdraw from the world, edify one another in their meetings with their different gifts, and quietly await the return of Christ: Dr. G. J. van der Flier, *Het Darbisme*, The Hague, 1879; art. “Plymouthbrüder” in Herzog².

Thus everything appears to point toward a dissolution of the church and a radical modification of the concept of the church. Yet a powerful reaction is not lacking. The Russian church, whose supreme administration rests with the Holy Synod and is bound to the emperor by means of the procurator, maintains its claim to the name of the only true, orthodox church and, with suppression of the sects, strives for unity of faith throughout the whole empire. Pobedonostsev, the present procurator, is an advocate of absolute monarchy and the state church and defends the doctrine that the masses find in authority the standard of truth; see his *Streitfragen*, authorized translation, Berlin: Deubner, 1897. The Roman church is still the same as it was previously, when it had heretics and schismatics persecuted and put to death, and in principle can acknowledge and tolerate no churches alongside itself. When Roman Catholics speak in the interest of religious freedom and separation of church and state, they must be regarded as friends of both from calculation but enemies of both from principle; cf., e.g., Stöckl, *Lehrb. d. Philos.* III 474; Cathrein, *Moralphilos.* II³ 555 f.; Hettinger, *Apol. des Christ.* IV² 407 f. In England Irvingism first appeared, wishing to reform the church by restoration of the apostolate, J. N. Köhler, *Het Irvingisme*, The Hague, 1876, and ritualism is now spreading in the Anglican church, transforming offices, sacraments, worship, and ceremonies more and more according to Roman doctrine and practice: art. “Tractarianismus” in Herzog¹; Mr. Walter Walsh, *Secret History of the Oxford Movement*, 1897; Williams, “The Crisis in the Church of England,” *Presb. and Ref. Rev.*, July 1899, pp. 389–412. Even among the Lutherans many confessionalists returned to the objective institution of church, office, and sacrament and bound all communication of grace to it: Löhe, *Drei Bücher von der Kirche*, 1845; Kliefoth, *Acht Bücher v. d. Kirche*, 1854; Münchmeyer, *Das Dogma v. d. uns. u. sichtb. Kirche*, 1854; Vilmar, *Theol. der Thatsachen*, 1876, p. 48 f.; idem, *Dogmatik* II 212; Stahl, *Die Kirchenverfassung nach Lehre u. Recht der Prot.*, 2nd ed., 1862, p. 67 f. But despite all this the church is increasingly losing its uniform character. Not only in Protestant countries, especially England and America, but also in Russia, the number of sects is increasing: J. Gehring, *Die Sekten der Russischen Kirche 1003–1897. Nach ihrem Ursprunge und inneren Zusammenhange dargestellt*, Leipzig: Richter, 1898. Even in the Roman church, which so gladly feasts upon Protestantism’s divisions, unity in many respects is more appearance than essence. Believers and unbelievers within its walls stand just as far apart as in many churches of the Reformation. The different orders frequently stand on anything but friendly terms with one another. *Dieselben Motive besonderer Frömmigkeit, welche auf katholischem Boden zu neuen Ordensstiftungen führen, wirken auf protestantischen Boden zur Bildung von Sekten*, Ritschl, *Gesch. des Piet.* III 303. And Reform Catholicism and the “Away-from-Rome” movement show how much discontent lies hidden in many a heart beneath the outward splendor of unity. Faith in the one, infallible, exclusively saving church can no longer be maintained over against the existence and flourishing of so many other churches. The doctrine is contradicted by life and by history as strongly as possible.

While Rome closes its eyes to this development of Christianity and the church, Protestant theology runs the danger, for history’s sake, of overlooking the divine institution of the church. According to Schleiermacher the church arose *durch das Zusammentreten der einzelnen Wiedergeborenen zu einem geordneten Auf- und Miteinanderwirken*, *Chr. Gl.*, § 115. Since, however, regeneration is not a magical change but an ethical renewal, there always remains in the regenerate a portion of the world, and therefore in the church a distinction must be made between what abides and what changes and disappears, ib. 125, 126. To this distinction Schleiermacher applies the former names invisible and visible church. Their earlier use was incorrect, for most of the invisible church is not invisible, since regeneration manifests itself outwardly in confession and life, and most of the visible church is not church, since it belongs to the world. Invisible church designates not so much persons as operations of the Spirit in persons, and visible church indicates that these operations of the Spirit in all believers still go together with aftereffects of sin, ib. 148. The two thus stand to one another as essence and appearance, as idea and reality, and are conceived in that same sense by many other theologians: Nitzsch, *Syst. d. chr. Lehre*, §§ 186–188; Lange, *Dogm.* II 1090 f.; Martensen, *Dogm.*, § 191; Müller, *Dogm. Abh.*, 332 f.; Thomasius, *Christi Person und Werk* II³ 505; Frank, *Chr. Wahrheit* II² 369 f.; Kaftan, *Dogm.*, 573, 585. Some abandon the old names in this connection and prefer to speak of kingdom of God and church: A. Dorner, *Kirche und Reich Gottes*, Gotha, 1883; Krauss, *Das prot. Dogma v. d. uns. K.*, 1876; or also of congregation and church: Stahl, *Kirchenverfassung*, 67; De la Saussaye in my *Theol. van Ch. d. l. S.*, 61; Van Oosterzee, § 129. One can then still, with Stahl, hold this visible church to be an institution of Christ, but most nevertheless conceive it as a product, a necessary form of existence of the congregation: Lipsius, *Dogm.*, § 859 f.; Tiele, *Inl. tot de godsdienstwet.* II, 1899, p. 141f.; or even judge that the institutional church has had its day and must pass over into the state—Strauss, *Dogm.* II 618; Rothe, *Ethik*, § 124 f., 440 f., 1167 f.—or into the confessing congregation: Rauwenhoff, *Wijsb. v. d. godsd.*, 843; Pfleiderer, *Religionsphilos.*, 1896, p. 745; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, passim; Chavannes, *Qu’est ce qu’une église*, Paris: Fischbacher, 1897.
