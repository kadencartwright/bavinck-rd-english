## 6.

The name קָהָל, ἐκκλησια, already by virtue of its derivation from verbs meaning to call together, designates an assembly of people who have gathered for some purpose, especially a political or religious one, or who, even if they are not assembled at a particular moment, are nevertheless mutually united for such a purpose. Under the Old Testament, Israel was the people called together and gathered by God for his service; and in the New Testament this people of Israel has been replaced by the congregation of Christ, which is now God’s holy people, chosen race, and royal priesthood. The words *kerk, church, kirk, kirche, chiesa*, by which ἐκκλησια is translated, do not express this character of the congregation of Christ as clearly as the original. The word is probably derived from κυριακη, *scil.* οἰκια, or κυριακον, *scil.* οἰκον, and thus originally denoted not the congregation itself but the place of its assembly, the church building; Suicerus, s.v.; Herzog² 7, 685. Today we use this word in the same sense of the church building, or in the sense of a worship service (for example, church begins at ten o’clock), or in the sense of an instituted body of congregations (the Roman Catholic Church, the Anglican Church, and so forth). The meaning of the New Testament word ἐκκλησια recedes into the background in the word *kerk*; at some times the consciousness that church is the name of the people of God was almost entirely worn away. This is also the reason why the Dutch States Translation rendered ἐκκλησια not by *kerk* but by *gemeente*; for this word once again brought out the fact that the essence of the church lies in the communion of saints. If there were no objections to it, replacing the word *kerk* with *gemeente* would deserve consideration. But society and the state have appropriated this word in the civil sphere; they speak of *gemeente* (municipality), *gemeenteraad* (municipal council), *gemeenteschool* (municipal school), and *gemeentehuis* (town hall), and have thereby rendered the word largely unusable for ecclesiastical life. *Gemeente* can and may indeed be used in the ecclesiastical sphere, but it then has a specific sense and is not as flexible as the word *kerk*, which can assume many meanings. For although this word originally designated the church building and then also the worship service and the ecclesiastical institution, the meaning of the people of God, of the assembly of those who believe in Christ, is nevertheless not foreign to it. In this sense it occurs in the twelve articles, in the Belgic Confession, arts. 27–29, and in the Church Order of Dort, *passim*. It is necessary only to revive this meaning in the consciousness. The words *gemeente* and *kerk* can then be used alongside one another, but they may never be distinguished in such a way that *gemeente* designates the assembly of believers in a particular place and *kerk* the joining of congregations into one whole. For *kerk* and *gemeente* are both translations of the same word ἐκκλησια and therefore have the same meaning. Both words are names for the assembly of believers, whether in a particular place, in a country, or over the whole earth. It is therefore entirely wrong, when thinking of *gemeente*, to think of true believers and, when thinking of *kerk*, of nominal believers; to identify the two concepts with those of the invisible and visible church; or to understand by *gemeente* the assembly of believers and by *kerk* the institution. At most they differ in this: *gemeente* makes one think more of the mutual fellowship of believers, and *kerk* more of those same believers as they are institutionally organized under office and the ministry of the Word. In both cases, however, it is nevertheless the same assembly of believers that is designated; only the viewpoint from which it is considered differs. While these two words thus have an established place in the ecclesiastical sphere, matters are entirely different with the word *kerkgenootschap* (church society). This name has a collegialist flavor. It came into use in this country in 1773 through the first versification of the twelve articles of faith; it was then included with the meaning of local or general church in the constitutions of 1798, 1801, and 1805; it was replaced in the constitutions of 1814 and 1815 by the words religions and religious denominations; and afterward it was introduced alongside the name religious denominations in the constitutions of 1848 and 1887 and again in the Law on Church Societies of September 10, 1853; *Heraut* 627. The joining of *kerk* and *genootschap* is a *mesalliance* and the mother of many errors. A church is precisely not an association, for it arises not through the voluntary accession of adult persons but from regeneration by the Holy Spirit. Finally, it is also not advisable to replace the word church in the sense of the people of God with the term kingdom of God. For between the two there is a difference of no little importance. The kingdom of God, with which Jesus’ preaching begins, is in the first place an eschatological concept for the approaching messianic kingdom with all its goods. And even insofar as this kingdom is already present here on earth in hearts through regeneration, forgiveness, and renewal, it consists much more in spiritual goods than in a community of persons. The kingdom of heaven is, or becomes, precisely the possession of the poor in spirit, the pure in heart, and the little children, and itself consists in peace, joy, and gladness through the Holy Spirit. Therefore, at least here on earth, it is not organized; it is present in principle wherever the spiritual benefits of Christ have been granted, and nowhere on earth is it rounded out and completed; vol. III, 233 f. But the church is especially a *diesseitig* concept, a community of persons equipped with offices and ministries and visibly appearing as the gathered people of God. The church is therefore the means by which Christ distributes the benefits of the kingdom of God and prepares its completion. And on its way to bring about the coming of the kingdom of God, the church takes into itself all kinds of elements that are impure and do not properly belong to it (hypocrites and also the old human being in believers), whereas the kingdom of God, consisting in goods, is pure and unmixed and embraces only the regenerate. Christ was given to the congregation as its head precisely so that at the end God might appear as King of his people and be all in all. Cf. Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 3, 203; Frank, *Chr. Wahrheit* II 375; Kaftan, *Dogm.* 584.

Now there is no doubt that, according to Holy Scripture, the essence of the church lies in its being the people of God. For the church is a realization of election, and this is an election in Christ unto calling, justification, and glorification, Rom. 8:28; unto conformity to the Son of God, Rom. 8:29; unto holiness and salvation, Eph. 1:4 f. The blessings granted to the church are in the first place inward and spiritual in nature and consist in calling and regeneration, in faith and justification, in sanctification and glorification. They are goods of the kingdom of heaven, benefits of the covenant of grace, promises for this life but above all for the life to come. On this ground the church is called the body of Christ, 1 Cor. 12:27; Eph. 5:23; Col. 1:18; the bride of Christ, 2 Cor. 11:2; Eph. 5:32; Rev. 19:7; 21:2; the sheepfold of Christ, who lays down his life for the sheep and is known by them, John 10; the building, the temple, the house of God, Matt. 16:18; Eph. 2:20; 1 Pet. 2:5, built up from living stones, 1 Pet. 2:5, upon the cornerstone Christ and upon the foundation of apostles and prophets, 1 Cor. 3:17; 2 Cor. 6:16, 17; Eph. 2:22; Rev. 21:2–4; the people, the possession, the Israel of God, Rom. 9:25; 2 Cor. 6:16; Heb. 8:10; 1 Pet. 2:9, 10. The members of the church are called branches on the vine, John 15; living stones, 1 Pet. 2:5; elect, called, believers, beloved, brothers and sisters, children of God, and so forth. Those who are not these in truth are regarded in Scripture as chaff among the grain, Matt. 3:12; as weeds among the wheat, Matt. 13:25; as bad fish in the net, Matt. 13:47; as a person without a festive garment at the wedding, Matt. 22:11; as called but not chosen, Matt. 22:14; as bad branches on the vine, John 15:2; as not Israel although they are from Israel, Rom. 2:28; 9:6; as evil persons who must be removed, 1 Cor. 5:13; as vessels for dishonor, 2 Tim. 2:20; as those who went out from us but were not of us, 1 John 2:19; and so forth. All this raises it above every doubt that the church according to its essence is an assembly of true believers. Those who do not partake of sincere faith may outwardly belong to the church; they nevertheless do not constitute its essence, its *forma*; they are in the *ecclesia* but are not the *ecclesia*.

This is further confirmed by the way in which Scripture speaks of the communion of saints. Believers have one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, and likewise they have one Spirit, Eph. 4:4–6, in whose communion they live, by whom they are regenerated, baptized into one body, and united with Christ, John 3:5; 14:17; Rom. 8:9, 14, 16; 1 Cor. 12:3, 13; 2 Cor. 1:22; 5:5; Eph. 1:13; 4:30; 1 John 2:20. And this Spirit does not annul the diversity that exists among believers within that unity but maintains and confirms it. Just as in creation and preservation he adorned and perfected all things in their own way, vol. II, 295, and under Israel granted all kinds of natural and spiritual gifts, vol. II, 230, so on the day of Pentecost he imparted himself, together with all his *charismata*, to the congregation of Christ. In a broader sense these *charismata* also include the benefits of grace that are the portion of all believers, Rom. 5:15, 16; 6:23; but in a narrower sense they designate those special gifts granted to believers in different measure and degree for the benefit of one another, Rom. 1:11; 1 Cor. 1:7; 2 Cor. 1:11; 1 Tim. 4:14; 2 Tim. 1:6; and especially Rom. 12:6–9 and 1 Cor. 12:12 f. The Holy Spirit, who takes them all from Christ, John 16:13, 14; Eph. 4:7, is the distributor of all these gifts. He distributes them to each one as he wills, yet not arbitrarily but in connection with the measure of faith, with the place someone occupies in the congregation, and with the task to which he is called, Rom. 12:3, 6; 2 Cor. 10:13; Eph. 4:7; 1 Pet. 4:10, so that every gift is a φανερωσις του πνευματος, 1 Cor. 12:7. These gifts are very numerous. Paul names various ones and by no means intends to give a complete list. Just as Roman Catholics speak of seven capital sins, seven virtues, and seven beatitudes, so, appealing to Isa. 11:2, 3, they also like to speak of seven *dona Spiritus Sancti*; Lombard, *Sent.* III, dist. 34; Thomas, *S. Theol.* I 2, qu. 68; II 2, qu. 8; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* V 5; Meschler, *Die Gabe des h. Pfingstfestes*, 3{te} Aufl., Freiburg, 1896, S. 233 f.; Simar, *Dogm.* 554; Heinrich-Gutberlet, *Dogm.* VIII 631. But this group of seven does not include the proper *charismata* enumerated by Paul and discussed in Roman Catholic theology rather under the name *gratiae gratis datae*; Simar, ib. 486. And these cannot be limited to a group of seven. To those listed by Paul can also be added the gifts of prayer and thanksgiving, of admonition and consolation, of generosity and hospitality, and so forth. It is therefore also difficult to give a classification. Some clearly bear a supernatural character or are first granted in or after conversion; others display more the nature of natural gifts elevated and sanctified by the Holy Spirit. The former came prominently to the foreground especially in the earliest period; the latter are more characteristic of the church in its historical, normal development. But whatever these gifts may be, they all serve for the benefit of the congregation. The *communio sanctorum* is a *sanctorum communicatio*; Calvin, *Inst.* IV 1, 3. The Holy Spirit distributes the *charismata* to the members of the congregation not for their own benefit but for the benefit of others. They may not be buried or neglected but must willingly and joyfully be employed for the benefit and salvation of the other members, Heidelberg Catechism 55; they serve for οἰκοδομη, 1 Cor. 14:12; Eph. 4:12, and are subordinate to love, which is the most excellent gift. For this love is something other than general love of neighbor; it is love for the brothers, for the household of faith. Jesus calls this love a new commandment, John 13:34, 35; 15:12; 17:26, because love under Israel did not bear a purely spiritual character but was interwoven with the bonds of blood, while the love he now brings about among his disciples is for the first time entirely pure, unmixed, and independent of earthly relationships. The members of Jesus’ congregation are brothers and sisters among one another, Matt. 12:48; 18:15; 23:8; 25:40; 28:10; John 15:14, 15; 20:17; Rom. 8:29; Heb. 2:11; and so forth. They are children of one family; God is their Father, Eph. 4:6; Christ their firstborn brother, Rom. 8:29; Jerusalem above their mother, Gal. 4:26. And thus they are to serve one another with all their spiritual and natural gifts. The church is a communion of saints. Theol.* II 2, § 19–23; Voetius, *Disp.* II 1086–1100; Neander, *Pflanzung u. Leitung d. christl. Kirche*⁵, S. 180 f.; Pfleiderer, *Der Paulinismus*, 242; Holtzmann, *Neut. Theol.* II 143, 175; art. “Geistesgaben” by Cremer in Herzog³; Lauterburg, *Der Begriff des Charisma u. seine Bedeutung für die prakt. Theol.*, Gütersloh, 1898.
