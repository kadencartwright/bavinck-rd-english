## 7.

As long as we hold fast to this essence of the church, its concept causes no extraordinary difficulty. The church is then always, in a broader or narrower sense, the assembly of believers. In the broadest sense it includes all who have been saved through faith in Christ or will yet be saved. Adam and Eve before the fall do not yet belong to it, for at that time they did not yet need a mediator. Nor can the angels be reckoned to it, although this was done by many. For Christ is indeed the Lord of the angels and through his cross placed all things, including angels and human beings, in the right relation to God and to one another. Yet angels were not created in God’s image, did not fall, and were not redeemed by Christ, and thus are also not members of the church that Christ gathers unto eternal life. According to Heb. 12:22 believers do indeed come into communion with myriads of angels, but these are clearly distinguished from the πανηγυρις και ἐκκλησια πρωτοτοκων, v. 23; cf. vol. II, 442–445; III, 405–408. Members of the church are only human beings who are saved through faith in Christ. To it therefore belong all believers who have lived on earth from the promise in paradise until this moment and were received not into the *limbus patrum* or purgatory but into heaven, Heb. 12:23. To it belong all believers who still live on earth at present. And to it in a certain sense also belong all those who will yet believe in Christ until the end of the ages. For the church, even when taken in this broadest sense, is no Platonic state that exists only in the imagination and never becomes reality. Rather, it has the guarantee of its existence now or in the future in God’s decree, in the stability of the covenant of grace, in Christ’s mediatorship, in the promise of the Holy Spirit. The greatest part of the members of this church, however, is then not on earth at any given moment. For from paradise until the present many thousands and millions have already been received into heaven, and their number increases daily, from moment to moment (*ecclesia triumphans*). And there are many who do not yet believe or have not even been born but nevertheless will infallibly come to faith. The church as the assembly of believers living on earth at a given moment (*ecclesia militans*) is therefore only a small part of the church taken in its broadest sense. Yet it is good and necessary to maintain the connection of the church on earth with the church in the past and in the future. For it is one assembly, one ἐκκλησια of those who are enrolled in heaven and who will one day stand before God’s face as a bride without spot or wrinkle. Maintaining this unity of the whole church heightens the sense of communion, steels courage, and incites to battle. If we further restrict ourselves to that part of the church that is on earth (*ecclesia militans*), this can again be taken in a broader or narrower sense. We can think of all believers together who are now present in all churches, among all peoples, in all lands (*ecclesia universalis*); of the believers in one country or in a province, Acts 9:31 (*ecclesia nationalis, provincialis*); or also of the believers in a particular place, whether city or village (*ecclesia particularis, localis*). In this connection it merits notice that the *ecclesia universalis* (*nationalis*) precedes the *ecclesia particularis*. The church of Christ is an organism in which the whole precedes the parts. It has its origin in paradise, Gen. 3:15, or also, for the days of the New Testament, in Jerusalem, Acts 1:8. As long as it alone existed, the church at Jerusalem was the *ecclesia universalis*, the church of Christ on earth, and the churches that shortly appeared alongside it did not arise autochthonously but came into being from Jerusalem through the preaching of the apostles and evangelists.

Up to this point the concept of the church is clear and plain. But now a twofold difficulty arises. The first consists in this, that this concept of the church is applied in Scripture to concrete, historically existing, bounded groups of persons among whom unbelievers are also always present. In the Old Testament the whole people were called the people of God, although by no means all who were from Israel were Israel. In the churches of the New Testament too, though to a much lesser degree, there was chaff among the grain and weeds among the wheat. And after the apostolic period the churches repeatedly became worldly, degenerated, and divided, and yet we still call them all by the name churches. Like Scripture, theology has at all times acknowledged this fact and, following its lead, has always declared that the essence of the church was determined not by the unbelievers but by the believers; above, p. 8f. Augustine clarified this presence of unbelievers in the church by the scriptural image of chaff and grain, or also by that of body and soul, outer and inner person, bad humors in the body. Unbelievers are in Christ’s body *quomodo humores mali*, in Seeberg, loc. cit., 45. The scholastic and Roman Catholic theologians also spoke in this way. Bellarmine, for example, does try to demonstrate that unbelievers too are members of the church, but he gets no further than the claim that they are so *aliquo modo*, *de eccles. mil.* III 2. They are only *de corpore*, not *de anima ecclesiae*. The *boni* are *pars interior*, the *mali* are *pars exterior* of the church. Unbelievers are *membra mortua, arida*, connected with the church only *externa conjunctione*. They belong to Christ’s kingdom *quoad fidei professionem* but to the devil’s kingdom *quantum ad morum perversitatem*. They are *filii propter formam pietatis, alieni propter amissionem virtutum*. There may not be two churches, but there are nevertheless two *partes* in the church, ib. III 9. And the *Catech. Rom.* says that in the church militant there are *duo hominum genera* and that according to Scripture there are bad fish in the net and weeds in the field and chaff on the threshing floor, foolish virgins among the wise, and unclean animals in the ark, I 10, qus. 6, 7. In theory this does not differ much from the doctrine of the Reformation. But in practice matters with the church looked entirely different toward the end of the Middle Ages, and Rome also constantly nourishes the idea that outward membership, historical faith, keeping the church’s commandments, and submission to the pope constitute the essence of the church. The Reformation revolted against this and set over against it the distinction between visible and invisible church. Augustine had already said of nominal Christians, *quum intus videntur, ab illa invisibili caritatis compage separati sunt*, *de bapt.* III 19, in Seeberg, loc. cit., 42. Properly speaking, Rome can have no objection to this distinction and itself accepts it insofar as it distinguishes *duo hominum genera, duas partes* in the one church. Bellarmine treats of the *infideles occulti*, *de eccl. mil.* III 10, and Möhler, *Symb.*, § 49, praises Luther when the latter conceives the church as a communion of saints and says that believers, *die Unsichtbaren*, are the bearers of the visible church. But the distinction between visible and invisible church can be conceived in different ways; above, p. 18. Most of these conceptions, however, must be rejected or at least do not come up for discussion in dogmatics. The church is not to be called invisible because Christ, the *ecclesia triumphans*, or the church completed at the end of the ages cannot now be perceived by us; nor because the church on earth in many places and lands is not seen by us, or is hidden in times of persecution, or is sometimes deprived of the ministry of word and sacrament. The distinction between visible and invisible church applies only to the *ecclesia militans* and then indicates that the church in its spiritual aspect or in its true members is invisible. Both these meanings have flowed together among Lutherans and Reformed and also cannot be kept apart. The church is an object of faith. The inward faith of the heart, regeneration, genuine conversion, hidden communion with Christ, and so forth are spiritual goods that cannot be perceived by the natural eye and nevertheless give the church its proper *forma*. And God has placed into the hands of no human being the infallible standard by which he can judge the spiritual life of others. *De intimis non judicat ecclesia.* The Lord alone knows those who are his. It is therefore possible, and has also always been a fact in the Christian church, that chaff was hidden among the grain and hypocrites among true believers. The name church, used of the *ecclesia militans*, of the assembly of believers on earth, has therefore among all Christians, Roman Catholic as well as Protestant, always had a transferred sense. It is so called not after the unbelievers who are present within it but after the believers who form its essential constituent and give it its essence. The whole is named after the part. A church is and remains an assembly of true Christian believers.
