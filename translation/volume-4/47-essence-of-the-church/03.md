## 3.

This spiritual unity of the church of Christ also continually comes to the foreground in the postapostolic period. Christians are the saints, the elect; they have one God, one Christ, one Spirit of grace, one calling, *Clem. 1 Cor.* 46. The church is a tower that forms one stone with the rock Christ, *Herm. Sim.* IX 13, 18, from which the stones that are impure and black and do not fit are removed, ib. 6, 7; the race of the righteous from which the ungodly are separated, ib. 17, 18. Christians are the soul of the world, *Ep. ad Diogn.* 6; the true Israel, the blessed people of God, Justin, *Dial. c. Tryph.* 116, 123, 135. They are all priests, Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* IV 8, 3; Tertullian, *de exh. cast.* 7; have all received the Holy Spirit, Irenaeus, ib. IV 36, 2; and together form a *communicatio pacis et appellatio fraternitatis et contesseratio hospitalitatis*, Tertullian, *de praescr.* 20; etc. At the same time, like Hermas, a distinction is made between true and false members of the church. With reference to the excommunicated Origen said: *ita fit ut interdum qui foras mittitur intus sit et ille foris, qui intus videtur retineri*. And elsewhere he repeatedly declares that many are called and few chosen, that there are spiritual and fleshly members, that there are weeds among the wheat, and that the walk of many conflicts with their confession; Seeberg, *Der Begriff der christl. Kirche*, Erlangen, 1885, p. 29. But soon a great change occurred in this conception of the church as *communio sanctorum*. When all kinds of sects and heresies arose in the second century, the question naturally arose: Which is the true church? And the answer given was: the one that remains with the whole and maintains communion with the catholic church. The church was already called catholic by Ignatius, *Smyrn.* 8; cf. Murat. and *Mart. Polyc.* 5, 16, 19, because throughout the whole earth, in all times and places, it embraces all believers and outside it there is no salvation, *Clem. 1 Cor.* 57; Ignatius, *Eph.* 16; *Trall.* 7; *Phil.* 3; Hermas IX 16. Over against heresy, however, this catholicity of the church was no longer conceived spiritually but was externalized and embodied in a visible institution. The bishop, descending in a direct line from the apostles and in possession of the pure tradition, became the criterion of the true church. The universal church ceased to be a logical *prius* and was conceived as a historical *prius* of all local churches. Thus a complete reversal occurred in the concept of the church. It is not the local churches that together form a unity, but the catholic church with the episcopate comes first, and the local churches are parts of the whole and are true churches only as long as they adhere and submit to that whole. The development of this catholic concept of the church was promoted by the opposition it encountered from the side of heresy. Gnosticism made the church into a school in which the πνευματικοι were far exalted above the popular representations of historical Christianity. Montanism wished to establish the church on the foundation of claimed inspiration and prophecy, denying every office and authority: *ecclesia proprie et principaliter ipse est spiritus*, Tertullian, *de pudic.* 21. Novatianism and Donatism contended for the holiness of the church at the expense of its catholicity. Against all these errors the church fathers took a stand and placed ever more emphasis upon the episcopal church institution. The church led by the bishops is the sole guardian and preacher of the truth, Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* I 10, 2; Tertullian, *de praescr.* 28, and therefore the indispensable institution of salvation, the mother of all believers, the distributor of grace, the mediatrix of salvation, *scala ascensionis ad Deum. Ubi enim ecclesia, ibi et spiritus Dei, et ubi spiritus Dei, ibi ecclesia et omnis gratia, spiritus autem gratia*, Irenaeus, ib. III 24, 1; Tertullian, *de or.* 2; Clement, *Paed.* I 6; *Strom.* VIII 17. Just as there is only one God and one Lord, so there is also only one church, one flock, one mother from whom all believers are born and outside whom there is no salvation. The ray of light cannot be separated from the sun, the branch from the tree, the stream from the spring, Cyprian, *de unit. eccl.* 5, 7. Augustine too moves within the same circle of thoughts. Although by its unity, catholicity, and majesty the church had already earlier made a deep impression upon him, only through his struggle against Donatism, 393–411, was he compelled to reflect more deliberately upon its essence. Even then, however, not the doctrine of the church but the doctrine of grace remains the center of his thought and life, while the doctrine of the church comes to stand alongside it to a certain extent detached, independent, and unreconciled. For if God is the sole and absolute cause of grace, as Augustine teaches, then the church cannot be this. He therefore immediately distinguishes between the church as *corpus verum* and the church as *corpus permixtum*, *de doctr. chr.* III 32. There are members of the true church outside the visible church, such as the angels, *Enchir.* 29; the thief on the cross, who received only the baptism of blood, *de bapt.* IV 22; and all non-Israelites who were saved before Christ’s coming, *de civ.* XVIII 23, 47, for the Christian religion is as old as the world, *Ep.* 102. To the true church also belong those who now still live ungodly lives or are entangled in superstition and heresy and yet are known by God. *Namque in illa ineffabili praescientia Dei multi qui foris videntur intus sunt, et multi qui intus videntur foris sunt. Ex illis ergo omnibus, qui ut ita dicam intrinsecus et in occulto intus sunt, constat ille hortus conclusus, fons signatus, puteus aquae vivae, paradisus cum fructu pomorum*, *de bapt.* V 27. Conversely, there are many within the visible church who do not belong to the *electi*. There is chaff among the grain, there are bad fish among the good, there are *plurimae oves foris, plurimi lupi intus*, *hom. in Joann.* 45; *c. lit. Petul.* III 3; *de bapt.* I 10. *Multi sunt in communione sacramentorum cum ecclesia, qui tamen non sunt in ecclesia*, *de unit. eccl.* 74. Because of this distinction Augustine was accused by the Donatists of teaching two churches, but he answered that he did not separate the two, any more than someone who distinguishes soul and body in the human being, and that according to Christ’s word weeds and wheat had to grow together. For Augustine the church is not the distributor of grace but nevertheless is the circle within which God as a rule distributes his grace. And so he defends it against the Donatists. The church is the mediatrix of salvation because in it alone the Spirit, love, and perseverance are present. Outside it there is no salvation. For heretics and schismatics can indeed take the word and sacrament with them, but not regeneration and love, which are granted by the Holy Spirit only within the church: *hunc spiritum, quod illi non habeant, qui sunt ab ecclesia segregati, Judas apostolus* 19 *apertissime declaravit*. They have the form but lack the essence, just as severed bodily parts are still indeed a hand, finger, ear, and so forth but have no life. Whoever does not have the church as mother does not have God as father, *de bapt.* VII 44; *de unit. eccl.* 1; *c. lit. Petul.* III 9. The church is *pia mater, sponsa sine macula et ruga, unica columba, sancta ecclesia*. And the church remains holy even if the ungodly are the majority within it, for its holiness, like its unity and catholicity, lies for Augustine much more in the objective institution of doctrine, means of grace, and worship than in the members of the church. Separation is therefore always impermissible, a proof of pride and disobedience, exchanging the universal church for a particularistic or even a national one, *c. Cresc.* II 37; *de unit. eccl.* 12, 14. And precisely through this strong emphasis that Augustine placed upon the institution of the church against the Donatists, he contributed in no small degree to the development of the Roman Catholic concept of the church. Cf., besides the works on the history of dogma by Harnack, Schwane, and others: Seeberg, *Der Begriff der chr. Kirche*, 1–56; Köstlin, “Die Kath. Auffassung v. d. Kirche in ihrer ersten Entw.,” *Deutsche Zeits. für chr. Wiss. u. chr. Leben*, 1856; H. Schmidt, “Aug. Lehre v. d. Kirche,” *Jahrb. f. deutsche Theol.*, 1861, pp. 197–250; Reuter, *August. Studien*, 1887, pp. 4–105; Dorner, *Augustinus*, pp. 276–295; Specht, *Die Lehre v. d. Kirche nach dem h. Aug.*, Paderborn, 1892.

In the Middle Ages this concept of the church was worked out practically in the development of the hierarchy, in the powerful organization of the ecclesiastical institution, in the church’s struggle with and elevation above the state. It is all the more remarkable that theoretically it was hardly treated at all. Not theology but jurisprudence then guided the development, Harnack, *D. G.* III 400. We find something about the doctrine of the church only in Hugh of St. Victor, *de sacr.* II 2; Halesius, *Summa* IV, qu. 4; Thomas, *c. Gent.* IV 76; *S. Theol.* I 2, qu. 101, art. 2; II 2, qu. 10, art. 10; qu. 88, art. 12; III, qu. 8, arts. 3, 4; qu. 68, art. 9. Only after opposition by Wycliffe, Hus, the Reformers, and others was the concept of the church more broadly developed and defended from the Roman Catholic side, especially by Torquemada, 1468; *Catech. Rom.* I, ch. 10; Canus, *Loci theol.* IV–VI; Bellarmine, *Disp. de controv.*, vols. I and II; Becanus, *de ecclesia Christi itemque de ecclesia Romana*, 1615; idem, *Manuale controv.* I 1–5; Bossuet, *Exposition de la doctrine de l’église cath. sur les matières de controverse*, 1671; etc. In this concept of the church the visible institution stands in the foreground. Christ, namely, established on earth a church in which the visible and invisible sides are inseparably joined. Just as in Christ a divine and a human nature, in every human being soul and body, and in the sacrament a sign and the thing signified are united, so in the church there are a visible and an invisible side. The visibility of the church rests upon the incarnation of the Word. Christ is the *causa efficiens, exemplaris*, and *finalis* of the church. He himself continues to live in it as prophet, priest, and king through the Holy Spirit and pours out in it all the gifts of his grace. He communicates these exclusively by means of office and sacrament. The institution thus precedes the organism; the church is a mother of believers before it is an assembly. The *ecclesia docens* with its hierarchical organization and its grace-effecting sacraments precedes the *ecclesia audiens* and stands far above it. To this *ecclesia docens*, therefore, all those attributes that the Roman Catholic Christian assigns to his church are applicable in the first place. It is the one, only, exclusively Christian, catholic, imperishable, infallible church, descending from the apostles through regular succession, which disputes every right of existence to all other so-called churches, is intolerant by its nature, tolerates or acknowledges no other churches alongside itself, and from which deviation in doctrine or separation in life is always sin and never permissible. For because Christ communicates all grace only through office and sacrament, the *ecclesia docens*, the Roman ecclesiastical institution, is the sole mediatrix of salvation, the guardian and distributor of all grace for all human beings, the sole ark of salvation for the whole human race. It alone leads the human being to Scripture, to the person of Christ, to fellowship with God. The order of salvation is not that through his Word God leads the human being to the church; conversely, it proceeds from the church and then leads to Scripture and to Christ. Therefore the church must be recognizable, identifiable, and even demonstrable to all. Through its attributes and marks it must stand out so clearly to the eye that no doubt regarding it is possible and only willful and culpable unbelief can fail to recognize and reject it. It is the very first and principal source of knowledge of the truth and for this reason is treated by many Roman Catholic theologians in the doctrine of the *principia*. The *ecclesia audiens* is completely dependent upon this *ecclesia docens*. It participates only passively in all the glorious attributes of the church. Its only task is to receive supernatural grace from the hand of the priest in the sacrament. Faith in what the church believes, obedience to the hierarchy, submission to the pope is its greatest virtue and necessary for salvation. *Ubi papa, ibi ecclesia.* The essence of the church therefore does not depend upon the quality of this *ecclesia audiens*. It is indeed good and useful that the members of the church are believers: *decor ecclesiae principaliter in interioribus consistit*, Thomas, *Sent.* IV, dist. 15, qu. 3 ad 1. But the *ecclesia docens*, the objective institution of salvation, remains the true church just as much even if its members are unbelievers and ungodly. All who find themselves outside the Roman Catholic church, such as the catechumens, the *excommunicati*, the *schismatici*, and so forth, are not members of Christ’s church. Their Christian faith, their devout walk, does not benefit them; they are outside the church that alone saves. But all who remain in communion with Rome are indeed members of the church, even if they are public unbelievers and ungodly. These are *de ecclesia* not *actu* but *potentia*. They belong not to the soul but to the body of the church. They are not *perfectissime de ecclesia* as are those who believe and live in the Roman church, but they nevertheless are members of the church and belong to it just as much as the body belongs to the essence of the human being. To belong to the church in some manner, more or less perfectly, no *interna virtus* of faith or love is necessary, but only *externa professio fidei et sacramentorum communio*. For the church is *aeque visibilis et palpabilis, ut est coetus populi Romani vel regnum Galliae aut respublica Venetorum*. It is, in a word, *coetus hominum ejusdem Christianae fidei professione et eorundem sacramentorum communione colligatus, sub regimine legitimorum pastorum ac praecipue Christi in terris vicarii Romani pontificis*, Bellarmine, *de eccl. mil.* III 2.
