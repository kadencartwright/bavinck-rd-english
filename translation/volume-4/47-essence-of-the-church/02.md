## 2.

This explains why Christian usage continued to employ the word συναγωγη only for the religious assemblies of the Jews and for the buildings in which they took place, Matt. 4:23; Acts 13:43; Rev. 2:9; 3:9, but otherwise began to use the word ἐκκλησια. This word does still occur a few times in the New Testament for assemblies of the people, Acts 7:38; 19:32, 39, 41, but ordinarily it has a religious character and designates the New Testament congregation. In patristic literature, and especially among the Ebionites, the Christian congregation is still occasionally designated by συναγωγη, but this word soon gives way entirely to ἐκκλησια; Schürer, loc. cit., 432; Sohm, _Kirchenrecht_, 16 f.; Cremer, s. v. It was, moreover, Christ himself who first applied the word קהל, ἐκκλησια to the congregation he gathered around himself, Matt. 16:18; 18:17. Many modern critics are of the opinion that this word was later put into Jesus’s mouth; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 210. But there is no ground for this, and there is nothing surprising in Jesus’s use of the word in that sense. Jesus did indeed come preaching the kingdom of heaven. But he immediately presented a conception of it altogether different from that of his contemporaries, and by no means expected from the outset that the whole people would repent and follow him. By the baptism of repentance John had already separated the true Israelites from the mass of the people. And from the beginning Jesus was conscious not only of his sonship, his messiahship, and his future suffering, volume III 235 f., but he also chose a number of disciples and gathered them around himself; he sent them out to preach and win disciples; he gave his followers laws different from those prevailing in the circles of the Jewish people, Matt. 5–7; 18:15–35; 20, 28, and so forth. Thus a company of disciples gradually formed around him that distinguished and separated itself from the Jewish people. And to this company Jesus now applied the word קהל, ἐκκλησια. They were the true ἐκκλησια, the genuine people of God, as Israel ought to have been but now showed itself not to be by rejecting the Messiah. For Jesus did not come to create something absolutely new but to bring the law and the prophets to fulfillment and to restore the true, essential קהל. Accordingly, when Jesus uses this word for his congregation in Matt. 16:18 and 18:17, he still uses it in a wholly general sense. He does not say that this קהל, ἐκκλησια will be local or will extend over the whole earth; the later distinction between the local and the universal church is not yet found here. But Jesus says quite generally that he will build _his_ ἐκκλησια, in contrast to that of the Jews, not upon the law but upon Peter’s confession of his messiahship, and that he will therefore also organize it independently and cause it to live according to its own laws. In the disciples whom Jesus himself gathered around him, the beginnings of the New Testament congregation are already present. But as long as Jesus was on earth, he himself remained the personal center, and the fellowship of the disciples still receded into the background. They were not yet independent and had to be taught and led by him daily. And the Holy Spirit was not yet, because Christ had not yet been glorified. But after Jesus’s departure they immediately drew more closely together, Acts 1:14, and on the day of Pentecost they received in the Holy Spirit a principle of life of their own, which made them independent over against the Jewish people and bound them most closely to one another. Then the congregation of Christ was in principle detached from Israel’s national existence, from priest and law, from temple and altar; it became a distinct, independent, religious assembly; it took the place of ancient Israel as the people, as the congregation of God.

This ἐκκλησια at first existed only in Jerusalem. But soon there were believers also in Samaria, Antioch, and many other places among Jews and Gentiles, and their assemblies too were designated by the name ἐκκλησια; they too were the people, the congregation of God, in that place. Thus the word gradually acquired different meanings. Jesus still uses the word in a general sense, without having the later distinctions in view. But after his departure it is applied to the circle of believers in a particular place, since this constitutes the people of God there. And then it is applied to them whether or not they have come together in a particular assembly. In Acts 5:11; 11:26; 1 Cor. 11:18; 14:19, 28, 35, the word ἐκκλησια clearly refers to the assembly or gathering of the congregation; but elsewhere it frequently occurs for the congregation itself, even when it is not assembled, and one can therefore speak of ἐκκλησιαι _in plurali_, Rom. 16:4; 1 Cor. 16:1; Gal. 1:2; 1 Thess. 2:14, and so forth. The word acquires a still narrower meaning when it is used for a portion of the believers who gather in a private home at a particular place. In cities where the number of Christians became very large, it was necessary to form so-called house congregations. In various places, for example in Rome, the Jews had more than one synagogue; and the Christians were all the more compelled to divide when they assembled because at first they had no church buildings but gathered in the home of one of the members of the congregation. According to Acts 19:9, the Christians at Ephesus assembled for a time in the hall of a certain Tyrannus, which was perhaps rented for that purpose, but as a rule their meeting place was in a private home. When the congregation expanded to any considerable degree, the believers therefore had to meet in different homes and form what might be called house congregations. This was the case in Jerusalem, where the congregation soon numbered thousands of souls, Acts 2:41, 46, 47; 4:4; 5:14; 8:3; 11:21; 12:12, 17; 21:8, and likewise in Rome, Rom. 16:23; Corinth, 1 Cor. 16:19; Colossae, Philem. 2; Laodicea, Col. 4:15. Each of these house congregations was called an ἐκκλησια. But in this the unity was not lost sight of for a moment. For although the believers in the same city sometimes met in different homes because of their great number, together they nevertheless formed the one ἐκκλησια in that place, Acts 5:11; 8:1, and so forth. If Tischendorf’s reading in Acts 9:31 is correct, all the congregations of Judea, Galilee, and Samaria are there comprehended under the one name ἐκκλησια _in singulari_. And in Rom. 12:5; 1 Cor. 12:12–28; 15:9; Gal. 1:13; Phil. 3:6; Eph. 1:22; 5:32; Col. 1:18, 24, 25, all the congregations are in the same way taken together as one ἐκκλησια and described as the body, the bride, the pleroma of Christ. Nor does this unity of all the congregations first come into being _aposteriori_ through confession, church order, and synodical association; the church is not an association of persons who first came to faith apart from it and afterward united themselves. Rather, it is an organism in which the whole precedes the parts; its unity precedes the plurality of local congregations and lies in Christ. It is he who, continuing his mediatorial work in the state of exaltation, unites and builds up his congregations from himself as their head, Eph. 1:23; 4:16; 5:23; Col. 1:18; 2:19; who gathers and governs it, John 10:16; 11:52; 17:20, 21; Acts 2:33, 47; 9:3 f.; who always remains with it, Matt. 18:20; 28:20; who is most closely united with it, John 15:1 f.; 17:21, 23; 1 Cor. 6:15; 12:12–27; Gal. 2:20; and who dwells in it by his Spirit, Rom. 6:5; 8:9–11; 1 Cor. 6:15 f.; Eph. 3:17, and so forth. To this extent Sohm’s assertion, _Kirchenrecht_, S. 20, that the universal ἐκκλησια precedes the local congregations is correct; it is indeed not the historical but nevertheless the logical _prius_; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 177. Every local congregation is the people of God, the body of Christ, built upon the foundation of Christ, 1 Cor. 3:11, 16; 12:27, because in that place it is the same as the congregation in its entirety, and Christ is to it what he is to the whole congregation; Zahn, _Einl. in das Neue Test._ I 355 f. In the various local assemblies of believers, the one congregation of Christ comes to manifestation. Its essence, both for the whole and for each of the parts in particular, lies in this: it is the people of God, Rom. 9:25; 2 Cor. 6:16, 18; Titus 2:14; Heb. 8:10; 13:12; 1 Pet. 2:9, 10, consisting of human beings who have joined themselves to the Lord and turned to him, Acts 5:14; 14:15, and who bear the names of disciples, brothers, elect, called, saints, and believers, Acts 1:15; 6:1; 9:1, 32; Rom. 1:7; 1 Cor. 1:2, and so forth. In the broadest sense ἐκκλησια is the assembly of all the people of God, not only on earth but also in heaven, Heb. 12:23, and not only in the past and present but also in the future, John 10:16; 17:20.
