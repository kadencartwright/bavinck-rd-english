## 11.

This development of the concept of the church, which can be observed in history itself, has its undeniable shadow side. The idea of one ecclesiastical institution embracing all believers has been permanently disturbed by it. Nor can it be denied that the endless division of the confessors of Christ offers the world a cause for joy and mockery and gives it a reason for its unbelief in the one sent by the Father, since it does not see the unity of believers in Christ, John 17:21. As Christians we cannot humble ourselves deeply enough over the division and discord that has existed throughout all the ages in the church of Christ. It is a sin against God, conflicts with Christ’s prayer, and is caused by the darkness of our understanding and the lovelessness of our heart; cf. Gunning, *De eenheid der kerk*, 1896; *Hooger dan de kerk*, 1897; *Rekenschap*, 1898. And it is understandable that many Christians have repeatedly allowed themselves to be enticed into the attempt to establish or preserve that ardently desired unity of Christ’s church, whether through violent means, especially by the strong arm of the government, or artificially, through syncretism and fusion; cf., e.g., J. von Döllinger, *Ueber die Wiedervereinigung der christl. Kirchen*, Leipzig: Mohr, 1897. But on the other hand we must nevertheless not forget that the failure of all these attempts has something to teach us. History, like nature, is a work of God; it does not take place outside his providence. Through his resurrection and ascension Christ was exalted as King at the Father’s right hand and will remain so until all his enemies have been placed beneath his feet. He reigns also over the divisions and schisms of his church on earth. And his prayer for its unity did not proceed from ignorance of its history or from impotence to govern it. In and through the division, it is daily answered and led toward its perfect fulfillment. The deep, spiritual sense in which Jesus conceives the unity of his disciples precisely excludes every violent or artificial attempt to introduce it. Christ, who prayed for it, can alone also bring it about. His prayer is the guarantee that it already exists in him and in his time will also become manifest from him in all believers. Therefore, for a right understanding of the division of Christ’s church, we must consider the following. 1º Every separation and schism that now exists in Christ’s church dates in principle already from the apostolic period. Although for all sorts of reasons the churches then felt themselves to be spiritually one much more than is now the case even between churches of the same confession, they were distinct in many respects. The apostles at Jerusalem and Paul, the congregations from the Jews and from the Gentiles, differed on many and even important points. There arose serious disagreement between Peter and Paul, Gal. 2:11, and between Paul and Barnabas, Acts 15:39. Heresies and schisms of all kinds already occurred then as well, 1 Cor. 1:10; 11:18, 19; etc. The congregation at Corinth was divided into parties, silently tolerated the scandalous life of one of the brothers, and in part did not even believe in so significant a fact as the bodily resurrection of Christ and believers. And the congregations of Asia Minor, a few decades after they had been founded by Paul, had fallen far below the standpoint in doctrine and life which they first occupied. 2º These separations and schisms in the apostolic period do not yet make so deep an impression because in the New Testament we always deal in the first place with local churches. There was still no bond other than a spiritual one joining all the congregations. But when the hierarchy developed in Christ’s church and considered itself the essence of the church, it was this false, unchristian idea of the church that throughout all the ages elicited schisms and heresies and alienated many true believers from itself. Everywhere and to the same degree that the hierarchy developed—in the Roman, Greek, and Anglican church—the sects repeatedly arose and, if they were not violently suppressed and eradicated, pushed the official church back and often grew above its head. The hierarchical idea of the church, which is primarily concerned with the unity of Christendom, has precisely throughout all the ages promoted division and caused schism. And Protestantism denies its principle if it seeks to maintain the unity of Christendom through any hierarchical coercion. 3º Precisely because the word is the mark of the church and no infallible interpretation of that word exists, every human being has been given by Christ himself the freedom to understand that word for himself as he sees it. Morally he is of course bound to Christ in doing so, and each will have to give account for himself of how he understood and practiced the word of Christ. But over against his fellow human beings and fellow Christians he stands completely free. Rome fears this freedom and reproaches Protestantism for its individualism, subjectivism, and sectarianism. But what is Rome’s weakness, since it must maintain itself through hierarchical means, is Protestantism’s strength, since no creature but Christ himself governs his church. It is entirely true that, if the word is the mark of the church and is placed into the hands of all people, each thereby receives the right to judge concerning the church and, if he sees fit, to separate from it. But this freedom is to be fully respected and hindered by no state or church. Even the dreadful abuse that can be and has been made of it may not for one moment entice us to abolish its use. 4º The division of Christ’s church without doubt has its cause in sin; in heaven there is no longer a place for it. Yet this does not say everything. God loves diversity in unity. Diversity existed among all creatures even when there was not yet sin. Through sin it degenerated and became corrupt, but in itself it is good and is also of significance for the church of Christ. Differences of sex and age, character and aptitude, understanding and heart, gifts and goods, place and century also benefit the truth that is in Christ. He takes them all into his service and adorns his church with them. Indeed, although the division of human beings into peoples and languages had its occasion in sin, it contains something good that is brought into the congregation and thus preserved for eternity. From many tribes and tongues and peoples and nations Christ gathers his church on earth. 5º If, therefore, once again according to New Testament usage, by churches we understand the local churches throughout the whole of Christendom, then there are no true and false churches in an *absolute* sense. A church is an assembly of true Christian believers in a particular place. If somewhere not a single believer remains, either *actu* or *potentia*, then the Word of God is also unknown there and there is no longer a church. And conversely, if the Word of God is still known to some degree in a particular place, it will surely do its work and a church of Christ is present, however impure and mixed. No indifferentism and syncretism is intended by this. Nothing is indifferent, least of all in the truth that accords with godliness. It is not the case that we can calmly surrender and deny the so-called *articuli non fundamentales* provided we accept the *articuli fundamentales*. While in relation to others we must apply Jesus’ word, “Whoever is not against me is for me,” we ought for ourselves to hold to that other word, “Whoever is not for me is against me.” There is great difference in the purity of confessions and churches, and we must stand and strive for the purest. Whoever therefore comes to the conviction that the Protestant church is better than the Roman, and that the Reformed is purer than the Lutheran or Remonstrant or Mennonite, must, without thereby judging his own church as a false church, leave it and join the other. And remaining in one’s own church despite much impurity in doctrine and life is a duty as long as it does not prevent us from being faithful to our own confession and does not, even indirectly, compel us to obey human beings more than God. For a church that compels its members to this would at that very moment, and insofar as it did so, reveal itself to the conscience of its members as a false church that ascribes more power and authority to itself and its ordinances than to the Word of God. 6º One should therefore be cautious with the names schism and heresy. Without doubt these are both great sins. Those make themselves guilty of schism who, although leaving the foundation of doctrine intact, nevertheless separate from the church over subordinate points of worship or church government. Those are heretics who err in the substance of the truth. The former break the communion of the church, the latter the communion of doctrine. Nevertheless, in practice it is difficult to indicate the boundary that separates a legitimate and obligatory breaking of communion with some church or doctrine from an impermissible breach. For Rome this is indeed easy, since it acknowledges only one church and one confession and pronounces the anathema over everything outside it. But Protestantism can at most indicate some general rules and must leave their application in every concrete case to the conscience of believers. The concepts of heresy and schism have thereby acquired an elasticity that counsels caution in their use. Since the Reformation the church has passed into the period of pluriformity, and this fact compels us to seek the unity of the church much more in the spiritual bond of faith than in the outward form of government. Cf. Gladstone, “The Place of Heresy and Schism in the Modern Christian Church,” *Nineteenth Century*, Aug. 1894, pp. 157–194; Kuyper, *Encycl.* II 607f.
