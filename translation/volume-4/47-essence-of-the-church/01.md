# Chapter IX. On the Church

# § 47. The Essence of the Church

## 1.

The community of those who partake of Christ and his benefits bears the name of the church. In the strict sense, therefore, it can be spoken of only within the bounds of Christianity. Yet this does not alter the fact that, just as there are analogies of priesthood, sacrifice, altar, and all kinds of other elements in dogma and worship, so there are also analogies of the church in the religions of the peoples. By nature the human being is already a social being, a ζωον πολιτικον; he is born from, in, and for community and cannot exist for a moment without it. Family, society, the state, and associations of every kind and for every purpose bind human beings together and cause them to live and act in community with one another. Stronger still than all these institutions and corporations is the bond that unites human beings in religion. There is a powerful social element in religion; Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.* § 6; A. Dorner, *Kirche und Reich Gottes*, Gotha, 1883, pp. 11–17. The reason for this is not far to seek: more deeply than anything else, religion is rooted in the human heart. It is immediately given with the human being’s creation in the image of God and is therefore ineradicably proper to his nature. In religion the human being regulates his relation to God, and this relation is central and fundamental. As our relation to God is, so is our relation to our fellow human beings and to all creatures. At the foundation of all questions lies the question of religion. Whoever agrees with us in religion agrees with us in the deepest, holiest, and all-governing convictions and sooner or later also reaches the same insight on derivative points; but a difference in religious conviction, upon serious reflection, causes people to diverge ever further on all subordinate questions. What unites human beings in religion is stronger than material interest, natural love, or enthusiasm for science and art; for religion the human being is prepared to sacrifice everything, even his life. For if he loses it, he loses himself; in religion, according to everyone’s conviction, the human being’s soul and salvation are at stake. Therefore every religion also seeks to propagate itself and to engage in missionary activity. Religion is never a private affair, a subjective opinion, a matter of taste; it always includes the claim to be the true and saving religion and therefore seeks acceptance among others and, if possible, expansion over the whole of humanity. It is never a matter of the individual alone but always also of the household, the family, the people, and the state. It therefore always produces a common dogma and a common worship, borne, as it were, by the consciousness that not the individual human being but humanity is the completed image of God, his temple and body. Outside the domain of special revelation, however, consciousness of the unity of God as well as of the unity of humanity has generally been lost. The unity of religion is limited to members of the tribe or people; civil and religious community coincide; the state itself is also a *Cultusgemeinschaft*. Religion does indeed manifest itself in part also in the independent organization of priesthood, sacrifices, ceremonies, religious associations, and secret societies. The Buddhist religion in Tibet displays so much similarity to that of Rome that when the Jesuit fathers first became acquainted with it, they saw in it a game of the devil. Yet none of the pagan religions attained such an independent organization as that which we find in the church under Christianity. Islam founded nothing other than a kind of theocratic state in which the Arabs are the lords of the subject peoples and the Quran is also the code for civil law. And Buddhism formed only associations of world-renouncing monks, which exercised a paralyzing pressure upon civil society and never became independent over against the state. Cf. Saussaye, *Religionsgesch.* I 132; Pfleiderer, *Religionsphilos.*³ 727 f.; Rauwenhoff, *Wijsb. v. d. godsd.* 835 f.; Tiele, *Inl. tot de godsdienstwet. Tweede reeks*, 1899, pp. 132–154; Falke, *Buddha, Mohammed, Christus* II 155 f.

The Christian church was prepared, however, in the days of the Old Testament. In the patriarchal period the households of believers were the religious congregations and the heads of the households were the priests; an ordered, common worship did not yet exist, although Gen. 4:26 already implies that, over against the Cainites, the Sethites began to call upon and proclaim the name of God, and although after the flood a separation came about among Semites, Japhethites, and Hamites. With Abraham this separation was even fixed for centuries. From then on God allowed the Gentiles to walk in their own ways and established with Abraham and his seed a covenant that also outwardly, by the sign of circumcision, separated the church from the world and that was confirmed at the foot of Sinai and elevated into a national covenant. Under Israel church and state were not one and the same; there was a distinction between priest and king, temple and palace, religious and civil laws. But the two were nevertheless so closely united that citizen and believer, nation and people of God, coincided, and it was the one divine law that governed Israel’s entire life. Israel as a people was an עֵדָה יהוה or a קָהָל יהוה. Both these words are used in the Old Testament for the assembly or congregation of Israel, without any difference of meaning. After the exile, however, Israel’s national existence underwent a remarkable change: the Jews ceased to be a people like the other peoples of the earth and became a religious community. In every place within and outside Palestine, gatherings of believers arose on the Sabbath, Ps. 74:8; Acts 15:21, for reading the law and being instructed in it; teaching was the principal component of the worship practiced in them, Mark 1:21; 6:2; etc. These assemblies, כְנֵסֶת, συναγωγη, increasingly became the center of the Jews’ religious life and acquired an independent organization in places with a mixed or predominantly Greek population. The temple in Jerusalem did indeed continue to exist and continued to be honored as the place of God’s special presence. But the Jews outside Jerusalem gradually acquired a worship that dispensed with temple and altar, priesthood and sacrifice, and consisted entirely in preaching and prayer. In this way the Christian congregation was already prepared in the days of the Old Testament. Like the two Hebrew words, the Greek words συναγωγη and ἐκκλησια were originally used interchangeably for these religious assemblies of the Jews. As a rule the LXX renders עדה by συναγωγη and קהל by ἐκκλησια, except in Ex., Lev., Num., and Josh., where קהל too is usually translated by συναγωγη. Gradually, however, this distinction already arose among the Jews: συναγωγη more often designates the empirical, actual assembly (*congregatio*, assembly), while ἐκκλησια becomes the word for the ideal congregation, insofar as it embraces those called by God to salvation (*convocatio*, congregation). Cf. Schürer, *Gesch. des jüd. Volkes im Zeitalter Jesu Christi* II³ 428–433.
