## 9.

If the church in its essence is an assembly of true believers in Christ, and these are known to God alone, the question by what means the church can be known by us becomes important. The Roman Catholic Christian therefore objects especially to the Reformation conception of the church, because it undermines the certainty of the church and thus of the salvation of his soul, and opens the door to doubt, division, and indifference. Bellarmine says as clearly as possible: _necesse est, ut nobis certitudine infallibili constet, qui coetus hominum sit vera Christi ecclesia, nam cum Scripturae traditiones et omnia plane dogmata ex testimonio ecclesiae pendeant, nisi certissimi simus, quae sit vera ecclesia, incerta erunt prorsus omnia._ Now this is impossible if sincere faith alone truly makes someone a member of the church, for this can never be known by us with certainty, and a _cognitio conjecturalis_ is insufficient; here we need a _certitudo infallibilis_, _De eccl. mil._ III, c. 10, for _tenemur omnes sub periculo mortis aeternae verae ecclesiae nos adjungere et in illa perseverare_, ibid., c. 12. The true church must therefore be as _visibilis_ and _palpabilis_ _ut est coetus populi Romani, vel regnum Galliae aut respublica Venetorum_, ibid., c. 2. Hence Bellarmine exerts every effort to prove the truth of the Roman Catholic Church. He enumerates no fewer than 15 _notae_, namely, _ipsum catholicae ecclesiae nomen, antiquitas, duratio diuturna, multitudo et varietas credentium, successio episcoporum, conspiratio in doctrina cum ecclesia antiqua, unio membrorum inter se et cum capite, sanctitas doctrinae, efficacia doctrinae, sanctitas vitae primorum patrum, gloria miraculorum, lumen propheticum, confessio adversariorum, infelix exitus eorum qui ecclesiam oppugnant, felicitas temporalis_, _De eccl. mil._ IV, c. 4–18. Roman Catholic theologians follow this example but usually reduce the fifteen _notae_ to the four named in the _symbolum Nic.-Const._, namely, _unitas, sanctitas, catholicitas_, and _apostolicitas_; Perrone, _Prael._ I 248; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._ I 255; Scheeben-Atzberger, _Dogm._ IV 372; Jansen, _Prael._ I 659; Hettinger, _Apol._, 7th ed., IV 411; V 106. In this connection it further deserves attention that Rome, properly speaking, has no _notae_ or _criteria_ by which the true church can be known. These, after all, presuppose a standard that stands above the church and by which everyone may judge it. And Rome has no such standard, for Scripture is dependent upon the church, and the church is itself the highest standard for doctrine and life. _Notae ecclesiae_ are therefore at Rome nothing other than _indicia_, properties in which the church comes to expression and reveals itself. The proofs for the church are the same as those for Christianity, for the two are one at Rome. And these proofs render the proposition that the Roman Catholic Church is the true church, not indeed _evidenter veram_ but nevertheless _evidenter credibilem_, ibid., IV, c. 3. The Vatican Council speaks similarly, III, c. 3: _Deus per Filium suum unigenitum Ecclesiam instituit, suaeque institutionis manifestis notis instruxit, ut ea tamquam custos et magistra verbi revelati ab omnibus posset agnosci. Ad solam enim catholicam Ecclesiam ea pertinent omnia, quae ad evidentem fidei christianae credibilitatem tam multa et tam mira divinitus sunt disposita. Quin etiam Ecclesia per se ipsa, ob suam nempe admirabilem propagationem, eximiam sanctitatem et inexhaustam in omnibus bonis foecunditatem, ob catholicam unitatem invictamque stabilitatem, magnum quoddam et perpetuum est motivum credibilitatis et divinae suae legationis testimonium irrefragabile._ The truth of the church is therefore not absolutely demonstrable to everyone; otherwise the church would not be an _articulus fidei_, and faith would not be free and meritorious. According to the Vatican Council, an _efficax subsidium ex superna virtute_ must accompany the testimony that proceeds from the church. In fact, Rome thereby adopts the same subjective standpoint as the Reformation. The motives, however strong, cannot actually move anyone to faith. It is the Spirit of God alone who can inwardly convince someone firmly and certainly of the truth of divine revelation. At Rome too the deepest ground of faith is not Scripture or the church but the _lumen interius_. With its infallible church and its infallible pope, Rome has in principle no advantage over the churches of the Reformation, for church and pope, however visible, are nevertheless _articuli fidei_; cf. vol. I 425, 426, 486–489.

The marks Rome gives for the true church are therefore in no respect clearer or more powerful than the pure administration of the Word, which the Reformation recognized as a mark of the church. Some of the marks Bellarmine names are of very subordinate value. The gift of miracles is by no means conclusive proof of the truth of the doctrine someone proclaims, Deut. 13:1, 2; Matt. 7:22, 23; 24:24, and so forth; cf. vol. I 429 f. The unhappy end of the church’s enemies and persecutors is for the most part only a legend, as Roman Catholics too now acknowledge; Nik. Paulus, _Luthers Lebensende_, Freiburg, 1898. And the church’s earthly prosperity is always temporary, alternates with persecution and oppression, and can just as well be adduced as evidence against the truth of the church, Matt. 5:10; 16:24; John 16:33; Acts 14:22; 2 Tim. 3:12. With other marks, everything depends upon the sense in which they are understood. The name catholic is also accepted by Protestant churches and is in itself no more a proof of the truth of the Roman Catholic Church than the name Christ, which false Christs appropriate to themselves, Matt. 24:24, or the name Israel or Abraham’s seed, in which the Jews boasted, John 8:33; Rom. 9:6. Antiquity, historical continuity, and uninterrupted succession belong not only to Rome but also to other churches, for example, the Greek, and in themselves prove no more for the truth of the Roman Catholic Church than they did for that of the Jewish congregation in Jesus’s day. Unity and catholicity are claims of Rome that cannot nullify the fact that millions of Christians live outside it; there is not only one church, there are many churches, and there is not one that encompasses all believers. The remaining marks—agreement with the doctrine of the apostles, holiness of doctrine, the renewing power that proceeds from it, and the holy life of many of its confessors—belong by no means to Rome alone but also to many other churches, and are subject to the same objections that Roman Catholics bring against the Protestant marks and that will be discussed presently. Cf. Beza, _De eccl. cath. notis_, _Tract. theol._ III 132; Polanus, _Synt._, p. 532 sq.; Amesius, _Bellarminus enervatus_ II 56–72; Maresius, _Syst. theol._ XVI 23 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XVIII, qu. 13; Mastricht, _Theol._ VII 1, 34; Moor VI 50; M. Vitringa IX 1, p. 98; Gerhard, _Loc._ XXII, c. 10, 11; Quenstedt, _Theol._ IV 503. The Roman Catholic Church does indeed rejoice in its unity and points with self-satisfaction to the divisions of Protestantism. But it pays a high price for this joy. First, it is compelled increasingly to transfer the essence of the church from the assembly of believers to the institution of the hierarchy, that is, ultimately to the pope. With more right than Louis XIV could say _l’état c’est moi_, the pope can declare: I am the church. _Ubi papa, ibi ecclesia._ If, as is proper, the church is then conceived not as the institution but as the assembly of believers, the division in the Roman Catholic Church is not so much less than in the Protestant churches. The only difference is that Rome, seeking its strength in the celibate hierarchy, quietly allows every tendency and opinion in the church to exist side by side and deprives its members, even the most unbelieving, of the energy and the sense of freedom and truth needed to break with the church and with their own false position. Second, Rome pays for this joy with the high price of _extra ecclesiam nulla salus_. The teaching of Scripture that salvation is bound to faith in Christ soon came to be understood over against schism and heresy to mean that everyone who wished to become a partaker of salvation in Christ had to be united with the bishop; Ignatius, _Ad Eph._ 4, 5; _Phil._ 3; _Trall._ 7. Those who wish to be saved must flee to the holy churches of God; Theophilus, _Ad Autol._ II 14. _Sola catholica ecclesia est, quae verum cultum retinet. Hic est fons veritatis, hoc domicilium fidei, hoc templum Dei; quo si quis non intraverit vel a quo si quis exiverit, a spe vitae ac salutis aeternae alienus est_; Lactantius, _Inst. div._ IV 30. The church fathers frequently used the image of the ark for the church, and Cyprian in particular employed it to raise _extra ecclesiam nulla salus_ above all doubt; for example, _De unit. eccl._ 6; Ep. 69, 2; 74, 11. Augustine held no other opinion: _manifestum est, eum qui non est in membris Christi, christianam salutem habere non posse_, _De unit. eccl._ 2. Outside the church one may have everything, _sed nunquam nisi in ecclesia catholica salutem potest invenire_, _Super gestis c. Emerito_. Councils and popes confirmed this doctrine. The Fourth Lateran Council declared in c. 1 that there is one catholic church of the faithful, outside which absolutely no one is saved. Trent said that without the catholic faith it is impossible to please God, Session 5. Boniface VIII declared that submission to the pope was _de necessitate salutis_. Eugene IV taught that no one outside the catholic church can become a partaker of eternal life. And Pius IX declared in the allocution of 9 Dec. 1854: _tenendum ex fide est, extra apostolicam Romanam ecclesiam salvum fieri neminem posse._ Rome must therefore be intolerant; it can acknowledge no churches alongside itself. It alone is the church, the bride of Christ, the temple of the Holy Spirit. Yet the facts have proved too powerful even for Rome. Over the course of the centuries, thousands and millions have broken fellowship with the Roman Catholic Church: Novatians, Donatists, Greek Christians, Arians, Monophysites, Monothelites, many sects in the Middle Ages, and then in the sixteenth century more than half of Christendom. And although Rome regained much through the Counter-Reformation, it now numbers scarcely half of the 500 million Christians and, in numerical strength, is declining rather than advancing. In the face of these facts, it is impossible to maintain that there is no salvation outside the Roman Catholic Church. Roman Catholics themselves find it difficult to remain faithful to this doctrine; many are inclined to make concessions. They distinguish between those who consciously, intentionally, _pertinaciter_, and therefore _culpabiliter_ leave the church, and those who are swept along and led astray, are _bona fide_ outside the church, and by _voto, desiderio, animo_ still belong to the church, _ad animam ecclesiae_. In the same spirit the Roman See rejected this proposition of Baius: _infidelitas pure negativa in his, quibus Christus non est praedicatus, peccatum est_; and Pius IX declared in the allocution of 9 Dec. 1854: _pro certo habendum esse, eos qui verae religionis ignorantia laborant, si ea invincibilis sit, nulla posse hujus rei culpa obstringi._ Bellarmine, _De eccl. mil._ III, c. 3, 6; Perrone, _Prael._ I 331; Klee, _Dogm._ I 141; Jansen, _Prael._ I 344; Schanz, _Apol._ III 188; Dublanchy, _De axiomate: extra ecclesiam nulla salus, dissertatio theologica_, Bar-le-Duc: Contant-Laguerre, 1895.
