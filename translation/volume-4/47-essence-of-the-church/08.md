## 8.

Conceived in this way, the distinction between visible and invisible church can be criticized by no one and should rather be acknowledged by all. But another difficulty is connected with the concept of the church. The assembly of believers on earth is organized not only charismatically but also institutionally. It is not only itself the possession of Christ but also serves to win others for Christ. It is *coetus*, but also *mater fidelium*; organism, but also institution; end and means at the same time. The relation of the church as organism to the church as institution first comes up for discussion in the next section, in connection with the government of the church. For just as the concept *state* is difficult to define and becomes clear only when people and government are distinguished and treated separately within it, so a good definition of the concept church can be given only if the assembly of believers is guarded from identification with its organization in the institution: Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII 3, 10; Stahl, *Kirchenverfassung*, 46. Many, however, connect the distinction between church as organism and as institution with that between invisible and visible and thereby imperceptibly give the latter a sense that does not belong to it. On the one side stand those who describe not only the church according to its idea or the *ecclesia triumphans* but also the *ecclesia militans* on earth as the assembly of the *praedestinati* or *electi* (Wycliffe), or of the *perfecti* (Pelagius according to Augustine, *de haer.* 88; the Anabaptists according to Calvin, *Inst.* IV 1, 8; and many others), or of those *qui nunquam lapsi sunt* (Novatian), or also of those members of the church who go to the Lord’s Supper (communicants, as many in America conceive the church). On the other side are the Roman Catholics, who shift the center of gravity of the church from the assembly of believers to the hierarchical institution, to the *monarchia externa et suprema totius orbis*, and seek its essence much more in the *ecclesia docens* than in the *ecclesia audiens*. And all those who, in order to retain unbelievers and hypocrites at least in some measure as true members, describe the church as the assembly of the called (Melanchthon, Löhe, Kliefoth, and others) or of the baptized (Münchmeyer, Delitzsch, Vilmar, and others) also move in that direction. Both these conceptions are one-sided and detract from the essence of the church. From the first standpoint the church becomes entirely invisible, remains an idea, and does not enter into reality. Election by itself does not yet make someone a member of the church on earth. The elect who have not yet come to faith do indeed belong to the church as it exists in the thought and decree of God. They can even be said to belong to the church *potentia*, but they are not yet members of it *actu*. Nor can the church be described as the assembly of the perfect, of those who have not fallen, or of communicants, for believers do not attain perfection in this life, are not guaranteed by God’s promises against every fall, and are not restricted to the number of those who attend the Lord’s Supper. Just as little is the second description named above in agreement with the essence of the church. For outward membership, calling, and baptism are no proof of genuine faith. Many are called who are not elect; many are baptized who do not believe; not all who are from Israel are Israel. Thus while the former arrive at no visible church, the latter neglect the invisible church. Both receive their due only when the church is conceived as the assembly of believers. For it is sincere, true faith that saves, receives forgiveness of sins and eternal life. That faith is a matter of the heart, but it does not remain enclosed within the human being; it manifests itself outwardly in confession and walk, Rom. 10:10, and confession and walk are signs of the inward faith of the heart, Matt. 7:17; 10:32; 1 John 4:2. It is true that faith and confession are also far from always agreeing. There is faith, for example among believers’ children, that is not manifested in deeds, and there is a confession that consists in crying, “Lord, Lord,” and is not born from true faith. Nevertheless, the conception of the church as an assembly of believers has this advantage over its description as an assembly of the called and baptized, that it maintains what matters for every human being and for the whole church. Being called and baptized is not decisive. Rather, whoever has believed and been baptized will be saved, while whoever has not believed, even if he was called and baptized, will be condemned, Mark 16:16.

From this it follows that the distinction between the church as institution and organism is entirely different from that between visible and invisible church and may not be identified with it. For institution and organism are both names for the church according to its visible side. One may not forget here that the church’s institution and organism, appearing visibly, also have an invisible, spiritual background. For office and gift, ministry of word and sacrament, brotherly love and communion of saints all rest upon operations that proceed from the glorified Head of the church through the Holy Spirit. The presentation as if the institution had been added mechanically to the church as the assembly of believers as something accidental and external therefore merits disapproval. Nevertheless, in the church as institution and as organism we think in the first place of the church according to its visible side, that is, of the offices and ministries with which it is equipped and of the communion of saints as it manifests itself in brotherly love. And precisely in both these the church appears outwardly and visibly. Thus the opinion that the church becomes visible only in the institution—in office and ministry, in word and sacrament, in some form of church government—is also incorrect. Even when all this is thought away, the church is nevertheless visible. For every believer manifests his faith in confession and walk in every domain of life, and all believers together stand over against the world with their faith and life. In heaven there is no longer office and ministry, word and sacrament, and yet the church will be fully visible. Visibility and invisibility thus distinguish the church from an entirely different point of view than institution and organism. The latter distinction tells us in what the church becomes visible and recognizable to us; the former teaches that this visible appearance has an invisible, spiritual side known to God alone. With this it is also naturally given that visible and invisible church are not two churches. This objection was already raised by the Donatists against Augustine and was later repeated by Roman Catholics against Protestants. But the charge rests upon misunderstanding. Rome itself acknowledges, as was shown above, that there are *duo hominum genera* in the church, that it has *duas partes*, and indeed tries to demonstrate that unbelievers belong *aliquo modo* to the church but nevertheless does not dare say that they constitute the essence of the church. In fact, therefore, it stands before the same difficulty as the Reformation. For that hypocrites belong *aliquo modo* to the church is no point of difference. Protestants too acknowledge that they are *in ecclesia* and belong to the church as the bad branches belong to the vine and the chaff to the grain. They deny only that these give the church its *forma*, for sincere faith and nothing else is what saves and incorporates into Christ. Unbelievers are therefore not the essence of the church; they are not the *ecclesia*. Invisible and visible church are therefore by no means names for the group of unbelievers and of believers present within a church. Discipline over doctrine and life must be maintained in the church according to the Lord’s command, but every attempt to separate believers and unbelievers and to establish an *ecclesiola in ecclesia* conflicts just as much with the Lord’s command. Matt. 13:30 does not forbid this, for the field intended there is not the church but the world, v. 38; but it follows from it that we are bound to confession and walk and cannot or may not judge the heart. Unbelievers therefore constitute the essence neither of the visible nor of the invisible church. They belong to the church in neither respect, although we lack the right and authority to separate them from believers and cast them out. It can even be said more strongly that the old person remaining in believers does not belong to the church either. Schleiermacher is not thereby right when he considers the essence of the church to lie in operations of the Holy Spirit, for the church is not an assembly of operations but of persons. It is human beings who are regenerated and brought to faith by the Holy Spirit and who as such, as new human beings, constitute the essence of the church. Yet the church is an assembly of believers, and whatever arises not from faith, from the new but from the old person, does not belong to the church and will therefore one day be cast out. Visible and invisible church are for this reason two sides of one and the same church. They are the same believers considered on the one occasion from the side of faith, which dwells in the heart and is certainly known to God alone, and on the other occasion from the side of confession and life, which is turned toward us and perceptible to us. Because the church here on earth is in process of becoming, these two sides are never equal to one another, not even in the purest church. There are always unbelievers within and believers outside the church: *multi lupi intus, multae oves foris*. The latter was the case, for example, under the Old Testament with Naaman the Syrian and still applies to all who for one reason or another live outside the communion of the instituted churches and nevertheless are partakers of true faith. But all this does not detract from the fact that the essence of the church lies in believers alone.
