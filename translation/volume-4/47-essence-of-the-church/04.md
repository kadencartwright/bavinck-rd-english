## 4.

In the same measure as this Roman Catholic concept of the church was practically realized in the Middle Ages, it met opposition and resistance from various sides. The catholicizing of the idea of the church had not occurred in the first centuries without forceful and persistent protest. Greek Christendom, although otherwise agreeing with Rome in the doctrine of the church, never acknowledged the primacy of the pope and thereby resisted the striving for absolute unity and catholicity. Likewise, in the Middle Ages various sects revolted against the development of the Roman concept of the church. The opposition proceeded from different principles. Among the Cathars, Albigensians, Bulgarians, the followers of Amalric of Bena, and the sects of the new and of the free spirit, it was the fruit of dualistic-Manichaean or Gnostic-pantheistic errors. Among others, such as the Waldensians, Bradwardine, Wycliffe, Hus, and so forth, Augustine’s conception continued to work, according to which the church was an assembly of the *praedestinati*. But the recognizability of this true church was then sought not objectively in the ministry of word and sacrament but in the holy life, in life according to the law of Christ, in love, poverty, and so forth. Hence the transition from the idea of the church to reality was lacking, and the attempt at reformation could not work through or also ended in disappointment, Harnack, *D. G.* III 392–419. Only in the sixteenth century did the Reformation place over against Rome’s a concept of the church that differed in principle. Luther found peace for his soul not in the sacrament working *ex opere operato* or in good works, but in the forgiveness of sins through faith alone. From this standpoint he attacked the Roman church, rejected priest, sacrifice, monasticism, infallible ecclesiastical institution, and magically working sacrament, proclaimed the freedom of the Christian person, and conceived the church as an assembly of believers, as a *communio sanctorum*, as it was confessed as an object of faith in the twelve articles. It cost Luther a fierce struggle to break with the Roman church and its concept of the church. He had no program of reformation; at first he was concerned only to oppose abuses. But he found and retained his stability in the justification of the sinner through faith alone and from there went much further than he had originally thought or intended. This principle also led him to another conception of the church, the one he found in Scripture. The church was not simply an assembly of *praedestinati*, nor of those who walk according to a few precepts of the Sermon on the Mount. Rather, it was an assembly of believers, of people who through faith had received forgiveness of sins and thus were all children of God, prophets, and priests. Naturally, it therefore had an invisible and a visible side. According to Seeberg, *Der Begriff der christl. Kirche*, 91, this distinction was first made not by Zwingli but by Luther. Yet he understood by it not two churches but two sides of one and the same church. For Luther the church is no Platonic ideal, no idea without reality, but exists concretely in people who live and through faith are partakers of the forgiveness of sins. On the one side it is invisible, an object of faith, for what one believes one does not see. On the other side it is visible, for it becomes manifest and is recognizable—not by papacy, bishops, vestments, and other externals, but by the pure ministry of word and sacrament. Where this is present, one can be certain that a church is present. There are true believers there, even if only among the children in the cradle: *Gottes Wort kann nicht ohne Gottes Volk sein*. For unbelievers can indeed be in a church, just as foreign constituents can be in a body, but the essence of the church is determined by the believers; the whole is named after its principal part. Cf. Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 317 f., II 534 f.; Seeberg, loc. cit., 84 f.; Gottschick, “Hus’, Luthers und Zwingli’s Lehre von der Kirche,” *Zeits. f. Kirchengesch.*, 1886. Accordingly, in the Lutheran symbols the church was described as a *communio* or *congregatio sanctorum et vere credentium, in qua evangelium recte docetur et recte administrantur sacramenta*. It is indeed visible, has offices and institutions, but *ecclesia non est tantum societas externarum rerum ac rituum sed principaliter est societas fidei et Spiritus Sancti in cordibus*, *Conf. Aug.* and *Apol.* 7, 8; *Smalc. art.* 12; *Cat. maj.* II 3. The distinction between visible and invisible church thus originally served only to declare over against Rome that the essence of the church lay in the invisible, in faith, in communion with Christ and his benefits through the Holy Spirit, but by no means to detract in any way from the visibility, the reality, of the church. Soon, however, it was used in another sense. One could not close one’s eyes to the fact that in the church *in hac vita multi mali et hypocritae admixti sunt*, who are indeed *socii verae ecclesiae secundum externos ritus* but nevertheless do not constitute the church and rather belong to the *regnum diaboli*, *Symb. B.*, ed. Müller, 153, 154, 155. The church could thus be taken in a narrower or broader sense as *ecclesia stricte et large dicta*, ib. 153. Luther sometimes spoke of *zwei Kirchen*, and Melanchthon called this a *discrimen duorum corporum ecclesiae*, *C. R.* 21, 507. Later theologians such as Heerbrand, Chemnitz, Hutter, Gerhard, and others applied to this the distinction between invisible and visible church. The church was now called invisible not because it had a spiritual side and was therefore an object of faith, but because the circle of believers could not be known by us. And visible church became the name not for the manifestation of believers in confession and walk, but for the unbelievers who previously by Luther and the confessional writings were reckoned not to the church but to the *regnum diaboli*. Idea and reality, essence and appearance, were thereby placed loosely alongside each other. The believers formed an invisible *ecclesiola* in the visible *ecclesia*. Cf. Gerhard, *Loc.* XXII 69 sq.; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 493 sq.; Hollaz, *Examen theol.*, 1278 sq.; Buddeus, *Instit. theol.*, 1207 sq.; Schmid, *Dogm. der ev. luth. K.*, 434, 440 f.; Schultz, “Das protest. Dogma v. d. unsichtbaren Kirche,” *Jahrb. f. prot. Theol.*, 1876, pp. 673–690; Krauss, *Das prot. Dogma v. d. uns. Kirche*, Gotha, 1876, p. 80 f.; Seeberg, loc. cit., 104 f., 141 f.; Nitzsch, *Ev. Dogm.*, 529 f.

The Reformed doctrine of the church agrees in the main with the Lutheran, but it nevertheless displays some not insignificant peculiarities. First, the institution of the church occupies a somewhat different place in it. Luther indeed understood the church as the *communio sanctorum*, but nevertheless sought its unity and holiness more in the objective institutions of office, word, and sacrament than in the subjective communion of believers, which frequently left so much to be desired. Thus the church increasingly became a divine institution that had to realize the unity and holiness of believers. In the same spirit Melanchthon in the *Loci* of 1543 described the church as *coetus vocatorum* and said that we should *nec alibi electos ullos somniemus, nisi in hoc ipso coetu visibili*. Later Lutheran dogmaticians found in this a point of difference, that according to their doctrine the elect were not to be sought *extra coetum vocatorum*, whereas according to the Reformed they could also occur outside it. And it is in fact Reformed doctrine that God ordinarily grants the benefits of Christ by means of word and sacrament but is nevertheless not bound to them and, though *rarissime*, grants salvation outside the institution of the church: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 16, 19; Ursinus, *Expl. catech.*, qu. 21; Bucanus, *Inst. theol.*, 400; Gomarus, *Theses* 30, 29. Second, the Reformed connected the church most closely with election and therefore frequently conceived its invisibility differently from the Lutherans. At first Zwingli did indeed apply invisibility to the *ecclesia universalis*, which was dispersed throughout the whole earth and therefore could not be empirically perceived by anyone, in contrast to the *ecclesia particularis*, which was present and visible in a particular place. But later he understood by the *ecclesia invisibilis* the totality of the elect as it is an object of faith in the twelve articles and will first become visible at the parousia. In distinction from this he called the *ecclesia universalis* and *ecclesia particularis* a visible (*visibilis, sensibilis*) assembly of believers, in which hypocrites can also be present, *Fidei ratio*, *Op.* IV 8. In his *Christianae fidei expositio* of the year 1531 he then speaks somewhat differently again and says that the church of believers on earth is invisible insofar as it includes only true believers, and visible insofar as all belong to it, *quotquot per universum orbem Christo nomen dederunt*, *Op.* IV 58. Calvin joins himself to this usage. When he first takes up the expression *ecclesia invisibilis* in the *Institutes* of 1543, he understands by it the totality of the elect, who are known to God alone, and then distinguishes the church as *universa hominum multitudo in orbe diffusa*, which is visible and also contains hypocrites but nevertheless is again invisible and an object of faith insofar as we cannot know who within it are true believers, *Inst.* IV 1, 1–9. The church could thus already be called invisible in three respects: 1º as *ecclesia universalis*, because a particular person cannot perceive the church in other places and other times; 2º as *coetus electorum*, which will first be completed and visible at the parousia; 3º as *coetus electorum vocatorum*, because in the church on earth we cannot distinguish true believers. Later still other points of view were added under which the church could be called invisible: because it is not of this world; because its head Christ, and therefore it itself as his body, is invisible; because its greatest part is in heaven; because it can temporarily and locally be deprived of the ministry of the means of grace; because in times of persecution it hides in deserts and caves; because it is indeed perceptible in its outward confession but not in the inward faith of the heart; because the church is never present in one place and at one time but expands through the ages and peoples, Polanus, *Synt. theol.*, 531. By contrast, the church was then called visible because it becomes manifest in confession and walk, or appears as an institution with offices and ministries, or contains not only true believers but also hypocrites. Confession and dogmatics among the Reformed proceeded sometimes from this and sometimes from that conception. Some placed the church as the communion of all the elect in the foreground and called this the *ecclesia invisibilis*: *Cat. Genev.* in Niemeyer, 135; *Scot.* I 16; *Westm. Conf.* 25; Alsted, *Theol. did. schol.*, 590. Since, however, the elect who do not yet live or have not yet been called can be called members of the church only *potentia*, others left this idea of the church aside and proceeded from the church as the assembly of all *electi et vocati*: *Basil.* I 5; *Helv.* I 15; *Heid. Catech.* 54; *Belg.* 27; *Helv.* II 17; Gomarus, *Theses theol.*, disp. 30; Polanus, *Synt.*, 530; Martyr, *Loci*, 390. Yet they then immediately had to distinguish between true believers and hypocrites and therefore began to speak of *ecclesia stricte* and *latius dicta*, of *esse de ecclesia* and *esse in ecclesia*, or also of *ecclesia invisibilis* and *visibilis*: Ursinus on qu. 54; Alsted, *Theol. did. schol.*, 598; Heidegger, *Corpus theol.* XXVI 29; etc. In connection with the corruption that entered the church, this led to the distinction of two circles or groups of people in the one church, Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII 3, 3, 24, and in the previous century led Janssonius, Van der Eerde, and others to place an external and internal covenant alongside one another, to separate the *forma externa* and *interna* of the church, and to proclaim the doctrine that even the ungraced, if they lived blamelessly, could be true members of the church and had a rightful claim to its goods and benefits; cf. vol. III, 227. In vain did others seek to maintain the unity of the church over against this by saying that invisible and visible church were two sides of the same matter: *Synopsis pur. theol.* 40, 34; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII 7, 4; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 1, 11, 13; Appelius, *De Herformde Leer*, 1769, p. 300f. The doctrine agreed ever less with life. And this was more serious for the Reformed doctrine of the church because it saw the essence of the church in the institution much less than the Lutherans did. For—and this is a third distinction, which will later come more clearly to light—the Reformed indeed also sought the mark of the true church in the pure ministry of word and sacrament, but they ordinarily added to this ecclesiastical discipline and the Christian walk. Election was the foundation of the church but first became manifest in faith and good works. Cf. further on the Reformed doctrine of the church: Beza, *Tract. theol.*, 1582, I 32; Hyperius, *Meth. theol.*, 529; Bullinger, *Huysboeck*, 1612, fol. 204; Bucanus, *Inst. theol.*, 455; Gomarus, *Op.* II 97, 202; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I, p. 1 sq.; Moor, *Comm.* VI 1–179; M. Vitringa, *Doctr. chr. relig.* IX; etc.; cf. Seeberg, loc. cit., 159 f.
