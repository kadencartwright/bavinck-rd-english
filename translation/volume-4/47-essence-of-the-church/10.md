## 10.

For Protestantism, the doctrine of the marks of the true church had an entirely different significance. The Reformation permanently broke the unity of Western Christendom, and different churches came to stand alongside and over against one another. The Reformers had to demonstrate that the church of Rome was not the true church and that the churches of the Reformation corresponded to the essence of the church as Scripture described it. Their reforming act presupposed that the church was not αὐτοπιστος, that it could err and deviate, and that there was a higher authority to which it too had to submit. And this could be none other than Holy Scripture, the Word of God. All the Reformers therefore unanimously returned to Scripture, regarded it also as the standard of the church, and accordingly determined the marks by which the true church could be distinguished from the false. There was indeed some difference in the enumeration of these *notae*. In his work *Von den Concilien und Kirchen*, Luther counted seven: the pure ministry of the word, of baptism, of the Lord’s Supper, and of the keys; the lawful election of ministers; public prayer and instruction; and the cross. Elsewhere, however, he named only two: the pure ministry of word and sacrament. Melanchthon did the same in *Conf. Aug.*, art. 8, and in the *Loci*, as did later Lutheran theologians, Gerhard, *Loc.* XXII § 131; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 503. Only in the *Examen ordinandorum* did Melanchthon add to these two a third, rather hierarchical mark: *obedientia ministerio debita juxta evangelium*. Among the Reformed, some, such as Beza, Sohnius, Alsted, Amesius, Heidanus, and Maresius, gave one mark, the pure ministry of the word; others, such as Calvin, Bullinger, Zanchius, Junius, Gomarus, Mastricht, Marck, and others, gave two, namely, the pure ministry of word and sacrament; and many, such as *Conf. Gall.*, *Belg.*, *Scot.* I, Hyperius, Martyr, Ursinus, Trelcatius, Walaeus, Amyraldus, Heidegger, and Wendelinus, added as a third the right administration of discipline or holiness of life. Alsted, Alting, Maresius, Hottinger, Heidanus, Turretinus, Mastricht, and others rightly observed, however, that this was more a difference in name than in substance and that there is really only one mark, namely, the one and same word, which is then ministered and confessed in different ways—in preaching, instruction, confession, sacrament, life, and so forth; M. Vitringa IX 1, p. 101–109. That the Reformation rightly sought the mark of the church in the Word of God is beyond all doubt when Scripture is in hand. For without the word of God there is no church, Prov. 29:18; Isa. 8:20; Jer. 8:9; Hos. 4:6. Through word and sacrament Christ gathers his church, Matt. 28:19, which is built upon the teaching of apostles and prophets, Matt. 16:18; Eph. 2:20. Through the word he regenerates, 1 Pet. 1:23; James 1:18; works faith, Rom. 10:14; 1 Cor. 4:15; and cleanses and sanctifies, John 15:3; Eph. 5:26. And those who have thus been regenerated and renewed through the word of God are called to confess Christ, Matt. 10:32; Rom. 10:9; hear his voice, John 10:27; keep his word, John 8:31, 32; 14:23; test the spirits, 1 John 4:1; and avoid anyone who does not bring this doctrine, Gal. 1:8; Titus 3:10; 2 John 9. The word is indeed the soul of the church, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 12, 1. Every ministry in the church is a ministry of the word. God gives his word to the church, and the church receives it, preserves it, ministers it, teaches it, and confesses it before God, before one another, and before the world in word and deed. The other marks are included in the one mark of the word as further applications of it. Where God’s word is rightly preached, there the sacrament too is purely administered, God’s truth is confessed according to the mind of the Spirit, and conduct and walk are ordered according to God’s testimony. Even Rome cannot deny that God’s word is the mark of the church. Gerhard, *Loc.* XXII § 138, cites many church fathers who state this plainly and clearly. Thus Tertullian says: *illae sunt verae ecclesiae, quae tenent quod ab apostolis receperunt*, *de praescr.* 21. Formerly, Chrysostom says on Matt. 24:15, it could be demonstrated in many ways which was the church of Christ, but since heresies have crept in, this can be shown only from the Scriptures. Those Scriptures, he explains, *hom.* 33 *in Act. Ap.*, are simple and true, so that it is easy to judge from them which doctrine is true. Augustine repeatedly speaks in this spirit: *inter nos et Donatistas quaestio est, ubinam sit ecclesia. Quid ergo facturi sumus? in verbis Donati eam quaesituri an in verbis capitis sui Domini Jesu Christi? Puto, quod in illius verbis eam quaerere debeamus, qui veritas est et optime novit corpus suum, novit enim qui sunt ejus*, *de unit. eccl.* 2. Bellarmine himself describes the church as *coetus hominum ejusdem Christianae fidei professione et eorundem sacramentorum communione colligatus*, and so forth; includes *sanctitas doctrinae* among the marks of the church, ib. IV 11; and concedes that in some cases, if Scripture is accepted as God’s Word, Scripture is better known than the church and proves its truth, ib. IV 2. In answering the question of what the distinguishing marks of the church are, Rome too must use Scripture as a ground of proof if it does not wish to rest content with *sic volo, sic jubeo, stat pro ratione voluntas*; cf. still others in Gerhard, ib. § 139; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII 12, 16; Scheeben–Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 375.

Yet Rome rejects the marks that the Reformation assigned to the true church. Bellarmine objects first that the pure ministry of the word at most indicates *where* the true church is, but not *which* the true church is—that is, who the true believers are, who alone, according to the Protestant definition, constitute the essence of the church, *de eccl.* IV 2. This objection is correct to a certain extent, but in fact poses no difficulty. For it is by no means necessary for us to know with infallible certainty who the true believers are. To seek infallible marks for that purpose, for example with J. Müller, *Dogm. Abh.*, 346 f., leads onto the false path of the Donatists. The pure ministry of the word is not a mark of the sincere faith of individual members but of the church as an assembly of believers. For God’s promise, Isa. 55:11; 2 Cor. 2:15, 16, and so forth, guarantees to us that wherever God’s word is preached, it will accomplish its work and will not return empty. *Gottes Wort kann nicht ohne Gottes Volk sein, wiederum Gottes Volk kann nicht ohne Gottes Wort sein* (Luther). The Reformers therefore named as the first and principal mark of the church not the confession and life of believers but the ministry of word and sacrament. For the believers who constitute the essence of the church become manifest in two ways: in the ministry of word and sacrament that takes place among them, and in the confession and walk by which they distinguish themselves from the world and also from other churches—that is, in the church as institution and in the church as organism. The nature of the case entails that the mark derived from the ministry of word and sacrament, from the church as institution, bears a less fallible, firmer, more constant, and more enduring character than the mark found in the confession and life of believers. Much may be lacking in the latter without the former therefore ceasing to exist. The Roman church demonstrates this to a very high degree, but it applies to the Protestant churches as well. However important the pure confession and holy walk of believers may be, the principal matter for everyone remains the pure ministry of word and sacrament. This should therefore count as the first and principal mark of the church. Yet the Reformed rightly emphasized that the church as the assembly of believers becomes manifest not only in the institution but also in faith, in fleeing sins, in pursuing righteousness, in love for God and neighbor, and in the crucifixion of the flesh; *Ned. Gel.* 29. The pure ministry of the word also includes the exercise of ecclesiastical discipline.

Another objection of Bellarmine is that the pure ministry of the word is far too general and unclear a mark for the true church to be judged by it. For, on the one hand, the ministry of the word in true churches, such as at Corinth and in Galatia, often still leaves much to be desired in purity, while on the other hand it has not been wholly lost in heretical and sectarian churches. Socinians and Remonstrants reasoned in the same way and disputed the necessity and usefulness of marks by which the true church could be recognized, *Cat. Rac.*, qu. 489; Episcopius, *Disp.* III 28, *Op.* II 2, p. 459. And although Lutherans and Reformed in the early period very emphatically maintained that they were the true church, the increasing impurity of their own churches and the appearance of other churches alongside them made it increasingly difficult to maintain this assertion in all its strictness. Indeed, from the beginning the attitude the Protestant churches adopted toward the Roman church was entirely different from the reverse. Rome can recognize sects but no churches alongside itself, Hettinger, *Apol. d. Christ.* V⁷ 118. The Protestants, however, while decisively rejecting Rome’s ecclesiastical hierarchy, continued fully to acknowledge what was Christian in Rome’s church. However corrupt Rome might be, there were still *vestigia ecclesiae*, *ruinae dissipatae ecclesiae* within it; some *aliqua ecclesia, licet semirupta* remained in the papacy, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 2, 11; cf. *Op.*, ed. Schippers, VIII 111, 309; IX *Epist.* 51, 57; Beza, *Tract. theol.* III 145, 192; Bullinger, *Huysboeck*, 1612, pp. 206, 207; Zanchius, *Op.* II, in the preface to *de natura Dei*; Polanus, *Synt.*, 535; cf. 496; Polanus a Polansdorf, *Part. Theol.*, p. 196; Junius, *Op.* II 1018–1023; Alsted, *Theol. schol.*, 696; Voetius, *Disp. causa papatus*, 699–703; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 1, 25; Turretinus XVIII 14, 24, 27. The Reformation was a separation *ab ecclesia Romana et Papali*, but not *a vera ecclesia*, Turretinus XVIII 15, 8. Id., *de necessaria secessione nostra ab ecclesia Romana, et impossibili cum ea syncretismo*, appended to his *Disp. de satisf. Christi*, 1691, and other anti-Roman Catholic works in Vitringa IX 1, p. 116; Moor VI 58. Further, the Reformers were, or at least soon became, conscious that the pure ministry of word and sacrament could not count as an absolute mark. Calvin warns most strongly against every arbitrary separation. Even if something is lacking in the purity of doctrine or the sacraments, even if holiness of life and the faithfulness of ministers leave much to be desired, one may not therefore leave the church immediately. Only when the *summa necessariae doctrinae*, the *praecipua religionis doctrina*, is exchanged for falsehood does separation become a duty, *Inst.* IV 2, 12–16; 2, 1; commentary on Matt. 13:40, 41; 2 Thess. 3:6. Later, when corruption increased in the state churches and many felt compelled toward separation, most teachers opposed separatism on the same grounds: Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* IV 488; Brakel, *Red. Godsd.*, ch. 25; V. d. Waeyen and Witsius, *Ernstige betuiginge der Geref. kercke aan hare afdwalende kinderen*, 1670; Koelman, *Hist. Verhaal nopende der Labadisten scheuring en velerleye dwalingen met de wederlegging derzelver*, 2 vols., Amsterdam, 1683–84; cf. *Hoe oordeelt de H. S. en hoe oordeelen de Geref. vaderen over Scheiding en Doleantie*, from J. Campen in Sneek. All felt compelled to acknowledge with Calvin that much impurity in doctrine and life can occur in a true church without this giving a right to separation, and that much good is often found in separated churches. Thus the concept of true and false church underwent a significant modification. On the one hand, it had to be conceded that a true church in the absolute sense is impossible here on earth. There is not a single church that absolutely and in every part—in doctrine and life, in the ministry of word and sacrament—meets God’s demand. On the other hand, it became clear that a false church in the absolute sense cannot exist either, since it would then no longer be a church. Although Rome was a false church insofar as it was papal, there were nevertheless still many remnants of the true church within it. There was therefore a distinction between *vera* and *pura ecclesia*: Polanus, *Synt.*, p. 532; Alsted, *Theol. schol.*, 601 sq.; *Synopsis* 40, 37; Maresius XVI 20; Vitringa IX 1, 79. True church became the name not for one church to the exclusion of all others but for many churches that still held fast to the principal truths of Christianity, the fundamental articles, cf. vol. I 520 f., yet otherwise diverged very widely from one another in degrees of purity. False church became the name for the hierarchical power of superstition or unbelief that asserted itself in local churches and ascribed to itself and its ordinances more power and authority than to the Word of God, *Ned. Gel.* 29.
