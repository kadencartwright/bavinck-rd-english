## 12.

In accordance with this, the so-called attributes (*attributa, proprietates, adjuncta, affectiones, epitheta, elogia*) of the church also receive from the Protestant standpoint an entirely different sense than in Rome. Rome has an absolute and exclusive concept of the church. It cannot acknowledge the ministry of word and sacrament as a mark of the church because this still occurs, though in impure form, outside the Roman church as well. It therefore also cannot distinguish between marks and attributes of the church, for the attributes are precisely the *indicia* that identify the one true church. And finally, it must conceive those attributes so sensibly, tangibly, and externally that they apply only to the Roman church and cause it to stand out to all as the church that alone saves. The first attribute, the *unity* of the church, does indeed indicate that the congregation has one Lord, one faith, one baptism. Yet according to Rome it comes to expression especially in the fact that the church established by Christ has one visible head in the pope (*unitas hierarchica, regiminis*) and can never have another church alongside it (*unitas simultanea*) or after it (*unitas successiva*). Properly speaking, the pope is the one decisive mark of the true church; cf. *Cat. Rom.* I 10, 10; *Schema const. dogm. de eccl. Christi* and the accompanying *adnotationes* at the Vatican Council, *Collectio Lacensis* VII 569, 586–588; Bellarmine, *de eccl. mil.* IV 9, 10; Scheeben-Atzberger IV 340; Schanz, *Apol. d. Chr.* III, § 6. Through the unity of the church thus conceived, Rome is obliged today to pronounce the anathema over half of the whole of Christendom. Even the thought of Pusey in his *Eirenikon* and of Palmer, *de doctrina christ.* I, ch. 5, that the Roman, Eastern, and Anglican churches together constitute the one church cannot be admitted. Outside communion with the pope there is no salvation. But in the unity of the church Protestantism thinks first of all of the unity of the Head of the church, Eph. 1:10; 5:22; of the communion of all believers through one and the same Spirit, 1 Cor. 6:17; 12:13; 1 Cor. 12:11; Eph. 4:4, with Christ and with one another, John 10:16; 15:1; Rom. 12:5; 1 Cor. 12:12, 13; Eph. 1:22; and then further of the unity of faith, love, hope, baptism, and so forth, Eph. 4:3–5. This unity is indeed in the first place spiritual in nature, but it nevertheless exists objectively and really and does not remain entirely invisible. It manifests itself, though in a very imperfect manner, outwardly and comes at least to some degree to light in what all Christian churches have in common. There is no Christianity above or beneath the divisions of faith, but there is indeed a Christianity present within those divisions. Because our eye is directed most toward the differences and schisms in Christendom, we always run the danger of failing to recognize this truly existing unity. What unites all true Christians is still always more than what separates them. By the *holiness* of the church Rome understands in the first place liturgical, ceremonial holiness, consisting in this, that the church as institution possesses the legitimate sacrificial worship and the salutary use of the sacraments, through which God, as through efficacious instruments of divine grace, works true holiness in believers. It then understands, second, personal holiness, which is indeed not and need not be the portion of all or even of most in the church, but is nevertheless always found in some and then again in very different degrees: *Cat. Rom.* I 10, 12; Bellarmine, ib., chs. 11–15; Scheeben-Atzberger IV 347; Schanz, *Apol.* III, ch. 10; Jansen, *Prael.* I 452. Because the Reformation caused the church once again to be known as a communion of saints, it sought holiness not first of all in the supernatural character of the institution of salvation but in the spiritual renewal of the members of the church. The church is holy because it is a communion of saints. Yet in this the Reformation did not lapse into the evil of Donatism and in practice rather neglected this attribute of the church too much. But that does not alter the fact that according to the principle of the Reformation the church is holy because it is a communion of saints. And believers are called holy, first, because objectively in Christ, by virtue of the imputation of his righteousness, they are reckoned by God as saints; and second, because, regenerated from water and Spirit and renewed according to the inner person, they have desire and longing to walk sincerely according not only to some but to all God’s commandments, John 17:19; Eph. 5:25–27; Titus 2:14; 1 Thess. 4:3; Heb. 12:14; 1 Pet. 2:9. This attribute of the church too is spiritual but not wholly invisible. Although even the holiest, as long as they are in this life, still have only a small beginning of perfect obedience, they nevertheless walk according to the Spirit and not according to the flesh.

The third attribute is *catholicity*. In Rome the church bears this name, first, because although forming one whole and a perfect unity, it nevertheless extends throughout the whole earth, while sects always remain limited to some country or part of the world. Second, it is catholic because, although previously existing in a less perfect form, it nevertheless has always been on earth from the beginning of the world and has included within itself all believers from Adam’s days onward, while sects always come and go. And third, it is so called because it perfectly participates in, preserves, and distributes all truth and grace destined by God for communication to human beings, and is therefore the one and necessary institution for salvation for all human beings, while sects always possess only a part of the truth. Since catholicity in Rome must be a clearly visible mark of the church, it is to be understood especially in this sense, that among all peoples where it exists the church numbers a conspicuous multitude of members. In the first period this was indeed not yet the case, but the church nevertheless soon came to great expansion. And now the demand of catholicity is that the membership of the true church need not be greater than that of all human beings living outside it, but must nevertheless be greater than the membership of every sect separately and probably also of all sects together. Cf. *Cat. Rom.* I 10, 13; Bellarmine, *de notis eccl.*, chs. 4, 7; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 351; Schanz, *Apol. d. Chr.* III, § 7; Söder, *Der Begriff der Katholicität der Kirche und des Glaubens nach seiner gesch. Entw.*, Würzburg, 1881. The Roman Catholic Christian thus seeks an essential mark of the true church in outward splendor and glory, in geographical expansion and numerical strength of members. Church fathers such as Tertullian, Origen, and Augustine already began to exaggerate the spread of Christianity among the nations. And their example is still always followed by many Roman Catholics, for example in missionary statistics. Yet today one can no longer close one’s eyes, as formerly, to the fact that there are still nearly one billion non-Christians and scarcely five hundred million Christians; that the latter are again divided into approximately 112 million Greek, 225 million Roman, and 160 million Protestant Christians; and that in this century Roman Christians are declining regularly in numerical strength almost everywhere and are being overtaken by Protestant Christians. According to this mark of catholicity supplied by the Roman church itself, the state of its truth grows steadily sadder. The name catholic applies ever less to the Roman church. Roman and catholic also contradict one another. Just as under the Old Testament the dispensation of grace had Jerusalem as its center and bound all believers to that place, so in the days of the New Testament the Roman church makes the faith and salvation of human beings dependent upon a particular place and a particular person and thereby detracts from the catholicity of Christianity. The name Roman or papal church therefore expresses its essence much better than catholic. A catholic church is believed and confessed by all Protestants in the Apostles’ Creed and sometimes also in their own confessions, *Ned. Gel.* 27; *Apol. Conf. Aug.*, arts. 7, 8. By it they ordinarily understood the *ecclesia universalis*, which embraced all true believers and came to manifestation more or less purely in the different churches, or also the church of the New Testament, which in distinction from that of the Old Testament was destined for all peoples and places of the earth. The word catholic does not occur in Scripture. But the texts to which the church fathers appeal for the catholicity of the church—such as Gen. 12:3; Ps. 2:8; Isa. 2:2; Jer. 3:17; Mal. 1:11; Matt. 8:11; 28:19; John 10:16; Rom. 1:8; 10:18; Eph. 2:14; Col. 1:6; Rev. 7:9; etc.—prove that its significance lies especially in this, that Christianity is a world religion, destined and suited for every people and age, every station and rank, every place and time. The most catholic church is the one that has most clearly expressed this international and cosmopolitan character of the Christian religion in its confession and applied it in practice. The Reformed had an eye for it when in the different countries and churches they confessed the truth in their own free, independent manner and at the Synod of Dort invited delegates from the whole of Reformed Christendom; cf. my address on *De Katholiciteit van Christ. en Kerk*, Kampen, 1888. The fourth attribute of the church is its *apostolicity*. According to Rome, this belongs to it because it was founded by the apostles, agrees in doctrine, organization, and worship with that of the apostles, but especially because its officebearers are successors of the apostles in an unbroken line and have received their power and authority from those who in their turn had received them from the apostles in legitimate succession. The first meaning is in this connection wholly subordinated to the second. The word of the apostles, that is, Holy Scripture, does not determine which church is apostolic, that is, agrees with the doctrine of the apostles. Conversely, the church descending from the apostles in unbroken succession decides what is apostolic, what the doctrine of the apostles is. Indeed, after the promulgation of the dogma of infallibility, the apostolic succession of officebearers is itself determined entirely by their communion with the pope. Even if a bishop participates in apostolic succession, it immediately becomes void if he breaks communion with the pope. Conversely, *kann der Papst vermöge seiner kirchlichen Vollgewalt jeden Mangel heben, der etwa der formalen Apostolicität irgend eines Kirchenvorstehers anhaftet. So ist die Einheit mit dem Papste nothwendig, damit ein Vorsteher rechtmässiger Nachfolger der Apostel werden oder sein kann, es ist aber jene Einheit auch sofort hinreichend, um die wahre Apostolicität des letztern zu erkennen*, Scheeben-Atzberger IV 1, p. 356. The pope makes everything right. Where the pope is, there is the true church, pure doctrine, apostolic succession. Now such an apostolic succession is to be found by no word in Scripture and is in itself no more a guarantee of the purity of doctrine than the hereditary high-priestly dignity in Caiaphas was proof of the rightness of his pronouncements and deeds. Therefore the Protestants rightly said that not the *successio locorum et personarum* but the *successio doctrinae* was a distinguishing attribute of the true church. If the latter was lacking, the former could make no church into a true church; and if it was present, the former was of very subordinate significance.

Finally, the *indefectibilitas* and *infallibilitas* also belong among the attributes of the church. Jesus promised his church that the gates of hell would prevail nothing against it and that he would preserve it until the end of the world, Matt. 16:18; 28:20; Eph. 4:11–13; 1 Tim. 3:15. Roman Catholics infer from this that their church, the papal church, will continue until the end of the world, and not only that, but also that this papal church will always remain the catholic church, visible and recognizable to everyone by the multitude of its members and its outward splendor: Bellarmine, *de eccl. milit.*, chs. 11, 13, 16; *de notis eccl.*, chs. 5, 6; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 1, p. 359. But sufficient ground for this claim is lacking. Not only was the church at different times—for example those of Noah, Abraham, Elijah, Christ, and so forth—limited to a few persons, but repeatedly particular churches in particular lands, for example in Asia Minor, also perished. Indeed, the New Testament says clearly that in the last days corruption will increase and the church will be exposed to all kinds of deception and persecution, Matt. 24:21, 22; Luke 18:8; 2 Tim. 3:1. Jesus’ promise thus does guarantee that there will always be an assembly of believers on earth, which Socinians and Remonstrants wrongly deny, Moor IV 122, but it does not in the least imply that a particular church in a particular country will always continue and be recognizable to everyone by its size and glory. And so it is also with the infallibility of the church. The Roman church long hesitated to give an answer to the question in whom infallibility ultimately resides and finally decided it at the Vatican Council in favor of the pope. The pope guarantees that the *ecclesia docens* cannot err *in docendo*. But Holy Scripture nowhere connects infallibility to a particular person or to a particular local church. There is indeed an infallibility of the church, which Protestants also gladly acknowledge, but this infallibility belongs to the church as the assembly of true believers and consists in this, that Christ as King of his church will ensure that there will always be an assembly of believers on earth, however small and insignificant, that will confess his name. Belg.*, art. 27; Witsius, *Exerc. in Symb.*, 24; Vitringa IX 1, p. 81; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 1, 9; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 482, 497; Thomasius, *Christi Person u. Werk* II 543; Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 3, p. 16 f.; Hase, *Handb. d. prot. Polemik* I, ch. 1; Van Oosterzee, *Dogm.*, § 130.
