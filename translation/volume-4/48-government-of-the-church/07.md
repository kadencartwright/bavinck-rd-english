## 7.

Christ’s kingship over his church consists in this, that through Word and Spirit he gathers and governs his own and protects and preserves them in the redemption acquired, *Heid. Cat.* 31, 54. The church has its foundation and unity in God’s counsel, in the covenant of grace, in the person of Christ. But because it consists of human beings, it must be gathered and brought in through Word and Spirit. This gathering is effected by Christ and proceeds from him. Even though he uses offices and means of grace in it, it is nevertheless he who distributes the benefits of the covenant and thereby establishes his church. He himself builds the church upon the rock of the confessing apostles, Matt. 16:18, and they are the ones who as instruments in his hand build the church upon him as the foundation, 1 Cor. 3:11. Christ is the vine, and believers are the branches that proceed from him, draw sap, and bear fruit, John 15. Christ is the head, and the church is the body that is joined together from him and receives its growth, Eph. 4:16; Col. 2:19. Christ is the Shepherd, and believers are the sheep who are brought in by him and joined into one flock, John 10:16. Christ is the Lord who adds to the church those who are being saved, Acts 2:47. Because the church is an organism, the head precedes the members and the *ecclesia universalis* precedes the *ecclesia particularis*. The church as a whole does not come into being through the atomistic joining of different parts. Rather, the *ecclesia catholica* exists first; it has its subsistence in Christ, first came to manifestation in the days of the New Testament in the church at Jerusalem, and then expanded from there into other places. Every *ecclesia particularis* (*localis*) is, in the place where it appears, a manifestation of the *ecclesia catholica*, of the people of God. Already by virtue of its origin it stands in an indissoluble connection with it. For no local church arises autochthonously from the unconscious but was planted through the seed of the word which another church caused to be sown in that place. According to the doctrine of Holy Scripture, every local church is indeed independent, an *ecclesia completa*, however small and insignificant it may be. There are no mother churches in the sense that one church may rule over another. Neither Jerusalem nor Rome has any claim to such government. All churches stand equal because all, even if one was mediately established by another, depend in the same manner—that is, directly and absolutely—upon Christ and are bound to his word. Therefore the Reformed not only broke the connection of their churches with that at Rome but also put an end to the diocese and the parish. For a diocese is the ecclesiastical territory of a bishop who, connected to the principal church, from there rules the whole circle of believers. And a parish designates the group of believers in a particular place only as object of the activity of the parish priest. Both concepts indicate that bishop and parish priest are sent to believers from outside and stand high above them as rulers. But this is not the case. Every church is independent, chooses and calls its ministers, and in the ecclesiastical federation into which it was received stands completely equal with all other churches. Sometimes—for example, among the churches of the Huguenots in France—it has the appearance as if the federation arose entirely freely through confederation. Yet this is not the Reformed conception, which on this point stands decidedly over against that of the Independents. In describing the essence of the church, all Reformed theologians proceeded from the *ecclesia universalis* and so descended to the *ecclesiae particulares*, M. Vitringa IX 60. The latter are local manifestations of the one mystical body of Christ, are therefore spiritually one, stand connected with one another by virtue of their historical origin, and are obligated by the Lord to maintain communion with all who partake of the same faith. Every local church is therefore at the same time an independent manifestation of the body of Christ and a part of a greater whole, an *ecclesia particularis* which proceeds from, and is spiritually and historically connected with, the *ecclesia catholica*; cf. Rieker, *Grundsätze ref. Kirchenverf.*, 80 f. And what applies to every local church also applies to each of its members in particular. No church and no living member owes its origin to its own will or to the work of human beings. Christ, even though through the ministry of the word, called and gathered him—and not only him, but all who are members of the church. It is therefore not we but Christ alone who determines who are members of the church and with whom we must live in communion. It is not at our pleasure whether to join this or that church, but it is the bounden duty of all believers to join that church which comes most purely to manifestation as the church of Christ, *Ned. Gel.* 28. Here too the Reformed stand over against the Independents. Believers do not arbitrarily divide themselves into conventicles and congregations and do not themselves select with whom they wish to assemble. Rather, in a particular place all believers belong together and are there together the people of God and the church of Christ. Just as it is God who appoints the times and the boundaries of each person’s dwelling, Acts 17:26, so it is also Christ who, joining himself to this ordinance of the Father, gathers believers locally and causes them to appear as an independent *ecclesia*. Nature and grace were not torn apart by the Reformed on this point either, nor set hostilely over against one another, for grace restores nature and the gospel is the fulfillment of the law. Yet so-called parish formation does not conflict with this unity of the local church. Various congregations in the New Testament—Jerusalem, Rome, Corinth, and others—were each in themselves a unity. That at Jerusalem stood under the same college of apostles and as a whole appointed seven deacons, and those at Rome, Corinth, and Colossae received letters from Paul in which all believers in the same place were comprehended by him as a unity. But that did not prevent those congregations from meeting in different buildings at their assemblies and so again forming distinct house churches. And every church that expands to a membership of thousands of souls must come to this. Just as it is permissible and obligatory to meet in different buildings, so in the interest of the spiritual welfare, government, and care of believers it is commanded that a definite number of ministers, elders, and deacons be attached to each group of believers meeting in a particular building. This need not detract from the unity of the church, since that unity can come to expression in the church council, in administration, in the calling of ministers, and so forth. And sometimes, with the great expansion of many cities in the present time, sections of a city differ more from one another in character than nearby villages or towns and the churches formed there.

In these local churches Christ pours out all kinds of gifts—not only saving gifts of regeneration, conversion, faith, and so forth, but also spiritual gifts known by the name *charismata*; above, p. 29. In the apostolic period there was a rich dispensation of them. But even though in part they have changed in nature and operation, they are still granted today by the Holy Spirit to believers so that with them they may serve one another and manifest themselves as one body. The congregation is not underage; it is no *ecclesia audiens* or *ordo oeconomicus* that has only to listen and be silent. Rather, it partakes of the anointing of the Holy One, consists of many members who all need one another, and may not neglect the gifts granted to it. Every congregation is and must be an army of salvation that under Christ fights against devil, world, and flesh and knows no soldiers at rest or on inactive status; a communion of saints in which all suffer and rejoice with one another and willingly and joyfully employ their particular gifts for the benefit and salvation of the other members. And just as all believers have a gift, so they all also stand in office. They have a calling and task imposed upon them by the Lord not only in the church as organism but also in the church as institution. The apostles indeed precede the church, are its founders, and bind it to themselves, that is, to God’s Word. But they do not appoint officebearers beforehand and by their own authority. Rather, they first establish congregations and then have elders and deacons chosen by those congregations themselves. The general office of believers therefore precedes the special office of overseer and caregiver of the poor. For Christ is in the midst where two or three are gathered in his name, Matt. 18:19, 20. He acquired the Holy Spirit for all, who dwells in all believers as his temple, Acts 2:17; 1 Cor. 6:19; Eph. 2:22; etc., so that, anointed with that Spirit, they are a holy, royal priesthood, 1 Pet. 2:5, 9; prophets, who proclaim God’s virtues, confess his name, and know all things, Mark 8:38; Matt. 10:32; 1 John 2:20, 27; priests, who present their bodies as a living, holy sacrifice pleasing to God, Rom. 12:1; 1 Pet. 2:5, 9; Heb. 13:16; Rev. 1:6; 5:10; kings, who fight the good fight, conquer sin and world and death, and will one day reign with Christ, Rom. 6:12, 13; 1 Tim. 1:18, 19; 2 Tim. 2:12; 4:7; 1 John 2:13, 14; Rev. 1:6; 2:26; 3:21; 20:6, and therefore bear the name Christians, anointed ones, Acts 11:26; 26:28; 1 Pet. 4:16; cf. *Heid. Cat.*, Q. 32. This prophetic, priestly, and royal activity of believers may be called the exercise of an office. For already in general the human being exists not for himself but for God’s sake. God created him in his image so that he might know, love, and glorify him, and thus serve him as prophet, priest, and king. But specifically Christ was appointed by the Father as mediator, servant of the Lord, prophet, priest, and king in order to restore and complete this work which the human being had neglected and disrupted. And believers are now also called to it. As anointed ones who partake in communion with Christ, they are called to the same work, service, and battle, John 12:26; 14:12. From the moment of their calling believers are no longer their own but belong to Christ. They are his servants, have to do his will and accomplish his work. They are the salt of the earth, the light of the world, and specifically have a threefold task in and with respect to the church. First, they are obligated to join the church. They do not stand by themselves but are members of Christ’s body and must therefore seek and maintain communion with it. Second, in that congregation they are called to all kinds of activity: to employing their gifts for the benefit of others, suffering and rejoicing with the brothers, attending the assemblies of believers, proclaiming the Lord’s death, watching over one another, serving and distributing in mercy, and so forth. And finally, each in his own way and measure is also obligated to the formation and reformation of the church. If somewhere in the world there are believers and no opportunity exists for ministers from elsewhere to direct the election to the offices instituted by Christ and lay hands upon those elected, then they themselves have the right to come together in the Lord’s name to elect and ordain officebearers. This in fact occurred at Mainz and Paris in 1555; cf. Lechler, *Gesch. d. Synodal- und Presb. Verfassung*, 1854, pp. 65, 66; Doumergue, *Jean Calvin*, 1899, I 232. So the Reformed judged, Voetius, *Disp. Causa Papatus*, p. 268 sq., and so was also Luther’s opinion, Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 327. The office does not depend upon any succession; it does not arise through transfer. It rests upon the gift and calling of Christ and upon the designation by his church. And that church itself is mature and partakes of the gifts of the Holy Spirit. The gifts necessary for office are not essentially different from those granted to all believers. Therefore it can designate from its midst those who are adorned in special measure with official gifts and in Christ’s name call and elect them to office. But from this it also follows that if necessary believers themselves may proceed to the reformation of the church. When a church in its offices and ministries shows that it ascribes more authority to itself and its ordinances than to the Word of God and clearly manifests itself as a false church, then believers have the holy office and bounden duty to separate themselves and once again begin to live ecclesiastically according to the Lord’s Word, *Ned. Gel.*, arts. 28, 29.
