## 3.

The church has therefore never lacked a government, and it did not provide this for itself but received it from God. The institute and organism of the church were each time called into being by God simultaneously and in connection with each other. It can even be said that the apostolate preceded the church of the New Testament; the apostles were the founders of the congregation, as it were the patriarchs of the people of God in the days of the New Testament. But this apostolate was not continued and, as an office for the founding of the church, was by its nature incapable of continuation; for us it lives on only in the apostolic Word, which remains the foundation of the church and brings us into fellowship with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ, 1 John 1:3. As soon as the apostles had founded congregations in various places, they instituted offices in those congregations that differed essentially from their own and did not come into being without the cooperation of the congregations themselves. There is a great distinction between the extraordinary offices of apostles, evangelists, and prophets, which were temporarily instituted for the founding of the church, and the ordinary offices of presbyters and deacons, which arose from the churches themselves under apostolic guidance. These latter offices presuppose the churches in the same way that government presupposes the people. They could therefore not be instituted directly and immediately by Christ, as was the apostolic office, but could arise only when the congregations had been founded and came to need regular leadership. This soon took place in the church at Jerusalem. Through the extraordinary blessing of Pentecost, this church quickly expanded greatly and numbered thousands of souls, Acts 2:41, 47; 4:4, 21, 32; 5:14; 6:1. This naturally made organization urgently necessary, and it too came into being under the apostles’ leadership. First, despite its unity, this congregation of thousands of souls was divided in one way or another. It could not meet in one building but had to assemble by sections in private homes. Without doubt, the first house congregations arose in Jerusalem, such as we also encounter elsewhere in the apostolic age; see above, p. 6. For we read that the believers assembled not only in the temple but also κατ’ οἰκον (not from house to house, but at home, in different houses), Acts 2:46; 5:42, among others in the house of Mary and of James, Acts 12:12, 17. In order that everything might take place decently and in order, all kinds of regulation were necessary for these gatherings. And perhaps the example of the Jewish synagogues, with their elders, officials, and servants and also with their Scripture reading, preaching, prayer, and blessing, exercised some influence in this connection, although certainly not a very strong one; Schürer, _Gesch. des jüd. Volkes im Zeitalter J. Chr._, 3te Aufl., II 437–459. The designation οἱ νεωτεροι, which occurs in Acts 5:6, 10, already points to such a regulation; the article indicates that the younger members of the congregation, like the הַחַזָּנִים, ὑπηρεται in the synagogues, Luke 4:20, were naturally designated to perform certain subordinate services. It is not improbable that as such they stood over against the older members of the congregation, οἱ πρεσβυτεροι. Under Israel the aged were honored for their gray hair and wisdom. From their midst were appointed the governing persons of the civil community and, in later times, also the caretakers and overseers of the synagogues. Thus there were also elders in the Christian congregation from the outset, that is, men and women who not only were older in age but had personally known or met Jesus, had heard his words and witnessed his miracles, had confessed him as the Messiah even before the day of Pentecost, or perhaps had even belonged to the seventy sent out by Jesus to the cities and villages of Palestine, Luke 10:1, and who for all these reasons quite naturally stood in high esteem among those who were later added to the congregations. They held no office but nevertheless occupied a prominent place in the congregation of Christ through their knowledge and godliness. A distinction must therefore be made between πρεσβυτεροι and ἐπισκοποι. The proofs for this are: 1º that the name πρεσβυτερος, as a designation of the office of overseer, is gradually defined more precisely and replaced by ἐπισκοπος, Acts 20:28; Phil. 1:1; 1 Tim. 3:2; Titus 1:7; 1 Pet. 2:25; 2º that after speaking about the offices in 1 Tim. 3, Paul nevertheless goes on in 1 Tim. 5 to indicate the attitude Timothy must adopt toward various members of the congregation—older and younger, men and women; cf. 1 Pet. 5:5; 3º that the apostolic fathers, Clement, _1 Cor._ 1, 3; 3, 3; 21, 6; 47, 6; 57, 1; 63, 3, 4, and Hermas, _Vis._ II 4; III 1, clearly speak of a class of elders that continues to exist in the congregation alongside the actual office-bearers and has a claim to reverent obedience; and 4º that the well-known text 1 Tim. 5:17, as will appear presently, yields no good sense without adopting this distinction. We should therefore probably picture the situation in this way: the twelve apostles were far from able to complete all the work in the large congregation at Jerusalem and consequently soon made use of the νεωτεροι for lower services and of some of the πρεσβυτεροι for higher services in the congregation. When and how this happened is not reported to us in Acts. We first find the πρεσβυτεροι mentioned in Acts 11:30; 14:23; 15:2, 6, 22; 16:4; 20:17, 28; 21:18; James 5:14, without anything being related about their origin. It is not impossible that the apostles began making use of the πρεσβυτεροι before Acts 6, that is, before the institution of the diaconal office; οἱ νεωτεροι in Acts 5:6, 10 points to a distinction from οἱ πρεσβυτεροι. In any case, however, the book of Acts teaches us that in various congregations men were soon appointed under the guidance of the Holy Spirit to exercise oversight over the congregation. At first, because they were generally chosen from among the elders, they bore the name οἱ πρεσβυτεροι, but later, with a view to their sphere of work, they received that of ἐπισκοποι. _Episcopi_ are therefore those _presbyteri_ who were designated for a particular service in the congregation; all _episcopi_ are thus _presbyteri_, but by no means were all _presbyteri_ _episcopi_; _presbyteri_ formed a class, while _episcopi_ held an office. When, however, as frequently happened at first, the _episcopi_ were called _presbyteri_, there was no difference in the name; _presbyteri_ and _episcopi_ were then the same persons and bearers of the same office, Acts 20:17, 28; 1 Tim. 3:1; 4:14; 5:17, 19; Titus 1:5, 7; 1 Pet. 5:1, 2. This presbyterial or episcopal office was first instituted in Jerusalem and in the congregations composed of Jews, Heb. 13:7, 17, 24; James 5:14, but then also in those composed of Gentiles. According to Acts 14:23, Paul and Barnabas designated elders in every congregation. Now in the letters to Rome and Corinth, Paul does not indeed mention this office in so many words. But various passages—Acts 20:17, 28; Rom. 12:8; 16:5, 10, 11, 14, 15; 1 Cor. 14–16; 16:15, 16; Phil. 1:1; 1 Thess. 5:12–14; 1 Tim. 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9; 1 Pet. 5:1; Rev. 4:4, 10; 5:6, 8 f.—prove that the office of elder was a familiar, universally occurring apostolic institution. And this is further reinforced by the testimony of Clement of Rome, _1 Cor._ 42, that the apostles, preaching in the country and in the cities, appointed the first converts as overseers and deacons over those who would afterward believe.

The task entrusted to these elders becomes clear from the description of their office. The name presbyters sheds no light on it and therefore gives way to other names, especially that of overseers, Acts 20:28; Phil. 1:1; 1 Tim. 3:2; Titus 1:7, just as Christ himself also bears that name, 1 Pet. 2:25. Further, they are called προισταμενοι, Rom. 12:8; 1 Thess. 5:12; κυβερνησεις, 1 Cor. 12:28; ἡγουμενοι, Heb. 13:7, 17, 24; ποιμενες, Eph. 4:11. They must care for the congregation not for filthy gain or with domineering conduct but with a ready mind, govern it as the flock of the Lord, and therefore satisfy various requirements, particularly also the requirement that they govern their own household well, Acts 20:28; 1 Tim. 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9; 1 Pet. 5:1–3. From this description it appears that the office of elder was entrusted in the first place with the oversight, government, and leadership of the congregation. Naturally, some knowledge of the truth was also necessary for this. According to Acts 15:4, 22, 23, at the assembly in Jerusalem they even had to participate with the apostles in judging and deciding the weighty question placed on the agenda by the conversion of the Gentiles concerning the relation to the Mosaic law. Nevertheless, the office of overseer was originally not an office of teaching but of government. Indeed, in the earliest period there was not yet any urgent need for a separate teaching office. Apostles, evangelists, and prophets at first acted as teachers, Acts 13:1; 1 Cor. 14:3; 1 Tim. 2:7; 2 Tim. 1:11, and in addition the charisma of teaching was granted to many who held no office in the congregation of Christ, Rom. 12:7; 1 Cor. 12:8, 28, 29; 14:26. The διδασκαλια was at first free, just as in the synagogue everyone was permitted to explain a portion of Scripture, Luke 4:16. But gradually it was brought into a closer relation with the episcopal office. When the congregations expanded, apostles, evangelists, and prophets could no longer provide for the need of Word and sacrament. A local and permanent office charged with their care was needed. In the long run it was also impossible to leave teaching entirely free, for this freedom gave rise to all kinds of abuses. Everything thus pressed toward entrusting the teaching to the office of overseer and thereby securing for it a permanent place in the congregation. From Heb. 13:7 we learn that the ἡγουμενοι are also the proclaimers of the Word of God. When Paul says in Eph. 4:11 that Christ has given some as apostles, some as evangelists, and then further some as pastors and teachers, he thereby teaches clearly that these last two persons do not hold essentially distinct offices but perform activities in the congregation that are closely connected and yet differ from each other. Probably in the earliest period more than one, or even all, of the elders were authorized to administer Word and sacrament. But in this too change soon had to come. The requirement did indeed remain for all overseers that they be διδακτικοι, able to teach, 1 Tim. 3:2. But two circumstances in particular brought about a distinction among the overseers between those entrusted only with government and others entrusted also with teaching. In the first place, the demands upon those who had to proclaim the Word of truth in the congregation grew increasingly weighty. The apostles and evangelists died; the extraordinary gifts ceased; all kinds of errors and heresies arose within and outside the congregation; the ability to teach consisted not only in instruction and exhortation but also in refuting those who contradicted, 2 Tim. 3:16; Titus 1:9; and training, preparation, and study became necessary for exercising this office in the congregation. Indeed, the Jewish scribes already had their schools. Jesus himself had trained his disciples and made them fit for their service. Paul had instructed Timothy and charged him to transmit this teaching as a precious treasure to trustworthy people who in turn would be able to teach others, 2 Tim. 2:2. In the second place, there was then added Jesus’s precept that the laborer in the ministry of the Word is worthy of his wages, Matt. 10:10; Luke 10:7, a precept that was universally acknowledged and followed in the Christian congregations, Rom. 15:27; 1 Cor. 9:6, 11, 14; 2 Cor. 11:7–9; Gal. 6:6; 1 Thess. 2:6; 1 Tim. 5:17, 18; 2 Tim. 2:6. This related first of all to apostles and evangelists, but it also applied further to those who labored in the Word and teaching and devoted their lives to it. The necessity of training and provision for their livelihood caused the ministry of the Word to be entrusted not to all but only to some overseers. The famous passage 1 Tim. 5:17, 18 raises this beyond all doubt. The πρεσβυτεροι there, cf. verse 1, are not overseers, for then Paul would make a contrast between overseers who govern badly and others who govern well, and would deem the former worthy of some honor but the latter of double honor. Rather, πρεσβυτεροι are older members of the congregation in general, who as such have a claim to honor. From them Paul distinguishes the καλως προεστωτες πρεσβυτεροι, those elders who also govern well, who are προισταμενοι, Rom. 12:8; 1 Thess. 5:12, that is, who hold the office of overseer. And these, because they belong to the older members of the congregation and are at the same time overseers, are worthy of double honor. And from them are further distinguished οἱ κοπιωντες ἐν λογῳ και διδασκαλιᾳ, those overseers who specifically labor in the Word and teaching and therefore, according to Scripture, have a claim to wages. Thus according to this passage there is a clear distinction between overseers to whom only government is entrusted and others to whom teaching and consequently also the administration of the sacrament are entrusted. And within the bounds of the New Testament we then encounter in the churches of Asia Minor the situation that only one among the overseers is entrusted with the ministry of the Word. He is the ἀγγελος, the messenger, who must teach and lead the congregation on Christ’s behalf and is responsible for its spiritual and moral condition, Rev. 1:20 f.

Alongside this office of overseer, distinguished into that of ruling elder and teaching elder, a second was soon instituted. In Jerusalem, according to Acts 6, discontent soon arose among the Christians converted from the Greek proselytes because their widows were being disadvantaged and neglected by the Christians gathered from the Jews in the private charity already being practiced at that time. The apostles then called the whole congregation together and declared that they did not think it right to devote themselves to the care of the poor by diminishing their labor in the Word. The congregation therefore had to look for and elect seven men, and the apostles would then appoint them to this ministry of mercy and, after prayer, lay their hands on them. It is clear from this that although the apostles indicated the number and requirements of the διακονοι, they granted to the congregation the right and authority to choose these men. The apostles themselves were indeed expressly appointed by Christ, but Matthias was already designated by lot as the twelfth apostle from a pair drawn up by the 120 assembled believers. According to Acts 13:1–3, Paul and Barnabas were set apart for the work of evangelists by the prophets and teachers present in the congregation at Antioch. Timothy was chosen for this same service by prophetic direction, in the presence of many witnesses and with the laying on of hands by Paul and the presbytery, 1 Tim. 1:18; 4:14; 6:12; 2 Tim. 1:6; 2:2. In 2 Cor. 8:19; cf. v. 23, mention is made of an evangelist appointed by the churches. The prophets and teachers were naturally not chosen by the congregations, since they acted freely and possessed a gift rather than an office. But quite differently from the apostles, they were judged by the congregations and were subject to them, 1 Cor. 2:15; 12:10; 14:29; 1 Thess. 5:19–21; 1 John 2:20, 27; Rev. 2:2, 6, 14, 15, 20; 3:1 f. It is therefore no bold assumption that the election of overseers, even less than that of evangelists, took place apart from the congregation. The words in Acts 14:23, χειροτονησαντες δε αὐτοις κατ’ ἐκκλησιαν πρεσβυτερους, say only that the apostles appointed certain persons as elders in every congregation but do not indicate how they did this; and Titus 1:5; cf. 2 Tim. 2:2, sheds no light on this either. But from postapostolic writings we know that in the selection of an _episcopus_ the congregation was consulted directly or indirectly; _Did._ 15; Clement, _1 Cor._ 44; Polycarp, _Phil._ 11; cf. Ignatius, _Philad._ 10; _Const. Apost._ VIII 4. And concerning the deacons, the New Testament reports very clearly in Acts 6 that they were designated by the congregation. There is, however, great disagreement over the nature of the office instituted here by the apostles. Some think that it was an extraordinary office that soon ceased to exist. Others judge that it comprised within itself the later offices of elder and deacon, and still others maintain that Acts 6 recounts the institution of the presbyterial office. All these opinions are based upon the fact that some of those chosen in Acts 6, such as Philip, also act as preachers of the gospel, Acts 8:5, 26 f.; 21:8, and that the presbyters in Jerusalem also receive gifts from the congregation at Antioch for the benefit of the brothers in Judea, Acts 11:30. This last proof, however, has little force. In Acts 11:30 we are dealing with a wholly exceptional case, namely, not with the distribution of the _naturalia_ laid upon the tables by the congregation at Jerusalem itself for its poor, but with the transmission of funds collected in Antioch on a special occasion for the brothers in Judea and now delivered by the hand of Barnabas and Saul to the presbyters. But according to Gal. 2:1, nothing came of this journey; we therefore do not know how and where those funds were transferred, nor by whom they were actually received and distributed. As for Philip, he was one of the seven in Jerusalem but, after persecution broke out and the congregation was scattered, acted as an evangelist in Samaria and elsewhere and remained one. He did not later return to Jerusalem but settled in Caesarea, Acts 21:8. Everything, by contrast, argues that in Acts 6 we have the institution of what was later called the diaconate. First, the name must be considered. In the New Testament διακονια designates every office and gift that, granted by the Lord, is employed in the service and for the benefit of the congregation. Every member of the congregation is a δουλος of Christ and, with all that he is and has, a διακονος of the brothers. There are therefore various διακονιαι, 1 Cor. 12:5, especially the ministry of the Word, Acts 6:4; 20:24; 1 Tim. 1:12, and the ministry of mercy to the poor, sick, strangers, and so forth, Rom. 12:7; 1 Cor. 12:28; 1 Pet. 4:11. Without doubt, such a ministry of mercy existed in the congregation at Jerusalem from the beginning. There was a διακονια καθημερινη, Acts 6:1, which perhaps stood under the supervision of the apostles but was nevertheless left to private persons. But the apostles brought order to this through the institution of a special office. There must now be a reason why the later diaconate was specifically designated by the name διακονια. That reason is to be found nowhere other than in Acts 6. There it is declared that the ministry of mercy is in a special sense a διακονια, since it is a serving of tables, διακονειν τραπεζαις. Second, precisely that is entrusted to the seven men which elsewhere in the New Testament is more particularly designated by the name διακονια. For in the διακονια in Acts 11:29; Rom. 12:7; 1 Cor. 12:5; 2 Cor. 8:4; 9:1, 12, 13; Rev. 2:19, and further everywhere the office of deacons and deaconesses is spoken of, we must think specifically of the ministry of mercy. And this is entrusted to the seven men in Acts 6. They must ensure that the widows of the Greek Christians are no longer passed over and are charged for that purpose in general with the service of the tables. These tables are not to be understood as the tables in the widows’ homes or as the money changers’ tables, Matt. 21:12, but simply as the tables of the Lord. In every meeting place of the congregation there was one or there were several tables at which they reclined in order to partake together, as members of the congregation, of the love feast, ἀγαπη, and the Lord’s Supper. On those tables the wealthier members of the congregation laid their gifts, consisting mostly of _naturalia_, so that the poorer members might also enjoy them and afterward still be supplied from them. These tables were the Lord’s tables; what was laid upon them belonged to the Lord; what was consumed at those tables was the Lord’s food and drink; and what remained and was distributed was the Lord’s gift. The seven men in Jerusalem were now designated to serve those tables, that is, to assist at the meals and further to distribute the Lord’s gifts honestly among the saints according to their needs. Third, the proposition that Acts 6 recounts the origin of the diaconate is supported by the fact that the requirements set for it are so high and in this respect agree with those in 1 Tim. 3:8–10, 12. We do not know why precisely seven men were chosen in Jerusalem; perhaps it was because the large congregation met in seven meeting places and needed a deacon in each of them. In any case, however, they had to be men who possessed the testimony in the congregation that they were πληρεις πνευματος και σοφιας, and the congregation therefore first had to look for them by examining and electing. So too Paul wishes the deacons first to be tested and to satisfy the many requirements that largely agree with those for the overseers. Hatch and Sohm go too far when they infer from this that the requirements for presbyter and deacon can scarcely be distinguished. For while, for example, emphasis in the elder falls upon the διδακτικον εἰναι, purity of conscience in relation to the content of the faith is required of the deacon, 1 Tim. 3:9. But otherwise the religious and moral requirements for elder and deacon are nearly the same. The presbyterate and diaconate, according to Acts 6 and 1 Tim. 3, stand in close connection. Fourth, there is little ground for the assertion that the diaconate arose only later, at approximately the same time as the episcopal office. Little may be inferred from the διακονια, Rom. 12:7, and the ἀντιληψεις, 1 Cor. 12:28. But everything argues that along with the presbyterate the diaconate too was transplanted by the apostles from the Jerusalem congregation into other congregations. Just as with elders, a need for deacons in the service of the tables must soon have arisen elsewhere as well. In Phil. 1:1 they are therefore mentioned in passing alongside and after the elders as something entirely ordinary; in 1 Tim. 3 Paul enumerates their requirements; and in Rom. 16:1, 2; 1 Tim. 3:11; 5:9, 10, deaconesses are spoken of. The apostolate may therefore precede the church as an assembly of believers as an extraordinary office, but the offices of teacher, elder, and deacon presuppose the congregation, which has the right to designate and elect their bearers.
