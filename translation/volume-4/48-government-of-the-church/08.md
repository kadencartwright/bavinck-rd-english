## 8.

Upon the basis of these gifts and this office of all believers, Christ also instituted special offices in the church. The apostles indeed performed a ministerial service in this, but it is nevertheless Christ who gives these offices and equips and chooses the persons for them. In the Roman church, however, Richer, *de ecclesiastica et politica potestate*, 1611, claimed that Christ entrusted all power *primarie, proprie et essentialiter* to the church and then *instrumentaliter, ministerialiter et quoad exsecutionem* to pope and bishops, Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 404. In the first period Luther inferred from the universal priesthood of believers that the ministry of word and sacrament was properly granted to all but for the sake of order was exercised by one of them, Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 327. Reformed theologians sometimes express themselves as if the power of ministers properly belongs to the congregation and is exercised by them in its name: Amesius, *Med.* I 35, 6; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII, qu. 24, 7, 8, 19, 26. The image is repeatedly used that, just as a human being sees through the eye and hears through the ear, so the congregation performs institutional activities through the offices. And in more recent times the conception that the office is an organ of the congregation is general. All this is only partly correct. In Matt. 18:17 Jesus does indeed give the power of the keys to the whole congregation, but he still uses this word there in a wholly general sense, without mentioning the organization that would later be introduced. As soon as the latter exists, we see that the power of the keys rests with the apostles and then with the overseers. Power in general can also be called granted to the church because it extends to its salvation and welfare and thus, if not *formaliter*, nevertheless *finaliter*, was granted to it. It is *toti quidem ecclesiae ad illius aedificationem destinata*, but *proprie a solis ejus ministris tractanda*, Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* XVI 70; cf., against Richer, Petavius, *de eccl. hier.* III, chs. 14–16. The offices in Christ’s church are not a ruling but a serving power. They exist for the sake of the congregation, 1 Cor. 3:22; Eph. 4:12. Paul even calls himself and his fellow ministers δουλους ὑμων δια Ιησουν, 2 Cor. 4:5. The goal of the church as institution lies in the gathering of the elect, in the building up of Christ’s body, in the perfecting of the saints, and thus in the glorification of God, Eph. 4:11. God certainly could also have led his people to salvation without any means of church or office, word or sacrament. But his good pleasure was to gather his elect through the service of human beings. The church has the *salus electorum* as its goal. The offices are necessary *necessitate hypothetica*: *Gall.* 25; *Belg.* 30; *Helv.* II 18; Voetius, *Pol.* I 17, III 213; Vitringa IX 131 sq.; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII, qu. 22. Yet although the offices in this sense exist for the congregation, they are nevertheless not its organ and did not receive their power from it. For in the Old Testament Moses and Aaron, priests and prophets, were called and appointed by the Lord. In the New Testament the apostles, including Paul, were chosen and equipped directly by Christ himself. False prophets and apostles precisely have no commission from God and come only in their own name, Jer. 23:21, 32; John 5:43, but true ministers appeal to their commission from God and derive their power and authority from it, Isa. 6:8; Jer. 1:4; Hos. 1:1; Rom. 1:1; Gal. 1:1; etc. Therefore, although they are also in the service of the congregation, they are nevertheless called διακονοι Χριστου, Col. 1:7; Acts 20:24; 1 Tim. 1:12; δουλοι Χριστου, Rom. 1:1; Gal. 1:10; 2 Pet. 1:1; ὑπηρεται Χριστου, Acts 26:16; 1 Cor. 4:1; δουλοι θεου, Acts 16:17; συνεργοι θεου, 1 Cor. 3:9. Being the mouth of God and ambassadors on behalf of Christ, they entreat on Christ’s behalf that people be reconciled to God, 2 Cor. 5:20, and without pleasing human beings proclaim the gospel entrusted to them, 1 Thess. 2:4, and distribute the mysteries of Christ, 1 Cor. 4:1. Therefore, as overseers and caregivers, they also stand above the congregation; are its ἐπισκοποι, προισταμενοι, ἡγουμενοι; are responsible for its spiritual welfare; and have a claim to its esteem and obedience. And this applies not only to the extraordinary but also to the ordinary offices. These too are given by Christ, Matt. 9:38; 23:34; Acts 20:28; 1 Cor. 12:5, 28; Eph. 4:11. There is no preaching without a commission, Rom. 10:15. No one may take this honor to himself except one called by God, John 10:1, 2; Heb. 5:4. Although all believers are called to proclaim the gospel, Acts 8:4; 13:15; 1 Cor. 14:26, doing this with power and authority in the Lord’s name, as an aroma from life to life or an aroma from death to death, requires a special commission and charge.

The way along which Christ places his ministers into office proceeds through calling, examination, and ordination. Since the calling no longer comes to someone in an extraordinary manner as it did to prophets and apostles, it is recognizable only by the agreement of the inward and outward calling. The inward calling, which must thus be clearly distinguished from the supernatural and extraordinary calling, consists 1º in the granting of the gifts required for the office; 2º in the pure, sincere, and steadfast desire that causes someone to strive for the office; and 3º in the opening of the ways that lead to the office: Gerhard, *Loc.* XXIII, ch. 3; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 529; Moor VI 282; Alting, *Theol. probl. nova* I 15; Brakel, *Red. Godsd.* XVII 12; Vitringa IX 298. This inward, subjective calling must receive its mark of authentication and seal in the outward calling through the congregation, since error and deception are not excluded in this domain either, *Ned. Gel.*, art. 31. Therefore this outward calling does not stand over against the inward, but proceeds from Christ just as much as the latter. He alone can call and truly calls. But this outward calling is mediated and occurs through the congregation in Jesus’ name. Scripture leaves no doubt here, Acts 1:23; 6:2–6; 2 Cor. 8:19; cf. above, p. 79. In the first centuries the congregation also actually exercised this right. Even the bishop was chosen by the congregation; cf. Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 59, 229, 271, 275, 282, 285; Achelis, *Lehrb. d. prakt. Theol.* 1², p. 147; and older literature in Vitringa IX 308–310. The election of the pope, the bishop of Rome, by the cardinals—that is, originally the presbytery of the local congregation there—is still a remnant of the former practice. But gradually the right of the congregation was restricted and finally in the Roman hierarchy assigned to the pope, and under the influence of a humanistic political law by Erastians and Remonstrants to the civil government. Even in the Reformed churches there was great disagreement about this. Although in theory it was maintained that the right to call ministers of the word rested with the congregation, in practice this was frequently greatly restricted and ceded to the church council, patrons, civil government, or mixed colleges: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 3, 11–15; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 557 sq., 580 sq.; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII, qu. 24; M. Vitringa IX 311–321; Moor VI 288–298. On the other hand, the error of Grotius, Pufendorf, and others must also be avoided, as if the election of church ministers were a natural right of believers, just as the right to elect a board rests with the members of an association; cf. M. Vitringa IX 310. For the church is not an association that comes into being through the will of human beings, but an institution of Christ. All power belonging to the congregation was therefore granted to it by Christ. It is not a right but a gift. The congregation is no democracy in which the people govern themselves. Rather, Christ governs within it, and the congregation’s election has no other meaning than that it observes the gifts and designates the persons whom Christ destined for the office. Hence the congregation indeed elects, but that election stands under the guidance of those already in their office—apostles, evangelists, and so forth, Acts 1:15; 6:2; 14:23; Titus 1:5, and later neighboring bishops, Sohm, loc. cit. Further, the election is not absolutely free but bound to conditions and requirements indicated by Christ for the office, Acts 1:21; 6:3; 1 Tim. 3. And finally, someone is not yet in office when he has been chosen by the congregation, but afterward hands must be laid upon him, Acts 6:6; etc. Election by the congregation and guidance by the church council must therefore go together in the calling to an office in the church of Christ, whether the church council in the call binds itself to a nomination by the congregation or to an election by the congregation from a nomination by the church council.

But with the election by the congregation and call by the church council (*vocatio stricte sic dicta*), the outward calling is not yet complete. It continues in the testing, investigation, or examination. Naturally this is not absolutely necessary. If the congregation certainly knows that the person it calls possesses the required gifts, further investigation is superfluous. But the congregation is not infallible and can err. It does not itself distribute the gifts but can only observe to whom Christ granted gifts for his service. In order to proceed in this as safely as possible, after the call it still institutes a test intended to give the congregation certainty that the person called possesses the required gifts. Paul therefore already required in 1 Tim. 3:10 that the deacons—and the και οὑτοι δε proves that this was already customary also with the presbyters—should be tested in some manner unknown to us and afterward, if they proved blameless in doctrine and life, should serve. Upon this rested the right which the church later began to exercise of imposing a probationary period before assuming office or also administering an examination: Bingham, *Origines eccles. or the Antiq. of the Christ. Church*, London, 1843, II 225. In the Netherlands, after the university at Leiden had been established, the Reformed churches ceded the right to administer the (peremptory) examination to the professors and contented themselves with their *testimonium academicum*, Synod of Middelburg, qu. 3; The Hague, art. 18. But gradually, everywhere except Groningen, with great difficulty and not without much opposition, even from Voetius and Maccovius, they succeeded in taking back from the professors and retaining for themselves the right to administer the peremptory and preparatory examinations. Leaving aside the question whether the churches do well when in administering examinations in the classes they make use of the professors’ help, according to Holy Scripture, the Reformed confession, and also the nature of the matter, the right to institute such an examination belongs to the churches. Let the school administer its examinations, but the churches retain the right to call, to test, to send, to give authority for the ministry of word and sacrament. The proper ecclesiastical examination is therefore the peremptory examination. The preparatory examination, although already mentioned in The Hague, art. 18, is of subordinate significance, was introduced only gradually, especially as a result of the Remonstrant controversies, and according to Voetius’s testimony, *Pol. Eccl.* III 517, was first in general use in 1669. It served only to allow those preliminarily examined to practice for a time in delivering propositions under the guidance of a minister and church council.

Finally, to calling and examination is added ordination, which occurs especially through the laying on of hands. This was in use under Israel in blessing, Gen. 48:14; Lev. 9:22; sacrifice, Ex. 29:10; Lev. 1:4; accusation, Lev. 24:14; in the consecration of Levites, Num. 8:10; in appointment to office, Num. 27:18–23; later also in the installation of judges and promotion of teachers, Schürer, *Gesch. des jüd. Volkes* II³ 199. Jesus laid on hands to heal, Matt. 8:15; 9:18; Mark 5:23; cf. 2 Kings 4:34; 5:11, and to bless, Matt. 19:15; Luke 24:50, and the people attached great value to it, Matt. 9:18; Mark 5:23; 7:32. But nowhere do we read that he also acted thus in appointment to an office. He appointed his apostles with the word alone, without any ceremony, Matt. 10:1f.; 28:19. At the appointment of Matthias, Paul, Barnabas, Silas, Luke, and others no laying on of hands is mentioned anywhere; it was certainly not in general use at induction into an ecclesiastical office. But laying on of hands took place in healing, Acts 9:12, 17; in communicating the gift of the Spirit, Acts 8:17–19; at the appointment of deacons, 1 Tim. 4:14; 2 Tim. 1:6. According to 1 Tim. 5:22 it was in general use at ordination to an ecclesiastical office, and according to Heb. 6:2 it belongs to the first principles of the doctrine of Christ. Yet it was not a real communication of the spiritual gifts of office. For Acts 6:3 teaches that the deacons who were chosen had to be already beforehand full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom. In Acts 13:3 the laying on of hands occurs not at ordination but at the sending of Barnabas and Paul, who already stood in office. According to 1 Tim. 1:18; 4:14, Timothy’s appointment as evangelist was confirmed by prophetic testimonies and by laying on of hands by the presbytery. And indeed in 2 Tim. 1:6 the official gift is conceived as occurring δι ἐπιθεσεως, but 1 Tim. 4:14 says that it was granted δια προφητειας and μετα ἐπιθεσεως, a proof that prophecy and laying on of hands were not the origin of the gifts but the means by which they were directed into and designated for the service of the congregation. From the apostles this practice of laying on of hands passed over into the Christian church, which applied it in baptism, healing, readmission of the fallen and heretics, marriage, penance, and ordination. In the last case the right to apply it was in later times assigned to the bishop alone and was conceived as the conferral of a special official gift. Over against Gnosticism and Montanism, the truth of the church was argued from the fact that the bishops in the congregations established by the apostles were the guardians of the pure tradition. They themselves had received it from the apostles and handed it down uncorrupted to their successors. The *successio ab initio decurrens*, argued with 2 Tim. 2:2, supplied the guarantee for this, for the office included the communication of a special official spirit which the officebearer retains, however ungodly he may personally be. The laying on of hands was certainly customary in the ancient church at ordination to presbyter, deacon, and the lower offices, was always accompanied by prayer, and was for a long time still conceived as a symbolic sign of the communication of the official gift. *Manus impositio, quid est aliud nisi oratio super hominem?* Augustine, *de bapt.* 3, 16. But gradually it was regarded as a sacrament that *ex opere operato* imparted a *character indelebilis*: *Trid.* 23, ch. 7; *de ref.*, chs. 3, 10; *Cat. Rom.* II 7, 29; Bellarmine, *de clericis* I 14. The Lutherans rejected it at first but later again adopted it and sometimes assigned great value to it, *Apol. Conf.*, art. 13; cf. Herzog² 11, 80. The Reformed unanimously judged that the laying on of hands was no command of Christ and therefore not absolutely necessary. But while some considered it useful, venerable, and worthy of imitation—Calvin, *Inst.* IV 3, 16; 14, 20; 19, 31; Aretius, Spanheim, Koelman, *Pligt der ouderl. en diakenen*, 1889, p. 53f.—others regarded it as an *adiaphoron* and advised against its use out of fear of superstition: Synod of Emden, art. 16; Dort, 1574, art. 24; Middelburg, 1581, art. 4; Dort, 1578, art. 5; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 452, 579; Moor V 352–356, VI 327–331; M. Vitringa IX 209, 353–357. It is not an essential element of ordination, for no mention of it is made either with Jesus himself, with the apostles, or with the elders, Acts 14:23; 20:28. Nor can or may it be conceived as the mechanical communication of a special official spirit. For it does not grant but, according to Scripture, presupposes the *charismata* required for office. It is also not identical with election or calling to office but follows upon it and can therefore be nothing other than a public designation of the one called to an office and a solemn induction and dedication to that office. Just as marriage before the civil government is not the essence of marriage and the coronation does not make the king, so the *ordinatio*, with or without laying on of hands, is no communication of the office or an official spirit. It is only the solemn, public declaration before God and his congregation that the one called is sent along a lawful way and therefore by God, that he possesses the required gifts, and that as such he must be received, acknowledged, and honored by the congregation. Cf. Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 56 f.; articles “Handauflegung” and “Ordination” in Herzog; Zahn, *Einl. in das N. T.* I 465; Achelis, *Lehrb. der prakt. Theol.*, Leipzig, 1898, I 139–173.
