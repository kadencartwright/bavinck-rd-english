## 2.

Holy Scripture sets this in a clear light. When Adam has fallen and hides from the Lord’s face, it is God himself who seeks and calls the human being, preaches the promise of the gospel to him, and thereby establishes his church. With Noah he establishes his covenant, communicates in it a treasury of blessings, and seals it with the bow in the clouds. He calls Abram from Ur of the Chaldeans, makes him his covenant partner, and gives him the sign of circumcision. In the patriarchal period the households were the congregations of believers. The fathers of households were the priests who communicated the promises to their children and brought God sacrifices of adoration and thanksgiving. At Sinai the people of Israel received not only a civil but also a religious organization and became manifest as the people of God in priesthood and sacrifice, in tabernacle and altar, in all kinds of laws and institutions. When John the Baptist appears at the beginning of the New Testament, he preaches the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins and thereby separates the people of God from sinful Israel. Jesus takes over this preaching and baptism of John, later adds the Lord’s Supper to it, gathers an ἐκκλησια around himself, governs it directly himself as long as he is on earth, and appoints twelve apostles who will shortly appear as his witnesses. The institution of the apostolate is especially a powerful proof of the institutional character Christ gave his church on earth. Christ is himself the ἀποστολος, Heb. 3:1, and continues this ἀποστολη in the twelve, John 20:21. This group of twelve did not gradually form of itself but was expressly called and appointed by Jesus himself. Although Jesus knew from the beginning whom he would choose as apostles and could therefore immediately say to them that he would make them ἁλεεις ἀνθρωπων, Mark 1:17, a clear distinction must nevertheless be made between their first calling to discipleship and their second calling to the apostolate, Matt. 4:18–22 and 10:1; Mark 1:16 and 3:14; Luke 5:1 and 6:13–16. This special calling to the apostolic office by Jesus is indeed denied by many, such as Schleiermacher, Volkmar, Harnack, Seufert, Holtzmann, and others. But the facts conflict with this claim. The group of twelve apostles was firmly established in the Christian congregations long before Paul’s appearance, Matt. 26:33; 28:18; Luke 24:47; John 20:19, 21; 1 Cor. 15:5, 7; Rev. 21:14. The name apostle, שָׁלוּחַ, was also given them by Jesus, Luke 6:13; cf. 11:49; Matt. 23:34; 10:2; Mark 6:30; Luke 9:10; 17:5; 22:14; 24:10, because they were sent out by him to preach, Mark 3:14. Jesus was himself the one sent by the Father, John 3:34; Heb. 3:1, and for the execution of his work needed witnesses who made known throughout the whole people of Israel the gospel that had appeared in him, Matt. 10:6. This naming by Jesus is confirmed by the fact that the word apostle was from the beginning the name of an office, so much so that the word ψευδαποστολος could be formed, 2 Cor. 11:13. The word שָׁלוּחַ, moreover, occurs only once in the Septuagint, 1 Kings 14:6, and the word ἀποστολος rarely in profane Greek. Yet these facts of Scripture concerning the apostolate appear to be contradicted by other data. First, it is uncertain who must be reckoned to this group of twelve apostles. Even if the difference between the four apostolic lists, Matt. 10:2; Mark 3:16; Luke 6:14; Acts 1:13, is resolved in the sense that Lebbaeus Thaddaeus and Judas of James are identified, it still remains that Judas fell away and was replaced by Matthias, Acts 1:15–26, and later Paul was still added to the twelve. The relation of Paul to the twelve is far from clear in this connection. Paul does indeed repeatedly make this distinction, that the apostles at Jerusalem would proclaim the gospel among Israel and he himself among the Gentiles, Acts 9:15; 13:47; 22:21; Rom. 11:13; Gal. 1:16; 2:7–9; Eph. 3:8; 1 Tim. 2:7; 2 Tim. 1:11. But this distinction is nevertheless very relative. For in his proclamation of the gospel Paul always turned first to the Jews, Acts 13:5, 14, 46; etc., and after his resurrection the twelve apostles received from Christ the express commission to preach the gospel to all nations, Matt. 28:19; Acts 10:42, and also fulfilled that commission to a greater or lesser degree. Not only the congregation from the Jews but the whole New Testament church rests upon the foundation of apostles and prophets, Eph. 2:20; Rev. 21:14, and through their word has communion with Christ, John 17:20; 1 John 1:3. Paul’s apostolate, however, bears a character very different from that of the twelve. Paul indeed maintains with all his power the divine origin, independence, and truth of his apostolic office against all opponents, Gal. 1–2; 1 Cor. 1:10–4:21; 2 Cor. 10:13. Nevertheless, he did not associate with Jesus during his walk on earth; he persecuted the church of God; he was called by the exalted Christ in an extraordinary manner and at an unusual time; he was the chief of sinners and the least of the apostles, 1 Cor. 15:9; Eph. 3:8; 1 Tim. 1:15. His apostolate, however independent and excellent, was a means to lay the apostolate of the twelve as the foundation of the whole church. Through his apostolate Paul did not restrict or undermine the apostolate of the twelve but, on the contrary, confirmed and expanded it. In the Gentile world he prepared the way for the apostolate of the twelve. On the one hand, he divested it of everything Jewish that still clung to its bearers; on the other, he grafted the Gentiles as wild branches onto Israel’s cultivated olive tree, Rom. 11:24. Upon Christ as cornerstone and the apostles as foundation, Paul built the one church, the one people of God, spiritual Israel. With this a second objection raised against Jesus’ appointment and naming of the twelve apostles has already been resolved in principle. For it is a fact that the word apostle, probably already in Jerusalem, Acts 14:4, 14; 2 Cor. 11:13; Rev. 2:2, but then especially by Paul, was used in a broader sense and applied also to others than the twelve. Paul had to do this because he knew himself to be a called servant of Jesus Christ, equal in office and honor to the other apostles. He was an apostle in a different sense than the apostles in Jerusalem, called in another way and at a later time and charged with a special task. But he had one thing in common with the apostles in Jerusalem: he was a called apostle of Jesus Christ who owed his calling, his gospel, and specifically also the distinctive content of his gospel—namely, that the Gentiles are fellow heirs—to a special revelation of Christ and not to human beings, 1 Cor. 9:1; 15:8; Gal. 1:1, 12, 15; 2:2; Eph. 3:3. But for his missionary labor he needed help. Besides the apostles, Jesus had already sent out another seventy to prepare for his coming in the cities and villages where he would come, Luke 10. When the congregation in Jerusalem was scattered by persecution, Philip, one of the seven men chosen in Acts 6, went to preach the gospel among the Samaritans, Acts 8:5; to the eunuch of Queen Candace, 8:26; cf. 11:20; and further as far as Caesarea, 8:40; 21:8. And so in his missionary labor Paul made use of men such as Barnabas, Mark, Luke, Silas, Tychicus, Aristarchus, Epaphras, Apollos, Timothy, Titus, and others, who stood beside him as his συνεργοι, 1 Thess. 3:2. These assistant missionaries of the apostles were now sometimes also called apostles by Paul because, although not sent directly by Jesus Christ, they were nevertheless sent by the congregation under the guidance of the Holy Spirit to proclaim the gospel in other places, Acts 13:2, 3; cf. 2 Cor. 8:23, ἀποστολοι ἐκκλησιων. Alongside its narrower sense, the word apostle thus also acquired a broader sense, Acts 14:4, 14; Rom. 16:7; 1 Cor. 4:6, 9; 9:5; 15:7; 2 Cor. 11:5, 13; 12:11; Gal. 1:19; 1 Thess. 2:6; Rev. 2:2, and so continued to live also later in the postapostolic period, for example in *Didache*, ch. 11. Elsewhere these apostolic helpers bear the name evangelists, Acts 21:8; Eph. 4:11; 2 Tim. 4:5, because, just as Christ was set apart by the Father, Luke 4:18, and the apostles by Christ, Luke 9:1, 6, so in their turn they were set apart by the congregation under the guidance of the Spirit for the proclamation of the gospel, Acts 8:5, 12, 40; 11:19, 20, 22; 13:2; 2 Cor. 8:18, 19, 23; Phil. 2:25; 1 Tim. 4:14. They thus agree with the apostles in the narrower sense in three respects. 1º They too are servants of God or of Christ, 1 Thess. 3:2; 1 Tim. 4:6; 6:11; 2 Tim. 2:24, and not only received a *charisma*, 1 Tim. 4:14; 2 Tim. 1:6, but actually bear an office by virtue of a special calling and appointment, under a definite name, Acts 21:8, with their own rank and place, Eph. 4:11, and with a special task, 2 Tim. 4:5. 2º Their office is not limited to a local church but extends to all churches, to the *ecclesia universalis*, Acts 13:4f., so that according to the ancient church’s explanation πανταχου περιιοντες ἐκηρυττον, and they had power and authority over all churches, Titus 1:5. And 3º they participate in the apostles’ foundational and church-establishing labor. They are their συνεργοι, 1 Thess. 3:2; συνεκδημοι, Acts 19:29; συνστρατιωται, Phil. 2:25; συνδουλοι, Col. 1:7; 4:7, who water what the apostles planted, 1 Cor. 3:6, and, though possessing relative independence, were nevertheless subject to the apostles, Acts 19:22; 1 Cor. 4:17; 1 Tim. 1:3; Titus 1:5; etc., and worked partly alone, partly also in the company of the apostles, ib. and Acts 11:30; 12:25; 13:2; etc. In the postapostolic period the office disappears entirely, and from Tertullian, Origen, and Eusebius onward the name becomes customary for the writers of the four Gospels, which render the persons of the evangelists superfluous, as it were. Cf. on the evangelists: Suicerus, s.v.; Witsius, *Misc. Sacra* I 315, II 564; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 364–369; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 2, 18; Lechler, *Die neut. Lehre v. h. Amte*, 1857, p. 220 f.; Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 3, 277; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 42; Zöckler, *Diakonen und Evangelisten*, Munich: Beck, 1893.

Alongside the evangelists, prophets also appear in the New Testament and are even named before them, Rom. 12:6; 1 Cor. 12:28, 29; Eph. 4:11; sometimes they are connected with the apostles without the evangelists, Eph. 2:20; 3:5, and thus stand above them in rank and honor. They were promised by Jesus, Matt. 23:34; Luke 11:49; were raised up by the Holy Spirit who had been poured out on the day of Pentecost, Acts 2:17, 18; 1 Cor. 12:10; Rev. 1:10; and then occur in large number and in almost all the congregations: in Jerusalem, Acts 6:5, 8; 11:27; Antioch, 11:27; 13:1; Caesarea, 21:9, 10; Corinth, 1 Cor. 12; and everywhere, as appears from their mention in Rom. 12:6; 1 Cor. 12:28; Eph. 2:20; 3:5; 4:11; 1 Thess. 5:20. They conclude with John the apostle, Rev. 1:1, and then disappear entirely as an order from the church. The apostolic fathers still speak of prophets, Hermas, *Mand.* 11; *Vis.* 3; *Didache* 11, 15, but by them they think of such men as traveled around and spoke concerning Christian truth in different congregations, though in doing so they had to be carefully examined and distinguished from false prophets. The time for prophecy had passed. Montanism and other enthusiastic tendencies of earlier and later times did indeed try to revive prophecy. Rome claims that the prophetic gift still continues, Bellarmine, *de notis eccl.*, ch. 15. Zwingli and many after him introduced so-called prophecies in which Scripture was explained for the people, art. “Prophezei” in Herzog² and Dr. H. H. Kuyper, *De opleiding tot den dienst des Woords bij de Gereformeerden*, 1891, p. 104f. But all this is essentially distinct from prophecy as it existed in the first Christian churches. The latter is distinguished by the following. 1º The New Testament prophets can indeed be called officebearers, but their office is nevertheless much more charismatic than that of evangelists and apostles. They are called and appointed neither directly by Christ nor by his church but receive a special *charisma* from the Holy Spirit and are consequently called to fulfill a special task in Christ’s church. 2º They have in common with the apostles and evangelists that they hold an office which applies to the whole church of Christ on earth and thus also collaborate in laying the foundation of the church, Eph. 2:20. But while the evangelists assist the apostles especially in their missionary and institution-establishing labor, the prophets stand beside them in their upbuilding, edifying, teaching activity. 3º New Testament prophecy is indeed conscious and therefore to be esteemed above glossolalia, 1 Cor. 14:5, 32, but is nevertheless momentary and extraordinary, a fruit of ἀποκαλυψις, 1 Cor. 14:30. It extends the natural measure of knowing and knowledge, encompasses both the form and content of the discourse, Matt. 10:19, 20, proves itself as truth through its inward, convincing power, 2 Cor. 2:14–17, and served especially to gain entrance among believers and unbelievers for the gospel proclaimed by the apostles—which was an offense to the Jews and foolishness to the Greeks and was not yet accessible to the whole church in the written Word—and thus to build up the church through teaching, exhortation, and consolation, 1 Cor. 14:3, in the grace and knowledge of the Lord Jesus Christ. Cf. on the New Testament prophets: Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 369; cf. *Disp. Sel.* II 1036 sq.; Witsius, *Misc. Sacra* I 282 sq.; Neander, *Gesch. d. Pflanzung u. Leitung der chr. K.*⁵, 182 f.; Bonwetsch, “Die Prophetie im ap. u. nachap. Zeitalter,” *Zeits. f. k. Wiss. u. k. Leben*, 1884, p. 408 f.; Burger, art. in Herzog² 12, 265; Zöckler, loc. cit., 71 f.; Weiszäcker, *Das apost. Zeitalter*, 584 f.; etc.

But however closely prophets and evangelists are related to the apostles proper, they are nevertheless essentially distinct from them. The apostles form a circle of their own; their office bears an entirely distinctive character and is recognizable by the following features. 1º The apostles are given to Christ by the Father, John 17:6; chosen and called by Christ himself, John 6:70; 13:18; 15:16, 19; 1 Cor. 1:17; 2 Cor. 5:20; Gal. 1:1; chosen by God for their office, Acts 10:41. 2º They were trained and equipped for their task by Jesus himself, were ear- and eyewitnesses of his words and deeds, beheld the Word of life with their eyes and touched it with their hands, and received their gospel not from any human being but from Christ himself, Luke 24:48; John 1:4; 15:27; Acts 1:21, 22; 26:16; 1 Cor. 9:1; 15:8; 2 Cor. 12:1f.; Gal. 1:12; Eph. 3:2–8; 1 Tim. 1:12; 1 John 1:1–3; etc. 3º They partake in a special measure of the Holy Spirit, who teaches them and leads them into all truth, Matt. 10:20; John 14:26; 15:26; 16:7, 13, 14; 20:22; 1 Cor. 2:10–13; 7:40; 1 Pet. 1:12. 4º Equipped with that Spirit, John 20:22; Acts 1:8; Eph. 3:5, they appear publicly as witnesses of Jesus, specifically of his resurrection, Acts 1:8, 21, 22; 2:14, 32; 3:15; 4:8; etc. They are trustworthy witnesses, Luke 1:2; John 19:35; 21:24; 1 Cor. 7:25; 1 Pet. 5:1; 2 Pet. 1:16; Heb. 2:3; Rev. 1:3; 22:18, 19, and proclaim God’s Word, John 1:14; 20:31; 1 Cor. 2:13; 2 Cor. 2:17; Gal. 1:7; 1 Thess. 2:13; 1 John 1:1–4; Rev. 22:18, 19. 5º Their testimony is sealed by God with signs and wonders and rich spiritual blessing, Matt. 10:1, 9; Mark 16:15f.; Acts 2:43; 3:2; 5:12–16; 6:8; etc.; Rom. 12:4–8; 15:18, 19; 1 Cor. 12:10, 28; 15:10; 2 Cor. 11:5, 23; Gal. 3:5; Heb. 2:4. 6º The church of all ages is bound to this testimony of theirs. There is no communion with Christ except through communion with the word and persons of the apostles, John 17:20; Gal. 1:7–9; 1 John 1:3. They are the foundation of the church, Matt. 16:18; 1 Cor. 3:10; Eph. 2:20; Rev. 21:14. Their word, preserved for us in the Scriptures of the New Testament, is *medium gratiae*, John 20:31; 1 Cor. 1:18f.; 15:2; 1 John 1:1–4. 7º Their office is thus not limited to a time or to a local church, but remains and extends to the whole church. It is the only office established directly by Christ and includes within itself all the authorities and activities that are distributed among the later offices: pastoral, presbyteral, diaconal, and even evangelistic and prophetic activity. From the beginning the apostles therefore also enjoy a universally acknowledged authority in Christ’s church. They are not only the overseers of the congregation at Jerusalem but the founders, the fathers, 1 Cor. 4:15, and leaders of the whole church. They supervise the believers in Samaria, Acts 8:14; visit the congregations, Acts 9:32; 11:22; institute offices, Acts 6:2; make decisions in the Holy Spirit, Acts 15:22, 28; appear with apostolic authority, 1 Cor. 4:21; 5:2; 2 Cor. 2:9; give binding commands, 1 Cor. 7:40; 1 Thess. 4:2, 11; 2 Thess. 2:15; 3:6, 14; etc.; and with their word still possess authority for the whole of Christendom. Apostolicity is an attribute and mark of Christ’s church. Cf. vol. I, 334f.; and further Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 351–363; Burmannus, *Exerc. Acad.* II 104 sq.; Spanheim, *Op.* II 289 sq.; Moor VI 250 and others in Walch, *Bibl. theol.* III 444; Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 3, 258; Gloël, *Der H. Geist in der Heilsverkündigung des Paulus*, Halle, 1888, p. 325 f.; W. Seufert, *Der Ursprung u. die Bedeutung des Apostolates in de chr. K. der ersten 2 Jahrh.*, Leiden, 1887; Köppel, “Der Ursprung des Apost.,” *Stud. u. Among the apostles Peter stands first. Simon or Simeon, son of John or Jonah, brother of Andrew, from Bethsaida, John 1:45, but probably living in Capernaum since his marriage, Mark 1:29, already at his first meeting with Jesus received the promise that he would later be called Κηφας, the Greek form of the Hebrew word כֵּף with the Aramaic article, the rock, ἡ πετρα, as masculine proper name Πετρος, John 1:43. Without doubt Jesus thereby alluded to his faithful character, which despite his sanguine, mobile nature was proper to him and came most clearly to expression at Caesarea Philippi. There, over against the people who with their earthly-minded expectations saw themselves disappointed in Jesus and left him, Peter maintained the confession of Jesus’ messiahship and openly expressed it in the name of his fellow disciples, Matt. 16:13–20; Mark 8:27–29; Luke 9:18–20; John 6:66–69. At this occasion Jesus also recalled the name he had already given him earlier, Matt. 16:18. Through his bold and steadfast confession of Jesus as the Christ, Peter showed himself to be the rock upon which Christ would build his church so firmly and securely that the gates of Hades would not overpower it. According to Launoy, 17 church fathers understood the rock as Peter, 8 as the apostles, 44 as Peter’s faith, and 16 as Christ, in Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 411. Later Roman Catholics mostly applied the rock to Peter and Protestants to his confession. But there is no dilemma here. The words “this *petra*” can refer only to the person of Peter, but he is a rock and proved himself to be one through his confession of Jesus as the Christ, a confession he owed not to himself but to the Father’s revelation. Precisely for this reason Jesus promises him that upon him as confessor of his sonship and messiahship he will build his church. Christ thus presents himself as the builder of his church and Peter the confessor as the rock upon which his church will rest. In Matt. 21:42; Acts 4:11; 1 Cor. 3:10; Eph. 2:20; Rev. 21:14; cf. 1 Pet. 2:4–6, the same image is used but applied in a different manner. There the apostles are conceived as the builders who through their preaching founded the church upon Christ as the foundation. But here in Matt. 16:18 Christ is the builder who builds his church upon the confessing Peter. And Christ fulfilled this promise. Peter is the first among the apostles, the principal founder of the church, the leader and captain of all confessors of Christ throughout the ages. Therefore in the apostolic lists he is always named first, Matt. 10:2; Mark 3:16; Luke 6:14; Acts 1:13. With John and James he belongs to Jesus’ intimate circle of friends who may follow him when the others must remain behind, Matt. 17:1; Mark 5:37; 13:3; 14:33. He is the spokesman and representative of the disciples, Matt. 16:17; 17:24; 18:21; 26:40. After Jesus’ ascension he comes to the foreground as the first witness among the apostles, Acts 1:15; 2:14; 3:1f.; 4:8; 5:3, 29; 8:14; 10:5f.; 12:3f.; 15:7f., and as *primus inter pares* is also honored by Paul, Gal. 1:18; 2:7–9. Cf. art. “Petrus” in Herzog² and the literature cited there.
