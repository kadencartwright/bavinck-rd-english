# § 48. The Government of the Church

## 1.

Government is indispensable to the church as the assembly of believers. Just as an architect belongs with a temple, a sower with a field, a husbandman with a vineyard, a fisherman with a net, a shepherd with a flock, a head with a body, a father with a household, and a king with a kingdom, so too the church cannot be conceived without an authority that sustains and leads, cares for and protects her. In an even more special sense than in the political sphere, this authority rests with God, who is not only the Creator of all things but also the Savior of the congregation. As the people of God, under both the New and the Old Covenant, the congregation is a theocracy; the Lord is her judge, lawgiver, and king, Isa. 33:22. But just as God has conferred sovereignty upon the civil government in the civil sphere, so in the church he has appointed Christ as king. Designated from eternity as mediator, Christ has exercised his prophetic, priestly, and royal office from paradise onward, continued it in the days of the Old Testament and during his walk on earth, and now completes it in heaven, where he is seated at the Father’s right hand; vol. III, 408–424. And this activity of Christ does not presuppose the congregation, except as conceived and willed in God’s eternal counsel, but precedes her and has her as its product. The congregation is built as a temple upon Christ as the rock and is born as a body from him as the head; here the king exists before his people. But in yet another sense the church cannot be conceived without government. It is true that Christ could have exercised his office without any service from human beings; had it pleased him, he could have distributed his spiritual and heavenly blessings without the help of institutions and persons. But this did not seem good to him. It was his good pleasure, without transferring his sovereignty in any degree to human beings, nevertheless to make use of their service in its exercise and through them to preach the gospel to all creatures. And in this sense too the church has never been without government; she was always organized and institutionally ordered in one way or another. This was necessary *necessitate hypothetica*, since the congregation here on earth is a congregation in the process of becoming. In heaven every office and every means of grace falls away because the kingdom of God is complete and God is all in all. But on earth it is otherwise. The church as the assembly of believers is herself used by Christ as an instrument to bring others into his congregation; through her Christ administers his mediatorial office in the midst of the world. Thus from the beginning the church appears in a twofold form: she is an assembly of the people of God in a passive and an active sense, is at the same time a *coetus* and a *mater fidelium*, or, according to another designation, organism and institution at the same moment. As was already said above, p. 36, this distinction is entirely different from that between the invisible and visible church. It is a distinction within the visible church and says that the church as the assembly of believers is revealed to us in two ways: in offices and means of grace as institution, and in communion of faith and life as organism. With this distinction the question of priority is continually raised. Some represent the matter in such a way that the institution of the church, with office and ministry, always precedes the church as the assembly of believers and thus place the emphasis upon *mater fidelium*. Others judge that the church as the assembly of believers occupies the first place and then itself assumes an institutional form in one way or another under the pressure of circumstances. In this is even sought the difference in principle between Protestantism and Roman Catholicism. Confusing the distinction between the church as institution and organism with that between the visible and invisible church, Schleiermacher says that Protestantism *das Verhältniss des Einzelnen zur Kirche abhängig macht von seinem Verhältniss zu Christo*, while Roman Catholicism, conversely, *das Verhältniss des Einzelnen zu Christo abhängig macht von seinem Verhältniss zur Kirche*; *Chr. Gl.* § 24. And according to Möhler, at Rome the visible church precedes the invisible, but among the Lutherans the latter precedes the former; *Symbolik* § 48. But this entire representation is far from complete and correct. For 1º from the days of Tertullian onward, *De orat.* 2; *De monog.* 7; *Adv. Marc.* V 4, the church has been called by all Christians not only a *coetus* but also a *mater fidelium*. Protestants agree with Roman Catholics in this, and Calvin even places very strong emphasis upon it, *Inst.* IV 1, 4. And according to their conviction the church was this, not because she freely and independently organized herself into an institution and gave herself a government of her own, but because Christ had ordered her in this way. The institution of the church is by no means, at least not according to the Reformed confession, a product of the congregation but an institution of Christ. And that this conviction rests upon good scriptural grounds will become clear in what follows. 2º The church as the assembly of believers does not come into being, as Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.* § 115 says, *durch das Zusammentreten der einzelnen Wiedergeborenen*. For the question from where those regenerate persons come then remains unanswered. They do not come into being because the Holy Spirit regenerates human beings atomistically and *unvermittelt* (not *unmittelbar*) and then joins them together. Rather, the Holy Spirit in all his operations, including that of regeneration, is bound to Christ, from whom he takes everything. And Christ is present on earth only where his Word is. God’s Word and God’s people belong together. It is true that little children are frequently regenerated without having been personally able to hear the preaching of the Word. But these are then little children who have been born within the covenant of grace, who live in the communion of the church, and who receive that inward calling which proceeds from Christ through the Holy Spirit. 3º The difference between Rome and the Reformation on this point does not consist in the priority of the visible or invisible church, of institution or organism, of communion with the church or communion with Christ; at least, it does not consist in this without sharper definition. Rather, it lies herein: Rome binds salvation to priest and sacrament, and the Reformation to the preaching of the Word. According to Rome, *gratia infusa* is communicated only through baptism, and baptism is therefore absolutely necessary. According to the Reformation, the Word is the first and principal means of grace and faith is therefore sufficient for salvation. And that Word operates as a means of grace by no means only when it is officially administered in the assembly of believers but also when it is brought to us in household and school through upbringing and instruction. God’s people are where God’s Word is; but that people and that Word can indeed exist, and frequently do exist, where there is no priest and no pope, no pastor and no presbyter. 4º According to the Reformation too, the church as the assembly of believers does not come into being *unvermittelt*, through an operation of the Spirit detached from the Word. Between Christ and the individual human being there certainly does not stand, as at Rome, the priest and sacrament, the *ecclesia docens*, but the Word of Christ certainly does; for according to the testimony of Scripture, communion with Christ is bound to communion with the word of the apostles, John 17:20, 21; 1 John 1:3. As it is in the natural sphere, so it is also in the spiritual. Every human being is a product of community, and the individual believer is born from the womb of the congregation. The *ecclesia universalis* precedes the *ecclesia particularis* and the individual *fideles*, just as in every organism the whole precedes the parts. The church of Christ is therefore indeed a mother, but she is this by no means only as institution but also as organism. The believers together are at the same time producer and product: *in visibili ecclesia invisibilis colligitur et formatur; invisibilis in visibili haeret ac continetur*; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 40, 34. Through the church Christ gathers his church. 5º By adopting this standpoint, the Reformation avoided both the hierarchy of the Roman Catholics and the enthusiasm of the Anabaptists, and did justice to the truth present in both. On the one hand, no binding of the operation of the Holy Spirit to priest and sacrament, and on the other, no operation of the Holy Spirit apart from Christ and his Word! The church as assembly is revealed in both institution and organism; she has as her marks the pure administration of the Word and the confession and walk of believers; she is institutionally and charismatically ordered. Office does not suppress the gifts but organizes them and keeps them on the right track, and the gifts do not set office aside but make it powerful and fruitful. Irvingism and Darbyism both contain a truth that ought to be acknowledged. Offices and gifts together have been given by Christ to his congregation for the perfecting of the saints and for the building up of his body, Rom. 12:5–8; 1 Cor. 12:25, 28; Eph. 4:11, 12. Therefore 6º the very question of the priority of the institution or the organism of the church already testifies to one-sidedness. Both are given with each other and continually act upon each other. In the state, people and government are always most closely connected. One can indeed investigate the origin of one form of government or another among a particular people; one can indeed demonstrate that political government was first instituted on account of sin; but wherever there are human beings there is also some form of government. Adam was immediately created as head of humanity. And so too the form of government of the church has by no means always been the same, but she has never lacked a government, either in the invisible sphere, in which Christ is her head, or in the visible, in which she has always partaken of a certain organization.
