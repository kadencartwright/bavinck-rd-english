## 9.

There is great disagreement in the Christian church about the number of offices Christ instituted in his congregation. In the apostolic age, by the nature of the case, the boundary between extraordinary and ordinary offices, and likewise between offices and gifts, had not yet been sharply drawn. But the hierarchical development that began with the rise of the episcopate deprived the congregation of all freedom and independence and separated the offices from it by a broad chasm. The members of the congregation became the *laici*, who, excluded from all government and absolutely dependent upon priest and sacrament for their salvation, have nothing to do but listen and obey. And separated from them by a special character and a particular official spirit, high above them stand the *clerici*, who form a distinct estate, perpetuate themselves through succession, and can belong to the class of the clergy even without a definite service in the congregation. These *clerici* are divided into two ranks, *ordines minores (non sacri)* and *ordines majores (sacri)*. The *ordines minores*, to which the *acoluthi, exorcistae, lectores*, and *ostiarii* belong, were at first voluntary services of congregational members but were organized at Rome in the first half of the third century into lower offices, because they were related to the sacred and participated in it to a greater or lesser degree. Although frequently existing only in name, they still serve even now as preparation for the higher offices; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 128; Moeller-von Schubert, *Kirchengesch.* I 370; Wieland, *Die genet. Entw. der sogen. ordines minores in den 3 ersten Jahrh.*, Freiburg: Herder, 1897, and on it Schürer’s *Theol. Lit. Z.* 1898, no. 1. Already in the *ordines minores* the tendency appears to detach them from the congregation and incorporate them into the ecclesiastical hierarchy. But this is the case far more strongly with the *ordines majores*. These comprise the three offices of bishop, presbyter, and deacon, but of these three only the episcopal office has really remained. Within this episcopate all kinds of distinctions in jurisdiction and dignity have indeed been introduced, so that one speaks of archbishops, patriarchs, metropolitans, and so forth, but these distinctions do not infringe upon the unity and essence of the episcopal office. Even the papal office is essentially an episcopal office, merely extended to the whole church and equipped with special gifts for that purpose, differing from the ordinary episcopal office not hieratically but only hierarchically. This episcopal office is properly the one true office in the Roman church. After developing out of the presbyterate in the second century, it drew doctrine, tradition, and jurisdiction to itself, separated itself from the congregation by succession, tonsure, and celibacy, and gradually made presbyters and deacons its organs. Still within the circle of the New Testament, we encounter at the head of the congregation a council of presbyters, a presbytery, 1 Tim. 4:14, and such a council continued for a long time to surround them even after one of their number had elevated himself to bishop. But this council increasingly lost every bond with the congregation, became the bishop’s chapter, and served to act under him, by authority granted by him, as ministers of sacred things, especially of the sacrament. In the same way the diaconate soon changed in character completely. When the idea of priest and sacrifice gained entrance, the διακονειν τραπεζαις, Acts 6:2, was no longer understood of caring for the needy but of auxiliary service at the Eucharist. The bishop became high priest, the presbyters became priests, and the deacons became Levites who, leaving the care of the poor to private persons and monastic orders, stood beside the bishop in the administration of the Mass. While presbyters and deacons were in this way separated entirely from the congregation and made organs of the bishop, the bishop himself is distinguished from all others specifically by one power. The episcopal office is a priestly office, but one joined with the power to propagate it, with the *vis generativa sacerdotii*; it guarantees the continued existence of the *sacerdotium* and thus the propagation of the church. The bishop is the *punctum saliens* in the church; laity, deacons, and presbyters can be temporarily absent, but not the bishop. Where he is, there is the church, for he is the bearer of doctrine, the propagator of the priesthood. The presbyters too are priests, authorized to administer the sacraments, but they may not ordain; they lack the *vis generativa sacerdotii*; their priesthood is unfruitful; they are servants and assistants of the bishop, because he cannot be everywhere and cannot do everything. At Rome the presbyterate and diaconate are extensions of the episcopate; they are three degrees within the one *sacerdotium*, not coordinated but subordinated. The presbyter is also a deacon; the bishop is also a presbyter. At each point the gift of office rises one step higher, until it culminates in the bishop or, as the following section will show, in the pope. Cf. Thomas, *S. Theol.*, suppl., qu. 34–40; Lombardus et al. on *Sent.* IV, dist. 24; Bonav., *Brevil.* VI 12; *Conc. Trid.*, sess. 23; *Cat. Rom.* II, ch. 7; Bellarminus, *de clericis* I, ch. 11 sq.; Dens, *Theol.* VII 50 sq.; Oswald, *Die dogm. Lehre v. d. h. Sakr.* II² 315–335; Seydl, *Der Diakonat*, Regensburg, 1884; Vering, *Lehrb. des kath. orient. und prot. Kirchenrechts*³, Freiburg, 1893, p. 558f.; etc.

Over against this hierarchy Luther contented himself with the restoration of the original preaching office. He did indeed consider a council of elders necessary for the exercise of discipline and a council of deacons for the care of the poor. But on account of the difficulties of the times these offices were nevertheless not restored; nor were they as necessary as the episcopal, spiritual preaching office, the *Pfarramt*, which is the principal office and through which Christ governs his church in a special way. The offices of elder and deacon were therefore replaced in the Lutheran church by the consistory and the board of church wardens; the Roman *ordines* gave way to the *ordo ecclesiasticus, politicus*, and *oeconomicus*. Cf. Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 538f.; *Conf. Aug.* and *Apol.*, arts. 5, 14, 28; Gerhard, *Loc.* XXIII, especially §§ 232, 233; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 460–542; Achelis, *Lehrb. d. prakt. Theol.* I² 60f. Presbyterian church government, by contrast, is owed to Calvin. Attempts had indeed already been made before him, among others by Oecolampadius at Basel in 1530, to institute the office of elders for the sake of church discipline; but Calvin was nevertheless the first to carry this out and made the office of elder a distinguishing mark of Reformed church government; Lechler, *Gesch. der Presb. u. Syn. Verfassung*, Leiden, 1854, and the article “Presbyter” in Herzog². In doing so he proceeded from the Word of God. For even though Calvin’s character and circumstances opened his eyes to the significance of the offices in Holy Scripture, presbyterian church government was nevertheless derived by him not from any abstract principle but from the Word of God and was introduced into the church on its authority. In more recent times people have indeed begun to speak of a *Gemeindeprinzip* and from it have constructed a kind of presbyterial and diaconal offices. A congregation, it was said, had the right to govern itself, just as in the political sphere the people increasingly obtain influence upon the government—so Stahl and many more recent church orders in Rieker, *Grundsätze ref. Kirchenverf.*, 130f.—and likewise a congregation needed organs, that is, deacons and deaconesses, to carry out the work of the Inner Mission; Paul Wurster, *Die Lehre v. d. inneren Mission*, Berlin, 1895, p. 128f. But this is an altogether different conception from the one found in Calvin and the Reformed. Although they do urge the government of the church on the ground that otherwise it can no more exist than a people or a society can—Calvin, *Inst.* IV 11, 1; a Lasco, *Op.* II 45—they nevertheless derive the offices not from the congregation but from the institution of Christ. The church as the communion of saints is not autonomous; she is not free to organize herself or not, in this way or that, but on this point too she is bound to the Word of God and finds indicated in it the principles and the lines she must follow in governing the church. The general conviction was that the government of the church must in substance rest upon a *jus divinum*: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 3, 1; 4, 1; *Conf. Gall.* 25, 29; *Belg.* 30; *Helv.* II 18; especially the Westminster synod in Neal, *Historie der Purit.* II 1, p. 182v. Yet in this they did not lose sight of the fact that Scripture was no law code, did not descend into all kinds of details, and left very much to the freedom of the churches: Synod of Wesel I 9, 10; Emden 19–21; Westminster I 6. Even concerning the offices Christ instituted in his church there was considerable disagreement. First, there were those who had no objection to an episcopate in the sense of a superintendency: a Lasco, *Op.* II 51, 57; Knox, *First Book of Discipline*; cf. Saravia, Scultetus, Bochartus, Spanheim Jr., Tilenus, Forbesius a Corse, and others in M. Vitringa IX 210 sq. Then there was disagreement about whether the office of doctor, understood as a professorship in theology, constituted a separate ecclesiastical office or whether, because it was not of apostolic institution, it could be so called only in a broader sense; cf. my address on *Het Doctorenambt*, Kampen, 1899. Further, apart from the doctorate, some preferred to speak of three offices—pastor, presbyter, and deacon: Calvin, *Ordonn. eccl.*; Synod of Wesel, chs. 2, 4, 5; Emden 13, 14; Dort 12v.; Middelburg 2; The Hague 2; Dort 2. Others named two offices, presbyter and deacon, and then divided the first into the teaching and ruling eldership: a Lasco, *Op.* II 51, and many Scottish and American church orders in Rieker, *Grundsätze ref. Kirchenverf.*, 104. There were even those who considered presbyterian church government useful but not necessary by virtue of a *jus divinum* and rejected the distinction between teaching and ruling elders: Cappellus, *Theses Salm.* III 330; Burmannus, *Synopsis* VIII 7, 41v.; and others in M. Vitringa IX 235 sq. Further, the distinction Calvin introduced between deacons for the poor and deacons for the sick, *Inst.* IV 3, 9, was only rarely adopted: Zanchius, *Op.* IV 767; Synod of Wesel, ch. 5. Others restored the office of deaconesses: Junius, *Op.* I 1567; Walaeus, *Op.* I 466; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* II 508 sq., 529. And according to some, Acts 6 did not report the institution of the diaconate, and this office was therefore not of divine origin: Cappellus and others in M. Vitringa IX 277 sq. Finally, there was still disagreement about the manner of election; about holding an office without a definite service in the congregation, Heidegger, *Corpus Theol.* II 571; about the usefulness of laying on of hands, both in general and especially at the installation of elders and deacons, Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 466; about repeating the installation when elders and deacons were reappointed, Moor VI 329, M. Vitringa IX 361; and about the duration of the eldership, Rutgers in *De Heraut* 944–948; etc. The treatment of all these subjects belongs to church polity. But this much may safely be said: by restoring the offices of elder and deacon alongside that of minister of the Word, the Reformed grasped the thought of Scripture most purely and acknowledged the rights of the congregation most forcefully. Over the church Christ alone is king; her government in the invisible sphere is strictly monarchic. And he was not king only in the past, but he still is. From heaven he governs his congregation on earth through his Word and his Spirit, through his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity. He continues these three offices on earth—not exclusively, but nevertheless also—through the offices he instituted. In the visible sphere his government is neither democratic, monarchic, nor oligarchic, but aristocratic-presbyterial. They are the ἀριστοι, the best, not in money and goods but in spiritual gifts, whom he himself equips and causes to be designated by the congregation for his service. Through them he cares for the spiritual and material interests of his congregation. Through the teaching office he instructs, through the office of elder he guides, through the office of deacon he cares for his flock; and through all three he demonstrates himself to be our supreme prophet, our eternal king, and our merciful high priest.
