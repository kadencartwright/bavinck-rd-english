## 5.

Although this hierarchical church government is in its origin much older than earlier Protestants were inclined to acknowledge and even goes back to the beginning of the second century, and although by the logical course of its development and the imposing character of its appearance it never fails to make an impression, it is nevertheless in principle and essence directly opposed to the government that Christ has given to his church. _First_, the distinction between clergy and laity that underlies this hierarchy is taught nowhere in the New Testament and is most decidedly contradicted by the organization of the church in the first century. Rome does indeed appeal, besides the *convenientia*, to the Old Testament priesthood, to the offices Christ instituted in his church, and to the power he entrusted to them. But this does not prove what must be proved. Scripture certainly distinguishes between shepherds and flock, builders and temple, planters and field, teachers and disciples, leaders and followers, and so forth; if the names clergy and laity meant nothing other than this distinction, they could be used without harm. But usage has attached an entirely different sense to them. In the Roman Church, the clergy has become the name of a special class of ecclesiastical persons who are separated from all others by tonsure and ordination, form an estate of “spiritual persons,” belong to God in an altogether special sense, are equipped with absolute sovereign power over the people, and serve as necessary and indispensable mediators of salvation for the laity, *Cat. Rom.* II 7, 13. Scripture knows no such estate. Even to the Old Testament the antithesis of clergy and laity is inapplicable; the whole people was the clergy, the possession and inheritance of the Lord, a priestly kingdom, a holy people, Ex. 19:5, 6; Deut. 7:6; 14:2; 26:18; 32:9; 1 Kings 8:51, 53; Ps. 135:4; Isa. 19:25; 41:8; Jer. 12:7, 8; Joel 2:17, and so forth. The priesthood, which was not appointed but called to this service by virtue of descent from Aaron, was nevertheless bound most strictly to the law of God, Lev. 10:11, and subject to the judgment of the prophets, Isa. 28:7; Jer. 1:18; 2:26; 6:13; Ezek. 22:26; Hos. 4:9, and so forth. Israel was a theocracy but not a hierarchy. Much less is there any mention of a clergy in the New Testament: the Holy Spirit has been poured out upon all, Acts 2:17, and now all are πνευματικοι, Rom. 8:14; 1 Cor. 2:15; 3:1; Gal. 5:25; 6:1, partakers of the anointing, 1 John 2:27, a royal priesthood, 1 Pet. 2:5, 9, a congregation of saints and called ones, Rom. 1:7, a people and possession of God, 2 Cor. 6:16; 1 Pet. 2:10; Heb. 12:22–24. Nowhere does the New Testament mention a special priestly office to be exercised by the ministers of the church or a special sacrifice to be offered by them; the office in the church of Christ is not a *magisterium* but a *ministerium*, not an ἱεραρχια but an ἱεροδουλεια, a διακονια, οἰκονομια, which altogether excludes every exercise of dominion over the Lord’s inheritance (των κληρων, 1 Pet. 5:3, that is, the congregations entrusted to the presbyters for their care), Matt. 20:25, 26; 1 Cor. 3:5; 4:1; 2 Cor. 4:1, 2; Eph. 4:12. Cf. Luther’s doctrine of the universal priesthood of believers in Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 316. 376. II 538; Gerhard, *Loci Theol.*, loc. 23 § 37; Calvin, *Inst.* IV 4, 9. 12, 1; *Comm.* on 1 Pet. 5:3; Junius, *Op.* II 1181; Amesius, *Bellarminus enervatus* III, ch. 1; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III, p. 2; Cappellus, *Synt. thesium theol. in Acad. Salmur.* III, pp. 272–279; M. Vitringa IX 1, pp. 423–437; Caspari, art. “Geistliche,” in Herzog³.

_Second_, Holy Scripture nevertheless teaches very clearly that Christ not only distributes gifts in his congregation but also instituted definite offices: extraordinary ones, such as those of apostle, prophet, and evangelist, and ordinary ones, such as those of elder and deacon. On the episcopate there is the greatest disagreement. Greeks, Roman Catholics (including Gallicans and Old Catholics), and Anglicans regard this as an office essentially and *jure divino* distinct from the presbyterate, descended through legitimate and unbroken succession from the apostolate, and possessing in particular the *potestas magisterii, jurisdictionis et ordinis*. The bishops alone properly possess the teaching office, the power to preach and administer the sacraments; in this they make use of the priests (pastors) as their *vicarii*, and they possess the exclusive right of mission and ordination. Patriarchs, metropolitans, and archbishops bear no other office but are distinguished from bishops only by an accidental power, by jurisdiction over a larger territory, and so forth. Proofs from Holy Scripture for this distinction can be drawn only from the offices held by Timothy, Titus, and others, and from the ἀγγελος in the seven congregations of Asia Minor, Rev. 1:20. But Timothy and many others were called by the apostles in that first period to the extraordinary office of evangelist, above, p. 66; and the angels of the congregations were no others than the first among their equals, like the *pastores* today, so that the singular in Rev. 2:10, 23, 24 also alternates with the plural. Bellarmine, *De membris eccl. milit.* I, ch. 14; Petavius, *De eccl. hierarchia* I, ch. 2, and others, therefore also make no mention of scriptural arguments but appeal to the tradition in Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius, and so forth, who say that the apostles appointed a bishop in various congregations and provide the lists of bishops for some congregations. But even if the monarchical episcopate arose very early in the church, one can nevertheless see in it a clear departure from the ordinances of the apostles. For the New Testament still knows nothing of an official distinction between ἐπισκοπος and πρεσβυτεροs. Although the name πρεσβυτεροι probably had a broader meaning in the earliest period and sometimes also designated the old and venerable members of the congregation, as a title of office it was nevertheless identical with that of the ἐπισκοποι. For there were many ἐπισκοποι in the congregations, Acts 20:17, 28; Phil. 1:1, and this επισκοπειν was entrusted precisely to πρεσβυτεροι, Acts 20:17, 28; 1 Tim. 3:1–7; 5:17; Titus 1:5, 7; 1 Pet. 5:1–3. Peter therefore also calls himself a πρεσβυτερος, 1 Pet. 5:1. Accordingly, the New Testament knows nothing of a special institution of the episcopate alongside the presbyterate. Apart from the extraordinary offices of apostle, prophet, and evangelist, there are only two ordinary offices, that of deacons and that of πρεσβυτεροι, Phil. 1:1; 1 Tim. 3:1, 8; ποιμενες και διδασκαλοι, Eph. 4:11; 1 Tim. 5:17; κυβερνησεις, 1 Cor. 12:28; προισταμενοι, Rom. 12:8; 1 Thess. 5:12; ἡγουμενοι, Heb. 13:7, 17. These testimonies of Scripture are so strong that not only Aerius in the 4th century, the Waldenses, Wycliffe, the Reformers, and others called the presbyteral and episcopal offices identical, but many church fathers, such as Theodoret, Chrysostom, Epiphanius, and others, also felt compelled to acknowledge that in the New Testament the names presbyter and *episcopus* were used interchangeably. Jerome, *Ep.* 65 *ad Evagrium* and *Comm.* on Titus 1:5, even says that *presbyteri* and *episcopi* were originally equal but that later one of them was placed over the others, *in schismatis remedium*, in Petavius, *Dissert. eccles.* I, chs. 1–3; cf. also Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dist. 24, 9. The Roman Catholics cannot reconcile their position on this point with Scripture. According to Acts 20:17, 28 and Phil. 1:1, there were undoubtedly several *episcopi* in one congregation; but the Roman Catholics cannot acknowledge this and therefore say either that the apostles sometimes gave the presbyters episcopal ordination at the same time (Petavius), that the bishops of the neighboring congregations were included among them (Franzelin), or that the names *presbyteri* and *episcopi* had not yet been distinguished, Scheeben, *Dogm.* IV 395; Ermann, *De Paus*, 2nd part, 96. In the last case, however, the alleged difference between the presbyteral and episcopal offices cannot be maintained. To this testimony of Scripture is then added the lesson of history: the Roman episcopate is the root of the hierarchy, opens the way to the papacy, brings with it the inequality of the congregations, and makes believers slavishly dependent upon the offices. All this too is altogether contrary to Scripture. There is no hierarchy in the church of Christ, Luke 22:25, 26; 2 Cor. 1:24; 1 Pet. 5:3. There is no diocesan, cathedral, patriarchal, or metropolitan church, for all congregations in the New Testament are equal and each has its own ἐπισκοποι. And nowhere are believers commanded to investigate the *legitima successio* of their ministers, but rather to search the Scriptures, remain in the doctrine, and so forth, John 5:39; Acts 17:11; 1 Tim. 4:13–16; 2 Tim. 1:13, 14; 3:14–17. Legitimate succession does not guarantee purity of doctrine, John 8:39; Rom. 2:28; 9:6, and would make salvation dependent on particular persons and upon an impractical, fallible, and often even impossible historical investigation. For these reasons the episcopate was unanimously rejected by the Reformers. The Lutherans were indeed willing to recognize the right it had acquired *secundum ecclesiasticam politiam*, provided that the essential element in the episcopal office remained the *ministerium verbi et sacramentorum* resting upon divine right, *Symb. Bücher*, ed. Müller, pp. 62. 205. 286. 340. The name bishop was also sometimes retained and transferred to the territorial prince, or in some Reformed as well as Lutheran churches one from a circle of ministers was charged, under the name of bishop or superintendent, with inspection over a group of congregations. Calvin and many others—Knox, à Lasco, Saravia, Tilenus, Scultetus, Bochart, Spanheim, and so forth—had no overriding objection to this, M. Vitringa IX, p. 210 sq. But this was nevertheless something essentially different from the episcopal office in the Roman Church, which according to the Council of Trent differs essentially from the presbyterate, rests upon divine right, and in the 17th century was also introduced and defended in the Anglican Church. Cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 3, 8. 4, 2 sq. 5, 1 sq.; Beza, *Responsio ad tractationem de ministrorum evangelii gradibus ab Hadriano Saravia editam*, 1592; Thomas Cartwright, † 1603, *A Directory for Church Government*, 1644; William Bradshaw, † 1618, *English Puritanism, Containing the Main Opinions of the Rigidest Sort of Those Called Puritans in the Realm of England*, 1604; David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum seu politica ecclesiastica obtrusa ecclesiae Scoticanae*, 1621; Robert Parker, *De politica ecclesiastica Christi*, 1616; Smectymnus, a reply to Hall’s *Episcopacy* by Stephen Marshal, Edmund Calamy, Thomas Young, Matthew Newcomen, and William Spurstow, 1641; Blondel, *Apologia pro sententia Hieronymi de episcopis et presbyteris*, 1646; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* III 832–869; Maresius, *Examen quaestionis de episcoporum origine*, 1657; Cappellus, *Synt. thes. theol. in acad. Salm.* III 296; Buddeus, *De origine et potestate episcoporum*, *Misc. Saera* I 131; M. Vitringa IX 1, pp. 141–229.

_Third_, according to Holy Scripture it is established that the apostolate was an exceptional, temporary, and unrepeatable office in the congregation of the New Testament. Even if the episcopate were a different office from the presbyterate, it would by no means follow that it was identical with the apostolate and its continuation. Naturally, it can be said in a proper sense that the *episcopi* or *presbyteri* are successors of the apostles, for the latter appointed them in all the congregations they founded and entrusted the care of those congregations to them. But this does not remove the great and essential distinction between the two. Nor can this distinction be erased on the Roman Catholic side. For the apostles shared in an altogether special guidance of the Holy Spirit and bore an office that extended to the whole church, indeed to the whole world, Matt. 28:20. But their successors, even if they were bishops in the Roman sense, possess no such office at all; according to Rome too they are not infallible, and they have care only over a small part of the church, over a diocese, Schwane, *D. G.* IV 267. 285. 294; Simar, *Dogm.*, 612; Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 247; Ermann, *De Paus*, 2nd part, 89f. But the distinction is even stronger: the apostles were ear- and eyewitnesses of Jesus’ words and deeds; they were called to their office immediately by Christ himself; they received the Holy Spirit in a special measure; and they had an entirely unique task, namely, to lay the foundation of the church and in their word to provide the abiding means of communion between Christ and his congregation. In all this they are distinguished from all others, stand high above all their followers, and occupy an office that is intransferable and unrepeatable. The extraordinary gifts in which they were privileged to share are granted to no other ministers in the church. As apostles in the proper sense they have no successors, even though the leadership of the congregation to which they were called was also entrusted after them to others, in another manner and within a more limited sphere.

_Fourth_, no proof can be found in Scripture that the primacy of Peter was essentially distinct from the apostolate he held in common with the others. Roman Catholic theologians also acknowledge that all Scripture relates about Peter’s precedence over the other apostles is still not sufficient to prove his *primatus jurisdictionis* over the other apostles, Scheeben–Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 405. The *sedes doctrinae* for this doctrine are only Matt. 16:18; Luke 22:32; and John 21:15–17. But these too do not contain what Rome infers from them. Matt. 16:18 teaches that Peter by his confession is the rock upon which Christ builds his congregation. This is, of course, figurative language, and without the figure it signifies neither less nor anything different than that Christ would make use of the person of Peter as the faithful confessor to gather his congregation. In the congregation as the building of God, believers are the living stones built upon the apostles as the foundation stones. Peter occupies that place in God’s building, and not he alone but all the apostles with him. For Peter made the confession of Jesus’ messiahship in the name of them all, Matt. 16:15, 16. Although he expressed it first and most clearly, he nevertheless gave voice to what lived unconsciously in all their hearts. And not Peter alone but all the apostles laid the foundation of Christ’s church through this confession, which they soon proclaimed in the midst of the world. Figuratively speaking, therefore, they were all with Peter the rock upon which Christ built his church; or, with another application of the same figure, Christ is the rock that the apostles together laid as the foundation of the congregation through their proclamation, Acts 4:11; Rom. 9:33; 1 Cor. 3:10, 11; Eph. 2:20; 1 Pet. 2:5, 6; Rev. 21:14. The power of the keys, which Peter already receives on account of his courageous confession in Matt. 16:19, is granted in 18:18 and John 20:23 to all the apostles. The special qualification and guidance of the Holy Spirit was not Peter’s portion alone but that of all the apostles equally, Matt. 10:20; John 14:26; 15:26; 16:13; 20:22; Acts 1:8; Eph. 3:5. By maintaining the apostolate as an exceptional and intransferable office, the Reformation does far more justice to all these texts, including Matt. 16:18, than Rome does. The apostles are and remain the founders of the church; by their confession they are the rock of the congregation; there is no communion with Christ except through communion with their testimony! As for the other passage, Luke 22:32, Jesus says there to his disciples that Satan will try to bring them all, ἱμας in the plural, to denial of their Master, but that he will pray particularly for Peter, περι σου in the singular, that his faith may not fail. Peter would need this before them all, because he would be the first and most vehement to deny his Master. That his faith then does not fail, he will owe solely to an altogether special intercession by Jesus. And when he is preserved through that prayer of Jesus and rises again from his fall, then, taught by the trial and strengthened in his faith, he will be able precisely to strengthen his brothers when they too perhaps later waver in their faith. Just as it is said of Paul with the same word, στηριζω, that he strengthened the disciples, Acts 18:23; cf. Rom. 1:11; 16:25; 1 Thess. 3:2, 13; 2 Thess. 3:3; 1 Pet. 5:10, so Peter is promised here that later he will serve his brothers for encouragement, perseverance, and strengthening. Legalistic Rome turns this glorious spiritual support into a *primatus jurisdictionis*! Finally, in John 21:15–17 Peter is restored to the rank he had formerly occupied with and among the apostles. He receives no new office surpassing that which he formerly possessed. For the address by the name Simon, son of Jonah; the threefold question; and the circumstances in which this event took place prove irrefutably that Peter is only restored to the rank he had forfeited through his denial of Jesus, hence to the apostolate and to the *primatus honoris* previously granted to him. And thus, too, when Jesus again entrusts him with the care and leadership of his flock in general and of the sheep in particular, he is charged with no other activity than that which was enclosed in the apostolate as such and therefore also belonged to all the other apostles, Matt. 28:19; Mark 16:15; 2 Cor. 11:28. The precedence Peter enjoyed among the apostles accordingly did not in the least prevent him from being rebuked by Jesus as a Satan and stumbling block when he wished to deter Jesus from his approaching suffering, Matt. 16:23; from being humbled for exalting himself above the other apostles, John 21:15; from being rebuked by Paul for his insincerity in Antioch, Gal. 2:11; from being sent to Samaria with John by the other apostles, Acts 8:14; from having no jurisdiction whatever over Paul, Gal. 2:6, 9; from never being named separately in Scripture as head and prince of the apostles, 1 Cor. 12:28; Eph. 2:20; 4:11; Rev. 21:14; and from himself attributing the preservation of believers solely to the power of God, calling himself a συμπρεσβυτερος, and warning against exercising dominion over the congregations, 1 Pet. 1:5; 5:1, 3.

_Fifth._ But even if Scripture were to ascribe to Peter a primacy in the Roman sense—which it by no means does—this would still gain nothing for the primacy of the bishop of Rome. For much more would still have to be proved for this, namely, 1º that Peter was in Rome, 2º that he held there the office of bishop and primate, and 3º that he knowingly and intentionally transferred both these offices to one specific successor. Now Baur and Lipsius have recently disputed, but wrongly, that Peter was in Rome. Even if 1 Pet. 5:13 is not decisive, where according to many Babylon must be understood as Rome, the testimony of tradition from the earliest period onward—in Clement of Rome, Ignatius, Marcion, Dionysius of Corinth, Irenaeus, the Muratorian Canon, Gaius, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, Lactantius, and others—is so established and unanimous that its truth cannot reasonably be doubted. It may also be regarded as established that Peter died as a martyr in the year 64 under Nero, either in the same year as Paul or a few years before him. Rev. 18:20, cf. 17:6; 19:2, indicates that Rome shed the blood of apostles, and the aforementioned ancient Christian writers all say unanimously that Peter and Paul suffered martyrdom in Rome, sometimes with the more precise chronological specification, under Nero. But it is entirely unprovable that Peter stayed in Rome for 20 à 25 years, that he was bishop of the congregation there and primate of the whole church, and that Linus succeeded him in the episcopate and the primacy. For, 1º Acts 12:17 reports that Peter left Jerusalem not long before the death of Herod, v. 23, who died in the year 44. That he then went to Rome is nothing but a supposition and lacks every basis. But even if this were so, one could think of nothing other than a short visit to Rome, just as according to Harnack he was perhaps also in Corinth at that time, 1 Cor. 1–3; 9:5. In any case, Peter was back in Jerusalem for the council in Acts 15, that is, in the year 47. 2º In the epistle Paul wrote from Corinth to the congregation in Rome around the year 54–58, not one word mentions Peter’s residence and work in Rome. Nor does this occur in the epistles to Philemon, Colossae, and Ephesus, which Paul probably wrote from Rome, or in the one to Philippi, which he certainly wrote from Rome in the years 57–58 or 61–63. And Peter too, in his first epistle written from Babylon, 1 Pet. 5:13—that is, perhaps Rome—does not mention Paul by a single word. Zahn therefore supposes that Peter was in Rome precisely between Paul’s first and second imprisonments, when the latter was traveling to Spain, and there suffered martyrdom in the year 64 under Nero and a few years before Paul. Thus Peter remained in Rome no longer than half a year or a year. 3º In agreement with these facts, the oldest tradition always names Peter and Paul alongside each other and says that not Peter alone but Peter and Paul founded and confirmed the congregation at Rome: Clement, *1 Cor.* 5; Gaius and Dionysius of Corinth in Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* II 25, 7. 8; Ignatius, *Ad Rom.* 4; Irenaeus, *Adv. haer.* III 1, 1. 3, 1–3; Tertullian, *De praescr.* 36. These ancient histories know nothing of Peter’s years-long residence and episcopate in Rome. On the contrary, according to Clement’s epistle from the years 93–95, the *Pastor* of Hermas written in Rome around 100 or 135–145, and Ignatius’s epistle to the Romans, the monarchical episcopate did not yet exist in Rome at that time—that is, in any case, around the end of the first century—but the congregation was led by a college of presbyters or *episcopi*. The lists of bishops in Hegesippus, Irenaeus, the Muratorian fragment, Hippolytus, Tertullian, and Epiphanius show that at the end of the second and even at the beginning of the third century Peter was not yet regarded as bishop of Rome. The ordinary conception was still at that time that Peter and Paul had founded the congregation and entrusted the ministry of the episcopate to Linus, Irenaeus, *Adv. haer.* III 3. Then from Linus as the first bishop onward the following bishops are designated as second, third, and so forth, in such a way that the apostles Peter and Paul, as εὐαγγελιζομενοι και θεμελιουντες την ἐκκλησιαν, ibid. III 1, 1, preceded them all, while the bishops came after them and succeeded one another ἀπο των ἀποστολων, that is, from the time of the apostles onward, Irenaeus I 27, 1; Eusebius, *Hist. Eccl.* V, preface; 28, 3; Epiphanius, *Haer.* 42, 1. 4º Only in the time of Victor or Zephyrinus, 180–217, was this old tradition altered so that Paul increasingly lost his share in the founding and confirmation of the congregation at Rome, while Peter alone was presented as the institutor of the episcopate and afterward also as the first bishop of Rome. According to Tertullian, *De pudic.* 21, Callistus already called himself bishop on the chair of Peter. Stephen claimed *per successionem cathedram Petri habere*, Cyprian, *Ep.* 71, 3. 75, 17. And Cyprian consistently designated the chair of the bishop in Rome as the *cathedra Petri*, *Ep.* 55, 8. 59, 14. Around this same time the legend first arose that Peter had labored in Rome for 20 or 25 years and had been bishop there for that long. In his church history Eusebius does not yet speak of Peter as bishop and still names Peter and Paul alongside each other, III 2, but in III 4, 9 names Peter alone and in II 14, 6 says that Peter had already come to Rome under Emperor Claudius to contend with Simon Magus. Here the origin of the legend is discovered at the same time. Already before the middle of the second century it was regarded as a fact in Rome that Simon Magus had come to Rome under Claudius. The *Acta Petri*, which originated around 160, taught in connection with Acts 8 that Peter and Simon Magus had contended with each other many times. These traditions were combined and so gave birth to the legend that Peter came to Rome under Claudius and lived there until his death in 64, thus for some twenty years. And Eusebius and Jerome made it a component of Roman tradition. Cf. Herzog² 11, 375f.; Hase, *Protest. Polemik*⁵, p. 150f.; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Conf.*, 90f.; Harnack, *Die Chronologie der altchristl. Litteratur bis Eusebius* I, 1897, passim, especially pp. 171f. 240f. 703f.; Th. Zahn, *Einl. in das N. T.* I, 1897; II, 1899, passim, especially II 17–27; Erbes, *Die Todestage der Apostel Paulus und Petrus und ihre röm. Denkmäler*, Leipzig: Hinrichs, 1899, who thinks that Paul was put to death on 22 Feb. 63, that Peter, having heard of this, came to Rome and died there as a martyr in the following year.

_Sixth_, although the Roman Catholics find support in the tradition after Irenaeus, they are in no small difficulty when confronted by the oldest testimonies, those dating from the first and second centuries. But even if these testimonies were more favorable and more to their advantage, they must nevertheless all acknowledge that the primacy of the bishop of Rome is built upon a historical supposition, namely, that Peter was in Rome, that he held the office of bishop and primate there, and that he transferred it to his successor. Now this tradition, even supposing that it confirmed all this, is nevertheless only a historical testimony that even according to Rome can claim not infallible certainty but only high probability. The primacy of the bishop of Rome, the ecclesiastical dignity of the pope, and therefore the truth of the Roman Church and the salvation of the members of the Roman Church are built upon a historical probability that could at any moment be nullified by new testimonies. Eternity hangs here by a spider’s thread. But in addition, another difficulty presents itself for Rome. The tradition in Julius Africanus, Origen, Eusebius, and others relates that before his journey to Rome, in the second year of Emperor Claudius, Peter was in Antioch and instituted the episcopate there, Harnack, op. cit., 118. 705. Let this tradition be unreliable in itself; it is nevertheless difficult for the Roman Catholics to deny its truth, since they then give the appearance of using a double standard. But even apart from this tradition, it is most probable that, like the other apostles, Peter too instituted the episcopate in various congregations. Why, then, is precisely the bishop of Rome Peter’s successor and the heir of the primacy? According to Rome, Peter was also primate when he stayed in Jerusalem, Antioch, and elsewhere. In his case, therefore, there was in any event no indissoluble bond between his primacy and the episcopate of the congregation at Rome. Thus he could just as well have transferred that primacy to a bishop other than the bishop of Rome. If, as is very probable, he also appointed *episcopi* in congregations elsewhere, did he transfer the episcopate but expressly reserve the primacy until he could hand this over to the bishop of Rome? And why did he do so? On what authority did he act thus? What proof is there that Linus is Peter’s successor, not only in the apostolate but also in the primacy? A historical, ecclesiastical right, according to which it has always been understood this way, is not sufficient here, for this concerns precisely the foundation upon which the whole Roman Church rests. There must be a divine right for it. But there is none. Christ did not speak a single word about Peter’s episcopate in Rome or about his successor there. Neither according to Scripture nor according to tradition did Peter himself indicate even remotely that the *episcopus* in Rome was his one true successor. The connection of the primacy with the Roman episcopate thus rests only upon the fact that Peter was in Rome and upon the unhistorical supposition that he held there the office of bishop and primate. Even if this last point were historically correct, it would still not provide what it must provide. For the strictly necessary proof would then still be lacking that Peter consciously and intentionally, by virtue of apostolic authority and divine commission, wished to transfer and actually did transfer this episcopate and this primacy to his successor in Rome. That is, the primacy of the Roman bishop over the whole church hangs in the air; it can point to no *jus divinum* upon which it rests, and it has not even a reliable historical foundation. The Roman theologians must therefore, despite themselves, acknowledge that the primacy of the Roman bishop possesses *neben einer unmittelbar göttlichen Grundlage, nämlich der Einsetzung des Primates als einer dauernden Institution* (which, however, is also unprovable), *auch eine menschlich vermittelte Grundlage von geschichtlicher Natur*, Scheeben–Atzberger, *Kath. Dogm.* IV 1, p. 425. Finally, therefore, the Roman theologians are also not agreed among themselves about the nature of the connection between the primacy and the Roman episcopate. Some, such as Dominicus Soto, Banner, Mendoza, and others, are of the opinion that this connection is only *ex jure ecclesiastico* and that the primacy of the Roman episcopal see can be transferred to another. Ballerini, Veith, and others leave the question undecided and regard it as extremely difficult to answer. But Cajetan, Canus, Suarez, and others are of the opinion that Peter, after instituting the episcopate in Antioch, received a special divine revelation and by virtue of it indissolubly connected the primacy with the episcopate at Rome, Schwane, *D. G.* IV 300. 303. 311. 341; and further, Bellarmine, *De Rom. pontif.*, bk. II; *Theol. Wirceb.*, ed. Paris, 1880, I 267–306; Perrone, *Prael. theol.*, Louvain, 1843, VIII 295–419; Scheeben–Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 424–435; Jansen, *Prael.* I 512–582; Hettinger, *Apol. des Christ.* IV, 1897, pp. 499–618; W. Esser, *Des h. Petrus Aufenthalt, Episkopat und Tod zu Rom*, Breslau, 1889; Joseph Hollweck, *Der apost. Stuhl und Rom*, Mainz, 1895. Although this dispute has not yet been formally decided, popes, councils, and theologians speak mostly in favor of the latter opinion; involuntarily they always proceed from the indissoluble connection between the two. By the Syllabus of Pius IX, prop. 35, it has been determined that those entitled to elect may not transfer the primacy from the city of Rome and its bishop to another city and bishop. The only question remaining is whether the pope himself would be permitted to do this; and this can, of course, be decided only by the infallible one himself. The Roman Christian is therefore bound to believe that communion with the local church at Rome is necessary for salvation. The so-called Catholic Church is in truth the Roman Church; that is its name and its essence.
