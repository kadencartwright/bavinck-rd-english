## 4.

This aristocratic-presbyterial form of church government did not continue for long but soon passed over into a monarchical-episcopal form. How this occurred we do not know; there is therefore a broad field here for guesses and suppositions; cf. Karl Sell, *Forschungen der Gegenwart über Begriff und Entstehung der Kirche*, *Zeits. f. Theol. u. Kirche* of Gottschick, 1894, pp. 347–417. But various circumstances certainly steered the church in this direction. First, it was natural that at the beginning the churches could not yet distinguish strictly between the extraordinary offices (apostles, evangelists, prophets) and the ordinary offices (*episcopi, diaconi*). The offices of apostle (in the broader sense of evangelist), prophet, and teacher, resting upon a *charisma*, continued in the congregations even after the twelve apostles and Paul had died, *Did.* 11 sq.; Hermas, *Mand.* 11; Eusebius, *H. E.* III 37. But when these offices ceased and increasingly degenerated, so that it became ever more difficult to distinguish between true and false, the evangelistic, prophetic, and didactic activity was attached to the office of the *episcopi*; *they* became the true evangelists, prophets, and teachers, *Did.* 15. As always happens, so too then the stream of free life was gradually led into the channel of a fixed organization. Second, the conditions that, according to the New Testament and the apostolic fathers, existed in the congregations clearly teach us that the first Christians were far from capable of receiving the truth of the gospel in all its richness and purity. The ideas of Paul especially were mixed with all kinds of foreign Jewish and pagan elements. Justification by faith was nowhere taught purely. Faith in God’s revelation in Christ retained significance only as a motive for a moral life, frequently already colored by asceticism; the gospel became a new law, observance of which bestowed eternal life, the ἀφθαρσια. This entire moral, legalistic tendency favored the elevation of the office as organ of divine revelation, made obedience and submission to ecclesiastical authority (μαθετε ὑποτασσεσθαι, *Clem. 1 Cor.* 57) the first Christian duty, and made heresy and schism the most dreadful of all sins. Third, the appearance of all kinds of heretical and sectarian tendencies made the organization and consolidation of the Christian congregations urgently necessary. The question concerning the true church acquired practical importance. And the answer was: the true church is the one that adheres to the whole, to the *catholica ecclesia*, and this church is where the bishop is, Ignatius, *Smyrn.* 8. This change in government did not come about in all churches at the same time. The Didache does not yet know it; it speaks not of *presbyteri* but only of *episcopi* and *diaconi*, who must be men worthy of the Lord and must fulfill the ministry of prophets and teachers, ch. 15. Hermas names apostles, *episcopi*, teachers, and deacons alongside one another without mentioning *presbyteri*, *Vis.* III 5, but appears to presuppose at the head of each congregation a college composed of *presbyteri*, II 4, and therefore does not yet make an official distinction between *presbyteri* and *episcopi*. Around the time in which the *Pastor* of Hermas was written—that is, in any case, in the first half of the second century—the monarchical episcopate thus did not yet exist in Rome. Indeed, it originated neither in Rome, as Sohm, pp. 157–179, claims, nor in the West, but in the East. The first epistle of Clement, written from Rome at the end of the first century, does say that the apostles appointed the first converts as *episcopi* and *diaconi* and that it is a sin to dismiss them when they fulfill their ministry blamelessly, but it does not yet know the distinction between *episcopi* and *presbyteri* and uses both names interchangeably, chs. 42, 44, 47. By contrast, the monarchical episcopate developed very rapidly in the East. The letters of Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, who died as a martyr in Rome under Trajan around 110–115, are an unimpeachable witness to it, and would remain so even if they were inauthentic, since in that case they still could not be later than the years 130–140. As many as 13 times Ignatius speaks of *episcopi, presbyteri*, and deacons as three distinct officeholders. He sees in the *episcopus* one sent by Christ, *Eph.* 6; a χαρις θεου, *Magn.* 2; a likeness of God or of Christ, *Magn.* 7; *Trall.* 2, 3; *Smyrn.* 8; and in the interest of the unity of the church he continually insists that the members of the congregation unite themselves with the bishop according to God’s command, do nothing ecclesiastical apart from him, and most strictly avoid every heresy and schism. Nevertheless, in Ignatius the episcopal idea is still only at the beginning of its development. The bishop is no bearer of tradition, no priest of the New Testament, no successor of the apostles; he is always surrounded by the council of presbyters and deacons, as Christ by his apostles; he holds an office in a local church and has no authority beyond it. In the congregations of Asia Minor in Paul’s day, Acts 14:23; 20:17; Phil. 1:1; Titus 1:5, and also later, *Clem. 1 Cor.* 42, 4; 44, 2, 4, 6, not one but several *episcopi* stood at the head. According to 1 Tim. 4:14 they already formed a college, a *presbyterium*. And according to Rev. 1:20 f., one among them came so prominently to the foreground that he could be designated the ἀγγελος and regarded as the representative of the whole congregation. The development of the monarchical episcopate probably joined onto this. The presbyter charged with leading the assemblies and perhaps also, in distinction from all his colleagues, alone charged with the ministry of the Word, gradually came to be regarded as the bearer of a special office. He alone was *episcopus*, while all the others were only presbyters. In this way it could also be explained that Ignatius presupposes the monarchical episcopate as having existed for a long time; that Clement of Alexandria, in Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* III 23, 6, speaks of John as ὁπου μεν ἐπισκοπους καταστησων; and that this episcopate was soon introduced everywhere, around the middle of the second century. If people had not thought that in this they rested upon an apostolic tradition, its rapid spread would be almost inexplicable. The new element that confronts us in Ignatius consists, then, in his using the name *episcopus* exclusively for the one who at first was only *primus inter pares*; in his still always keeping this *episcopus* in connection with, but nevertheless already elevating him far above, the *presbyteri* and *diaconi*; in his repeatedly comparing him with God or with Christ; and in his demanding for him from the members of the congregation an almost unlimited obedience. The development of that episcopate continued in this direction. If there could be only one bishop in a congregation, it followed naturally that in a large congregation with many church buildings a certain precedence was acquired by the church to which the bishop was attached; that the rural congregations established from the cities became daughter churches of the mother congregation and a διοικησις (*dioecesis*, only since the 9th century; before that time παροικια) of the bishop; and that he alone could perform the proper ecclesiastical acts, such as the Eucharist, ordination, absolution, and so forth. With this the whole earlier relation was reversed in principle. In the apostolic period there were first congregations, assemblies of believers, in which the apostles appointed *episcopi* and *diaconi* who were chosen with the consent of the congregations and subject to their judgment. But now this was reversed: the true church, doctrine, baptism, Eucharist, and communion with God are where the bishop is, as Ignatius repeatedly says and Irenaeus, Cyprian, and others elaborate after him. The struggle against Gnosticism, which appealed to tradition and thereby sought to prove its right and truth, made it necessary to set the genuine apostolic tradition over against it. And this was found in the bishops as successors of the apostles and bearers of tradition. They had been appointed by the apostles in the congregations, had succeeded one another in regular succession, and were therefore bearers of the *charisma veritatis certum*, Irenaeus, *Adv. haer.* III 2, 2; 3, 1, 2; IV 26, 2; cf. *1 Clem.* 42, 2; 44, 2; Cyprian, *Ep.* 66, 4; 75, 16; *De unit.* 4. This entirely new conception of the episcopate as the continuation of the apostolate and of its inviolable authority in the church was completed by the introduction, from the second half of the second century, of the distinction between clergy and laity. *Clerus*, κληρος, lot, inheritance, possession, originally designated the congregation of Christ as God’s inheritance or possession, Deut. 4:20; 9:29; 1 Pet. 2:5; Ignatius, *Eph.* 11, 2; *Trall.* 12, 3; *Phil.* 5, 1. But gradually the use of this word was restricted and applied first only to the presbyters, Tertullian, *De monog.* 12; *De exhort. cast.* 7; Cyprian, *Ep.* 15, 1; then also to the deacons, Clement of Alexandria, *Quis dives* 42; Tertullian, *De fuga* 11, 12; Hippolytus, *Philos.* IX 12; and finally also to all the *ordines minores* (*acoluthi, exorcistae, lectores, ostiarii*) that arose at the beginning of the third century, Cyprian, *Ep.* 29; 34, 4; 59, 9. All these ministers of the church gradually became more and more independent of the congregation and were made officials of the bishop, and then together with him, as *clerus*, as *ecclesia docens*, were set over against the laity, the *ecclesia audiens*, who no longer had anything to say but only to listen and obey; cf. Cremer, s.v.; Harnack, *D. G.* I 383; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht* I 157–247. Thus arose the episcopal system of church government, which sees in the bishop the lawful successor of the apostles and the spiritual prince of believers. Various Christian churches are ordered according to that system: the Greek church and many other Eastern churches and sects, Hofmann, *Symboliek* §§ 44, 55, 62 f.; further, the Roman church, which, however, advanced from the episcopal to the papal system and was therefore continually opposed by the adherents of the pure episcopal system, such as the men of the reforming councils, the Gallicans, the Jansenists, the Febronians, and the Old Catholics, Council of Trent, sess. 23, ch. 4 and can. 6; Bellarmine, *De membris ecclesiae militantis*, *Controv.* II 2; Petavius, *De eccl. hierarchia*, in 5 books, *Theol. dogm.*, Paris, 1870, VIII 97–406; C. Pesch, *Prael. dogm.* I, prop. 33; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 1, p. 480; Simar, *Dogm.* 624; Vatican Council, sess. 4, preamble; and finally, the Anglican church, which in the earliest period through Cranmer, Pilkington, Whitgift, Usher, and others defended the episcopal system only as a permissible and useful *jus ecclesiasticum*, but later, especially after the archbishops Bancroft and Laud, as a *jus divinum*: Richard Hooker, *The Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity*, 1593, and so forth, reprinted several times, best edition by Keble, Oxford, 1845; Joseph Hall, *Episcopacy by Divine Right Asserted*, 1640; Makower, *Die Verfassung der Kirche von England*, Berlin, 1894; art. “Anglik. Kirche” in Herzog³.

The Roman church, however, did not stop with the episcopal system but developed it further into the papal system. Rome had already been the world city for centuries and immediately assumed an important place in the Christian church as well. Paul was indeed not the founder of the congregation there, but he greatly desired to see it and addressed to it his greatest and most important epistle, Rom. 1:9 f.; 15:22 f. Later he stayed there for some time, as did Peter, and both suffered martyrdom there. It soon became famous for its generosity and helpfulness toward other, weak congregations, Ignatius, *Rom.*; Eusebius, *H. E.* IV 23, 10; VII 5, 2. As appears from the first epistle of Clement, it exercised a motherly care for the congregation of Corinth. It became involved in all the great questions that arose in the second and third centuries through Gnosticism and Montanism and threw the greatest weight into the scale. There, around the middle of the second century, the first list of bishops was composed; there arose the idea of the succession of the bishops and of their apostolic dignity. Roman and catholic were connected with each other from the beginning and developed hand in hand, Harnack, *D. G.* I 400 f. The congregation of the world city became the center of the Christian church. The central significance Rome had in the pagan empire was transferred to the congregation and elevated it to the head of all Christendom. This primacy of the congregation at Rome, however, had at first no character in church law but only a moral-religious character. Rome was *prima inter pares*; all other congregations stood equal with it; all bishops held the same rank as the bishop of Rome. Irenaeus does indeed say in the famous passage, *Adv. haer.* III 3, that every church and all believers must agree with the church of Rome *propter potiorem principalitatem*, since in it the apostolic tradition has been preserved purely; but he nevertheless cites the church of Rome by way of example, ascribes *principalitas* to all churches founded by the apostles, and says only that Rome possesses a *potior principalitas* because it is the greatest, oldest, best-known church, founded by the apostles Paul and Peter. Nor does he speak a word about the primacy of Peter or of the bishop of Rome; he places all emphasis upon the church of Rome. Later he therefore also opposed the excommunication that Victor I had pronounced upon the Quartodecimans of Asia Minor, with the result that Victor had to revoke it, Eusebius, *H. E.* V 24. This opposition may have been regarded as a matter of discipline, but it nevertheless demonstrates the boldness and independence of Irenaeus and others over against the bishop of Rome. Tertullian likewise places all churches founded by the apostles—Ephesus, Corinth, Philippi, and so forth—on one level, although he also says that Carthage has its authority in Rome and that the apostles in Rome *totam doctrinam cum sanguine suo profuderunt*, *De praescr.* 36; cf. 20; *De virg. vel.* 2. In his Montanist period he opposed the edict of Callistus concerning the readmission of the fallen, mockingly called him *pontifex maximus, episcopus episcoporum*, and saw in this a far-reaching presumption, *De pudic.* 1, 13, 21. Cyprian too still occupies the same standpoint: all bishops are equal, partake of one and the same episcopal dignity, are as it were one bishop, stand at the head of the church, and must preserve among themselves *caritas animi, collegii honor, vinculum fidei et concordia sacerdotii*, *Ep.* 43, 5; 49, 2; 55, 24; 73, 26; *De unit. eccl.* 5. Therefore in the controversy over heretical baptism he still resisted Stephen, the bishop of Rome, *Ep.* 71–74. While preserving unity, each bishop is nevertheless independent to a certain degree and responsible to God alone, *Ep.* 72, 3. But the new conception of the episcopate mentioned above had by its nature to benefit Rome. The center of gravity had shifted from the congregation to the bishop. The latter was regarded as successor of the apostles, as bearer of the treasure of truth and of apostolic power. If this was so, what bishop could make more claims and assert more rights than the bishop of Rome? No church stood equal in power and prestige with that of Rome; it left all behind and surpassed them all—the churches of Palestine and Asia Minor, and soon also those of Antioch and Alexandria. And the bishops of Rome knew how to make use of their position and gradually proceeded to claim as a right what had at first been moral influence. Nevertheless, recognition of this right did not come so quickly. Tertullian still denied that Matt. 16:18 gave the bishop of Rome any power over other churches, since it contained only a promise to Peter, *De pudic.* 21. Cyprian indeed placed strong emphasis upon the unity of the church and also made it rest upon the identity of episcopal power, but in the *cathedra Petri* at Rome he nevertheless saw no more than a symbolic unity, *De unit.* 4. In canon 6 the Synod of Nicaea still placed the bishops of Rome, Alexandria, and Antioch on an equal footing, accorded to the latter two in their provinces the same ἐξουσια as the bishop of Rome already possessed in Italy, ἐπειδη και τῳ ἐν τῃ Ῥωμῃ ἐπισκοπῳ τουτο συνηθες ἐστιν, and maintained for these and also for still other churches the primacy due them, τα πρεσβεια or τα πρωτεια. After the development of episcopal power in the third century followed that of archiepiscopal or metropolitan dignity in the fourth century. Not Rome alone but many churches alongside it had a certain precedence or primacy in their provinces; in the course of the fourth century the bishops were subordinated to the archbishops. The East continually continued to resist the all-dominating primacy of the bishop of Rome. Alongside Alexandria and Antioch, Constantinople rose increasingly in ecclesiastical power from the middle of the fourth century. The Council of Constantinople in 381, canon 2, says that the bishop of Alexandria possesses ecclesiastical power only in Egypt, that Antioch retains the rights that belong to it according to Nicaea, and that the bishops of the East shall govern only the East. And after it has thus restricted the power of the bishop of Alexandria to Egypt, it adds in canon 3: τον Κονσταντινου πολεως ἐχειν τα πρεσβεια της τιμης μετα τον της Ῥωμης ἐπισκοπον, δια το εἰναι αὐτην νεαν Ῥωμην. After the bishop of Rome, it is not the bishop of Alexandria, although he has the oldest rights and the oldest documents, but the bishop of Constantinople who shall have the precedence of honor, not on the ground of any ecclesiastical or spiritual prerogative but solely for the political consideration that Constantinople is the new Rome. The West was left to the bishop of Rome, but the East refused to bow to him and came increasingly under the jurisdiction of Constantinople. The Council of Chalcedon in 451, canon 28, acknowledged the precedence, τα πρεσβεια, of old Rome because it was the imperial city, δια το βασιλευειν την πολιν ἐκεινην, but ascribed equal precedence, τα ἰσα πρεσβεια, to the holy see of new Rome. Despite the protests of Rome, Constantinople maintained its rights. The papal power of the bishop of Rome rested to a large degree upon the political prestige of the city; Constantinople, as the second Rome, could therefore advance these same claims. The bishop of Rome has therefore never been shepherd of all Christendom; he became the head only of Western, Latin Christendom. And this he became by right only in the fourth century. Already the Synod of Sardica in 343, later not recognized by the church, assigned to the bishop of Rome the decision whether, in case a bishop had been deposed by a synod, a new synod should or should not be convened. In the year 380 Emperor Theodosius issued the famous edict in which he commanded: *cunctos populos, quos clementiae nostrae regit temperamentum, in tali volumus religione versari, quam divinum Petrum apostolum tradidisse Romanis religio usque nunc ab ipso insinuata declarat*. Among the church fathers of the fourth and fifth centuries there is no longer any doubt that they regard communion with and submission to Rome as necessary for the essence of the church. From the church at Rome proceed all the rights of ecclesiastical communion: *inde enim in omnes venerandae communionis jura dimanant*, Ambrose, *Ep. cl.* 2, ep. 2. *Ecclesiae salus in summi sacerdotis dignitate pendet*, Jerome, *C. Lucif.* 9. With him rests the uncorrupted tradition of the fathers; he is *lux mundi, sal terrae, aurea vasa et argentea*, Jerome, *Ep.* 15 *ad Dam.* What is Roman is the standard of what is catholic; if Rufinus adheres to the faith of Rome, he is catholic: *si Romanam responderit, ergo catholici sumus*, Jerome, *C. Ruf.* 1, 4. When Innocent I had confirmed the decisions of the synods of Carthage and Mileve against Pelagianism and had condemned Pelagius and Coelestius, Augustine says, *causa finita est, utinam aliquando finiatur et error!* *Serm.* 132 *De script.* Leo I, 440–461, then clearly and consciously developed this primacy of the Roman see in various letters and elevated it to the rank and value of a religious dogma that possessed its scriptural foundation in Matt. 16:18; cf. Heinrich, *Dogm. Theol.* II 325 f.

Once the development had advanced this far, the question naturally had to arise as to what the pope owed so eminent a place and so high an authority. In the Old Catholic period, even after the introduction of the monarchical episcopate, the emphasis always fell upon the local church. The church at Rome had been founded by Peter and Paul and therefore had the purest tradition; in order to be Christian and catholic, all churches had to agree with it in faith, Irenaeus, *Adv. haer.* III 3. The bishop was therefore dependent upon his congregation; he was chosen by it and consulted with it in all weighty matters, especially in excommunication. Cyprian says expressly in his letters to the presbytery of Carthage that he wishes to do nothing *sine consilio vestro et sine consensu plebis*, *Ep.* 14, 4; 17, 1, 3; 19, 2; and so forth. In difficult cases the counsel and assistance of delegates from neighboring congregations were invoked, Acts 15:2; *Const. Apost.* ch. 1; *Clem. 1 Cor.* 63; Cyprian, *Ep.* 17, 3. The oldest ecclesiastical assemblies were congregational assemblies, at most assisted by delegates from neighboring congregations. But when the bishop was regarded as successor of the apostles and was distinguished from all precisely by this, he could no longer be chosen by the congregation or be dependent upon it. He had to receive his office from above in the way of succession and thus be appointed and ordained by a synod of bishops or by a chapter. He no longer had to consult with the congregation but was sovereign and determined everything alone, at most after consultation with the chapter that gradually developed from the presbyterate. And what thus occurred from the fourth century onward in the local or diocesan churches repeated itself on a larger scale in the universal church. In the course of time the papal system emerged from the episcopate, and the old congregational assemblies expanded into synods and councils, which at first were still composed of presbyters, deacons, and *lectores*, but subsequently only of bishops as representatives of the congregations. These synods did not regard themselves as infallible; in the fourth century a subsequent synod repeatedly nullified what the previous one had established. And from that time until the 9th century they also bore a political character, were imperial synods, and were convened, officially or unofficially led, and ratified by the emperor. But the pope increased in power. As bishop of Rome and archbishop of Italy he already had power to convene and lead provincial and national synods, just as other bishops possessed that right elsewhere. From the 12th century he succeeded in expanding these provincial and national synods, just as the bishop of Constantinople had already done with his συνοδοι ἐνδημουσαι for the Greek church, into ecumenical synods of the whole Western church. The ecumenical councils of Western Christendom thus developed from the Roman synods and were therefore convened, led, and ratified by the pope. The reforming councils in the 15th century did indeed attempt, under the influence of the humanist theory of the superiority of the people, to make themselves independent of the pope as assemblies of representatives of the whole church and to place themselves as infallible above him. But the pope succeeded in maintaining himself even against and above the ecumenical synods and therefore had to partake of a prerogative granted to no other bishop. Since from the days of Irenaeus onward agreement in faith with the church at Rome had already long been regarded as necessary for the essence of Christian churches because there, together with lawful succession, rested the *charisma veritatis certum*, it was gradually expressed ever more clearly that this *indefectibilitas* of the church of Rome had its ground in a special gift granted by the Holy Spirit to the bishop of Rome as successor of Peter. At first the emphasis was still placed upon the church at Rome: it could not fall away because it had been founded by the apostles Peter and Paul and was continually led by their lawful successors. Thus Irenaeus says of the *presbyteri* that they *cum episcopatus successione charisma veritatis certum secundum placitum Patris acceperunt*, *Adv. haer.* IV 26. But this provided no sufficient guarantee, especially when many churches, despite their apostolic foundation with their lawfully succeeding bishops, fell away and disappeared entirely. Therefore the ground for the *indefectibilitas* of the church at Rome was increasingly sought in the fact that at its head stood a bishop who, as successor of Peter—who himself occupied an entirely special place among the apostles—partook of a special gift and guidance of the Holy Spirit. Augustine derived the immovability of Peter’s faith from the intercession of Christ, Luke 22:32; *De corr. et gr.* 8. Ephraem Syrus said in his eulogy upon Peter, Paul, and Andrew: *lucerna Christus, candelabrum est Petrus, oleum autem subministratio S. Spiritus*. Leo the Great spoke in his letter to the bishops of the ecclesiastical province of Vienne of a *mirabile munus gratiae* by which the building of the eternal temple is confirmed upon the steadfastness of Peter, and declared elsewhere that the see of Peter *tanta divinitus soliditate munita est, ut eam neque haeretica unquam perrumpere pravitas, nec pagana potuerit superare perfidia*. Pope Hormisdas testified in his *libellus* of the year 516 that the truth of Christ’s promise to Peter is confirmed by the facts, *quia in sede apostolica immaculata est semper catholica servata religio*. Pope Agatho likewise declared in a letter to the emperors in the year 680 that through the grace of God and the protection of Christ the church at Rome had never departed from the way of truth and, by virtue of the promise of the Lord, never would depart from it. It was therefore no longer an innovation when Gregory VII declared in his *Dictatus Papae*, *Romana ecclesia nunquam erravit, nec in perpetuum Scriptura testante errabit*, and Boniface VIII decreed in the bull *Unam Sanctam* in 1302, *subesse Romano Pontifici omni humanae creaturae declaramus, definimus et pronunciamus esse de necessitate salutis*; cf. Heinrich, *Dogm. Theol.* II 357 f. The theologians agreed with this practice and theory of the popes. Some, such as Bede, Alcuin, Paschasius Radbertus, Damian, Anselm, Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dist. 24, and so forth, speak only incidentally and in a few words about the authority of the pope. This is still the case even with Thomas, *C. Gent.* IV 76; *S. Theol.* II 2, qu. 1, art. 10; qu. 11, art. 2; *Sent.* IV, dist. 7, qu. 3, art. 1; dist. 20, qu. 1, art. 4; and so forth (cf. Leitner, *Der h. Thomas v. Aq. über das unfehlbare Lehramt des Papstes*, 1872), and Bonaventure, *Breviloquium adjectis illustrationibus ex aliis operibus ejusdem S. Doct. depromptis opera et studio Ant. Mar. de Vicetia*, Freiburg, 1881, VI, ch. 12. But the various attempts undertaken from the 14th to the 16th century to reform the church brought Rome to self-consciousness. Papalism or curialism proper arose, clearly asserted the *plenitudo potestatis* and the infallibility of the pope, and frequently developed it to its most extreme consequences. Canus, *Loci theol.*, bk. 6; Bellarmine, *De summo pontifice*, in vol. I of his *Controv.*, pp. 188–255; Becanus, *Manuale Controv.* I, ch. 4; *Theol. Wirceb.*, ed. 3, Paris, 1880, I 267 sq.; Joseph de Maistre, *Du pape*, 1819; Perrone, *Prael. Theol.*, 1843, VIII 295–536; Heinrich, *Dogm. Theol.* II 163–476; Scheeben, I 220 f.; IV 397–458; Simar, *Dogm.* 598 f.; Jansen, *Prael. Theol.* I 512–658; Ermann, *De Paus*, Utrecht, 1899; and so forth. On 18 July 1870 the *constitutio dogmatica de ecclesia Christi* was adopted at the Vatican Council, and it was there determined: 1º that the *primatus jurisdictionis in universam Dei ecclesiam* was promised and entrusted immediately and directly by Christ to Peter; 2º that this primacy of Peter continues in the bishop of Rome as his successor; 3º that this primacy of the pope consists in *plena et suprema potestas jurisdictionis in universam ecclesiam, non solum in rebus, quae ad fidem et mores, sed etiam in iis, quae ad disciplinam et regimen ecclesiae per totum orbem diffusae pertinent*, so that he is *judex supremus fidelium*, possesses the highest decision in all matters touching the churches, is elevated above everyone’s judgment, and is subject to no council; 4º that this primacy also includes *suprema magisterii potestas*, so that the pope indeed receives no new revelations and only under the guidance of the Holy Spirit purely preserves and expounds the revelation handed down, but nevertheless does this in such a way that, when he speaks *ex cathedra* and as Shepherd and Teacher of all Christians defines a doctrine concerning faith or morals, he partakes of infallibility through divine assistance, of himself and not first as a consequence of the consent of the church. Cf. *Acta et decreta sacr. conc. rec. Collectio Lacensis* VII, Freiburg, 1890, pp. 262–498.
