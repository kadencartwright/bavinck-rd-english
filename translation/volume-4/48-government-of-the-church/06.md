## 6.

As the Roman hierarchy developed further, it elicited ever more serious resistance and opposition. In the Middle Ages various sects arose that rejected Rome as Babylon and the pope as the antichrist. And in the age of the Reformation this opposition spread throughout the whole of Western Christendom. In an understandable reaction many came to reject every church institution, or to see in it only a free, arbitrary creation of the congregation, or also tacitly to assign all government of the church to the Christian civil government; above, p. 20f. Even among the Lutherans the independent government of the church did not receive its due. Luther did originally proceed from the universal priesthood of believers and from the church as the communion of saints. But here too his standpoint was too anthropological for him to derive from the confession of Christ’s kingship a government proper to his church. To a certain extent he regarded the form of church government as an external, indifferent matter. If necessary, papal or episcopal government was acceptable to him, provided it placed no obstacle in the way of the proclamation of the gospel, *Art. Smalc.* II 4. The church becomes visible only in word and sacrament but not at all in any manner of organization or form of government. Christ rules in his church only through the preaching office. This conviction, joined to his conception of the civil government as *praecipuum membrum ecclesiae*, led Luther already in his work *An den christl. Adel deutscher Nation* of 1520 to summon the civil government to the work of reformation. In 1526 he even asked the elector of Saxony to undertake the work of visitation. On August 27, 1526, at Speyer the reformation was placed under the protection of the princes and estates. And from 1527 onward the government of the church rested in the hands of the territorial civil government. The *ordo ecclesiasticus* (the *pastores*) indeed retained the ministry of word and sacrament, and the *ordo oeconomicus* (the congregation) received the right of *consensus* and *approbatio*. But the *ordo politicus* (the civil government) received the whole *externa gubernatio*, that is, the right to appoint, maintain, and dismiss the *pastores*; to establish churches and schools; to regulate worship services; to reform doctrine; and so forth, and under Melanchthon’s inspiration exercised this power from 1529 onward through consistories. The grounds upon which the Lutherans assigned this extensive right over the church to the civil government were different. But whether the civil government was regarded as the bishops’ substitute, or it was supposed to have received this power tacitly from the church, or it was honored as the principal member of the church, it always came down to this, that the church was deprived of almost all government of its own. But all these systems of church government that arose during and after the Reformation in many Protestant churches do not answer to what Scripture teaches concerning it. For 1º however closely religious and civil life were connected under Israel, there was nevertheless a distinction even then. Alongside the kings were the priests, who occupied an independent position and were called to their own task. The church as the people of God became much more independent still in the days of the New Testament. For not only was it then detached from Israel’s national relations, but on the day of Pentecost it also received in the Holy Spirit an independent principle of life that grants it its own nature and an independent existence over against state and society. 2º The essence of the church consists in this, that it is an assembly of Christian believers. As such it is not and cannot be a human institution. It arises not from the will of the flesh or from the will of man but from birth from God. It is no product of human association or of the state’s pleasure, but in its origin and essence is a miracle, the fruit of a special, gracious activity of God and therefore also by its nature autonomous, independent, free over against all favor or disfavor of human beings. 3º From this alone it follows that the congregation must have a government of its own. It has a life of its own, bears within that life a distinctive law of life which God placed in it, and therefore naturally demands a free, independent expression for it. It is not correct to say that the manner of organization and form of government is an indifferent matter for Christ’s church. Essence and form, invisible and visible, inward and outward never stand so loosely and indifferently alongside or over against each other. It is true that the church comes to expression first of all in the ministry of word and sacrament, in purity of doctrine and life, but the government of the church does not stand outside all this but is very closely connected with it. A good government is needed precisely so that word and sacrament may be purely administered and doctrine and life ordered accordingly. The confession is the principal matter, but the church order is the means to maintain the confession. And just as an impure confession also falsifies the government, a bad government exerts a corrupting influence upon the confession. 4º Christ therefore granted his church a government of its own. He himself called, equipped, and ordained the apostles, who are the foundation of the church. And these apostles in turn, under his guidance, instituted the ordinary offices of overseers and deacons so that the congregations of Christ would not be deprived of government in their absence and after their death. These ordinary offices too have their origin in God, Acts 20:28; 1 Cor. 12:28; Eph. 4:11, and did not end in the apostolic period but were instituted to remain until the end of this dispensation, Acts 14:23; 1 Tim. 3; Titus 1:5. Scripture is no church order, but it nevertheless contains principles of church government which cannot be neglected without injury to spiritual life. 5º Therefore it is also not good to say that the congregation of Christ can itself give itself a government according to the demands of circumstances, or tacitly or expressly entrust it to the Christian civil government. For although it can be said in a certain sense that the congregation itself gives itself its government and establishes the institution of the church, since the apostles consulted the congregations in instituting the ordinary offices and the latter designated the persons for those offices, this is nevertheless the case only in a certain sense. It is always Christ who calls and equips for the offices. The congregations can and may designate the persons, but in doing so they are not independent and autonomous but bound to the Lord’s ordinances. In establishing the institution they may not proceed arbitrarily and according to their own insight, but in this too must ask what the Lord wills them to do. Therefore the congregations are also not free to abolish the offices or entrust government to the Christian civil authority. For although it is true that under a Christian civil government and in a Christian society there will increasingly come agreement and cooperation with the church in the standard, judgment, and maintenance of doctrine and life, nevertheless even then the tasks of church and state remain essentially distinct. The same sin is punished differently in church than in state. The discipline exercised by the former differs as heaven from earth from the punishment imposed by the latter. The care of the poor, oversight of the flock, ministry of word and sacrament, calling and election of ministers remain the inalienable right and costly duty of the congregation.

The Reformed perceived this, thanks to their deep awareness of the sovereignty of God. Whoever proceeds one-sidedly from God’s goodness or love or fatherhood does not arrive at it. But whoever places in the foreground not one of God’s attributes but all those attributes together and proceeds from God as God can do nothing other than place every creature beneath him in dependence and humility. God is sovereign, always and everywhere, in nature and grace, in creation and re-creation, in world and church. His ordinances and rights are the rule of our life, for the human being is his creature, subjected to him and obligated to absolute obedience. In the church this naturally led to the glorious confession of Christ’s kingship. For just as on account of sin God had instituted the civil government in civil life, so he anointed his Son as king over Zion, the mountain of his holiness, and gave him to the church as Head over all things, Ps. 2:6; Eph. 1:20; Phil. 2:9–11. Christ is not only prophet, who instructs through his word and example; not only priest, who reconciles through his sacrifice; but he is also king, who preserves and protects his own, who for that purpose is clothed with power in heaven and on earth and is king in a much truer sense than any earthly prince. He is this not only according to his divine but equally according to his human nature. The man Christ Jesus was exalted to the Father’s right hand. And he was all this not only from eternity and in the days of the Old Testament and during his stay on earth, but is all this still today and to the end of the ages. He is the same yesterday and today and forever. Indeed, in the state of exaltation he is it now in a much richer sense than he was in the state of humiliation and in the time preceding it. For he was indeed anointed king from eternity and exercised this office together with that of prophet and priest immediately after the fall and until the death of the cross. But on account of his humiliation God highly exalted him and gave him a name above every name. Through the resurrection he was ordained and appointed Son of God in power, became κυριος, received all power in heaven and on earth, and now reigns until he has completed the kingdom and placed all enemies beneath his feet, vol. III, 415f. This kingship of Christ divides into two. On the one hand it is a *regnum potentiae*, Ps. 2:8, 9; 72:8; 110:1–3; Matt. 28:18; 1 Cor. 15:27; Eph. 1:21; Phil. 2:9–11; Heb. 1:6; 1 Pet. 3:22; Rev. 17:14. In order that Christ may truly be king over his people who redeems, protects, and preserves them, he must have power in heaven and on earth, over Satan and world. It is a kingship of power, subordinate to and a means for his kingship of grace. It does not imply that the Father has relinquished the government of the world and that all authority in creation now descends from Christ and is exercised in his name. Rather, on the ground of his perfect obedience God granted the mediator Christ the right and power to gather his people from the world, protect them against all enemies, and completely subject those enemies themselves to him. God governs the world in such a way that Christ may demand the nations for his inheritance and the ends of the earth for his possession. In the exaltation the Father acknowledged and appointed his Son as a κληρονομος παντων, Heb. 1:2. But on the other hand Christ’s kingship is a *regnum gratiae*, Ps. 2:6; Isa. 9:5, 6; Jer. 30:9; Ezek. 37:24; Luke 1:33; John 18:33; Eph. 1:22; 4:15; 5:23; Col. 1:18; 2:19. And because this kingship bears a wholly different character than that of the princes of the earth, in the New Testament Christ is called Head much more than king of the church, vol. III, 421. For it is a kingdom of grace in which Christ reigns through his Word and Spirit. His Word comes to us from the past, binds us to the historical person and the work of Christ accomplished in time, and asks of us faith in the sense of *assensus, cognitio*. But the one who descended is the same who also ascended far above all the heavens, who is seated at God’s right hand and with his deity, majesty, grace, and Spirit dwells in us and nevermore departs from us. It is the living Christ, exalted at God’s right hand, who consciously and sovereignly gathers his church, conquers his enemies, and leads world history toward the day of his parousia. He is still always active in heaven as mediator and present on earth through his Spirit in church and office, in word and sacrament. The application of salvation too is his work. He is the acting one, and offices and ministries are nothing but means in his almighty hand. It is therefore absurd to think that he would have transferred the government of his church to some human being, to a bishop or pope, to an institution or sacrament. He is and remains the Lord from heaven, exalted as Head of the church precisely so that he himself might govern and fill all things, vol. III, 409, 449. This kingship of Christ was the *Materialprinzip* of Reformed church government. It was already expressed by Zwingli and developed and maintained by Calvin. It found a place in almost all confessional writings and from the 16th century until the present day was the driving force for opposing all human dominion in Christ’s church and for regaining and preserving its freedom and independence. Vitringa IX 125; etc.; cf. Rieker, *Grundsätze ref. Kirchenverf.*, Leipzig, 1899, p. 105 f.
