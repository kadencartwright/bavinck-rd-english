## 5.

The connection that exists between the sign and the thing signified was sometimes called *forma sacramenti* and sometimes also *unio sacramentalis*. Gomarus, *Theses theol.*, disp. 31 § 36, objected to the latter designation not without reason. The relation, he says, that exists between sign and thing signified and unites both in a certain sense, *nova et obscura phrasi, unio sacramentalis, Ubiquitariorum imitatione, non satis recta, vulgo nominatur. Quia res significata nobis verius unitur quam signis idque signis denotatur ac confirmatur*; cf. also Heidegger, *Corp. Theol.* XI 58. Roman Catholics and Lutherans can, to a certain extent, speak at the Supper of a *unio* between the *materia externa* and *interna* in the sacrament, for they teach that the thing signified enters into and is contained in the sign; they assume a physical, corporeal, local union. But even on their position there is an objection. For among the Roman Catholics the sign changes into the thing signified, and therefore no proper union is possible; and among the Lutherans the thing signified is indeed in, with, and under the sign, and thus brought together with the sign in the same place and space, but such a juxtaposition is still something entirely different from a union. Furthermore, the *unio sacramentalis* in the Word and baptism, and also in the other sacraments accepted by Rome, bears so different a character from that in the Supper that one cannot simply speak in general of a *unio sacramentalis*. On the Reformed position, however, there is a much greater objection. Scripture calls the sacraments signs and seals and thereby indicates the connection that exists between *materia interna* and *externa*. No one will call the connection between a word and the thing it denotes, between an image and the person it represents, or between a pledge and that of which it is the pledge, a union. And yet the relation that exists between sign and thing signified in the sacrament is of the same kind. It is not a physical, local, corporeal, substantial connection. The signs of water, bread, and wine are not *miracula, medicamenta*, ὀχηματα, *vehicula, canales, causae physicae* of the thing signified. Rather, it is an ethical connection, a relation entirely like that between Christ and the gospel, between the benefits of the covenant of grace and the Word of God that makes them known to us: Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 3, 8; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XIX 4, 3. With the Word, that connection to the things it describes is given of itself. But this is not the case with the signs in the sacrament. Water, bread, and wine are not by nature signs and seals of Christ and his benefits. No one could or might see that in them if God had not expressly declared it. This does not mean that these signs were chosen by God wholly arbitrarily from among visible things. On the contrary, now that God has told us so in his Word, we discover the most striking correspondence between sign and thing signified. Indeed, it is the same God and Father who rules in the kingdom of nature and the kingdom of grace. He created the visible in such a way that we can understand the invisible from it; the natural is an image of the spiritual. Yet a special Word of God was necessary in order to see in the signs of baptism and the Supper a depiction of the spiritual goods of salvation. And this was all the more necessary because water, bread, and wine do not merely depict grace but also seal it and thus serve in God’s hand to strengthen our faith. The *forma sacramenti* therefore consists in these two things: in the relation mentioned above between sign and thing signified (*forma interna*) and in the divine institution that through the Word establishes such a connection between the two: Bucanus, *Instit. theol.*, 561; Junius, *Theses theol.* 49, 8; Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* XVIII 19; M. Vitringa VI 400.

In accordance with their doctrine of the *unio sacramentalis*, Roman Catholics and Lutherans ascribe to the word of institution a different power than the Reformed do. Among them it must serve to change the sign into the thing signified or to bring the thing signified into the sign. It therefore has a consecratory and operative power, is directed more toward the element than toward the hearers, and for that reason is spoken in the Roman Catholic Church by the priest in a mysterious, whispering manner and in the Latin language: Bellarmine, *de sacr.* I 19. But among the Reformed the word of institution spoken by the minister possesses no hidden, mysterious, magical power. It does not serve, and need not serve, to incorporate the thing signified into the sign. Rather, it is a *verbum concionale seu praedicatum*, which is spoken aloud to the congregation, brings about no change whatever in the sign, but only sets it apart from common use in the consciousness of the hearers and gives it a special purpose *hic et nunc*: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14, 4; Turretinus, *Th. El.* XIX, qu. 6. Without that Word and outside its use, water, bread, and wine are nothing but ordinary, daily food. *Detrahe verbum et quid est aqua nisi aqua; accedit verbum ad elementum et fit sacramentum.* Therefore, even after he has spoken the word of institution, the minister holds nothing but the sign in his hand and distributes nothing but the sign to believers. God alone has bound himself, where the sacrament is administered according to his command, to grant the invisible grace himself through his Spirit. God, and he alone, remains the dispenser of grace; in the sacrament too the Christian depends not upon the minister but upon God alone and must expect everything from him. This dependence upon God alone is changed by Roman Catholics and Lutherans into a dependence upon the minister, and at Rome the latter is strengthened further by requiring in ministers, when they administer the sacrament, at least the intention to do *quod facit ecclesia*, *Trid.* VII, can. 11. In dogmatics this intention is weakened to an extreme degree. An *intentio generalis*, not to administer this sacrament but to administer a sacrament, is sufficient; and even that intention need not be *actualis*, for it is already sufficient as *virtualis*. It is not even necessary to have the intention to do what the Roman Catholic Church does. If a minister in the church at Geneva merely has the intention to do what the church he regards as true does, then he meets the requirement, and Rome recognizes the sacrament administered by him: Schwane, *D. G.* III 600; Bellarmine, *de sacr. in genere* I 27. Nevertheless, without further explanation, the requirement of intention remains in the Tridentine canons as an infallible pronouncement and keeps the serious Roman Catholic Christian in continual uncertainty. And when Calvin calls attention to this in his *Antidotum*, Bellarmine answers only that a person in this life does not need such an infallible certainty of his salvation, that a human, moral certainty is sufficient, and that this can be adequately obtained even if one depends upon the intention of the minister, *cum habere intentionem sit facillimum*, ibid., 28. Cf. M. Vitringa VI 458 sq.

Although the connection between sign and thing signified does not consist in a corporeal and local union of the two, it is nevertheless objective, real, and essential. Roman Catholics and Lutherans, however, have a different conception of reality than the Reformed. If the thing signified is not physically united with the sign, they think that the connection between the two is not essential and that Christ with his benefits is not truly communicated and enjoyed in the sacrament. Yet the difference in sacramental doctrine does not concern whether God actually communicates his grace, but the manner in which he does so. And the Reformed said: in a spiritual manner, because only in this way is grace truly communicated and can it be communicated. Physical communication of Christ and his benefits conflicts with the nature of the Christian religion, with the essence of grace, and with the nature of re-creation, and even if it were possible, it would still be of no benefit, John 6:63. But the spiritual manner in which Christ with his benefits is communicated in the sacrament is so little antithetical to true reality that it instead fully establishes and guarantees it. It is no different with the sacrament than with the Word. In the Word Christ is truly and essentially offered and given to everyone who believes. And just as really he is communicated to believers in the sacrament. The sacrament gives the same full Christ as the Word, and in the same manner—that is, spiritually through faith—even though the means differs, audible in the one case and visible in the other. Therefore, in Reformed theology the so-called *phraseologia sacramentalis* comes into its own just as well as in Lutheran and Roman Catholic theology. Scripture, because of the relation God has established between *signum* and *signatum*, sometimes designates the thing signified by the name of the sign, Rom. 2:29; or the sign by the name of the thing signified, Matt. 26:26; or also ascribes the property and effect of the thing signified to the sign, Acts 22:16; 1 Cor. 11:24. For this usage does not detract from the fact that according to Lutherans and Roman Catholics too, God is the proper dispenser and worker of grace in human hearts. And with this the Reformed agree wholeheartedly. Whether God communicates that grace in, with, and under the sign; through the sign as through a channel; or in connection with the sign does not affect the reality of that communication itself. In Scholastic and Roman Catholic theology too, there has always been disagreement about the manner in which the sacraments effect grace. The Thomist school ascribed a physical operation to the sacrament, the Scotist school a moral one. According to Thomas and his followers, God works through the sacrament in such a way that it itself physically causes grace in the recipient. According to Scotus, however, God had bound himself to cause grace to follow upon the performance of the sacramental action, without therefore conducting it through the sign as through a channel; he represented the sacrament as a promissory note upon which the recipient obtains grace from God. Rome therefore indeed teaches that the sacrament works *ex opere operato*, but leaves undecided the manner in which God, the *causa principaliter efficiens*, communicates grace through the sacrament as the *causa instrumentalis*; cf. Schwane, *D. G.* III 595; IV 363; Jansen, *Prael.* III 317. Although the Reformed therefore reject the doctrine that grace is conveyed to us through the sign as a channel, they have thereby in no respect detracted from the truthfulness of the sacrament. Indeed, by this they have maintained the spiritual nature of grace much better than Rome and Luther. For the rest, the manner in which God makes use of Word and sacrament in dispensing his grace remains a mystery. Scripture says of the Word of God too that it creates and re-creates, regenerates and renews, justifies and sanctifies. But who can describe the manner in which God makes use of the Word and likewise of the sacrament in doing so?
