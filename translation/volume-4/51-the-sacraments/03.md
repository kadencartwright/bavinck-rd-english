## 3.

Scripture does not know the word *sacrament* and even in the abstract contains no doctrine of the sacraments. It does indeed speak of different ecclesiastical acts in the Old and New Testaments, but does not bring them together under a common concept. And in the Christian church too, not the concept but the thing was first; different doctrines and practices of the church were gradually designated by the name sacrament. It is therefore understandable that very many had objections to this name and preferred to replace it with that of signs, seals, secret signs, mysteries, *Geheimnisse*. Not only Carlstadt, Zwingli, Socinus, Schleiermacher, Doedes, and others disapproved of the word; but Luther too said in his *Praeludium de captivitate Babylonica* that Scripture did not know the word in the meaning it had in theology. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14, 13, observed that the church fathers had placed a new sense into the Latin word. In the first edition of his *Loci*, Melanchthon replaced the word *sacramenta* with *signa*, and Musculus, Hottinger, Burman, Coccejus, and others also preferred the scriptural names signs and seals. This objection to the name is further strengthened by the fact that the Greek meaning of the word μυστηριον, translated into Latin by *sacramentum*, exerted influence upon the conception of the ecclesiastical solemnity designated by that name. Yet for all this the word is not objectionable. For theology makes use of many words that do not occur in Scripture and that have acquired a technical meaning within its domain. If theology had to abstain from them, it would have to cease all scientific labor, and all preaching and exposition of God’s Word—indeed, even every translation of Holy Scripture—would be impermissible. For that reason it is also not objectionable to have a treatment of the doctrine of the sacraments precede that of baptism and the Supper. For although no separate doctrine of the sacraments is to be found in Scripture and this doctrine must rather be constructed from what Scripture teaches concerning the particular institutions of circumcision, Passover, baptism, and the Supper, a preliminary chapter on the sacraments in general enables us precisely to bring together what those particular institutions in Scripture have in common and to set this correct, scriptural conception over against the impure doctrine that gradually entered the Christian church concerning the sacraments. In their definition of the sacraments, therefore, the Reformed aligned themselves as closely as possible with Scripture. Scholasticism disputed whether a definition of the sacraments could in fact be given, since, as composites of *res* and *verba*, they were no *ens reale*, no *unum per se*; cf. Bellarminus, *de sacr. in genere* I 10. Nevertheless, from Augustine, who repeatedly distinguished in the sacraments a visible and an invisible component, the definition was derived that they were a *sacrum signum* or *signum rei sacrae*: Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 60, arts. 1, 2; Hugh of St. Victor, *de sacr.* I 9; or also *invisibilis gratiae visibilis forma*, Lombard, *Sent.* IV 1. Although not incorrect, this definition is nevertheless too broad. Later Roman theologians therefore ordinarily adopted the definition of the *Catech. Rom.* II 1, 6, 2 and described the sacraments as *signa quaedam sensibus subjecta, quae ex Dei institutione sanctitatis et justitiae tum significandae tum efficiendae vim habent*. Although this definition can be understood correctly, in Roman theology it nevertheless acquired a sense that conflicts with Holy Scripture and therefore renders it unusable for the Reformation. For Roman theology understands the sacrament as a *res sacra, abdita atque occulta* and places in it the sense not of the biblical but of the Greek μυστηριον. And further, it places all the emphasis upon the sacraments’ containing grace within themselves, communicating it *ex opere operato*, and upon this grace consisting especially in the *gratia sanctificans*. Scripture, however, speaks of the rainbow and circumcision as אוֹת הַבְּרִית, Gen. 9:12, 13, 17; 17:11; cf. Ex. 12:13; Acts 7:8; and calls the latter a σημειον περιτομης, a σφραγις της δικαιοσυνης της πιστεως, Rom. 4:11. And it likewise connects baptism and the Supper most closely with the covenant of grace, with the mediator and benefits of that covenant, and specifically with the forgiveness of sins, Mark 1:4; 14:22–24; etc. In accordance with this, Reformed theology described the sacraments as sacred visible signs and seals, instituted by God, through which he causes believers to understand more clearly and assures them of the promises and benefits of the covenant of grace, and through which they on their part confess and confirm before God, angels, and human beings their faith and love. Here it deserves attention, first, that God is named as the institutor of the sacraments. In general there is no difference about this among the Christian churches. All confess that God alone can be the author, the institutor, the *causa efficiens* of the sacraments. For he alone is the possessor and distributor of all grace; he alone can determine to what means he wills to bind himself in the distribution of his grace. Further, Christ too as mediator has the right to institute sacraments, for as mediator he is the acquirer of all God’s grace. *Hominum non est instituere et formare Dei cultum, sed traditum a Deo recipere et custodire*, *Helv.* II 19. But Rome is in a peculiar difficulty here. Since no sacraments other than baptism and the Supper were instituted by Christ, it had to be claimed concerning the other sacraments either that they were not sacraments or that the apostles too had the right to institute sacraments. Before the Council of Trent many—Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dist. 3; Hugh of St. Victor, *de sacr.* II 15, 2; Halesius, Bonaventure, and others—maintained that the sacraments, for example confirmation and confession, were instituted not immediately by Christ but by the apostles, Schwane, *D. G.* III 597. But the Council of Trent, sess. 7, can. 1, expressly determined that all seven sacraments were instituted by Jesus Christ our Lord himself (not mediately, for all acknowledged that, in which case no conciliar decision would have been needed, but immediately, Bellarminus, *de sacr.* I, ch. 23), and thereby imposed an impossible obligation upon theology. Yet the council was right insofar as it acknowledged that the right to institute sacraments cannot be transferred by God even to creatures. Human beings can make known the institution of a sacrament, Ex. 12:1; Mark 1:4; 11:30; 1 Cor. 11:23; can distribute its sign; can announce the grace of God; but by the nature of the case they cannot *realiter* bestow this grace. For grace is no material good but is God’s favor and fellowship, inseparable from him and therefore communicable by no creature, human being, or angel. Therefore God in Christ through the Holy Spirit is the only institutor but also the only distributor of the sacrament. Only that sacrament is genuine which is administered by God himself. It is Christ himself who baptizes and holds the Supper in his church. He has not transferred his office or appointed a substitute on earth; he himself governs, and just as he alone as prophet ministers the Word, so he is also the only minister of the sacrament, although in this too he uses human beings as his instruments: *Helv.* II 19; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 43, 8; Turretinus, *Th. El.* XIX 1, 14; Amyraldus, *Theses Salm.* III 10; Vitringa VI 338. Second, it is also remarkable in the Reformed definition of the sacraments that they are described as signs. Although some Reformed also reckoned them, either generally or alternately, among the *ceremoniae, ritus*, or *actiones*—Bullinger, *Huisboek* V 6; Trelcatius Jr., *Loci Comm.*, p. 141; Junius, *Theses Theol.* 50, 6; etc.—by far the majority nevertheless reduced them to the generic concept of *signa, sigilla, imagines, symbola, typi, antitypi*; cf. Moor V 231; Vitringa VI 341. In this they departed partly from the Roman Catholics but especially from the Lutherans, who were particularly intent upon description by *actiones* and saw in it an important point of dispute with the Reformed, Gerhard, *Loc.* XVIII 22 sq. This is surprising from the Lutheran side, because with respect to the Word they teach that the power of the Holy Spirit lies enclosed within it, even before and outside its use. Analogy would require that the emphasis fall not upon the act but upon the sign in the sacrament. Yet this is not the case. The Lutherans see in the sacrament first of all an act consisting in the communication of grace in, with, and under the sign. Now the Reformed by no means denied that there was an act in the sacrament. But this was a hidden, invisible act of Christ, who inwardly bestows grace in the hearts through the Holy Spirit. By contrast, the chief thing in the sacrament does not lie in the act of the minister, as if it were of such importance and even brought about a con- or transubstantiation, but in the sacrament’s being a sign. It depicts and assures the act of Christ; indeed, the act of the minister of the sacrament, although an *actio*, is itself an *actio significativa*. Scripture therefore calls the sacraments by the name signs and seals and obliges Roman Catholics and Lutherans also to approve this designation: Bucanus, *Instit. theol.*, 559; Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* XVIII 8; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 3, 14; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XIX 3, 9; Vitringa VI 341. Third, the Reformed definition of the sacraments is further distinctive in that it unites with each other God’s act and the confession of believers observable in them. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14, 1, in this way reconciled Luther and Zwingli with each other. With Luther he agreed that God’s act occupied the first and principal place in the sacrament; but with Zwingli he judged that in the sacrament believers also made confession of their faith and love before God, angels, and human beings. In the sacrament God first comes to believers to signify and seal his benefits to them. With visible pledges he assures them that he is their God and the God of their seed; he affixes seals to *his* Word to strengthen *their* faith in that Word, Gen. 9:11–15; 17:11; Ex. 12:13; Mark 1:4; 16:16; Luke 22:19; Rom. 4:11; etc. But on the other hand, the sacraments are also acts of confession. In them believers confess their repentance, faith, obedience, communion with Christ, and communion with one another. While God assures them that he is their God, they solemnly attest that they are his children. Every use of the sacrament is a covenant renewal, a vow of fidelity, an oath obligating to the service of Christ, Mark 1:5; 16:16; Acts 2:41; 8:37; Rom. 6:3f.; 1 Cor. 10:16f.; cf. *Ned. Gel.* 36; forms for baptism and the Supper; Vitringa VI 423 sq.; Heppe, *Dogm. d. ref. K.*, 441.
