## 2.

The development of this doctrine of the sacraments shows that it had strayed farther and farther from Scripture. This is especially the case in three respects. First, the grace communicated by the sacrament is conceived by Rome only as a *gratia sanctificans*, that is, as a power infused into the human being, elevating him to the supernatural order and making him a participant in the divine nature. Grace is almost entirely detached from the guilt and forgiveness of sins and changed into a supernatural gift that comes to the human being from without. Second, the bond of the sacrament with the Word has been almost entirely broken by Rome. The Word does indeed have some significance, but only a preliminary and preparatory one. The faith worked by the Word is nothing but a historical faith that is insufficient for salvation and must be supplemented by love, that is, by the *gratia infusa*. And this *gratia* is communicated only through the sacrament, which therefore occupies its own independent place alongside the Word and far surpasses it in value. Third, faith is absolutely no longer a requirement in the recipient of the sacrament. Grace as *gratia sanctificans*, as something material, lies enclosed in the sacrament, is communicated by it *ex opere operato*, and therefore presupposes at most that the recipient place no insuperable obstacle in its way. Thus the sacrament works physically and magically by virtue of a power granted by God to the priest as an instrument in his hand. On all three points the Reformation revised and modified the Roman doctrine of the sacraments according to Scripture. Zwingli, Luther, and Calvin were in agreement here and jointly affirmed that the grace communicated in the sacrament was in the first place forgiving grace and concerned not the lower nature deprived of the *donum superadditum* but sin; that the sacrament was a sign and seal attached to the Word, communicated no grace whatever that was not granted through the Word, and thus had no value whatsoever without the Word; and that not the sacrament itself but certainly its operation and fruit depended upon faith and therefore always presupposed saving faith in the recipient. In other respects, however, significant differences soon manifested themselves among the Reformers in the doctrine of the sacraments. The sacraments became the center of conflict among Roman Catholics, Lutherans, Zwinglians, Reformed, Anabaptists, and others. They became the shibboleth of every dogmatic system. In them the principles from which people proceeded in church and theology, in doctrine and life, were embodied practically and concretely. The relation of God and world, creation and re-creation, the divine and human nature of Christ, sin and grace, spirit and matter came to practical application in the sacrament. The entire conflict revolved around the *unio sacramentalis*, the union of sign and signified reality, the connection between *sacramentum* and *res sacramenti*. At first, in 1518 and 1519, Luther placed all the emphasis upon faith, which alone makes the sacrament operative and thus gives us participation in Christ and his benefits. Then, from 1520 to 1524, he brought the sacrament especially into connection with the Word, of which it is a sign and seal. And finally, after 1524, out of fear of the Anabaptists he increasingly came to regard the sacraments as indispensable, to maintain their objective character on the ground of Christ’s institution, and to conceive the connection between sign and signified reality temporally, corporeally, and locally, so that according to the later Lutheran conception the *res coelestis* is hidden *in*, *with*, and *under* the element, just as the power of the Holy Spirit entered into the Word, and grace works through the sacraments as through its *instrumenta, media, adminicula, vehicula, organa*; Luther in Köstlin II 503 f.; Heppe, *Dogm. d. d. Prot.* III 39 f.; *Symb. Bücher*, ed. Müller, pp. 39, 41, 202, 264, 321; and so forth; Gerhard, *Loc.* XVIII; Quenstedt, IV 73–88; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.* § 53. Over against this Zwingli taught that, since the sacraments are administered only to those who possess faith and, through that faith, partake of Christ and all his benefits, they are in the first place signs and proofs of faith, acts of confession, and then in the second place also means for strengthening faith, since they remind us of the benefits to which our faith relates, direct our faith ever more away from ourselves toward God’s grace in Christ, and thereby exercise and strengthen that faith; Zwingli, *Fidei ratio* in Niemeyer, *Conf. Ref.* 24–26; *Expos. chr. fidei*, ib. 50–53; and further Zeller, *Das theol. System Zw.* 111 f. Now Calvin too does conceive the sacraments as acts of confession, as *mutua nostra erga Deum pietatis testificatio*. But in him they are such only in the second place. First of all, the sacraments are *divinae in nos gratiae testimonia externo signo confirmata*, signs and seals of God’s promises in his Word, mirrors in which we behold the riches of his grace. The invisible element, the *materia* and *substantia* of the sacrament, is thus the Word, the promise, the covenant of grace, the person of Christ with all his benefits. But the visible element does not contain these spiritual goods within itself; it does not grant them to us *propria, intrinseca virtute*. God does not relinquish his work to the signs in the sacrament; he and he alone is the possessor and also remains the sole distributor of grace. The signs perform only an instrumental, ministerial service; God makes use of them to communicate his grace. But he communicates this grace only to those who believe and then strengthens and nourishes their faith; unbelievers receive only the sign without the signified reality. How God now makes use of the sacraments in distributing his grace does not become clear in Calvin or in the later Reformed either. There therefore remains room for all kinds of questions. Is grace always connected with the sign, so that the sacrament objectively always remains the same? Or does God connect grace with the sign only when the sacrament is received by believers? Or does God also offer grace with the sign to unbelieving recipients, so that it is their fault if they accept and receive only the sign? In what is sacramental grace distinguished from the grace believers received earlier, and how does it differ from the subjective operation of the Holy Spirit that opens the eyes of believers to the sacrament and opens their hearts to it? Does the distribution of grace accompany the administration or the reception of the sign? Does it take place simultaneously with it, or can it also occur earlier or later, so that the fruit of the sacrament can be enjoyed before, during, and after its reception? Cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14; *Catech. Genev.* 5; *Cons. Tigur.*; Usteri, *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1884, pp. 417–455; *Conf. Gall.* 34, 37; *Belg.* 33; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 66, 67; and so forth; a Lasco, *Op.*, ed. Kuyper, I 115–232, 511–514; Bullinger, *Huysboek* V 6; Sohnius, *Op.* I 55; Beza, *Tract. Theol.* I 206; Junius, *Theses Theol.* 49, 50; Zanchius, *Op.* VIII 511; Polanus, *Synt. Theol.* VI 49–51; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 43; Turretin, *Theol. El.* XIX, qu. 1–10; Moor V 218–267; Vitringa VI 308; and so forth.

Outside the Lutheran and Reformed churches, which in imitation of Luther and Calvin maintained the objective character of the sacraments, Zwingli’s doctrine gained increasingly wide acceptance. The Anabaptists still accepted foot washing, baptism, and the Lord’s Supper as sacraments, but nevertheless saw in them only signs and symbols, not seals. The sacraments do visibly set before the eyes the benefits believers have received from God, but they do this as confessions of our faith and communicate no grace, Cloppenburg, *Op.* II 238. Like Zwingli, the Socinians disapproved of the name sacrament, regarded the Lord’s Supper as a memorial meal and a declaration of what we have in Christ, and denied that baptism rested upon a command of Christ and was a permanent institution, Fock, *Der Socin.* 559 f. In their confession, ch. 23, the Remonstrants did still confess that in the sacraments God displays his benefits and *certo modo exhibet atque obsignat*, but their *Apology* showed that by this they understood no sealing of God’s promise and no communication of his grace. The sacraments are signs of the covenant between God and human beings, in which the latter bind themselves to a holy life and God visibly sets his grace before their eyes; cf. Limborch, *Theol. Christ.* V 66. Among the Rationalists the sacraments became signs of remembrance and confession whose purpose was to promote virtue and whose number could therefore readily be increased by other solemn ceremonies, Wegscheider § 165. The Quakers rejected the sacraments as Jewish ceremonial, construed baptism as Spirit-baptism, which cleanses us from our sins, and the Lord’s Supper as a representation of the nourishment of our soul by Christ, Barclay, *Verantwoording*, 1757, p. 325. This process of dissolution was checked to this extent by Schleiermacher, who ventured an attempt to maintain the objective character of the sacraments and unite all the different conceptions in a higher unity. For this purpose he described them as *fortgesetzte Wirkungen Christi, in Handlungen der Kirche eingehüllt und mit ihnen auf das Innigste verbunden, durch welche er seine hohepriesterliche Tätigkeit auf die Einzelnen ausübt und die Lebensgemeinschaft zwischen ihm und uns, um derentwillen Gott allein die Einzelnen in Christo sieht, erhält und fortpflanzt*, *Chr. Gl.* § 143. But this attempt at reconciliation did not succeed. The *Wirkungen Christi, in Handlungen der Kirche eingehüllt*, left undecided what was first and principal in the sacraments. Thus theology soon diverged again in different directions. Some continued to occupy the mediating standpoint, Nitzsch, *Chr. Lehre* § 191; Kaftan, *Dogm.* 595; Van Oosterzee, *Dogm.* II 815. Others reproduced Zwingli’s view, Lipsius, *Dogm.* § 807 f., 840 f.; Schweizer, *Chr. Gl.* II 400 f.; Biedermann, *Chr. Dogm.* II 632; Ritschl, *Unterricht in der christl. Rel.* § 83; Scholten, *L. H. K.* II 310, or returned to the confessional standpoint of the Lutheran or Reformed churches, Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 2; Hodge, *Syst. Theol.* III 485. And finally, the Neo-Lutherans in Germany and the Zwinglians and Puseyites in England advanced a doctrine of the sacraments that in many respects recalled Rome, allowed grace through the sacrament to operate not only upon the soul but also upon the *geistleibliche* nature and upon the body of the human being, and frequently expanded the number of sacraments; Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe*, Erlangen, 1859, I 18–20; Hofmann, *Schriftbeweis* II 2, pp. 167 f.; Thomasius, *Christi Person u. Werk* II³ 355 f.; Delitzsch, *Bibl. Psych.*² 340, 350, 354; Martensen, *Dogm.* §§ 247–258; Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 226 f.; Paget, “Sacraments,” in Gore’s *Lux Mundi*, 1892, pp. 296–317.
