## 4.

The names sign and seal imply that the sacrament consists of two parts, which are distinguished as *verbum* and *elementum, res sacramenti* and *sacramentum* (in the narrower sense), *signatum* and *signum, res coelestis* and *res terrestris, materia interna* and *externa*. For both a sign and a seal point to something else of which they are the sign and seal. Signs are many and various. As Augustine, *de doctr. christ.* II 1, already observed, there are natural signs and positive, adopted, instituted signs. To the former belong, for example, smoke, which makes one think of fire; dawn, of the sun; a footprint, of the walker; fragrance, of the flower; laughter, of joy; a tear, of sorrow. Positive signs are those that have been fixed by agreement, convention, custom, or usage and adopted and acknowledged within a narrower or broader circle; to this kind belong all written characters, passwords, standards, insignia, and so forth. All these signs are ordinary, in turn distinguished from the extraordinary, among which miracles occupy the principal place. In Scripture these are frequently designated by the name σημεια, because they are a proof and sign of God’s presence, his grace or power, his truth or righteousness. Signs are also brought into different groups according as they recall something past, like memorials, Josh. 4:6; something future, like predictive signs, Gen. 4:15; or something present and abiding, like many other signs, Deut. 6:8. Now sacraments belong to the instituted, extraordinary signs, which are taken by God—not arbitrarily, but according to an analogy preformed by him—from visible things and used to designate and illuminate invisible and eternal goods. Besides being signs, the sacraments are also seals, serving for confirmation and strengthening. For seals are distinguished from signs in that they do not merely recall the invisible thing to our thought but also authenticate and ratify it for our consciousness. Since there is so much deception and falsehood in the world, all kinds of means are used to distinguish the true from the false, the genuine from the counterfeit. Thus a trademark serves to authenticate the genuineness of the product, a standard the accuracy of measures and weights, a coin the correct value of money, and a seal the integrity of documents. A seal is also repeatedly mentioned in Scripture when something must be authenticated as genuine and preserved from falsification. Letters of rulers, 1 Kings 21:8; Neh. 9:38; Esther 3:12, or of other persons, Jer. 32:10, and further laws, Isa. 8:16; books, Dan. 12:4; Rev. 22:10; Daniel’s lions’ den, Dan. 6:18; Jesus’ tomb, Matt. 27:66; and so forth, are sealed and thus protected from violation. God too has a seal, Rev. 7:2. He seals the stars when he hides them and covers them with clouds, Job 9:7. He seals the book of judgment so that no one but the Lamb alone can open and read it, Rev. 5:1. He seals the abyss in which Satan is shut so that the latter may no longer deceive, Rev. 20:3. He seals his servants in the final tribulation so that they may not be harmed, Rev. 7:3; 9:4. He seals all believers with the Holy Spirit so that as heirs they may be preserved for future salvation, 2 Cor. 1:22; Eph. 1:13; 4:30. He seals the Christ by all kinds of signs as the giver of the food that endures to eternal life, John 6:27. In the blessing upon his labor he gives Paul a seal, a confirmation of his apostleship, 1 Cor. 9:2. He impresses his seal upon the building of the congregation as a pledge that it is his possession, 2 Tim. 2:19. Seals are therefore always means to guarantee the genuineness of persons and things or also to preserve them from violation. Thus in the sign of circumcision Abraham too received a seal—that is, a confirmation, ratification, pledge—of the righteousness he had through faith, Rom. 4:11. Therefore, besides being signs, sacraments are also seals affixed by God to his Word in order to cause it to gain in credibility and reliability—naturally not in itself, for then it is firm enough as God’s Word, but for our consciousness. Yet in the sacraments the sign and seal are not only the visible elements of water, bread, and wine; the various ceremonial acts accompanying them also possess signifying and sealing power. The sprinkling or immersion at baptism; the blessing, breaking, distribution, and reception of the bread in the Supper are no arbitrary and indifferent customs, but also constitute the components of the sacraments, cause us to understand the promises and benefits of the covenant more clearly, and together with the elements form the sacraments into signs and seals of the invisible goods of salvation, Vitringa VI 352.

The *materia interna* in the sacrament, the invisible thing depicted and sealed in it, is the covenant of grace, Gen. 9:12, 13; 17:11; the righteousness of faith, Rom. 4:11; forgiveness of sins, Mark 1:4; Matt. 26:28; faith and repentance, Mark 1:4; 16:16; communion with Christ, with his death and resurrection, Rom. 6:3f.; with his flesh and blood, 1 Cor. 10:16; etc. In summary, therefore, one can say that Christ—the whole, rich, full Christ, according to his divine and human nature, with his person and work, in the state of his humiliation and that of his exaltation—is the *materia interna*, the *res coelestis*, the thing signified in the sacrament. For this Christ with all his benefits and blessings is the mediator of the covenant of grace, the head of the congregation, the yes and amen of all God’s promises, the content of his Word and testimony, the wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption of believers, the prophet, priest, and king in whom alone God communicates all his grace and who is the same yesterday and today and forever. Jesus Christ is the truth of the sacraments, without whom they would be nothing at all, *Ned. Gel.* 33; cf. *Conf. Belg.* 34, 35; *Cat. Heid.* 67; *Conf. Helv.* II 19; *Scot.* 21; *Westm.* 27, 1; Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14, 16, 17; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 43, 20, 21; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 3, 7; etc. By the *materia interna* of the sacraments Roman Catholics understand sanctifying grace—that is, the created grace added to nature and enabling one to do good works and one day to behold God—which does not differ essentially from uncreated grace, that is, from God himself, for properly in every created grace God gives himself, Heinrich-Gutberlet, *Dogm.* VIII 550f. And they teach that this grace has entered into and is contained in the sign as something objective, is thus communicated with it *ex opere operato*, and is obtainable in no other way—namely, through faith alone in God’s Word. *Conc. Trid.* VII, can. 4 sq. In the first period the Lutheran dogmaticians called the two components of the sacrament *verbum* and *elementum*, and thus had the sacrament communicate the same grace as the Word. But as a consequence of their doctrine of consubstantiation, they gradually came, especially since Gerhard, to assume in the sacrament a *materia coelestis* alongside the Word. Through the word of consecration, the element not only ceases to be an ordinary external element, as the older dogmaticians said, but also takes up into itself a particular divine power, which is distinguished from the Word, is designated as *materia coelestis*, and works through the element as its *medium* and *vehiculum*. Therefore a difference arose between the benefits of grace communicated through the Word and those communicated through the sacrament; for in the Supper Christ’s own flesh and blood were consumed. But because such a *materia coelestis* distinct from the benefits of salvation could be identified only in the Supper and not in baptism, Baier and others objected to speaking of a *materia coelestis* in connection with the sacraments *in genere*, Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.*, 391f. Yet more recent theology has repeatedly advanced a similar conception of the sacrament and distinguished Word and sacrament in such a way that the former exercises a *Personwirkung*, the latter a *Naturwirkung*; that the one is a means of the metanoetic operation of the Holy Spirit and the other a means of his anagennetic operation; that the Word changes consciousness, but the sacrament changes the spirit, the self, indeed, the *geistleibliche* nature of the human being. All this, however, conflicts with Scripture and is an introduction of Roman error. The sacrament grants no single benefit that is not also received from the Word of God through faith alone. For whoever believes is regenerated, John 1:12, 13; has eternal life, John 3:36; is justified, Rom. 3:28; 5:1; sanctified, John 15:3; Acts 15:9; glorified, Rom. 8:30; has communion with Christ, Eph. 3:17; with his flesh and blood, John 6:47f.; with his Father, 1 John 1:3; with the Holy Spirit, John 7:39; Gal. 3:2, 5; etc. The Word contains all God’s promises, and faith accepts them all. The content of the Word is Christ, the whole Christ, and he is also the content of the sacrament. There is no single benefit of grace withheld in the Word and now communicated to believers along a particular way, through the sacrament. There is neither a separate baptismal grace nor a separate grace of the Supper. The content of Word and sacrament is entirely the same; both contain the same mediator, the same covenant, the same benefits, the same salvation, the same communion with God. They are one even in *modus* and *instrumentum perceptionis*, for in the sacrament too Christ is enjoyed not bodily but spiritually, not by the mouth but by faith. They differ only in *forma externa*, in the *way* they offer us the *same* Christ. In a certain sense the Word too is a sign and seal: a sign that causes us to think of the thing it denotes; a seal that confirms what exists in reality. This applies generally to every word, but it applies especially to the Word of God; whoever denies it makes God a liar, 1 John 5:10. But the Word signifies and seals Christ to us through the sense of hearing; the sacrament signifies and seals Christ to us through the sense of sight. Together they offer us Christ with all his benefits along the way of the two higher senses God granted to the human being, without the other senses—smell, taste, and touch—being thereby entirely excluded. From this difference in the *way* they offer us Christ with his benefits it further follows that the sacrament is subordinate to the Word. It is a sign of the content of the Word; a seal God affixed to his testimony; a pillar, as Calvin says, erected upon the foundation of the Word; an appendix that comes alongside the gospel and is added to it. Thus the Word is something, and much, without the sacrament, but the sacrament without the Word is nothing and then has no value or power whatsoever. It is nothing less, but also nothing more, than a *verbum visibile*. All goods of salvation are obtainable from the Word and through faith alone, but there is no single benefit that could be received from the sacrament alone, without the Word and apart from faith. The Word therefore works and strengthens faith; it addresses both unbelievers and believers. But the sacrament says nothing and contains nothing for unbelievers; it is intended exclusively for believers and can only strengthen faith that is present, for it is nothing but a sign and seal of the Word. In this consists its sole, but nevertheless according to God’s intention its sufficient, value and power: to illuminate and confirm the Word and thereby strengthen faith. In the conflict against Rome, maintaining this agreement and this difference between Word and sacrament is of the highest importance. Whoever defines the two differently and ascribes to the sacrament a different operation of grace than to the Word divides Christ and his benefits, breaks the unity of the covenant of grace, materializes grace, makes the sacrament independent over against and above the Word, reverses the relation of Scripture and church, makes the sacrament necessary to salvation, and makes the human being dependent upon the priest. Therefore the Reformed, like the Lutherans in the first period, never tired of bringing this correct, scriptural relation between Word and sacrament to light again and again and continually arguing that the sacrament is subordinate to the Word and that both serve to point our faith to the sacrifice of Jesus Christ upon the cross as the only ground of our salvation. Gall.* 34; *Belg.* 33; *Cat. Heid.* 66; *Helv.* II 19; Polanus, *Synt.* VI 51; Mastricht, *Theol.* VIII 3, 11; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XIX 3, 6.
