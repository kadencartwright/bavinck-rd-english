# § 51. The Sacraments

## 1.

Alongside the Word, the sacrament comes as the second means of grace. Holy Scripture does not know this name and also has no doctrine of the sacraments in general. It does indeed speak of circumcision and Passover, of baptism and the Supper, but does not bring these institutions together under one concept. Nor did this yet happen in the first Christian congregations. Although we read in Acts 2:42 that the congregation at Jerusalem persevered in the apostles’ teaching, in fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers, and can infer from Acts 6:4 that there was a ministry of the Word, we nevertheless know little about the arrangement of the congregational assembly at Jerusalem. From 1 Cor. 11:1–14:40 we know somewhat more about the worship services of the Gentile believers. After the believers, upon the basis of personal confession, had through baptism into the name of Christ been received into the congregation, εἰς ἑν σωμα, Rom. 6:3–5; 1 Cor. 12:13; Gal. 3:27, they assembled regularly on the Lord’s Day, Acts 20:7; 1 Cor. 16:2; Rev. 1:10. Probably on that day they held two assemblies: one for the ministry of the Word, to which nonmembers also had free access, 1 Cor. 14:23, in which a portion of the Old Testament Scripture, and later also of the apostolic writings, was read, every member of the congregation was free to speak, 1 Cor. 14:26, and there were prayers and singing, Acts 2:42; Rom. 12:12; 1 Cor. 14:14, 15, 26; and another for the celebration of the Supper, εἰς το φαγειν, 1 Cor. 11:33, in which only believers were permitted to participate, 10:16f.; 11:20f., and in which there was first prayer and thanksgiving, then a common meal (ἀγαπη), Jude 12; 2 Pet. 2:13, from the gifts the believers brought together, afterward again prayer and thanksgiving (εὐχαριστια), and the celebration of the Supper. Jülicher, Spitta, Haupt, Hoffmann, Drews, and others indeed maintain that in the apostolic age the Supper was not distinguished from the ordinary meal but that the whole ἀγαπη was an εὐχαριστια, a δειπνον κυριακον, but this view is rightly rejected by Harnack, Zahn, Grafe, and others. Jesus instituted the Supper after the ordinary meal, Matt. 26:26; 1 Cor. 11:25, and Paul distinguishes the two in 1 Cor. 11:20, 21 and proposes to separate them entirely by having the ordinary meal eaten beforehand at home, 1 Cor. 11:22: Weizsäcker, *Das apost. Zeitalter*², p. 566f.; Moeller-von Schubert, *Kirchengesch.* I 96f., 130f.; Zahn, article “Agapen,” and Drews, article “Eucharistie,” in Herzog³; W. Schmidt, *Dogm.* II 466. But in the second century a great change gradually occurred here. For whatever reasons—cf. Drews in Herzog³ 5, 562—the celebration of the Supper was detached from the *agapae*, and the two assemblies, that for the ministry of the Word and that for the Supper, were united. From then onward the celebration of the Supper took place in the ordinary worship service after the ministry of the Word. The worship service was distinguished into two parts. At the first, the ministry of the Word, Gentiles too, or at least catechumens and penitents, were permitted to be present; but the second, the celebration of the Supper, was open only to the baptized and increasingly acquired a mysterious character through the addition of the baptismal confession, administration of baptism, *oratio dominica*, and all kinds of ritual and symbolic acts. The widely diffused Greek mystery cult exerted influence upon Christian religion. In the New Testament μυστηριον is the name for words and deeds of God that were formerly hidden but have now been disclosed, vol. I, § 30. But this word soon acquired an altogether different meaning in the Christian church and became the designation of everything in Christian religion that was mysterious and incomprehensible. In Latin this word was translated by *sacramentum*, which had the meaning of an oath, especially one to be sworn by a soldier to the standard, or of a sum of money that in a lawsuit had to be deposited in a *locus sacer* and, upon loss of the suit, was forfeited to the gods, but which now took up into itself the idea of a mysterious sacred act or thing. In this sense everything that stood in some relation to God and his revelation could be called a sacrament: revelation itself and its content, doctrine, the Trinity, the incarnation, and so forth; further, all kinds of signs, such as the sign of the cross and the salt given to catechumens; finally, all sacred acts—ordination to the priesthood, marriage, exorcism, Sabbath observance, circumcision, and all ceremonies. Cf., in addition to the works named in vol. I, 530f., Moeller-v. Schubert I 332f. and the literature cited there; Herzog², article “Sakrament”; Ziegert, “Ueber die Ansätze zu einer Mysterienlehre,” *Stud. u. Kr.*, 1894. Although among these sacred acts designated by the name sacrament baptism and the Supper still clearly stood in the foreground, the vagueness of the name nevertheless caused the number of sacraments to remain undetermined for a long time. Augustine too still uses the concept in a broader sense and proceeds from that meaning in his definition. Only Pseudo-Dionysius in the 6th century, in his *eccl. hier.* IV 2 sq., counted six sacraments: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, ordination to the priesthood, monastic consecration, and funeral rites. But in determining the concept of sacrament scholasticism aligned itself with Augustine and counted different sacred things and acts under it. Hugh of St. Victor, *de Sacr.* I 9, 7, names no fewer than 30 sacraments and divides them into three classes: those necessary to salvation (baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, and so forth); those that communicate a higher grace (the use of holy water and so forth); and those that serve as preparation for the other sacraments (consecration of sacred implements and so forth). Abelard counts five: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, extreme unction, and marriage, while Robert Pullen also names five but replaces the last two with confession and ordination to the priesthood. Bernard gives no number but in different places names baptism, foot washing, confirmation, Eucharist, confession, extreme unction, ordination to the priesthood, investiture, and marriage as sacraments. Only Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dist. 2, has the familiar seven, but even after him theologians and synods (for example, the Lateran Council of 1179) still speak of sacraments in the broad sense. And this continued until Lombard’s *Sententiae* became the general textbook for the study of theology and the Council of Florence in 1439 fixed the number at seven; cf. Schwane, *D. G.* III 584f.; Harnack, *D. G.* III 463. The definition of the concept went hand in hand with this restriction of the number. Many sacred acts, especially baptism and the Supper, were indeed highly exalted among the church fathers and conceived as supplied with a supernatural power and grace. But a doctrine of the sacraments is lacking; the relation of the sensory and spiritual elements is not clearly defined and the mode of operation not clearly described. Augustine distinguishes two components in the sacraments: *dicuntur sacramenta, quia in eis aliud videtur, aliud intellegitur. Quod videtur, speciem habet spiritalem*, *Serm.* 272; *detrahe verbum et quid est aqua nisi aqua? accedit verbum ad elementum et fit sacramentum*, *in Ev. Joh.* 80, 3. Sometimes he places so much emphasis upon the Word, and for the operation of the sacrament upon the faith of the recipient, that the sign becomes only an image of the thing signified. But on the other hand, he gave such a broad definition of the concept of sacrament that all kinds of ecclesiastical acts could fall under it, and against the Donatists he bound the sacraments so closely to the church that they could indeed be carried away by the heretics but could nevertheless communicate grace only within the church: Hahn, *Die Lehre v. d. Sakr.*, Breslau, 1864, p. 11f.; Harnack, *D. G.* III 141.

The proper doctrine of the sacraments is a product of medieval scholasticism. It was the first to institute a precise and frequently hair-splitting investigation into the concept, institution, minister, necessity, suitability, number, components, relation of the *sacramentum* and the *res sacramenti*, the distinction between the sacraments in paradise, the Old and the New Testament, the mutual difference of the seven sacraments, their physical or moral operation, the different grace they communicate, and the requirements for the distribution and reception of the sacraments: Hugh of St. Victor, *de sacramentis*; Lombard, *Sent.* IV 1, 2, and commentaries by Thomas, Bonaventure, and Duns Scotus; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qus. 60–65; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VI 1–5; Schwane, *D. G.* III 579–605; Harnack, *D. G.* III 466f. The result of this scholastic development was that, partly already at earlier councils but especially at Trent, the following was established concerning the doctrine of the sacraments in general. 1º All the sacraments of the New Covenant were instituted by Christ and are seven in number: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance, extreme unction, ordination to the priesthood, and marriage. 2º These are all true and proper sacraments, essentially distinguished from those of the Old Covenant but nevertheless differing from one another in value. 3º Although not all are necessary for every human being, they are necessary to salvation, so that without them, or at least without desire for them, the grace of justification cannot be obtained by faith alone. 4º They not only signify grace but also contain it and communicate it *ex opere operato*. 5º On the part of the minister it is required for the genuineness of the sacrament at minimum that he have the intention to do what the church does, but whether he is otherwise in mortal sin is immaterial. 6º The lawful distributors of the sacraments are only ordained priests, but confirmation and ordination to the priesthood are performed only by the bishop, and in a case of necessity baptism may also be administered by laity. 7º On the part of the recipients it is necessary only that they have the intention to receive what the church gives and place no obstacle in the way of grace. 8º Every sacrament grants a particular grace, and baptism, confirmation, and ordination grant a *character indelebilis*. Louvain, V 301–391; Simar, *Dogm.*³ 659–693; Oswald, *Die dogm. Lehre v. d. h. Sakr. der Kath. Kirche*² I 20–132; Möhler, *Symbolik*, § 28; Jansen, *Prael.* III 281–367; etc. On the doctrine of the sacraments in the Greek church compare Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Confessionskunde* I 393f.
