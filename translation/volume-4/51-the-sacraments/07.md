## 7.

The number of the sacraments is determined very differently, according as the concept of sacrament is taken in a narrower or broader sense. If one says with Augustine, *omne signum sacrum est sacramentum*, the number is greatly expanded. And if, with Calvin, sacraments are understood to include all the signs God ever gave to human beings to assure them of the truth of his promises, Holy Scripture gives us a whole series of sacraments, *Inst.* IV 14, 18. The Reformed accordingly enumerated many, especially when the doctrine of the covenants was later elaborated and every covenant and each covenant dispensation was thought to require the necessary number of sacraments. Thus some counted as sacraments in the covenant of works before the fall—although at that time one could not properly speak of means of *grace*—the Sabbath and paradise, the tree of knowledge, and the tree of life. And in the Old Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace, not only circumcision and Passover but frequently also the expulsion from paradise, the making of garments, Abel’s sacrifice, Noah’s bow, the passage through the Red Sea, the manna, the water from the rock, the bronze serpent, Aaron’s rod, Gideon’s fleece, Hezekiah’s sundial, and so forth, were regarded as sacraments, Polanus, *Synt. Theol.* VI 50–54; Witsius, *Oec. foed.* I 6, IV 7, 10; Moor V 258–267. Upon coming to the New Testament sacraments, however, the concept was immediately taken in a narrower sense and their number limited to two, although Calvin sometimes still called the laying on of hands a sacrament, *Inst.* IV 14, 20, and Luther and Melanchthon called absolution one, *Symb. B.*, ed. Müller, 173, 202. Rome, however, expanded the number of the sacraments to seven and introduced alongside them a large number of so-called *sacramentalia*. The distinction between the two consists in this: the sacraments were instituted by God, the sacramentals by the church; the former work by the power God granted them, the latter through the intercession and blessing of the church; the former directly effect the inward sanctification of the human being, the latter merely contribute to it by granting subordinate graces and preserving from temporal calamities; the sacraments are necessary by virtue of God’s command, while the sacramentals are commended by the church as useful and salutary. The sacramentals include objects such as churches, altars, priestly vestments, chalices, bells, water, oil, salt, bread, wine, palms, and so forth, which are consecrated by the church and set apart for religious use, and also the acts of exorcising and blessing that the church employs to withdraw things and persons from the malign influence of the devil and transfer them to the church’s sacred precincts. For Rome, creation is of a much lower order than re-creation; creation is nature, re-creation is grace, that is, *elevatio naturae*. The world bears a profane character and moreover stands under Satan’s influence. Everything, therefore, that passes from the world into the service of the church must be withdrawn from the devil’s power and consecrated and blessed for the service of God. Thus, while the sacramentals form the great enclosure that separates the church from the world, the sacraments are the means by which God inwardly sanctifies the members of the church, communicates supernatural grace, and makes them participants in his nature. They are the means for the redemption and elevation of the entire visible creation, which is represented in the four elements they use, namely, water, oil, bread, and wine; and they are seven in number because, by joining the number of the Godhead to the number of the world, they sanctify the whole creation through supernatural grace and consecrate it to the service of God. Baptism, instituted by Christ, Matt. 28:19, not only takes away all the guilt and punishment of sin but also delivers from the stain of sin, implants in the soul through regeneration the principle of grace and holiness, the germ of the new life, and thus makes the human being a living member of Christ’s mystical body and receives him into fellowship with the triune God. Just as Adam entered a higher world, the kingdom of grace, through the *donum superadditum*, so the baptized person is elevated to the state of supernatural holiness. But just as Adam had to preserve the grace bestowed upon him by his free will, so the Christian too must appropriate baptismal grace by his free will. The second sacrament, confirmation, serves to grant him the power to do so. Roman Catholics cannot prove that Christ instituted this sacrament and commanded the apostles to administer it, but they must nevertheless believe this because the church says so, and they therefore appeal to Acts 8:15; 19:6; Heb. 6:2, where only the extraordinary gifts of the Holy Spirit are in view, which were communicated by the apostles with the laying on of hands, as is clearly evident from Acts 8:18; 10:44, 45; cf. 1 Cor. 14:1, 15, 37. Besides the laying on of hands, confirmation further consists in anointing and in the bishop’s pronouncement of a formula that is entirely unknown to Holy Scripture and was only gradually introduced into the church. According to Rome, this sacrament grants baptized children, when they have come to the use of reason, the power of the Holy Spirit to preserve the life of grace received in baptism and steadfastly to confess the faith in word and deed. This power of the new life is nourished and strengthened by the third sacrament, called the sacrament of the altar or the Eucharist, in which Christ himself is present in his divine and human natures, offers himself for sins in an unbloody manner, and gives his true body and blood to the communicants for the nourishment of their souls. Because, however, the life of grace can be damaged and lost through all kinds of sin on account of human weakness, Christ instituted a fourth sacrament, that of penance, in order to restore or renew his sanctifying grace. For its institution by Christ, Rome appeals to the power Christ granted his apostles to forgive sins, Matt. 16:19; 18:18; John 20:22, 23. This commission of Christ is indeed certain, but it is nowhere said to bear the character of a sacrament. It lacks a sign, and Rome can say nothing other than that contrition, confession, and the disposition to make satisfaction are the sign in this sacrament of penance. At Rome, therefore, the sacrament of penance has become a tribunal in which the priest judges the sins confessed in the confessional according to the standard of the *libri poenitentiales* and, although absolving the sinner from guilt and eternal punishment, nevertheless makes him expiate those sins through all kinds of temporal punishments on earth or in purgatory, which can then in turn be remitted by indulgences. The sacrament of extreme unction serves not to heal the sick person, as the proof text cited, Jas. 5:14, would lead one to expect, but to prepare the dying person for death. The anointing with holy olive oil signifies the anointing of the Holy Spirit, the communication of grace, which frees the soul from its defects and grants the necessary power for the final struggle. To these five sacraments are then added the sacrament of ordination, which distinguishes the priest from the layperson by a ministerial gift of the Holy Spirit and grants him the power to change bread and wine into the body and blood of Christ in the Mass and to forgive the penitent sinner’s sins in Christ’s name; and the sacrament of marriage, which, according to the word of Eph. 5:25, makes the married state an image of the union between Christ and his church, to that end binds the married couple to one another not only by natural bonds but by supernatural grace, and grants them the power to persevere in mutual love until death and to raise their children in the fear of God. Cf. Lombard, *Sent.* IV; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 65 sq.; *Trid.* sess. 7, 13, 14, 21–24; *Catech. Rom.*, pars II; Bellarminus, *Controv.*, Tom. III; and so forth.

On the Protestant side, this set of seven sacraments has sometimes been excessively extolled, for example, by Leibniz, *Syst. der Theol.*, Mainz, 1825, p. 195f.; Goethe, *Aus meinem Leben* II 179; Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 227; Bilderdijk, *Opstellen* I 61; *Brieven* IV 68, 174, V 42; and so forth. Indeed, a tendency arose time and again in various quarters to expand the number of sacraments and ceremonies and to enrich the Protestant churches with Rome’s symbolic rite. Yet there is no reason to envy Rome’s concept and number of sacraments. With every appreciation for the beauty expressed in them, what is decisive in advance for the Protestant Christian is that scriptural proof is lacking for the five sacraments Rome added to baptism and the Lord’s Supper. Sometimes this is frankly acknowledged from the Roman Catholic side. Thus Deharbe, for example, says of confirmation, *Verklaring der Kath. Geloofs- en Zedeleer* IV 174, that we read nowhere that Christ instituted this sacrament or commanded his disciples to administer it. And the same applies equally to confession and extreme unction, marriage and priestly ordination. But apart from this, what does Rome achieve with all these sacraments? It appears that the treasury and distribution of grace at Rome are extraordinarily rich, but in reality the latter is so poor that each time only a small portion of sins and punishments is forgiven and can be forgiven, and each time a new sacrament is needed to communicate grace and grant release from punishments. Indeed, even if one has received baptism and the Lord’s Supper, penance and extreme unction, satisfaction in purgatory is still necessary after this life. Sins and punishments are so endlessly divided and split up by Rome that all the sacraments together, even with indulgences added to them, still cannot deliver completely from them. Over against this stands the fact that the saints can progress so far that they even earn beyond what is required and increase the *thesaurus meritorum*. But that is an exception. It is possible only for saints who, in addition to the *praecepta*, fulfill the *consilia* and lead an exclusively religious life, and it gives even them no certainty or comfort. The ordinary Roman Catholic Christian who lives in the midst of the world is in still greater uncertainty. Even after his death he continues to stand before God as before a Judge whom he must satisfy and still appease through all kinds of acts of penance. His state of grace is never sure and firm. He is always afraid that he may not truly be in the state of grace and may fall from it the next moment. And this uncertainty is not removed in the least by the fact that the sacraments work *ex opere operato*. For although baptism, confirmation, and ordination confer a *character indelebilis*, the sanctifying grace the sacraments communicate is always liable to be lost again; its reception in penance, communion, extreme unction, and marriage depends upon a contrite disposition; and even where it is granted, it does not deliver from all punishment. A *satisfactio operis* always remains necessary, even after this life in purgatory. And what kind of grace does the Roman Catholic Christian receive in the sacrament? Not the grace of forgiveness and adoption as God’s children, but a grace that is added to nature as a *donum superadditum*, that never becomes one with the human being, and therefore either drives him into the monastery or causes him to lead a dualistic life in the world. By contrast, the Word and the two sacraments instituted by Christ are sufficient for the Protestant Christian. In them, if he receives them in faith, he has the whole Christ, the full treasury of his merits, perfect righteousness and holiness, and unbreakable fellowship with God. He is freed from all guilt and released from all punishment. In baptism he is assured of this, and in the Lord’s Supper he is continually strengthened and confirmed in that faith. Thus he no longer needs a separate grace in confirmation, penance, and extreme unction, for through the Word, baptism, and the Lord’s Supper he receives all the grace he needs in life and death, for time and eternity. His only comfort is that he belongs to Christ; in that comfort he lives, and in that comfort he dies. Christ has accomplished everything for him. No penance or punishment is required of him, either in this life or in the life to come. And all this grace the Christian receives is so far from standing above nature or over against it that it instead renews and sanctifies everything natural. Therefore, when he marries, he needs no new sacramental grace, for marriage is holy by virtue of its origin and so does not need to be elevated above its institution but only restored and renewed in its natural order. Or if he desires the office of overseer, he is not incorporated into a special estate by sacramental grace but is called by God to a ministry in his church and equipped for it by the same grace of Christ. In baptism and the Lord’s Supper the Protestant Christian possesses infinitely more than the Roman Catholic does in his seven sacraments, for it is not the number of sacraments that decides but Christ’s institution and the fullness of grace he communicates in them. El.* XIX, qu. 31; Moor V 330 sq.; Gerhard, *Loc.* XIX 60 sq.; Hase, *Prot. Polemik*⁵ 414f.; Tschackert, *Evang. Polemik* 67f.; and so forth.
