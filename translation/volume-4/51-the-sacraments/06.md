## 6.

As the connection between sign and signified thing is conceived differently, so too the power and operation ascribed to the sacrament differ. Since at Rome the visible sign has taken up the invisible grace into itself, the sacrament works *ex opere operato*, without anything other or more being required in the recipient than the purely negative *non obicem ponere*, *Trid.* VII, cans. 6–8. Augustine had already sought the difference between the sacraments of the Old and New Covenants in this: *sacramenta novi testamenti dant salutem, sacramenta veteris testamenti promiserunt salvatorem*, *Enarr. in Ps.* 73, 2. Scholasticism elaborated this in such a way that the sacraments of the Old Covenant, because they prefigured the future Christ, had no power in themselves to communicate grace but worked only from and through faith. Under the Old Testament, therefore, everything depended upon the *opus operans*, that is, upon the believing subject who looked toward the future Christ. But with the sacraments of the New Testament it is different. Christ’s sacrifice has been brought, and therefore the sacraments now work *per se, propria virtute, ex opere operato*. This last expression, introduced into theology by William of Auxerre and Alexander of Hales, at first did not yet form an antithesis to the requirement of a certain disposition in the recipient of the sacrament—Thomas, for example, still says that *virtus sacramentorum praecipue est ex fide passionis Christi*, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 62, art. 5, ad 2—but signified only that the New Testament sacraments had received from Christ’s accomplished suffering the power *ad conferendum gratiam justificantem*, Thomas, ibid., art. 6. But maintaining this objective, causative character of the sacraments naturally led to the gradual weakening of the required disposition in the recipient and finally to its reduction to the negative *non obicem opponere*. Formerly this expression, which already occurs in Augustine, *Ep.* 98, 9, was understood to involve a positive disposition, a *bonus motus inferior*. But the New Testament sacraments worked *ex opere operato* and excluded all power and merit on the part of the recipient; a *bonus motus*, however, is meritorious according to Rome and therefore unnecessary for the sacrament. Indeed, even the seven *praeparationes* that precede baptism in adults still possess a *meritum de congruo* and are therefore superfluous for the sacrament. The sacrament works grace in everyone who does not deliberately harden himself, who places no positive obstacle in its way, who relates to it negatively and passively: Schwane, *D. G.* III 581; Harnack, *D. G.* III 479f.; Bellarminus, *de sacr. in genere* II 1 sq.; Simar, *Dogm.*, 686. The grace communicated through the sacraments is the *gratia habitualis, infusa, sanctificans*, whether it is bestowed for the first time, as in baptism; renewed after being lost, as in penance; or increased, as in the other sacraments. From this is further distinguished the *gratia sacramentalis*, which adds to the *gratia sanctificans* a *divinum auxilium ad consequendum finem sacramenti*, differs in the various sacraments, and enables one to attain that special goal proper to each particular sacrament, Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 62, art. 2. Finally, there are still three sacraments—baptism, confirmation, and ordination—which, besides this grace, further impress a *character indelebilis*, *Trid.* VII, ch. 9. The church fathers already said that baptism was a spiritual sign and seal impressed upon the baptized person; like a mark branded into a soldier’s hand, it always causes him to be recognized and continues to obligate him. Thus baptism too impresses a πνευματικη σφραγις, *nota, character*, which is never lost, Schwane, *D. G.* II 734. Scholasticism also brought this point of doctrine to further development. It described the *character* as a *habitus* or *virtus* impressed upon the soul and giving the human being right and power *ad cultum divinum*. For there were three sacraments that incorporated the human being into a different hierarchical estate and irrevocably caused him to be known as a member of it before God, angels, and human beings. Baptism incorporates into the *status fidei genitae*, separates believers from unbelievers, and conforms us to Christ as our spiritual head. Confirmation incorporates into the *status fidei roboratae*, separates the strong from the weak, and makes us soldiers under Christ as king. Ordination incorporates into the *status fidei multiplicatae*, separates priests from laity, and elevates them to conformity with Christ as high priest. It is a *signum dispositivum, distinguitivum, configurativum, obligativum* that dedicates the human being to God, obligates him to his service, and is never effaced, not even in the lost: Schwane, *D. G.* III 592; Thomas, *S. Th.* III, qu. 63; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VI 6; Bellarminus, *de sacr.* II 18–22.

This doctrine was unanimously rejected by the Reformation. For Scripture clearly taught that the sacraments are signs and seals of the covenant of grace, that they are intended only for believers and thus always presuppose faith, Mark 16:16; Acts 8:37, 38; 9:11, 17, 18; 10:34; Rom. 4:11; etc. Only when faith is present are they means in God’s hand to signify and seal the invisible goods of grace, Acts 2:38; 22:16; Eph. 5:26. Further, Scripture makes absolutely no distinction between the *gratia sanctificans* bestowed in the sacrament and a different, special *gratia sacramentalis*. For the grace sealed in the sacrament is no less, but also no other, than that bestowed through the Word in faith and which consists first in forgiving and thereafter also in sanctifying grace. Finally, in Scripture there is not a word about a *character indelebilis* impressed through baptism, confirmation, and ordination. The texts 2 Cor. 1:22; Eph. 1:13; 4:30, to which Bellarminus, loc. cit., ch. 22, appeals, do indeed deal with a sealing of believers by the Holy Spirit unto future salvation, but mention no sacrament to which that sealing is bound, nor a separate *habitus* or *virtus* in which that sealing would consist. Even though it is true that God holds everyone to account and judges according to the measure of grace granted him, no juridical belonging to the church of Rome or any other church is thereby impressed, Calvin, *Antid.* 7, 9. The Lutherans later departed from their original standpoint insofar as they had regeneration in children worked through baptism and had unbelievers in the Supper consume the flesh and blood corporally. But in this they nevertheless always maintained that in adults faith was absolutely necessary for a salutary reception of the sacrament. The Protestants were thereby confronted with the task of maintaining the objective, real character of the sacraments despite their operation depending upon faith. Among the Roman Catholics, and also among the Lutherans, this character appears better preserved because the operation of grace is incorporated into Word and sacrament. The Reformed, by contrast, apparently face a twofold difficulty. First, they teach that grace is distributed by God not *per verbum et sacramentum* but only *cum verbo et sacramento*. Second, they maintain that a *vocatio externa* is insufficient for salvation, that a calling by the Holy Spirit must accompany the calling by the Word, and thus that *per consequentiam* the sacrament by itself, without a particular operation of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers, does not fulfill its purpose. Yet by this the reality and objectivity of the sacrament are not in the least destroyed. For 1º the bond between sign and signified thing in the sacrament is no other, but also no less, than that existing between the Word of the gospel and the person of Christ. Whoever accepts the Word in faith truly obtains Christ as his portion according to God’s promise; and likewise, whoever receives the sacrament in faith receives in the same way and according to the same promise of God the whole Christ with all his benefits and goods. Whoever, by contrast, rejects the Word through unbelief thereby rejects Christ himself, even though he has heard the Word and even accepted it historically; and in the same way, whoever despises the sacrament thereby despises Christ himself. Although he receives the sign, he does not partake of the thing signified. One rule applies to both. Objectively the bond of Word and sacrament with Christ remains, for God himself established that bond. He said: if anyone receives my Word and my sacrament in faith, he shall not perish forever. 2º Roman Catholics and Lutherans assure to the sacrament no other or better reality than that which also belongs to it according to the Reformed confession. For nothing other, but also nothing less or more, is necessary to salvation than the whole Christ, who is offered in Word and sacrament and accepted by faith; and one can do nothing more terrible than reject that same Christ in and with Word and sacrament. Whether unbelievers then eat Christ’s own flesh and blood corporally and locally in the signs of bread and wine adds nothing to the objectivity of the sacrament, is wholly useless, and does not even serve to aggravate judgment, for moral rejection—that is, unbelief—is the great sin. The grace of God in Christ is spiritual in nature and can therefore be accepted only spiritually. 3º The Lutherans too do not dare abandon the requirement of faith in adults for the reception of the sacrament. In children, like the Roman Catholics, they have regeneration brought about through baptism, but children form a category of their own, which according to the Reformed confession is in no respect in a less favorable condition than according to the Lutheran and Roman confessions. In adults, however, faith is required. And even Roman Catholics require in that case that the recipient of the sacrament place no obstacle in its way. Thus, according to both, the sacrament does not work absolutely *ex opere operato*. There are cases in which the sacrament does not work—that is, bestows no grace—and nevertheless retains its objective character. Scholasticism still taught unanimously that *qui sine fide vel ficte accedunt, sacramentum, non rem, suscipiunt*, Lombard, *Sent.* IV, dist. 4. And Roman theology still deals with the question whether the sacrament, which was at first rejected through an *obex*, can still produce its operation later, Schwane, *D. G.* IV 371; Jansen, *Prael. Theol.* III 330. According to no confession does the operation of grace always accompany the sacrament at the same time. And nevertheless the objectivity and the bond between sign and signified thing are maintained. 4º Roman Catholics and Lutherans ultimately face the same difficulty as the Reformed—that is, the question when and in what case the sacraments serve for the salvation of recipients and communicate grace to them. The question is the same as the one that arose earlier with the calling through the gospel. There it was answered in this sense, that a *vocatio interna* had to accompany the *vocatio externa*. It is no different with the sacrament. The *unio sacramentalis* taught by Roman Catholics and Lutherans, however intimate, is nevertheless not by itself able to communicate grace, for then it would have to communicate grace always, everywhere, and in every case. In adults something must be added on the side of the subject; the *obex* must be removed and faith placed in its stead. Objectively the sacrament is indeed sufficient; in the sacrament the whole Christ is granted just as really as in the Word. But in the subject it is necessary that his understanding be illuminated and his will inclined so as truly to understand and accept the sacrament. If one says that unbelief is the subject’s fault, one speaks the truth but does not name the last and deepest cause of the difference observable in the use of the sacrament, just as in the hearing of the Word. And therefore the Reformed said that, although Christ is indeed objectively, truly, and earnestly offered to all users of the sacrament, as in the Word to all who hear it, subjectively an operation of the Holy Spirit was nevertheless necessary to enjoy the true power of the sacrament. *Non omnibus promiscue, sed electis Dei tantum, ad quos interior et efficax Spiritus operatio pervenit, prosunt signa*, *Cons. Tigur.* in Niemeyer, 209. 5º For believers, for those who receive and enjoy the sacraments in faith, they are signs and seals of the covenant of grace. In view of the many who receive the sacrament and nevertheless do not believe, a distinction between an inward and outward covenant was already made by Gomarus, *disp. de sacr.*, § 31; Essenius, *Comp. Theol. dogm.* XI 6; Dutch Annotations on 1 Cor. 7:14. And when the state of the church increasingly displayed a separation and antithesis between the two, this distinction occasioned repeated conflict. On the one hand, it was maintained that an outward covenant had existed in the Old Testament but that now there was only an inward covenant (C. Vitringa, Labadie, and others). On the other hand, many said that even now an outward covenant still existed, in which all who made profession were participants and had right and claim to the sacraments (Swarte, van Eerde, Janssonius). And between the two stood those who attempted in more or less successful ways to unite outward and inward covenant (Koelman, Appelius, Bachiene, Kessler); cf. Vitringa VI 361–398. In fact the older theologians and confessional writings know nothing of such a separation. Inward and outward covenant are no more two covenants than invisible and visible church are two churches. And the sacraments therefore cannot be only signs and seals of an outward covenant, to which even “unscandalous unregenerate persons” would have a right. They are not a confirmation only of the gospel’s declaration that whoever believes will be saved, but for believers they are seals of the whole covenant of grace, all its promises, the whole Christ, and all his benefits. They therefore assure not merely a general truth but are seals upon the promise: I am your God and the God of your seed. They present to us both that which God gives us to understand through his Word and that which he inwardly does in our hearts, making binding and firm the salvation he communicates to us, *Ned. Gel.* 33. They cause us to understand them better and seal to us that, on account of the one sacrifice of Christ accomplished on the cross, God grants us from grace forgiveness of sins and eternal life, *Cat. Heid.* 66f. This does not deny that those who receive the sacrament without faith might enjoy some temporal fruit from it, for God is rich in mercy and grants many benefits even to those who reject his Word and sacrament in unbelief; cf. vol. III, 491f. But the full, true fruit of the sacraments, like that of the Word, is only for believers. By them they are assured of their salvation.

From all this follows the value of the sacraments. They are not necessary in themselves, for God did not need to institute them; and his covenant and grace, his Word and promise, as those of a truthful God, are sufficiently firm without needing the confirmation of the sacraments, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 14, 3, 6. Nor are they absolutely necessary to salvation, for Scripture binds salvation only to faith, John 3:16; Mark 16:16. And although Roman Catholics and Lutherans apparently teach their absolute necessity and therefore defend emergency baptism, in practice they do not maintain this and say, for example, with Augustine, that in the case of the thief on the cross the baptism of blood replaces baptism with water. Not *privatio* but *contemptus sacramenti* makes one guilty before God. But nevertheless the sacraments have great value. Because we are not spirits but sensory, earthly creatures who can understand the spiritual only under sensory forms, God instituted the sacraments so that by seeing those signs we might acquire a better insight into his benefits, receive a more powerful confirmation of his promises, and thus be supported and strengthened in our faith. The sacraments do not work faith but strengthen it, as the wedding ring strengthens love. They infuse no physical grace but bestow the whole Christ, whom believers already possess through the Word; but they bestow that same Christ upon them in another way and along another path and strengthen faith. Further, they renew the believers’ covenant with God, strengthen them in the communion of Christ, bind them more closely to one another, separate them from the world, and attest to angels and human beings that they are God’s people, the congregation of Christ, the *communio sanctorum*, M. Vitringa VI 422, 437.
