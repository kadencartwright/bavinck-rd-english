# Chapter X. On the Means of Grace

# § 50. The Word

## 1.

All salvation and blessedness flow to fallen humanity from grace as an attribute of God. Objectively, that grace with all its benefits has appeared in Christ, who acquired and distributes it in the way of the covenant. The communion of those who partake of Christ with all his benefits bears the name church or congregation. The question now arises whether or not Christ makes use of means in communicating these benefits. The mystics are all inclined to answer this question in the negative. Entirely in agreement with their dualistic starting point, they cannot conceive grace as dependent upon or bound to external signs and actions. God himself alone—or the Christ within us, the Spirit, or the inward word or light—works grace in the human being, and word and sacrament can do nothing other than signify and portray that inward grace. The written word expresses what stands written in the heart of every believer, and the sacraments outwardly set before the eyes what Christ has inwardly granted through his Spirit; cf. Moor I 359 sq. Mysticism ultimately amounted to the same thing as rationalism, which, represented by Socinianism, Fock II 559 f., and Remonstrantism, *Conf.* and *Apol. Conf.*, ch. 23; Limborch, *Theol.* V, ch. 66, saw in the sacraments only *praecepta ceremonialia*, memorial signs, and acts of confession. Directly opposite it stands Romanism, which conceives grace as absolutely bound to means. According to Rome, after all, the church—the visible church borne by the invisible Spirit—is the proper, true, complete means of grace, the sacrament par excellence. In her Christ continues his divine-human life on earth, fulfills his prophetic, royal, and especially his priestly office, and communicates the fullness of his grace and truth. The church is Christ on earth, Christ as he, after completing his work of redemption, has entered into the development of the human race bound to space and time, Oswald, *Die dogm. Lehre v. d. h. Sakr. der Kath. Kirche* I² 8. And the grace Christ merited and communicated to his church serves above all to elevate the human being from the natural to the supernatural order. It is a *gratia elevans*, a supernatural physical power infused into the natural human being by the priest in the sacrament *ex opere operato*, vol. III 444. Just as the divine and human natures in Christ and the invisible Spirit and visible institution in the church are inseparably joined to each other, so too in the sacrament are spiritual grace and the visible sign. Outside Christ, outside the church, outside the priest, outside the sacrament, there is therefore no salvation. It is true that in the church Christ continues not only his priestly but also his prophetic and royal office. But the doctrine of Christ proclaimed in the church serves only to awaken faith, that is, assent, and the discipline maintained in the church serves only to cultivate obedience to the moral law. Faith and obedience, however, are neither of them grace itself but its preparation and its fruit, Oswald, op. cit., 10. The Word of God, which at Rome is moreover contained in Scripture and tradition and is conceived as a law, therefore has only a preparatory, pedagogical significance, and faith is merely one of the seven *praeparationes ad gratiam*, vol. III 442. The sacrament administered by the priest is the proper means of grace, through which *vera justitia vel incipit, vel coepta augetur, vel amissa reparatur*, *Trid.*, sess. 7, preamble. Between this mystical depreciation and magical overvaluation of the means of grace, the Reformation took its position and brought about a great change in the place and character of church, office, and means of grace. For according to the Reformation, Christ is the perfect Savior, the only mediator between God and humanity, and the church is first of all the *communio sanctorum*, not the mediatrix of salvation but the assembly of believers who live in communion with Christ. Christ did indeed institute offices in that congregation, but all those offices are not a *sacerdotium* but a *ministerium*, bound absolutely to Christ’s Word and possessing no other power than the power of that Word. The relation between Scripture and church is therefore entirely different in Protestantism than in Romanism, vol. I 367. At Rome the church precedes Scripture; the church is not built upon Scripture, but Scripture proceeded from the church; Scripture needs the church, but the church does not need Scripture. The Reformation, however, placed the church again upon the foundation of Scripture and Scripture again high above the church. Not the church but Scripture, the Word of God, became the preeminent means of grace. Even the sacrament was subordinated to the Word and without the Word had no significance and power whatever. Now that Word was indeed, according to Christ’s institution, administered by the teacher in the midst of the congregation. But that did not alter the fact that this Word was placed in everyone’s hands, that it was clear to everyone who searched it with a desire for salvation, and that it accomplished its work not only when publicly proclaimed but also when studied and read at home. Thus the Christian who received that Word not merely with historical faith but with trust of the heart was freed from priestly domination. No one and nothing stood between him and Christ; through faith he possessed the whole of salvation, and in the sacrament he received its sign and seal. Thus the Reformation modified the Roman doctrine of the means of grace. But on the other hand there threatened the danger of mysticism, which rejected the means of grace altogether and could advance all kinds of grounds for doing so. God’s omnipotence, after all, could not be bound by such external means. He was sovereign and free and could indeed use such means to distribute the treasures of grace, but did not need to do so. That grace was also no *materielles Etwas*, no physical power, no *donum superadditum*, no *elevatio naturae humanae*, but consisted principally in restoration to God’s favor, in the forgiveness of sins, and in renewal after his image. Therefore it could neither be enclosed in a sensible sign as in a vessel nor be distributed by the minister. Christ was and remained the only one who, after acquiring it, could also distribute it. He appointed no vicar on earth and installed no priest, but continued himself from heaven to exercise his prophetic, priestly, and royal office. He could entrust the sign to his minister, but he himself nevertheless remained the sole distributor of the thing signified. And was this not also evident in reality? Thousands received every day the sign of Word and sacrament without partaking of grace, and conversely thousands of little children of the covenant died every day who had never received a means of grace and whose salvation believers nevertheless, on the ground of God’s Word, were not permitted to doubt. Frightened by these considerations, the Lutherans partly retraced their steps and again bound grace absolutely to the means, introduced emergency baptism, regarded baptism as itself the washing away of sins, and denied faith to little children. And in the Reformed churches too, particularly the Anglican, this same Romanizing tendency repeatedly rose to prominence and dominion. But originally the Reformation adopted a different standpoint. God’s omnipotence and freedom could not be restricted. Even without external means he could glorify his grace in the hearts of sinners. If he made use of human beings and signs in doing so, this was to be ascribed only to his good pleasure, to his great love and grace. Zwingli therefore taught that God had even elected and regenerated heathens who had never heard the gospel and had led them to heavenly blessedness. And although the other Reformers did not go so far, they nevertheless had to concede, especially in the case of covenant children who died in infancy, that God could regenerate and save even without Word or sacrament, through the Holy Spirit alone, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 16, 17. 18. Yet they presented these cases as exceptions and maintained as the rule that for those who grew up, Word and sacrament were the ordinary means through which God gave his Spirit and communicated his grace. The working of regeneration and faith through the preaching of the Word is the *ordinaria Domini oeconomia et dispensatio, quam tenere in vocandis suis solet*, Calvin, IV 1, 5. 16, 19; cf. *Conf. Belg.* 24; *Cat. Heid.* 64; *Conf. Helv.* II 18; *Conf. Westm.*, chs. 10. 14; *Form. Conc. Sol. Decl.* 11, 27: *Dominus non solet homines immediate vocare*, Gerhard, *Loc.* XX 121; cf. above, p. 17. This answer is not satisfactory, since the number of children saved without means of grace is much greater than is generally supposed and cannot be recorded as an exception to the rule; and also because, in those who grow up, regeneration by the Holy Spirit, if it does not always precede baptism, the hearing of God’s Word, and faith, can certainly precede them. Hence among the Lutherans grace became increasingly bound to the means, and regeneration particularly to baptism; while among the Reformed, who understood the sacraments as signs and seals of grace granted, regeneration was conceived as preceding baptism, so that the means of grace served not to regenerate but to bring the regenerate to faith and conversion. Yet this later development too could not be acquitted of one-sidedness. The Lutheran development led back to Rome, and the Reformed ran the risk of regarding Word, sacrament, church, and office, indeed even the person and work of Christ, as superfluous for the acquisition and application of salvation and still necessary only for the outward manifestation of life and truth in the world. But in this way the significance of the means of grace is weakened and their concept far too severely restricted. For the means of grace of Word and sacrament do not stand independently on their own but are closely connected with church and office, with Christ’s person and work. One can indeed ask whether God could not regenerate and save without Christ and forgive sins without satisfaction. But such questions lead nowhere. We must rest in God’s good pleasure, which distributes salvation in no other way than in and through Christ. He is the mediator between God and humanity, the only name given under heaven among human beings for salvation. But further, it was equally God’s good pleasure to distribute salvation in no other way than through and in Christ’s congregation. Whether God, as Zwingli taught, also caused his electing grace to work among the heathens can be left undiscussed here, since in any case, according to the confession of all Christian churches, this counts as an exception. The rule is that God voluntarily binds the distribution of his grace to Christ’s congregation. The church is the communion and thereby also the mother of believers. God establishes his covenant with the parents and in them with their children. He distributes his benefits in the way of the covenant. In that sense it is correct that the church as the communion of saints is the great means of grace Christ uses according to his good pleasure to gather his elect from the beginning to the end of the world. The church in this sense most certainly intends the *salus electorum*. That is not her only reason for existence; she also serves for the perfecting of the saints, for building up the body of Christ, for preaching the gospel to all creatures, and for glorifying God. But she nevertheless exists also to form here on earth that holy circle within which Christ communicates all his benefits, including that of regeneration. In order that she might be capable of this, he communicated his Spirit to her, poured out all kinds of gifts in her, instituted the offices with her, and entrusted to her the ministry of Word and sacrament. And all these too are means Christ uses to bring in those given to him by the Father and lead them to heavenly blessedness. Indeed, the entire guidance of life, with its alternations of prosperity and adversity, is frequently in the hand of the Holy Spirit a means to bring the elect to Christ or into closer communion with him. The concept of means of grace can even be understood more broadly, so that it also includes what is needed on our part to enjoy the benefits of the covenant for the first time and continually, such as faith, conversion, struggle against sin, and prayer; cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV; *Helv.* II, art. 16; *Westm.* 14, 1; Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.* § 127. Now it certainly deserves no recommendation to include all this among the *media gratiae*. For Christ is not a means but mediator, acquirer, and applier of salvation. The church is not a means of grace alongside Word and sacrament, for all the power entrusted to her consists in nothing other than the ministry of both; church and office grant no grace in themselves but only through Word and sacrament. And faith, conversion, and prayer are fruits rather than means of grace; they are not objective institutions but subjective conditions for possessing and enjoying the other benefits of the covenant. In the strict sense, only Word and sacrament are to be regarded as means of grace, that is, as external, sensible actions and signs that Christ granted to his church and to which he bound the communication of his grace. Yet these may not for one moment be detached from the person and work of Christ, nor from the church as organism and as institution. Christ brings in his own in many ways, and he can do this because he alone, just as he is the acquirer, is and remains the distributor of grace. He therefore does it without or through Word and sacrament, but he nevertheless always does it through the inward calling of that Spirit whom he granted to the congregation; in the communion of that church which he charged to preach the gospel to all creatures; in the way of that covenant which received the gospel as its content and the sacrament as its sign and seal.
