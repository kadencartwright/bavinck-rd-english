## 3.

The word God uses to make known and accomplish his will in the moral and spiritual sphere must be distinguished as law and gospel. When Jesus appears on earth to announce the coming of the kingdom promised in the Old Testament, Mark 1:15, and to bring to tax collectors and sinners, to the poor and prisoners, the gospel of forgiveness and salvation, Matt. 5:1 f.; 11:5, 28–30; Luke 4:18, 19; 19:10; and so forth, he naturally comes into conflict with the Pharisaic, nomistic conception of religion that prevailed in his day. Yet although he rejects the human ordinances of the elders, Matt. 5:21 f.; 15:9, and although he has a different conception of murder, Matt. 5:21; adultery, 5:27; the oath, 5:33; fasting, 6:16; divorce, Matt. 19:9; and the Sabbath, Mark 2:27, he maintains the whole law, including its ceremonial components, Matt. 5:23, 24; 17:24–27; 23:2, 3, 23; Mark 1:44; 11:16. He expounds it in its spiritual sense, Matt. 5–7; emphasizes its ethical content; regards love for God and neighbor as its chief summary, Matt. 7:12; 9:13; 12:7; Mark 7:15; 12:28–34; and demands a different, more abundant righteousness than that of the Pharisees, Matt. 5:20. Although he is greater than the temple, Matt. 12:6, he himself also placed himself under the law, Matt. 3:15, and came to fulfill the law and the prophets, 5:17. And therefore he knows that, although he never insists upon the abolition of the law, his disciples are inwardly free from the law, Matt. 17:26; that his congregation is founded not upon the law but upon the confession of his messiahship, Matt. 16:18; that a new covenant is established in his blood, Matt. 26:28; that, in a word, the new wine also requires new wineskins, Matt. 9:17; and that the days of temple, people, and law are numbered, Mark 13:2. Jesus desires no revolutionary overthrow of the legal dispensation of the Old Covenant but a reformation and renewal born of itself from its complete fulfillment; cf. vol. III, 217. And this is also how it actually occurred. In the earliest period the congregation at Jerusalem still adhered to temple and law, Acts 2:46; 3:1; 10:14; 21:20; 22:12. But a new conception was being prepared. With the conversion of the Gentiles, the question of the significance of the Mosaic law came to the fore. And Paul was the first to understand fully that in the death of Christ the written record of the law had been erased, Col. 2:14. By νομος Paul, unless a further qualification indicates otherwise, for example, Rom. 3:27; Gal. 6:2, always understands the Mosaic law, the whole Torah, including the ceremonial commandments, Rom. 9:4; Gal. 2:12; 4:10; 5:3; Phil. 3:5, 6. And he regards that law not, as does the epistle to the Hebrews, as the imperfect, preparatory, Old Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace that disappears when the high priest and surety of the better covenant has come, but as revelation of God’s will, as a religious-ethical demand and requirement, as the ordering willed by God of the relation between himself and the human being. And of this law, so conceived, Paul now teaches that it is indeed holy and good and given by God, Rom. 2:18; 7:22, 25; 9:4; 2 Cor. 3:3, 7, but that instead of being able to grant righteousness, as the Pharisees claimed, it is powerless through the flesh, Rom. 8:3; arouses covetousness, 7:7, 8; increases the transgression, 5:20; Gal. 3:19; effects wrath, curse, and death, Rom. 4:15; 2 Cor. 3:6; Gal. 3:10; and entered in only for a time, for pedagogical reasons, Rom. 5:20; Gal. 3:19, 24; 4:2, 3. Therefore now this law too has reached its end in Christ, the seed of the promise, Rom. 10:4. The believer is free from the law, Gal. 4:26 f.; 5:1, because through Christ he is redeemed from the curse of the law, Gal. 3:13; 4:5, and partakes of the Spirit of adoption, the Spirit of freedom, Rom. 8:15; 2 Cor. 3:16, 17; Gal. 5:18. This freedom of faith, however, does not abolish the law but establishes it, Rom. 3:31, since its righteous requirement is fulfilled precisely in those who walk according to the Spirit, 8:4. For that Spirit renews believers so that they delight in God’s law according to the inward human being and discern what God’s holy will is, Rom. 7:22; 12:2; Eph. 5:10; Phil. 1:10, while through all kinds of motives—the great mercy of God, the example of Christ, the costly price for which they were bought, the communion of the Holy Spirit, and so forth—they are urged to do God’s will.
