## 2.

The first and principal means of grace is the Word of God. Lutherans and Reformed agree with each other in this. Yet the latter do not discuss the Word of God under the *media gratiae*, because in dogmatics they have ordinarily already treated it earlier in a separate chapter—Calvin, *Inst.* II 7–9; Musculus, *Loci Comm.*, §§ 11, 20; Junius, *Theses Theol.*, §§ 23, 24; *Synopsis pur. theol.*, disps. 18, 22—or also treat the law in connection with the covenant of works and the gospel in connection with the covenant of grace, Marck, *Med. Theol.*, chs. 11, 17. This distinctive method of treatment gives no right to the claim that the Reformed did not acknowledge the Word of God as a means of grace, for they repeatedly express the opposite; cf., for example, *Conf. Belg.* 24; *Cat. Heid.*, qu. 65. But one may indeed infer from it that for the Reformed the Word of God had a much richer significance than merely serving as a means of grace in the narrower sense of the term. The Word of God is also distinguished from the sacrament in that the latter serves only to strengthen faith and thus has a place only in the midst of the congregation. But the Word of God, both as law and as gospel, is revelation of the will of God, is the promulgation of the covenant of works and the covenant of grace, concerns all human beings and creatures, and possesses a universal significance. The sacrament can be administered only by the lawfully called minister in the assembly of believers, but the Word of God also has an existence and place outside it and even then exercises its manifold activity. As a means of grace in the proper sense alongside the sacrament, the Word of God comes into consideration only insofar as it is publicly preached by the teacher; all the emphasis then falls upon the Word *preached* in God’s name and by virtue of his commission. But as a rule, long before people hear that Word publicly proclaimed in the congregation, they have already come into contact with it in the household, at school, through address or reading. The public ministry of the Word therefore by no means comprises all the power proceeding from the Word. It does indeed serve to work faith in those who still lack it, but nevertheless much more to strengthen it among believers in their assembly. In a Christian society the Word of God comes to a human being in all kinds of ways, in all kinds of forms, from all kinds of directions, and it comes to him from his earliest youth onward. Indeed, in the inward calling God frequently brings that Word to the hearts of children already before consciousness has awakened, to regenerate and sanctify them, just as from the first moment of existence he writes the work of the law upon the heart of every human being and implants the *semen religionis* within him. Therefore a distinction must indeed be made here between the Word of God and Scripture. Not in the sense that the Word of God is merely found in Scripture and is not Scripture itself, but in this other sense: that the Word of God by no means always, and not even in most cases, comes to us as Scripture, in the form of Scripture; rather, having been taken up from Scripture into the consciousness of the congregation, from there it goes out again to the most diverse people in the form of admonition and address, upbringing and instruction, book and writing, tract and discourse, and performs its work. And God always stands behind that Word. It is he who causes it to go out to human beings in those various forms and thus calls them to conversion and life. Accordingly, in Scripture the expression *Word of God* is never identical with Scripture, although Scripture may without doubt be called God’s Word by us. A single passage may perhaps be identified where the expression *Word of God* is applied to a part of Holy Scripture, for example, to the written law. But otherwise *Word of God* in Scripture is never the same as Scripture, which is already impossible for the additional reason that Scripture was not yet complete at that time. The expression *Word of God* has many meanings in Scripture and can denote the power of God by which he creates and maintains the world, or his revelation to the prophets, or the content of revelation, or the gospel proclaimed by the apostles; cf. vol. I, 338. But it is always a Word *of God*, that is, never sound alone but power, not mere announcement but at the same time an accomplishment of his will, Isa. 55:11. Through the Word God creates and maintains the world, Gen. 1:3; Ps. 33:6; 148:5; Isa. 48:13; Rom. 4:17; 2 Cor. 4:6; Heb. 1:3; 11:3. By the Word Jesus calms the sea, Mark 4:39; heals the sick, Matt. 8:16; casts out demons, 9:6; raises the dead, Luke 7:14; 8:54; John 5:25, 28; 11:43; etc. Through the Word he also works in the moral and spiritual domain.
