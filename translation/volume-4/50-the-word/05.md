## 5.

Besides the relation between law and gospel, there is also an important difference in Christian theology concerning the power, the *efficacia*, of the Word of God and thus concerning the relation between Word and Spirit. Here too nomism and antinomism stand as the extremes on the left and the right. Nomism, which runs from Judaism through Pelagianism into more recent rationalism, is satisfied with an external calling, with an intellectual, moral, or aesthetic operation of the Word, and regards a special, supernatural power of the Holy Spirit in addition to it as superfluous; cf. vol. III 434f. 451f. Rome too clearly shows itself related to this tendency insofar as it weakens the *gratia praeveniens*, ascribes to faith only the preparatory significance of a historical assent, moves increasingly in the direction of Molinism and Congruism, and has proper supernatural grace and the indwelling of the Holy Spirit first communicated by the sacrament; cf. vol. III 439–444. The opposite extreme is occupied by antinomism, which first opposes the law and the Old Testament but then soon opposes every external word and every objective, historical mediation of salvation and expects everything from the operation of the Holy Spirit, from the Christ within us, from the inward word and the inward light. This tendency expressed itself most clearly on this point in the Anabaptism of Schwenckfeld, Franck, Denck, and others. The outward and inward word stand to each other as body and soul, death and life, earth and heaven, flesh and spirit, shell and kernel, dross and silver, image and truth, sheath and sword, lantern and light, manger and Christ, nature and God, creature and Creator. Knowledge of the Word therefore gives nothing in itself and leaves us cold and dead. To understand it, the inward word is already needed beforehand. Just as words can teach us only when we know the things, so Scripture teaches us something only when Christ already dwells inwardly in our heart. The Word is only a sign, a shadow, image, symbol, and merely expresses, indicates, and recalls what is inwardly written in our heart. The inward word therefore precedes and stands higher than Scripture, which is only a paper word and is moreover obscure and full of contradictions. And that inward word is nothing other than God or Christ or the Holy Spirit himself, who is one in all human beings and is the whole, full truth. Thus to find God and know the truth, we need not go outside ourselves to Scripture or the historical Christ. Rather, by descending into ourselves, withdrawing from the world, putting intellect and will to death, and passively awaiting inward, immediate revelation, we find God, live in his communion, and are blessed in the vision of him. Cf. above, p. 193, and further Cloppenburg, *Op.* II 200; Hoornbeek, *Summa Controv.*, bk. V; Episcopius, *Op.* I 527; Quenstedt, *Theol.* I 169; Maronier, *Het inwendig woord*, Amsterdam, 1890; A. Hegler, *Geist und Wort bei Seb. Franck*, Freiburg, 1892. In fact, this Anabaptism was a revival of pantheistic mysticism, which sees in the finite an eternally changing form of manifestation of the infinite and therefore seeks communion with God in the depth of feeling, where God and humanity are one. Over against both these tendencies of nomism and antinomism, the Reformers jointly maintained that the Word alone is insufficient to bring about faith and conversion, that the Holy Spirit can indeed work but ordinarily does not work without the Word, and that Word and Spirit therefore go together in the application of Christ’s salvation to the human being. At first there was no difference about this between Lutherans and Reformed. The former too taught that the Holy Spirit, though working through Word and sacraments as his instruments, nevertheless works and can work faith only through a special power and that he does this *ubi et quando visum est*, *Symb. B.*, ed. Müller, pp. 39. 455. 456. 471. 524. 601. 712. 720; cf. Luther in Köstlin II 494; Otto, *Geist u. Wort nach Luther*, Gött., 1898; Melanchthon, *Ex. ordin.*, *C. R.* 23, 15, 18, 37; *Loci*, ibid. 21, 761, 765; and others—Flacius, Chemnitz, Hunnius, Hutter, and Gerhard—in J. Müller, *Das Verhältniss zw. der Wirksamkeit des H. Geistes u. d. Gnadenmittel des göttl. Wortes*, *Dogm. Abh.*, 1870, p. 155 f. Yet there was some difference from the outset. While the Reformed usually represent the matter in such a way that the Holy Spirit accompanies the Word, *cum verbo*, the Lutherans prefer to express themselves in such a way, and place ever stronger emphasis on the fact, that the Holy Spirit works through the Word as his instrument, *per verbum*. And while the Reformed always distinguished between the ordinary and extraordinary manner in which God worked grace in the heart, the Lutherans, out of fear of the Anabaptists, increasingly omitted the extraordinary manner and said, *Deum nemini spiritum vel gratiam suam largiri nisi per verbum et cum verbo externo et praecedente*, *Smalc.* III 8, or, as Luther repeatedly said: *Deus interna non dat nisi per externa*. And when in 1621 the Danzig minister Hermann Rathmann, † 1628, published a work in which he taught that the Word alone had no power to convert the human being unless the Holy Spirit came to it with his grace, Herzog² 12, 506, almost all Lutheran theologians rose against him and developed as the genuine Lutheran doctrine that the Word of God possesses in itself the power of the Holy Spirit for conversion, that this power was placed in it by divine appointment and is so inseparably connected with it that it still inheres in the Word even *ante et extra omnem usum legitimum*, just as the human hand, even when it is not working, nevertheless always retains the *vis operandi*, Quenstedt, *Theol.* I 169; Hollaz, *Ex.* 992; Buddeus, *Inst. theol.*, 110; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.* § 51; Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 2, p. 29 f.

Now the Word that proceeds from God’s mouth is indeed always a power that accomplishes that for which God sends it. It is this in the natural sphere in creation and preservation, and it is this too in the moral and spiritual sphere in the work of re-creation. And this applies not only to the gospel but also to the law. Paul does indeed say of the Old Testament legal dispensation that το γραμμα ἀποκτεννει, 2 Cor. 3:6, but by this he expresses as strongly as possible precisely that it is no dead letter. Rather, it is so powerful that it works sin, wrath, curse, and death: ὁ νομος ὀργην κατεργαζεται, Rom. 4:15; it is δυναμις της ἁμαρτιας, 1 Cor. 15:56; διακονια της κατακρισεως, του θανατου, 2 Cor. 3:7, 9. And over against it now stands the gospel as a δυναμις θεου εἰς σωτηριαν, Rom. 1:16; 1 Cor. 1:18; 2:4, 5; 15:2; Eph. 1:13. Because it is not a human word but God’s Word, Acts 4:29; 1 Thess. 2:13, it is living and abiding, 1 Pet. 1:25; living and powerful, Heb. 4:12; spirit and life, John 6:63; a light shining in a dark place, 2 Pet. 1:19; a seed sown in hearts, Matt. 13:3, which grows and multiplies, Acts 12:24, and is of great value even though those who plant and water are nothing, 1 Cor. 3:7; a sharp two-edged sword that penetrates the innermost being of the human and judges all his thoughts and deliberations, Heb. 4:12. And therefore it is not empty and vain, but it works, ἐνεργειται, in those who believe, 1 Thess. 2:13. The works it brings about are regeneration, 1 Cor. 4:15; James 1:18; 1 Pet. 1:23; faith, Rom. 10:17; illumination, 2 Cor. 4:4–6; Eph. 3:9; 5:14; 1 Tim. 1:20; instruction, correction, consolation, and so forth, 1 Cor. 14:3; 2 Tim. 3:15. Even in those who perish, the gospel exercises its operation. To them it is for a fall, for a stumbling block and foolishness, for a stone against which they strike, for an aroma from death to death, Luke 2:34; Rom. 9:32; 1 Cor. 2:14; 2 Cor. 2:16; 1 Pet. 2:8. Over against spiritualism, this power of the Word of God, and particularly of the gospel, must be maintained with the Lutherans in its full, rich significance. The antithesis between the inward and outward, the spiritual and material, eternity and time, essence and form, and so forth, comes from a false philosophy and conflicts with Scripture. God is the Creator of heaven but also of earth, of both soul and body, of spirit and matter together. And therefore the Word too is no idle sound, empty sign, or cold symbol. Rather, every word, including a human word, is a power greater and more enduring than the power of the sword; thought, spirit, soul, and life reside in it. If this applies to the word in general, how much more to the Word that proceeds from God’s mouth and is spoken by him? That is a Word that creates and preserves, judges and kills, re-creates and renews, always accomplishes its work, and never returns empty. With a human word it makes a great difference whether it is written or printed, read or heard; and with the spoken word, form and delivery are in turn of the greatest significance. The power of the human word also depends on the measure in which someone has laid his heart and soul in it, on the distance that exists between the person and his word. But with God it is different. It is always his Word; he is always present with it; he constantly sustains it by his almighty and omnipresent power. It is always he himself who, in whatever form and through whatever means, brings it to human beings and calls them through it. Therefore, although the Word of God freely proclaimed by ministers or brought to human beings in admonition, address, book, or writing is indeed taken from Holy Scripture but is not identical with that Scripture, it is nevertheless a Word of God that comes to the human being from God, is spoken by the Holy Spirit, and therefore always accomplishes its work. The Word of God is never detached from God, from Christ, from the Holy Spirit; it has no existence in itself; it cannot be deistically separated from its creator and author. Just as Scripture was not inspired once by the Holy Spirit but is continually sustained, preserved, and made powerful by that Spirit, so it is also with the Word of God that, taken from Scripture, is preached to human beings in one way or another. Jesus spoke through the Spirit, John 6:63. The apostles, who received that Spirit, Matt. 10:20; Luke 12:12; 21:15; John 14:26; 15:26, proclaimed the gospel not only in words but also in power and in the Holy Spirit, 1 Thess. 1:5, 6, in demonstration of Spirit and power, 1 Cor. 2:4, and wielded it as the sword of the Spirit, Eph. 6:17. To that extent the Lutherans too are entirely right: the Word of God is always and everywhere a power of God, a sword of the Spirit; *semper huic verbo adest praesens Spiritus Sanctus*. Nevertheless, Scripture and experience teach that this Word of God does not always produce the same operation. In a certain sense it is always *efficax*; it is never powerless. If it does not raise up, it strikes down; if it is not for a resurrection, then it is for a fall; if not for an aroma of life, then for an aroma of death. The question therefore arises when the Word of God is *efficax* in the sense that it leads to faith and conversion. The Lutherans, in order to render the human being inexcusable, enclose this divine, supernatural *efficacia* in the Word, but thereby make no progress and, in order to explain the different outcome of the Word in human beings, must take refuge in free will. The Reformed, however, reckoned with the fact of that twofold outcome, regarded the *efficacia* not as an impersonal, magical power placed in the Word, but always thought of that Word in connection with its author, with the Christ who administers it through the Holy Spirit. And that Holy Spirit is not an unconscious power but a person who is always with the Word, always sustains it and makes it operative, but does not always make it operative in the same way. According to God’s unsearchable good pleasure, he uses that Word for conversion but also for hardening, for a resurrection but also for a fall. He always works through the Word but not always in the same way. And when he wills to work through it in such a way that it leads to faith and conversion, he need add nothing objectively to the Word. That Word is good and wise and holy, a Word of God, a Word of Christ, and the Holy Spirit takes everything from Christ. But for the seed of the Word to bear good fruit, it must fall into well-prepared soil. The field too must be made ready to receive the seed. This subjective activity of the Holy Spirit must therefore be added to the objective Word. By its nature it cannot be enclosed and contained in the Word; it is another, additional, subjective activity, not an activity *per verbum* but *cum verbo*: an opening of the heart, Acts 16:14; an inward revelation, Matt. 11:25; 16:17; Gal. 1:16; a drawing to Christ, John 6:44; an illumination of the understanding, Eph. 1:18; Col. 1:9–11; a working of willing and working, Phil. 2:13, and so forth; cf. vol. III 498. Thereby the Spirit is not detached or separated from the Word, not even when, as in little children, he works regeneration without any means of grace. For the Spirit who regenerates is not the Spirit of God in general but the Spirit of Christ, the Holy Spirit, the Spirit Christ acquired, through whom Christ reigns, who takes everything from Christ alone, and who was poured out by Christ into the congregation and is thus the Spirit of the congregation. Leaving aside whether the Holy Spirit sometimes also works and can work in heathens, which in any event is exceptional, as a rule he works regeneration only in those who live under the administration of the covenant. Little children regenerated by him too are children of the covenant, of that covenant which received the Word of God as its content and the sacrament as its sign and seal. The Holy Spirit therefore follows Christ in his course through history; he binds himself to the Word of Christ and works only in Christ’s name and according to his command. Individually and subjectively, when, for example, a child is conceived apart from its whole environment, from the church into which it is born, it may appear as if the Spirit worked without the Word. Objectively and materially, the Holy Spirit works only where the covenant of grace with the ministry of Word and sacrament has extended. And therefore regeneration in little children, when they grow up, is always recognized and proved genuine by its passing over into acts of faith and conversion and then joining itself to the Word of God that lies objectively before us in Holy Scripture. The Holy Spirit, who in regeneration applies nothing other than the Word, the power, and the merit of Christ, naturally also leads the conscious life toward that Word which he took from Christ and caused to be written by prophets and apostles; cf. vol. III 484. 504. 525, and further *Conf. Belg.* 24; *Heid. Cat.*, qu. 65. 67; *Helv.* II 18; *Can. Dordr.* III 6. V 7. 14; Calvin, passim, for example, *Inst.* III 2, 33. IV 14, 11; *Adv. Libert.*, ch. 9; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XV, 4, 23 sq.; Hodge, *Syst. Theol.* III 466–485; Bartlett, *The Letter and the Spirit*, Bampton Lectures, 1888; Müller, *Dogm. Abhandl.*, 127–277; Dorner, *Chr. Gl.* II 799; Frank, *Chr. Wahrheit* II 243.
