## 4.

This antithesis between law and gospel was, on the one hand, sharpened still further by antinomianism in its various forms of Gnosticism, Manichaeism, Paulicianism, Anabaptism, Hattemism, and so forth, and turned into an irreconcilable conflict. The whole Old Testament was derived from a lower God, from a wrathful, jealous, avenging God, and had now been replaced by the altogether different revelation of the God of love, the Father of Christ. On the other hand, the antithesis between law and gospel was weakened and erased by nomism in its various forms of Pelagianism, Semi-Pelagianism, Romanism, Socinianism, Rationalism, and so forth. Already by the church fathers and later by scholastic and Roman theologians, law and gospel were identified with Old and New Testament and were then regarded not as antithetically opposed to each other but as a lower and higher revelation of God’s will. Law and gospel do not differ in that the former only demands and the latter only promises, for both contain commands, threats, and promises; *mysteria, promissiones, praecepta*; *res credendae, sperandae*, and *faciendae*. Not only Moses, but Christ too was *legislator*. But in all this the gospel of the New Testament, the *lex nova*, far surpasses the law of the Old Testament, the *lex vetus*. The mysteries (Trinity, incarnation, satisfaction, and so forth) are revealed much more clearly in the New Testament; the promises are much richer in content and especially embrace spiritual and eternal goods; the laws are far more glorious and lighter, since ceremonial and civil laws have been abolished and replaced by a few ceremonies. Further, the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. The law was temporary and intended for one people; the gospel is eternal and must be brought to all peoples. The law was imperfect, a shadow and type; the gospel is perfect and the body of the goods itself. The law cultivated fear and servitude; the gospel awakens love and freedom. The law could not justify in the full sense; it gave no abundance of grace and bestowed no eternal salvation; but in the sacrament the gospel bestows the power of grace that enables one to fulfill God’s commandments and acquire eternal life. In a word, the law is the imperfect gospel, the gospel the perfect law; the gospel was contained in the law *as arbor in semine, as granum in spica*. Cf. vol. III, 197, and further Suicerus, s.v. νομος and εὐαγγελιον; Augustine, *de civ.* VIII 11; *In ev. Joh.*, tract. 30; *de spir. et litt.* 19, 20; Lombardus, *Sent.* III, dist. 25, 40; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 106–108; *Conc. Trid.* VI, cans. 19–21; Bellarminus, *de Justif.* IV, ch. 2 sq.; etc. Insofar as the Old and New Testament dispensations of the covenant of grace can, according to their conspicuous form and following Holy Scripture, be designated by the names law and gospel, the distinction Rome makes between them can be approved, though not in every part, nevertheless in the main. But Rome wholly identified Old and New Covenant with law and gospel, failed to recognize the gospel in the Old and the law in the New Testament, understood the whole doctrine proclaimed by Christ and the apostles as gospel, included in it not only promises but also laws and threats, and thus made the gospel into a second law. The Pauline antithesis between law and gospel was erased. For even when Paul understands by the law the entire Old Testament dispensation, he then regards it precisely in its *legal* form and as such sets it directly over against the gospel. And even when he does so, he acknowledges that the legal dispensation by no means annulled the promise already made to Abraham, Gal. 3:17, 21; that in the days of the Old Covenant too the gospel was proclaimed, Gal. 3:8; and that then too righteousness was obtained from and through faith, Rom. 4:11, 12; 11:32; Gal. 3:6, 7. Concerning the law as law, considered apart from the promise to which it was made subservient in the Old Testament, Paul asserts that it cannot justify, that it increases sin, that it is a ministry of condemnation, and that precisely thereby it prepares the fulfillment of the promise and makes necessary another righteousness—namely, the righteousness of God in Christ through faith. And this antithesis between law and gospel was again perceived by the Reformation. Statements that testify to a better insight do indeed occur among the church fathers; cf. quotations in Suicerus, loc. cit.; in *Bibliotheca studii theol. ex plerisque Doctorum prisci seculi monumentis collecta*, apud Is. Crispinum, 1565, pp. 195–216; Gerhard, *Loc.* XIV 16. But it never attains clarity, because they repeatedly confuse the distinction between law and gospel with that between Old and New Covenant. Yet the Reformers, while on the one hand upholding against the Anabaptists the unity of the covenant of grace in its two dispensations, on the other hand grasped the sharp contrast between law and gospel and thereby again restored the distinctive character of the Christian religion as a religion of grace. For although law and gospel in a broader sense can be used for the old and new dispensations of the covenant of grace, in their proper meaning they nevertheless denote two revelations of God’s will that differ essentially from each other. The law too is God’s will, Rom. 2:18, 20; holy and wise and good, spiritual, Rom. 7:12, 14; 12:10; giving life to the one who keeps it, Rom. 2:13; Gal. 3:12. But through sin it has become powerless; it does not justify but arouses desire, increases sin, works wrath, kills, curses, and condemns, Rom. 3:20; 4:15; 5:20; 7:5, 8, 9, 13; 2 Cor. 3:6f.; Gal. 3:10, 13, 19. Over against it stands the gospel of Christ, the εὐαγγελιον, which contains nothing less than the fulfillment of the Old Testament ἐπαγγελια, Mark 1:15; Acts 13:32; Eph. 3:6; which comes to us from God, Rom. 1:1, 2; 2 Cor. 11:7; has Christ as its content, Rom. 1:3; Eph. 3:6; and brings nothing other than grace, Acts 20:24; reconciliation, 2 Cor. 5:18; forgiveness, Rom. 4:3–8; righteousness, Rom. 3:21, 22; peace, Eph. 6:15; freedom, Gal. 5:13; life, Rom. 1:17; Phil. 2:16; etc. As demand and gift, command and promise, sin and grace, disease and healing, death and life, law and gospel here stand over against one another. Although they agree in this, that they both have God as author, both speak of one and the same perfect righteousness, and both address the human being to bring him to eternal life, they nevertheless differ in this: that the law proceeds from God’s holiness, the gospel from God’s grace; that the law is known by nature, the gospel only through special revelation; that the law demands perfect righteousness and the gospel bestows it; that the law leads through works to eternal life and the gospel causes works to proceed from the eternal life bestowed in faith; that the law now condemns the human being and the gospel acquits him; that the law addresses all human beings and the gospel only those who live under it; and so forth. In connection with this distinction disagreement even arose over whether the preaching of faith and repentance, which nevertheless appeared to be a condition and demand, belonged to the gospel or should rather, with Flacius, Gerhard, Quenstedt, Voetius, Witsius, Coccejus, Moor, and others, be reckoned to the law. And indeed, in the strictest sense there are no demands and conditions in the gospel, but only promises and gifts; faith and repentance are benefits of the covenant of grace just as much as justification and so forth. But concretely the gospel never occurs in this way. In practice it is always joined with the law and throughout the whole of Scripture is always interwoven with the law. The gospel always presupposes the law and also needs it in its ministry. For it is brought to rational and moral human beings who are personally responsible to God and therefore must be called to faith and repentance. The demanding, calling form in which the gospel appears is borrowed from the law. Every human being is obligated not first by the gospel but by nature through the law to believe God upon his Word, and thus also to accept the gospel in which he speaks to the human being. Therefore the gospel immediately lays claim to all human beings and binds them in their conscience, for the God who speaks in the gospel is no other than the one who made himself known to them in his law. Faith and repentance are therefore demanded of the human being in the name of God’s law, by virtue of the relation in which the human being as a rational creature stands to God; and that demand is addressed not only to the elect and regenerate but to all human beings without distinction. Yet they themselves are nevertheless content of the gospel, not operations or fruits of the law. For the law indeed demands faith in God in general, but not that special faith which directs itself to Christ; and the law can indeed work μεταμελεια, *poenitentia*, but not μετανοια, *resipiscentia*, which is rather a fruit of faith. And precisely because faith and repentance, although the human being is obligated to them by nature through the law, are content of the gospel, there can be a law, a command, an obedience of faith, Rom. 1:5; 3:27; 1 John 3:23; and there can be disobedience to and judgment according to the gospel, Rom. 2:16; 10:16; etc. Law and gospel, considered *in concreto*, differ not so much in that the law always appears in commanding form and the gospel in promising form, for the law too has promises and the gospel admonitions and obligations. Rather, they differ especially in content: the law demands that the human being work out his own righteousness, and the gospel invites him to renounce all self-righteousness and accept the righteousness of Christ, and even bestows for this purpose the gift of faith. And law and gospel stand in this relation not only before and at the beginning of conversion; they continue to stand in this relation throughout the whole Christian life, even unto death. The Lutherans have eyes almost exclusively for the accusing, condemning operation of the law and therefore know no higher blessedness than liberation from the law. The law is necessary only because of sin. In the state of perfection there is no law. God is free from the law; Christ was by no means subject to the law for himself; the believer no longer stands under the law. The Lutherans do indeed speak of a threefold *usus* of the law: not only a *usus politicus (civilis)* to restrain sin and a *usus paedagogicus* to awaken knowledge of sin, but also a *usus didacticus* to be a rule of life for believers. But this last *usus* is nevertheless necessary solely because, and insofar as, believers still remain sinners and must be restrained by the law and led to continual knowledge of sin. In itself, with faith and grace the law ceases and loses all its significance. But the Reformed thought altogether differently about it. The *usus politicus* and the *usus paedagogicus* of the law became accidentally necessary only through sin. Even when these fall away, the principal *usus*, the *usus didacticus, normativus*, remains. For the law is an expression of God’s being; Christ as a human being was naturally subject to the law for himself; Adam before the fall had the law written in his heart; in the believer it is again engraved upon the tablets of his heart by the Holy Spirit; and in heaven all will walk according to the law of the Lord. The gospel is temporary, but the law is eternal and is restored precisely through the gospel. Freedom from the law therefore does not consist in the Christian having nothing more to do with that law, but lies in this, that the law can no longer demand anything from the Christian as a condition of salvation and can no longer condemn and damn him. Otherwise, however, he delights in the law of God according to the inward person and meditates upon it day and night. And therefore that law must always be proclaimed in the midst of the congregation in connection with the gospel. Law and gospel, the whole Word, the full counsel of God, are the content of preaching.
