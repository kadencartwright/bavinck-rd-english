## 2.

After some had led the way in earlier times, in recent years the institution of the Supper by Jesus has been very seriously disputed by Jülicher, Spitta, Mensinga, Brandt, Grafe, and others. As grounds they adduce that the words “do this in remembrance of me” are lacking in Matthew and Mark and occur only as a free addition by Paul in 1 Cor. 11:24, 25 and Luke 22:19; that some manuscripts, especially D, omit in Luke 22:19, 20 the words το ὑπερ . . . ἐκχυννομενον and thus know nothing of a giving of Christ’s body, of doing this in remembrance of him, and of the cup of thanksgiving; that the Gospel of John makes no mention of it and the other reports differ strongly among themselves; and especially that Jesus could not connect bread and wine with his death, much less give them to be eaten as signs of his body and blood, but that these conceptions could arise only after a certain view of the person and death of Jesus had formed within the congregation. But all these arguments have little force. For it is a fact that at the time when the Gospel of John was written, the Supper was generally celebrated in the Christian church. The silence about its institution can therefore in no case arise from ignorance. It is the same with Luke. The absence of the above-mentioned words in ch. 22:19, 20 cannot presuppose that at the time people thought nothing else had happened at the Last Supper than what is contained in these words: και λαβων ἀρτον εὐχαριστησας ἐκλασεν και ἐδωκεν αὐτοις λεγων τουτο ἐστιν το σωμα μου. For then the act that took place would be entirely unintelligible. The text in Codex D presupposes that more happened and was spoken on that occasion and is therefore either corrupt or, in the most favorable case, an incomplete report of what happened at the Last Supper. From the time of John and Luke we can ascend to that of Paul; and then it appears that this apostle proceeds from the assumption that the Christian church generally knows and celebrates the Supper as an institution of Christ. Indeed, he even says that he received from the Lord what relates to the institution of the Supper and delivered it to the Corinthians, παρελαβον ἀπο του κυριου, 1 Cor. 11:23. The time when Paul received this instruction not παρα but ἀπο του κυριου, from the Lord’s side, undoubtedly coincides with that of his conversion, Acts 9, and is thus only a few years removed from the final night in which Christ instituted his Supper. From this it follows that already in the very first years after Jesus’ death, the Christian congregation unanimously with all the apostles knew the Supper as an institution of Christ. This is more than sufficient to raise the truth of that institution above all reasonable doubt.

Another question, however, is how Christ instituted this Supper and what he intended by it. Then it deserves attention first of all that Jesus instituted his Supper on the occasion of the Passover meal. The celebration of Passover had in Jesus’ time been expanded with all kinds of ceremonies and, in brief, took place in the following manner. As the feast approached, thousands upon thousands of Israelites journeyed to Jerusalem, bought a lamb there, and in the afternoon of the 14th of Nisan had the Levites slaughter it in the forecourt. Priests stood ready to catch the blood in silver and golden bowls, pass these from one to another, and finally pour it all at once over the altar. Meanwhile, with the Levites singing the Hallel, the animals were hung up and eviscerated, and the sacrificial portions were brought to the altar in a vessel by the priest. Thereafter those who had presented the lamb for slaughter, whose number was ordinarily between ten and twenty men, took the slaughtered animal to a private residence and roasted it there without being permitted to break a bone. The meal itself began with the passing around of a cup and with thanksgiving. Then bitter herbs and a dish of puree were brought to the table and eaten, and thereafter the lamb with unleavened cakes was set out. Before these were eaten, the householder, or later a reader, related the history of the exodus; the table companions took up the first part, Pss. 113–114, of the Hallel, Pss. 113–118; and the second cup was passed around. Only then did the proper meal begin. After it was finished, the third cup was blessed by the householder and drunk with the table companions. The whole was then concluded with pouring the fourth cup, singing the second part of the Hallel, Pss. 115–118, blessing the fourth cup by the householder with the words of Ps. 118:26, and its being emptied by the guests reclining at table. These four cups were required at the meal, but sometimes a fifth cup was passed around while Pss. 120–137 were sung; cf. Keil, *Arch.*, § 81; Herzog² 11, 268–270. Now Jesus probably instituted the Supper after the Passover lamb had been eaten, μετα το δειπνησαι, Luke 22:20, at the third cup, the cup of thanksgiving. In doing so, he takes some of the ordinary bread and ordinary wine used at Passover and, according to the testimony of all four reports, Matt. 26:26–29; Mark 14:22–25; Luke 22:19, 20; 1 Cor. 11:23–25, connects them directly with his death. There is no reason whatever to doubt this. Jesus knew and foresaw his death and had repeatedly explained its significance to his disciples, vol. III, 354f. Specifically, he understands his death here as a sacrifice, for only in this way can the words and acts accompanying the institution of the Supper be explained. The Passover had just been eaten, and that Passover had been the beginning and foundation of the covenant God established with Israel in the wilderness. For first, because the lamb was slaughtered and its blood shed and sprinkled upon the altar, it served as a sacrifice of atonement; and thereafter it was used as a sacrificial meal to signify the communion of God with his people. Christ transfers all this to himself. He is the true Passover lamb who through his death, through the breaking of his body and shedding of his blood, works reconciliation with God and lays the foundation of a new covenant. Jesus clearly indicates this by instituting the Supper on the occasion of Passover, by for this purpose making use of the bread and wine from the Passover table, by not only taking and blessing the bread but also, according to all four reports—cf. Acts 2:42—breaking it, and especially also by the words he spoke in doing so. While breaking and distributing the bread, he said: τουτο ἐστιν το σωμα μου, Matt., Mark; τουτο ἐστιν το σωμα μου, το ὑπερ ὑμων διδομενον, Luke; or τουτο μου ἐστιν το σωμα το ὑπερ ὑμων, Paul (κλωμενον is lacking in the most important manuscripts and alternates in others with θρυπτομενον and διδομενον). And when giving the cup he spoke: πιετε ἐξ αὐτου παντες, τουτο γαρ ἐστιν το αἱμα μου της διαθηκης το περι πολλων ἐκχυννομενον εἰς ἀφεσιν ἁμαρτιων, Matt.; τουτο ἐστιν το αἱμα μου της διαθηκης το ἐκχυννομενον ὑπερ πολλων, Mark; τουτο το ποτηριον ἐν τῳ αἱματι μου το ὑπερ ὑμων ἐκχυννομενον, Luke; τουτο το ποτηριον ἡ καινη διαθηκη ἐν τῳ ἐμῳ αἱματι, Paul. The divergent readings prove sufficiently that Jesus prescribed no fixed, unalterable formula at the Supper any more than at baptism. It is even impossible to determine which words were literally spoken by Jesus himself on that occasion. He did not determine what had to be said at the Supper, but described what the Supper was and had to be. And this is entirely clear from the four reports. Jesus elevated the bread and wine of the Passover table to signs of his body and blood, specifically of that body and blood as they would soon be given over in death as a sacrifice of atonement. The objection that Jesus’ body was not broken into pieces upon the cross like the bread, and that his blood was not shed there as the wine at the Supper, and that these images therefore do not fit, has little force. For Jesus proceeds from the Passover and the Old Testament sacrifice, adopts the act and terminology customary with it, and applies them to his death. Therefore Luke and Paul add to the words τουτο ἐστιν το σωμα μου the explanation το ὑπερ ὑμων διδομενον or only το ὑπερ ὑμων. Whether Jesus literally spoke these words in this way or not, he nevertheless intended this when he indicated in the bread a sign of his body. It is a sign of Jesus’ body as it is offered in death for the atonement of sins. Hence also the meaning of the second sign is described much more fully in all four reports. In the sacrifice the shedding of blood is the chief thing, Heb. 9:22. The blood of Christ, of which the wine is a sign, is sacrificial blood, blood of atonement shed for many and thereby the beginning and inauguration of a new covenant. As Passover and old covenant, so Supper and new covenant belong together. The cup is therefore το αἱμα μου της διαθηκης or ἡ καινη διαθηκη ἐν τῳ αἱματι μου, that is, covenant blood, Ex. 24:8, which as sacrificial blood was necessary and serviceable for effecting the covenant with God, or the covenant itself, which comes into being through that blood—since it works and brings with it the forgiveness of sins—and rests in it. The disciples were sorrowful, dreaded Christ’s death, and did not understand it. But here at the institution of the Supper Jesus explains to them that this death is to their benefit, for through it that forgiveness and that covenant come into being which were foreshadowed and predicted in the Old Testament. The time of promise is past; that of fulfillment dawns. The old has passed away; behold, all things have become new.

But Jesus does not leave it at this. In the signs of bread and wine he not only gives an explanation of his death, but also distributes those signs to his disciples for consumption. According to Matthew, Jesus expressly used the words λαβετε, φαγετε, and πιετε ἐξ αὐτου παντες. And although Mark mentions only the word λαβετε and Luke and Paul make no mention of any of these words, upon the basis of all the reports it is nevertheless established that Jesus not only elevated the bread and wine to signs of his body and blood but as such also distributed them to his disciples and gave them to enjoy. After the Passover had been eaten, Jesus instituted a new meal, whose components were bread and wine, not in and for themselves but as signs of his broken body and shed blood. Against this Spitta has objected that eating the slain body of Jesus and drinking his blood are “*ebenso schaurige wie für ein israelitisches Bewustsein unverträgliche Gedanken*.” People have therefore believed that, even if Jesus designated bread and wine as signs of his body and blood, he nevertheless did not as such give them to his disciples to enjoy; that he only explained what would soon happen to him, but not what his disciples would receive and enjoy; that eating Jesus’ body and drinking his blood at that moment, when Jesus himself sat at the table, was impossible; that if this enjoyment were the proper character of the Supper, then the Supper later after Jesus’ death had a different nature than at its institution; and that consuming bread and wine as such signs matters so little that until the middle of the second century, at least in the churches of Rome and Ephesus, the Supper was celebrated not with wine but with water. There is an element of truth in these objections. Although the Passover in the Old Testament pointed forward to Christ, eating the Passover lamb was nevertheless not an eating of Christ’s broken body, as the enjoyment of the bread in the Supper now is. And the blood was indeed shed and sprinkled at the sacrifices of the Old Covenant, but it was never drunk. The conception that Jesus’ flesh had to be eaten and his blood drunk was therefore so foreign to the Jews that they took offense at it and left Jesus, John 6:52, 60, 66. The institution of the Supper does indeed connect with the Old Testament conceptions of sacrifice but nevertheless again rises above them. The Supper is related to but not identical with the Passover. As new covenant stands to old covenant, and the sacrifice of Christ to that of the Old Testament, so the Supper stands to the Passover. The Passover was a *sacramentum* upon the basis of a *sacrificium*, but in both parts a shadow and prophecy of the goods of the New Testament. Now, however, through Christ the true and perfect sacrifice has been brought, and therefore upon the basis of that sacrifice communion with God in the Supper is much richer and fuller than it could be in the days of the Old Testament. The Supper is a meal, the essential meal of God and his people; a sacrificial meal, the sacrificial meal *par excellence*, where believers enjoy Christ himself as he died for them. Jesus expresses this by giving his disciples bread and wine as signs of his broken body and shed blood to enjoy. He gives himself not only *for* his own; he also gives himself *to* his own. Cup and bread in the Supper are κοινωνια του αἱματος και του σωματος του Χριστου, 1 Cor. 10:16. The above-mentioned objections further have force insofar as they clearly demonstrate that a Capernaistic eating and drinking of Christ’s body and blood is altogether excluded. The Supper Christ himself instituted while sitting at the table is the same as that celebrated in the Christian church after his death to the present day. Bread and wine do not relate to the person of Christ without further qualification, but specifically to Christ as crucified. In them he sets his sacrifice before our eyes, but also causes us to enjoy it. And this enjoyment very much matters in the Supper. Jesus *gave* the signs of bread and wine. He did not keep them in his hand but distributed them. He commanded his disciples to take and eat them. And according to Luke (only when distributing the bread) and Paul (also when handing over the cup), he added the words τουτο ποιειτε εἰς την ἐμην ἀναμνησιν. That these words are lacking in Matthew and Mark does not in the least prove that either they were not spoken by Jesus or were added by Luke and Paul contrary to his intention. For doing this in remembrance of him is naturally implied in eating and drinking the bread and wine as signs of Jesus’ broken body and shed blood; the former is impossible without the latter. Further, these words do not imply that the Supper is merely a memorial meal, but express that the whole Supper, which in its essence is a sacrificial meal and an exercise of communion with Christ, must be celebrated in remembrance of him. They do not describe the essence of the Supper but presuppose that Jesus will soon be absent and prescribe that even then the Supper must be celebrated in remembrance of him, as a continual proclamation of his death, 1 Cor. 11:26. Therefore in Paul the words are also added, “as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup,” 1 Cor. 11:25, 26. The Supper was instituted by Christ as a permanent good for his congregation. It is a benefit added to all the other benefits to signify and seal them. And it will remain until Christ’s return. His death must be proclaimed until he comes. For in this dispensation the cross is and remains the cause of all blessings, the center of the congregation’s remembrance. Jesus himself said that from then onward, from the institution and use of the Supper, he would no longer drink of the fruit of the vine until he drank it new with his disciples in his Father’s kingdom, Matt. 26:29; Mark 14:25; cf. Luke 22:16, 18. For he went to heaven to prepare a place for his disciples. And only when he returns and has taken his disciples to himself will he sit with them at the wedding table of the Lamb and with them drink of the new wine the kingdom of his Father will produce in the new heaven and new earth. For the intervening time he instituted the Supper as a remembrance of his suffering, a proclamation of his death, a means of his rich grace. Cf.
