## 3.

From the beginning this Supper occupied a prominent place in Christian worship. It was ordinarily celebrated in a special gathering of the congregation on the Lord’s Day in the evening and in connection with an ordinary meal; see above, p. 216. Little is known about the manner of this celebration. We know only from *Did.* 9, 10, 14 that first there was a confession of sins, then a prayer of thanksgiving was pronounced separately over the cup and over the bread, afterward the meal was held, and the whole was concluded with thanksgiving. But in the second century a separation between the ἀγαπαι and the εὐχαριστια gradually arose throughout the church. The former went their own way and soon degenerated, Zahn, article “Agapen” in Herzog³; and the latter was moved to the morning worship service and connected with the ministry of the Word. Various changes accompanied this. The single worship service was soon divided into two parts, the first of which, the ministry of the Word, was also open to catechumens, penitents, and unbelievers, while the second, the celebration of the Supper, was accessible only to the baptized. The latter increasingly acquired a mysterious character; see above, p. 217. Baptism and the Supper became a μυστηριον, a *sacramentum*, Tertullian, *adv. Marc.* 4, 34, and through this name as well as through all kinds of additional ceremonies were shrouded in mysterious darkness. It is therefore altogether wrong to interpret the conceptions of those early times according to the opinions later cherished in the West, and especially in the 16th century, concerning the *unio sacramentalis*. Scripture said that the bread is the body and the wine the blood of Christ, and this manner of speaking was adopted and understood and explained by each in his own way. An official interpretation was lacking; no controversy arose about it; and the question concerning the nature of this union of sign and signified thing did not arise. A realistic con- or transubstantiation lay as far beyond the consciousness of that time as an exclusively symbolic or figurative meaning of the Supper. Symbol and thing were much more intimately connected for Eastern thought than for Western thought. As Harnack rightly says, by a symbol we understand a thing that is not what it signifies, whereas at that time one understood by a symbol a thing that in one way or another also is what it signifies, *D. G.* I² 397. From the beginning it was settled in the Christian church that bread and wine were the body and blood of Christ; but the way in which they conceived the union of bread and wine with Christ’s body and blood is not clear and is therefore open to different interpretations. This applies to Ignatius, *Smyrn.* 7; *Eph.* 20; Justin, *Apol.* I 66; Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* IV 18, 5; and many other writers. But the development of the doctrine of the Supper was led especially onto a wrong path by the application of the idea of sacrifice. In the New Testament the sanctification of the body, Rom. 12:1; prayer, Heb. 13:15; cf. Rev. 5:8; 8:3; beneficence and generosity, Heb. 13:16; Phil. 4:18, are indeed called an offering, but the Supper never is. In the earliest period, however, the Supper was connected with an ordinary meal, and for that meal the more prosperous members of the congregation brought the necessary ingredients—bread, wine, oil, milk, honey, and so forth—which were probably received by the deacons, placed before the bishop on main and side tables (the later side altars), served at the meal, and afterward designated for the support of the ministers and the relief of the poor. These gifts received the names προσφοραι, *oblationes*, θυσιαι, *sacrificia*, offerings, and were blessed by the bishop with a prayer of thanksgiving, εὐχαριστια. This conception was transferred to the whole Supper. From the prayer of thanksgiving pronounced over the gifts, the Supper itself and also both elements of the Supper soon came to be called εὐχαριστια, *Did.* 9; Ignatius, *Philad.* 4; *Smyrn.* 7, 8; Justin, *Apol.* I 66, and it too was soon conceived as an offering brought to God by the congregation, *Did.*, θυσια καθαρα, already with an appeal to Mal. 1:11. This was still relatively innocent as long as the Supper was truly regarded as a meal and the thanksgiving was offered in the name of the whole congregation. The content of the offering was not the body and blood of Christ but the gifts brought together by the congregation, so that in the earliest period one thought only of a thank offering and not at all of a propitiatory sacrifice. Yet it contained a dangerous element that would later have a harmful effect, especially when reinforced by the clerical conception of office. Clement already compares the *episcopi* and *diaconi* with the priests and Levites of the Old Testament, describes their activity as a προσφερειν τα δωρα της ἐπισκοπης, and brings their εὐχαριστια into connection with the Old Testament θυσιαι, 1 Cor. 40–44. And when the Supper and the *agapae*, the presentation of bread and wine for the Supper, and the contributions for the support of the ministers and the relief of the needy became separated from one another, the Supper increasingly acquired the character of an offering that was brought not by the congregation but by the bishop, consisted not in its presentation but in his thanksgiving and consecration, and concerned not the gifts for the Supper but its elements, Justin, *Dial.* 41, 70; Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* IV 18, 5. This conception of the Supper as an offering in turn influenced the idea people formed of the *unio sacramentalis*, just as conversely the latter influenced the former. The more the bishop was regarded as a priest, the thanksgiving as a consecration, and the Supper as a sacrifice, the more strongly the realistic union of bread and wine with the body and blood of Christ commended itself. In connection with Eastern Christology, the symbolic, spiritualistic conception of Origen—also found in essence in Eusebius of Caesarea, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, and others—gave way in Cyril, Gregory of Nyssa, Chrysostom, and John of Damascus to the realistic doctrine of μεταβολη, μεταποιησις, or transformation, which later passed over into the doctrine of μετουσιωσις, or transubstantiation: Damascenus, *de fide orthod.* IV 14; *Conf. orthod.*, qu. 107; *Conf. Dosith.*, decr. 17; cf. Steitz, *Die Abendmahlslehre der gr. K. in ihrer gesch. Entw.*, *Jahrb. f. d. Theol.*, 1864, pp. 409–481; 1865, pp. 64–152, 399–463; 1866, pp. 193–252; 1867, pp. 211–286; 1868, pp. 2–67, 649–700; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Konf.* I 410f.; Loofs, article “Abendmahl” in Herzog³ 1, 38–57.

The development proceeded much more slowly in the West, although it later led to the same result. Cyprian strongly emphasized the sacrificial character of the Supper but nevertheless saw in it only an imitation of what Christ had truly done on Golgotha, *Ep.* 63, 2, 14. And although Augustine repeatedly, in the language of Scripture, calls the bread the body and the wine the blood of Christ, nothing of the later theory of transubstantiation is yet to be found in him. On the contrary, Augustine distinguishes as sharply as possible between *verbum* and *elementum*: *accedit verbum ad elementum et fit sacramentum*. Bread and wine are therefore called sacraments, *quia in eis aliud videtur, aliud intelligitur*. The sign is indeed frequently called by the name of the thing signified, but that is because it exhibits a likeness of it. *Si enim sacramenta quamdam similitudinem earum rerum, quarum sacramenta sunt, non haberent, omnino sacramenta non essent. Ex hac autem similitudine plerumque etiam ipsarum rerum nomina accipiunt. Sicut ergo secundum quendam modum sacramentum corporis Christi corpus Christi est, sacramentum sanguinis Christi sanguis Christi est, ita sacramentum fidei fides est*, *Ep.* 23 *ad Bonif.* Bread and wine are *similitudines, signa, commemorationes* of the body and blood of Christ; *Dominus non dubitavit dicere: hoc est corpus meum, cum signum daret corporis sui*, *c. Adam.*, ch. 12. Christ is also no longer bodily present with us but has ascended into heaven: *secundum praesentiam majestatis semper habemus Christum, secundum praesentiam carnis, recte dictum est discipulis: me autem non semper habebitis*, *Tract.* 50 *in Ev. Joann.* Thus, when Augustine frequently calls the Supper a *sacrificium*, he does not mean that Christ is truly offered again, even if in an unbloody manner. Rather, he calls it this because it is a memorial of Christ’s sacrifice on the cross. *Hujus sacrificii caro et sanguis ante adventum Christi per victimarum similitudinem promittebatur; in passione Christi per ipsam veritatem reddebatur; post ascensum Christi per sacramentum memoriae celebratur*, *c. Faustum* 20, 21. *Christus se ipsum obtulit holocaustum pro peccatis nostris et ejus sacrificii similitudinem celebrandam in suae passionis memoriam commendavit*, 83 *qu. qu.* 61; *de doctr. chr.* III 16. Or he also understands by the body of Christ offered in the Supper the congregation. How, he asks, is the bread the body of Christ? And he answers: *corpus Christi si vis intelligere, apostolum audi dicentem fidelibus: vos estis corpus Christi et membra. Si ergo vos estis corpus Christi et membra, mysterium vestrum in mensa propositum est, mysterium vestrum accipitis*. And the bread is the sign of this, *quia panis non fit de uno grano sed de multis*, *Serm. ad infantes*; cf. *de civ.* 10, 6; 22, 10. Augustine therefore never wearies of affirming that the use of the Supper is not sufficient in itself, that it is a blessing only to believers but destruction to others, and that the true eating of Christ’s body consists in believing: *crede et manducasti*, *Tract.* 25 *in Joann.*, and so passim; cf. *Bibl. studii theol. apud J. Crispinum*, 1565, pp. 89–100; Dorner, *Augustinus*, 263–276; and so forth. Through its powerful influence, Augustine’s doctrine long held back the full development of the realistic theory and still dominated among the Carolingian theologians, but in the conflict of Ratramnus against Paschasius Radbertus, and later in that of Berengarius against Lanfranc, it was increasingly pushed aside and finally replaced altogether by the metabolic theory. We first encounter the word transubstantiation in Hildebert of Tours, † 1134, but he uses it in a sermon, so it must already have been fairly widely known at that time; cf. Deutsch in Schürer’s *Theol. Lit. Z.*, 1898, col. 442. The thing expressed by it had already been established earlier. In 1079 Berengarius had to sign the formula *panem et vinum... per mysterium sacrae orationis et verba nostri Redemptoris substantialiter converti in veram et propriam ac vivificatricem carnem et sanguinem Domini nostri J. C.*; and the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 determined that the body and blood of Christ *in sacramento altaris sub speciebus panis et vini veraciter continentur, transsubstantiatis pane in corpus et vino in sanguinem potestate divina*, Denzinger, *Enchir.*, 298, 357. This dogma presented to the dialectical spirit of scholasticism a series of problems concerning the nature, time, and duration of the change; the relation of substance and accidents; the manner in which Christ was present in both elements and in every part of them; the preservation and adoration of the host; and so forth: Lombard, Thomas, Bonaventure, and Duns Scotus on *Sent.* IV, dist. 8–13; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qus. 73–83; *c. Gent.* IV 61–69; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VI 9; cf. Schwane, *D. G.* III 628–661; Harnack, *D. G.* III 488–498; and so forth. Rome, then, teaches the following concerning the sacrament of the Supper. The signs in this sacrament are unleavened bread and wine mixed with a little water. These elements are changed by the word of consecration into Christ’s own body and blood. For when the Savior spoke the words at the last meal, “This is my body, this is my blood,” he not only caused bread and wine to be changed into his body and blood on that one occasion, but at the same time thereby appointed his disciples as priests and placed in the words he spoke a power to change the substance of bread and wine. The administration of the Eucharist is therefore the preeminent priestly work and may never be performed by anyone other than an ordained priest. When the priest pronounces the words of consecration, the substance of bread and wine changes into the substance of Christ’s body and blood. For the bodily presence of Christ in the Eucharist cannot be brought about by Christ’s leaving heaven and taking up residence in the sacrament, since Christ is and remains seated at the right hand of the Father. Nor can it be effected by creation, for then the bread would not be Christ’s own body, not the same body in which he was born of Mary and died on the cross. It can therefore occur only in such a way that the substance of bread and wine passes over into the substance of Christ’s body and blood, much as the food we consume is converted into a component of our body. The *accidentia* of bread and wine—namely, form, taste, smell, color, and even nutritive power—do indeed remain after the consecration, but they no longer inhere in a subject. The substance whose properties they were has been removed and replaced by a completely different one, of which they are not properties but which they conceal from the eye by their appearance alone. Since Christ’s body and blood cannot be separated from his human nature, nor this nature from his deity, the whole Christ is fully present in each element and in every part of both elements. It is therefore not necessary, although neither is it unlawful and it may sometimes be permitted, for the Eucharist to be received *sub utraque specie*, for the whole Christ is present even in the smallest piece of bread. And this Christ is present in the elements from the moment of consecration until the time they are consumed, and thus also *ante* and *extra omnem usum*. The Eucharist is therefore distinguished from all other sacraments. For these are all completed through their use. Baptism, for example, becomes a sacrament only when a human being is actually washed by it. But this is not the case with the Eucharist. The formula spoken here is addressed not to the recipient but to the element and serves to change that element into the body and blood of Christ. In the Eucharist, therefore, the use—that is, communion—is secondary; it belongs to the *perfectio* but not to the *necessitas* of the sacrament. The sacrament of the Eucharist consists essentially in the consecration itself, in the transubstantiation thereby brought about, in the act of the priest—that is, in the offering. At Rome the Eucharist is not only a *sacramentum*; in the first place it is a *sacrificium*. When Christ spoke the words “This is my body,” he offered himself to God at that same moment; and when he said, “Do this in remembrance of me,” he thereby ordained that his priests should repeat this offering from day to day, Mal. 1:11. The offering made by the priest in the Mass is the same as that accomplished on the cross. It is not merely an image, symbol, or memorial of it but is completely identical with it; it is entirely the same offering, with this difference alone: the offering on the cross is bloody and that in the Mass is unbloody. For it is also the same priest who offers himself at the cross and here, since it is Christ himself who by means of the priest offers himself to God and therefore speaks through his mouth the words, “This is my body.” The sacrifice of the Mass is therefore not only a sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving but also a true propitiatory sacrifice, no less efficacious and fruitful than the sacrifice on the cross. While the Eucharist as sacrament nourishes the spiritual life, preserves from mortal sins, remits temporal punishments, unites believers, and guarantees future glory, as the sacrifice of the Mass it obtains the remission of temporal punishments and the grace of penance, not only for those who attend the Mass but also for the absent, not merely for the living but also for penitents in purgatory. The Mass is the center of Roman worship, a φρικτον μυστηριον, a *tremendum mysterium*. And because the whole Christ is bodily present in the elements of bread and wine, these must be carefully preserved, displayed to the people for adoration in a monstrance, carried around in solemn procession on the *festum corporis Christi*, and can also be administered to the sick in their homes and sent along with the dead as a *viaticum*.
