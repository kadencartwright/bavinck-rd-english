## 7.

Even better than the Lutherans, the Reformed purified the Supper of Roman admixtures and restored it to its original significance. According to Christ’s institution, the Supper was a meal, a real meal, in which bread and wine were used as food and drink for strengthening the body and above all served as signs and seals for exercising communion with the crucified Christ. It is an ordinary, natural but at the same time extraordinary, spiritual meal, in which Christ the host offers his own crucified body and shed blood for the nourishment of our souls. Therefore in this meal Christ instituted everything is important; nothing in it is without significance; everything has a profound meaning. First, the signs of bread and wine were not arbitrarily chosen but are excellently suited to give us an idea of the spiritual food and drink Christ prepared for our souls in his death. Second, all the acts Jesus performed at the institution of the Supper have significance. He does not receive the bread and wine from the hand of others, δεξαμενος, as he did first at the Passover, Luke 22:17, but takes them himself from the table, λαβων, v. 19, as proof that he is the host and provides the food and drink. He blesses (εὐλογησας, Matt. 26:26; Mark 14:22, alternating at the cup with εὐχαριστησας, Matt. 26:27; Mark 14:23, while Luke 22:19, 20 and Paul, 1 Cor. 11:24, 25, have only εὐχαριστησας) the bread and later likewise the cup. By this blessing is not to be understood that Christ asks a blessing from God over the bread and wine, but the interchange with εὐχαριστησας proves that Jesus blesses God—that is, praises and thanks him for the gifts he bestowed. The content of that praise and thanksgiving is not mentioned but certainly related to the gifts of creation represented in bread and wine, and further especially to the gifts of grace that would be acquired through Christ’s death and were offered to the disciples in his body and blood. Already through that thanksgiving bread and wine were set apart from common use and destined for a higher purpose, and at the same time the disciples were prepared for a correct understanding of the significant words, “this is my body,” and so forth, which Jesus would soon speak in explanation. Further, Jesus broke the bread, in which the Reformed rightly saw an act belonging indeed not to the *essentia* but nevertheless to the *integritas sacramenti*. For not only is this breaking mentioned in all four reports, but the whole Supper is named after it, Acts 2:42. Just as breaking the bread is necessary to make it consumable for the guests, so Christ must give his body in death so that it may be food for our souls, John 6:51; 12:24. Finally, Jesus himself distributes the bread and wine to his disciples so that they should eat and drink from them. He does this with the express words λαβετε, φαγετε, πιετε ἐξ αὐτου παντες, which most strongly condemn the Roman Mass without communicants. The *communio* belongs to the essence of the Supper. Third, the words Jesus speaks in distributing bread and wine are important. When he gave the bread to his disciples, he said, “This is my body, which is given for you,” Luke 22:19; and when handing over the cup he spoke, “This cup is my covenant blood,” or, “the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you or for many for the forgiveness of sins.” The claim that these words possess a consecratory, operative power finds no support whatsoever in the text of the reports. But after Jesus through thanksgiving set bread and wine apart from common use, destined them for a higher purpose, and prepared his disciples, he now speaks the words “This is my body and blood.” He does not say, “Let this bread become my body.” He does not command and order but declares and explains. For it is a symbolic act he performs, a spiritual meal he institutes. And the food and drink of that meal are his body and blood as they are given over in death. All the signs, acts, and words in the Supper are directed to this, that they point our faith to the sacrifice of Jesus Christ on the cross as the only ground of our salvation. Yet the Supper is not therefore exhausted in a meal in remembrance of Christ and his benefits. For under the signs of bread and wine Jesus gives his own body and blood to enjoy. The table of the Supper brings about a genuine communion between Christ and believers, a communion not only in the benefits but also and before all else in the person of Christ, according to both his human and divine nature.

There is no disagreement among Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed Christians concerning the reality of that communion; in this respect they stand together over against Zwingli. But they do disagree among themselves about the nature of that communion and the manner in which it is enjoyed in the Supper. Roman Catholics and Lutherans believe that it cannot truly and fully come about unless they cause Christ to descend bodily from heaven to earth and eat and drink his body and blood not only spiritually but also with the bodily mouth. Over against this, from the beginning and repeatedly Calvin emphasized that believers’ communion with Christ, also according to his human nature, is spiritual in character and comes about not by Christ descending bodily, but by our spiritually lifting our hearts upward to heaven, where Jesus Christ, our advocate, is at the right hand of his heavenly Father. And this doctrine rests upon Scripture and agrees with the nature of the New Testament dispensation. For 1º the communion of Christ and believers is indeed so intimate and indissoluble that it can scarcely be expressed in words and can only be clarified to some extent under images such as head and body, vine and branch, bridegroom and bride. But it is nevertheless no pantheistic mingling or identification, no interflowing of substance, no unity of essence like that of the three persons in the Trinity, no personal union like that of the two natures in Christ. Christ and believers remain distinct; their personality is maintained. The *unio mystica* is a union of persons, even though not only according to their will and disposition but also according to their being and nature. 2º This communion is effected by the Holy Spirit, who dwells in Christ as the head and in believers as his members. There is no other way to partake of that communion. A physical union such as trans- and consubstantiation, with the *manducatio oralis* connected to it, seeks to bring about is wholly empty and useless. Only the Holy Spirit, who is the Spirit of God and the Spirit of Christ, can so unite human beings with Christ that they partake of his person and benefits and cannot be separated from him by death or grave, world or Satan. Therefore this communion is also always spiritual in character. It does indeed also embrace Christ’s human nature and believers according to their bodies. For Christ as mediator cannot be conceived without his human nature, and he purchased believers not only according to their soul but also according to their body. Yet the union remains spiritual in character because it comes about only through the Holy Spirit. 3º The communion with Christ strengthened in the Supper is no other than that which also comes about through the means of grace of the Word. The sacrament adds no single grace to that offered in the Word; it only strengthens and confirms what has been accepted from the Word through faith. Thus when Roman Catholics and Lutherans object that communion with Christ in the Supper according to the Reformed conception is no genuine communion with Christ’s own body and blood, it needs only to be observed against this that the communion assured in the Supper is and can be no other than that through the Word. In precisely the same way that a human being is incorporated into Christ through faith, he is also strengthened and confirmed in that communion through the Supper. There is no other, higher communion. Whoever believes the Word becomes Christ’s possession in body and soul; and whoever receives the Supper in faith is made certain and assured of this. The sacrament bestows no other grace but bestows the same grace, for the strengthening of faith, only in another way. 4º Among the Reformed too, therefore, Christ is truly and essentially present in the Supper with his divine and human nature, but in no other way than he is present in the gospel. He is not bodily enclosed in bread and wine any more than in the proclaimed Word, but whoever accepts the sign in faith truly receives, according to God’s ordinance, communion with the whole Christ. Not in and under, but with the sign Christ bestows the thing signified—that is, himself with all his benefits. For while among Roman Catholics and Lutherans grace is something objective and passive that is received bodily even by the unbeliever, among the Reformed it is the personal, living Christ himself, who in the Supper communicates himself as spiritual food to believers, Müller, *Dogm. Abh.*, 458. Thus according to the Reformed he is present in the Supper not less, but much more strongly and truly than according to Rome and Luther, for he is present not physically and locally within the signs but spiritually, as the acting Christ himself, in the hearts of believers. *Aliud est praesentem Christi substantiam, ut nos vivificet, in pane sistere; aliud vivificam esse Christi carnem, quia ex ejus substantia vita in animas nostras profluit*, Calvin, *adv. Westph.* in Müller, 443; cf. *Conf. Angl.* 28 and Ryle, *Knots Untied*, 235–254. 5º Therefore faith is an indispensable requirement for the reception of the sacrament. The truth of the sacrament does not indeed depend upon that faith. For just as with the Word, God has bound himself at the Supper to grant Christ and his benefits truly to everyone who believes. But by the nature of the case the unbeliever receives only the sign, just as with the Word he only hears the sounds and does not partake of the thing itself denoted by them. To partake of the promises and benefits of Word and sacrament, therefore, an operation of the Holy Spirit in the human heart is necessary; and it is precisely this operation of the Spirit which outside and in the Supper brings about and maintains communion with Christ. 6º The benefits enjoyed in the Supper can easily be inferred from this. The strengthening of communion with Christ stands in the foreground. The believer already partakes of that communion through faith and receives in the Supper no other than that which he already enjoys through faith. But when Christ himself, by the hand of the minister, under the signs of bread and wine gives him his body to eat and his blood to drink, then he is strengthened and confirmed by the Holy Spirit in that communion and united ever more intimately, according to body and soul, with the whole Christ according to both his divine and human nature. For *manducatio corporis Christi nihil aliud est, quam arctissima cum Christo conjunctio*, Junius, *Theses theol.* 52, 7. The Supper is here distinguished from baptism in that baptism is the sacrament of incorporation, the Supper the sacrament of growth in communion with Christ. Through baptism we are buried with Christ in his death and raised in his resurrection and are thus passive; but in the Supper we ourselves act, eat the body and drink the blood of Christ, and are thus nourished through his communion unto eternal life. But if we partake of the person of Christ, we naturally also partake of all his benefits. Among these, the forgiveness of sins is named in Scripture in the first place and with the greatest emphasis. In the Supper Christ gives his body and blood as food for our souls, but that body and blood are such food not *quia est corporea substantia, quo modo esset cibus corporalis sed quatenus corpus Christi est datum pro mundi vita*, Junius, loc. cit. Therefore body and blood are presented separately, each under a sign of its own, in the Supper. Therefore Christ expressly says that his body is given and his blood shed for the forgiveness of sins. Therefore the significance of the blood in the words of institution is illuminated and explained even more fully than that of the body, for it is the blood that makes atonement for sins upon the altar. Although Christ is now glorified, the communion that comes about through faith and is strengthened in the Supper is and remains a communion with his crucified body and shed blood. From the standpoint of trans- and consubstantiation this is impossible; there the dead Christ recedes behind the glorified Christ. But if eating Christ’s body and drinking his blood is equivalent to *intima nostri cum Christo conjunctio*, Bucanus, *Inst. theol.*, 677, then this and every other benefit are exclusively a fruit of Christ’s death and we are therefore nourished from Christ only because he was crucified for us. And among the benefits Christ acquired through his death, forgiveness of sins then stands first. This benefit too is not bestowed for the first time in the Supper, for the Christian already possesses it through faith and received its sign and seal in baptism. It is therefore incorrect to distribute the different graces over the sacraments with Rome in such a way that in each sacrament a particular group of sins is forgiven and a particular grace bestowed, for the forgiveness that Word, baptism, and Supper offer us is always the same. The forgiveness communicated in the Supper therefore by no means relates only to *culpae quotidianae*, to *peccata venialia*, *Trid.*, sess. 13, ch. 2; 22, ch. 1. Rather, it is the same full, rich benefit of forgiveness offered in the Word, accepted through faith, and signified and sealed through the sacraments of baptism and the Supper. In this benefit it is clearly evident that the sacrament adds no single new grace to the Word. Because of our weakness it gives the same grace only in another way, so that we might firmly believe and be healed of all doubt. With this benefit of forgiveness comes that of eternal life. The Supper is a spiritual meal where Christ nourishes our souls with his crucified body and shed blood. Eating and drinking them serves to strengthen spiritual, eternal life, for whoever eats the flesh of the Son of Man and drinks his blood has eternal life and is raised on the last day, John 6:54. It is clearly evident from this that eternal life is a benefit bestowed upon the whole human being, not only according to his soul but also according to his body. Some have wrongly inferred from this that from Christ’s body enjoyed in the Supper there proceeds a direct operation upon our body, so that it is healed from all kinds of disease and weakness and in principle transformed into a new resurrection body. It is understandable that especially from the Lutheran standpoint people came to this view, for if the *manducatio oralis* does not have *this* utility, it has no value at all. Yet Holy Scripture teaches nothing concerning this. In 1 Cor. 11:30 Paul does indeed say that as a consequence of the dreadful abuse of the Supper in Corinth many illnesses and deaths occurred. But this is clearly a κριμα, v. 29, a punishment God caused to follow upon this abuse of the Supper, and by no means proves that use or faithful use of the Supper also serves to heal diseases of the body. Moreover, John 6 may indeed be used to illustrate the Supper but does not deal directly with it; and this chapter too teaches only that whoever through faith, even without the Supper, eats Christ’s flesh and drinks his blood has eternal life and will be raised on the last day; cf. 6:40. Thus by no means only through the *manducatio oralis*, but in general through faith, does the human being partake of eternal life and the hope of resurrection. The Holy Spirit who dwells in believers is the most certain pledge for the resurrection of the body and the day of redemption, Rom. 8:11; Eph. 1:14; 4:30. But this Spirit of Christ does indeed make use of the Supper to strengthen the believer in the hope of eternal life and blessed resurrection at the end of the days. *Denn wo die Seele genesen ist, da ist dem Leibe auch geholfen*, Luther in Müller, *Dogm. Abh.*, 419. *Praesentia corporis affert non modo indubitatam vitae aeternae fiduciam animis nostris, sed de carnis etiam nostrae immortalitate securos nos reddit, siquidem ab immortali ejus carne jam vivificatur et quodammodo ejus immortalitati communicat*, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 17, 32; cf. also in Ebrard, *Dogm. v. h. Ab.* II 460; and further Philippi, *K. Gl.* V 2, p. 282f.; Müller, *Dogm. Abh.*, 417. In this sense the Supper may be called φαρμακον ἀθανασιας, Ignatius, *Eph.* 20. Finally, as a celebration in remembrance and proclamation of Christ’s death, the Supper further serves for the confession of our faith over against the world and for the strengthening of the communion of believers with one another. In 1 Cor. 10:17 the apostle argues that the bread must indeed be communion with the body of Christ, for how otherwise could believers, who considered in themselves are many, be one? That unity comes about only because in the one bread they have communion with the one body of Christ. Believers are one in Christ and therefore also with one another. Just as from many grains one bread is baked and from many grapes pressed together one wine and drink flows, so all who are incorporated into Christ through true faith are together one body; and at the Supper they confess this over against the world, which does not know their unity.
