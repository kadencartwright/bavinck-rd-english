## 5.

In Scripture the Supper is designated by the names δειπνον κυριακον, 1 Cor. 11:20; τραπεζα κυριου, 1 Cor. 10:21; κλασις ἀρτου, Acts 2:42; 20:7; ποτηριον του κυριου, 1 Cor. 11:27; ποτηριον της εὐλογιας, 1 Cor. 10:16. And the church later added many others, such as ἀγαπη, because in the first period the Supper was connected with a love feast; εὐχαριστια, already in *Did.* 9; Ignatius, *Smyrn.* 7, 8; Justin Martyr, *Apol.* I 66, because at the Supper believers thanked God for the gifts of his grace; εὐλογια, because the blessing was pronounced over bread and wine through thanksgiving and praise of God; συναξις, κοινωνια, since believers coming together at the Supper exercised communion with one another; προσφορα, θυσια (ἁγια, πνευματικη, μυστικη, ἀναιμακτος, etc.), because believers brought and offered to God the ingredients for the meal, or because the Supper was an image and remembrance of Christ’s sacrifice on the cross; and further still δειπνον μυστικον, εὐωχια, ἐφοδιον, *viaticum*, λατρεια, λειτουργια, φρικτον μυστηριον, *tremendum mysterium, sacramentum corporis et sanguinis Domini, sacramentum altaris, missa*, derived from the *missio catechumenorum* after the didactic portion of the worship service had ended, and so forth. Although Luther continued to speak of *sacramentum altaris*, *Art. Smalc.* III 6; *Cat. maj.* 5, Protestants generally preferred the name *sacra coena* or *coena Domini* and in this too maintained that the Supper was a real meal. Some Roman theologians, such as Maldonatus on Matt. 26:25, did indeed object that the expression δειπνον κυριακον in 1 Cor. 11:20 referred not to the Supper but to the meal held after the Supper and offered by the richer congregational members to the poorer, M. Vitringa VIII 10. But the Supper was held not before but after the ordinary meal, v. 21, and is if not alone then nevertheless also included under the expression. Yet even if this were not the case, the incorrectness of the name would by no means thereby be proved. For according to the plain teaching of Scripture the Supper is nevertheless a real meal. Its connection with the *agapae*, its institution on the occasion of the Passover meal, its components of bread and wine, the eating and drinking of them—everything indicates that in the Supper we are dealing with a real meal and may never deprive it of this character. But it is a δειπνον κυριακον, a meal of the Lord. Jesus is its institutor and in it too accomplished the will of the Father, to do which was his food. The Supper, like baptism, is of divine origin and must be so in order to be a sacrament, for God alone is the distributor of grace and he alone can bind its distribution to means ordained by him. And Jesus institutes this Supper specifically as mediator. In it he appears as prophet who proclaims and explains his death; acts as priest who gave himself for his own upon the cross; and appears as king who freely disposes of the grace acquired and gives it to his disciples to enjoy under the signs of bread and wine. And as he is the institutor, so he is also the host and minister of the Supper. He himself takes the bread and wine, blesses them, and distributes them to his disciples. He was host and minister not only when he bodily reclined at table with his disciples, but is and remains so everywhere and always that *his* meal is celebrated. Every Supper administered according to his institution is a δειπνον κυριακον. For Christ is institutor not only by example but also by precept. It is a meal in remembrance of him, 1 Cor. 11:24; for the proclamation of his death, v. 26; for communion with Christ’s body and blood, 1 Cor. 10:16, 21; 11:27. In the Supper Christ meets with the congregation and the congregation with Christ, bearing witness to their spiritual communion; cf. Rev. 3:20. The minister who blesses and distributes bread and wine therefore does this in Christ’s name and is only an instrument in his hand. Because Paul in 1 Cor. 10:16 speaks in the plural of those who bless the cup, and Tertullian, *Exhort. ad cast.* 7, says, *ubi ecclesiastici ordinis non est consessus, et offert et tingit sacerdos, qui est ibi solus; sed et ubi tres, ecclesia est, licet laici*, some, such as Grotius, Salmasius, Episcopius, and others, maintained that if a priest or teacher was lacking, the Supper might also be administered by an ordinary member of the congregation. But this view lacks sufficient ground. In Matt. 28:19 the ministry of baptism together with that of the Word is entrusted to the apostles. They together with the teachers are distributors of the mysteries of God, proclaimers of the mysteries God revealed in the gospel of Christ, 1 Cor. 4:1; stewards of God who have to distribute his grace, 1 Cor. 9:17; Titus 1:7. Undoubtedly with these mysteries one must think first of all of the Word of the gospel. But the sacrament follows the Word and is always joined with the Word. The apostles in Jerusalem exercise there the ministry of prayer and the Word, Acts 6:4. At the breaking of bread, Acts 20:7, 11, Paul speaks. Pronouncing the thanksgiving at the Supper was a part of the ministry of the Word and thus entrusted to the teacher, although it, like the breaking of bread, is represented in 1 Cor. 10:16 as an act of the congregation, Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 69. According to *Did.* 10, 7, the εὐχαριστειν therefore also belongs to the prophets; according to Ignatius, *Smyrn.* 8, to the bishop; according to Justin, *Apol.* I 65, to the προεστως, while the deacons rendered their service and handed bread and wine to the communicants; cf. Suicerus, s.v. διακονος and συναξις; Voetius, *Pol. eccl.* I 746–751; Moor V 638–641; M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 327 sq. This close connection of the ministry of the Supper with that of the Word proves that the minister acts in Christ’s name and works as steward and distributor of his mysteries. The Supper is a meal of which Christ is the host.

Further, the idea of meal in the Supper emerges very strongly in the food and drink distributed and consumed in it. No more than water in baptism were the signs of bread and wine in the Supper chosen arbitrarily or accidentally. In the sacrifices of the Old Testament flesh and blood were the chief thing, since they typically pointed forward to Christ’s sacrifice upon the cross. But the Supper is itself no sacrifice but a remembrance of the sacrifice on the cross and expresses believers’ communion in that sacrifice. Therefore Christ chooses not flesh and blood but bread and wine as food and drink in the Supper, to indicate that it is no sacrifice but a meal, a meal upon the basis of, in remembrance of, and for the exercise of communion with the crucified Christ. And the signs of bread and wine are excellently suited for this. In the East they were the ordinary components of a meal; they are still easily obtainable everywhere and at all times; they are the principal means for strengthening and gladdening the human heart, Ps. 104:15; and they are an eloquent symbol of believers’ communion with Christ and with one another, M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 43. Here it is immaterial whether the bread consists of wheat, rye, or barley and the wine is white or red; whether according to the usage of the Greek church the bread is eaten leavened or according to that of the Roman church unleavened; and whether according to the doctrine of the Armenian Christians the wine is used unmixed or, according to the positive pronouncement of Trent XXII, ch. 7, mixed with water. Christ determined or prescribed none of this. The Reformed did not even hesitate to say that, in case bread and wine were absolutely lacking, another food and drink—for example, rice and bread—might also be used as signs in the Supper, Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 732, 738; Moor V 575; M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 46. But arbitrary departure from Christ’s institution is not thereby declared permissible. As in this age, so also in the first centuries there were some Christians (Tatianites, Severians, Gnostics, Manichaeans, Aquarians) who from an ascetic principle replaced the wine with water at the Supper. But we must not be wiser than Christ, who expressly ordained wine as the sign of his blood and whose command in this was followed at all times by the Christian church, M. Vitringa VIII 1, pp. 71–78. For Harnack’s assertion that the custom of using water at the Supper was quite general in the first and second centuries and still had to be opposed in the fifth century, and that even Paul himself, in speaking of the cup, does not definitely have a cup of wine in mind, has been sufficiently refuted by Zahn, *Brot und Wein im Abendmahl der alten Kirche*, Erlangen, 1892; cf. also W. Schmidt, *Christl. Dogm.* II 465. The practice of Roman and Lutheran Christians of administering the bread in the form of a wafer is likewise to be rejected (*oblie, oblata*, because believers in ancient times themselves offered what was needed for the Supper; *hostie, hostia*, because the bread is a sign of Christ’s sacrifice). For although neither the quantity nor the quality of the bread is determined, the character of a meal must nevertheless be preserved, and this is almost entirely lost with the use of a small round wafer, Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 733; M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 49. Finally, the place and time in which the Supper was instituted and celebrated in antiquity also clearly bring out that it is a real meal. For Jesus instituted the Supper on the occasion when he reclined with his disciples at the Passover table. And in the first period the Supper was celebrated in connection with an ordinary meal, Acts 20:7, 11; 1 Cor. 11:21; in the public assembly of the congregation, 1 Cor. 10:17; 11:18, 20, 21, 33; and daily or at least every day of rest, Acts 2:46; 20:7. Only gradually was the Supper detached from the *agapae*, moved from the evening to the morning worship service, administered outside the assembly of the congregation also to the sick and dying in their homes, celebrated as the Mass altogether outside and without an assembly of the congregation, and the use of the Supper for believers fixed at three times or a single time per year as a minimum, *Trid.*, sess. 13, can. 9. Although some Reformed were of the opinion that in very special cases the Supper might also be administered to the sick in their residence, but then nevertheless in the presence of others—Calvin in Henry II 210; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 764—they nevertheless generally held to the idea that as a part of the *cultus publicus* it belonged in the assembly of the congregation and might not be celebrated privately. And although practice proved stronger than doctrine and the celebration of the Supper was ordinarily limited to six or four times a year—Dort, 1578, art. 73; Middelburg, 1581, art. 45; The Hague, 1586, art. 56; Dort, 1618, art. 63—Calvin’s original desire was nevertheless to celebrate it at least once a month, Kampschulte, *Joh. Calvin* I 460; cf. a Lasco in Dalton, 383; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 758–767, 801, 802; Moor V 660 sq., 671 sq.; M. Vitringa VIII 1, pp. 406–414. If baptism as incorporation into the Christian church already ought to take place in the public assembly of believers, this applies still more to the Supper, which is essentially a δειπνον, συναξις, *convivium* and includes not only communion with Christ but also communion among believers. Therefore the determination of the character of the Supper ultimately centers entirely upon the question whether it must be administered upon a table or an altar. Jesus and his disciples reclined at a table when they ate the Supper, and the first Christians likewise knew nothing of an altar. But gradually the distinction between the Old and New Testament dispensations was lost; the place of assembly was changed into a temple, the minister into a priest, the Supper into a sacrifice, and the table into an altar. In the Roman and Greek churches the whole worship is governed by this conception; the Anglican church largely adopted it and now increasingly inclines toward it; the Lutheran church retained the altar and regarded it as an *adiaphoron*. But the Reformed restored the scriptural idea of the Lord’s meal also in the table of the Supper. For the distinction between the worship of the Old and New Testaments consists in this, that temple and altar, priest and sacrifice, are no longer upon earth but in the heavens. The Jerusalem above is the mother of us all, Gal. 4:26. There Christ, the eternal high priest, entered for us, Heb. 6:20, after he had brought about an eternal redemption through one sacrifice, 9:12, to appear before the face of God for us, 9:24. And there Christians have their sanctuary, into which they enter with boldness in the blood of Jesus, 4:16; 10:19; 12:22. Here on earth we have only a mutual assembly in which there is no place for sacrifice, 10:25. The only altar of Christians is the cross upon which Christ brought his sacrifice, 13:10; cf. 7:27; 10:10. From that altar—that is, from the sacrifice brought upon it—they eat when through faith they have communion with Christ and his benefits. Believers have no other sacrifice to bring than sacrifices of praise, that is, the fruit of lips that praise his name, 13:15. The Supper is a sacrificial meal, a meal of believers with Christ upon the basis of his sacrifice, and therefore must be administered not upon an altar but upon a table. *Certissimum est, everti Christi crucem, simulac erigitur altare*, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 18, 3; Voetius, *Pol. eccl.* I 792; Moor V 659; M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 414; Herzog³, article “Altar.”
