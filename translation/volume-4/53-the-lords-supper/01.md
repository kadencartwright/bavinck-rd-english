# § 53. The Lord’s Supper

## 1.

Alongside baptism, the Supper comes as the second sacrament, which under the Old Testament had its type in the Passover. As among the Gentiles, Num. 25:2, so also in Israel meals were frequently connected with the sacrifices. Sometimes the sacrifices were entirely burned upon the altar of burnt offering, but with others only a portion was burned and the remainder reserved for consumption: either by the priests alone at the altar, Lev. 2:3, 10; 6:16, 25–30; 7:1–10; 10:12, 13, for atonement, Lev. 10:17; or by the officiating priest with his family in a clean place for his maintenance, Lev. 7:12–14, 31–34; 10:14; or by the one bringing the sacrifice with his family and guests, provided they were Levitically clean, outside the sanctuary, Lev. 7:19–21; Deut. 12:7, 12; 1 Sam. 9:13f.; 2 Sam. 6:19. The meaning of these meals was that God met with his people and, upon the basis of the sacrifice brought and accepted, united himself with his people in joy. In this sanctuary God comes to the children of Israel and dwells among them, Ex. 20:24; 29:42–46; 33:7; Num. 11:25; 12:5; 17:4; Deut. 31:15. He is the host who yields a portion of the sacrifice brought to him and invites his people to the meal; whoever participated in it entered into covenant with him. Participation in pagan sacrificial meals was therefore forbidden to Israel, Ex. 34:15. It was צמד, a binding oneself to, an associating oneself with the false gods, Num. 25:3, 5; Ps. 106:28. The apostles later forbade them to Christians, Acts 15:29; 21:25, or warned against them for the sake of the weak in faith, 1 Cor. 8:1f.; 10:18f. As such unions of God with his people, and also of the people with one another, these meals bore a character of gladness and joy and frequently did indeed give occasion for revelry and drunkenness, 1 Sam. 1:13; Isa. 28:8; Prov. 7:14, but on the other hand also served as images and pledges of the highest joy in God, Deut. 27:7; Ps. 22:26f.; Isa. 25:6; 62:8, 9; cf. article “Opfermalzeiten” in Herzog². Such a sacrificial meal occurred especially at Passover. Over against Rome many Protestants maintained that the Passover was entirely exhausted in this, Moor V 322; Witsius, *Oec. foed.* IV 9, 6. But this is undoubtedly incorrect. The Passover was first of all a *sacrificium* and thereafter a *sacramentum*. In Ex. 12:27; 34:25, it is called a זֶבַח for the Lord; the act accompanying it is called עֲבֹדָה, Ex. 12:26; and its celebration is called bringing a קָרְבַּן to the Lord in Num. 9:7. Further, after the householder had set apart four days before the feast, on the 10th of Abib or Nisan, a year-old, male, unblemished lamb, on the 14th of that month between the two evenings he had to slaughter it and with a bundle of hyssop smear some of the blood on the two doorposts and the lintel of the house door, Ex. 12:3f. This blood served for atonement. In itself Israel deserved death just as much as the Egyptians; but it is nevertheless not treated as Egypt is, but is graciously rescued by the Lord from death and delivered from the land of bondage. The blood had to be the sign of this. When the Lord saw that blood at the house door, he was reconciled, laid aside his wrath, and passed by sparingly (פסח, to step over something, to pass by, Isa. 31:5, hence פֶּסַח, πασχα), Ex. 12:13, 23, 27. The sacrificial character of the Passover is even more clearly evident from the manner in which it was later celebrated in Canaan. There the lamb is no longer slaughtered by the householder but by the Levites, 2 Chron. 30:16; 35:11; Ezra 6:19; the blood is sprinkled upon the altar by the priests, 2 Chron. 30:16; 35:11; the portions of fat are burned upon the altar, 2 Chron. 35:14; and the meal is held at the sanctuary, Deut. 16:2. But although the Passover is thus in the first place a sacrifice, it is nevertheless not exhausted in that; it immediately becomes a meal. After the lamb had been slaughtered between the two evenings on the 14th of Abib and its blood smeared upon the house door or in later times sprinkled upon the altar, it had to be roasted whole in the fire, without a bone being broken, with head, legs, and entrails. Thereafter in the same night of the 14th of Abib it had to be eaten with unleavened bread and bitter herbs by all who were in the house, thus also by the women, but not by uncircumcised strangers, foreigners, or hired servants; in haste, with loins girded, sandals on their feet, and staff in hand; in the house or later at the sanctuary; and what remained had to be burned with fire, Ex. 12:1–28, 43–49; 13:3–9; 23:15; Lev. 23:5–14; Num. 9:10–14; 28:16–25; Deut. 16:1f. The Passover thereby occupied an entirely distinctive place in Israelite worship. It was a sacrifice but thereafter immediately passed over into a meal. It did not belong to the sin offerings, for it was eaten; nor to the thank offerings, for atonement preceded the meal. It was moreover instituted by God on a particular occasion, before all other sacrifices, and bears a nature of its own. It is a sacrifice for atonement and a meal of communion with God and with one another; it is *sacrificium* and *sacramentum* at the same time. The New Testament ascribes a typological significance to this Passover, so that it is not only a remembrance of deliverance from Egypt but also a sign and pledge of deliverance from the house of bondage to sin and of communion with God in the promised Messiah. Jesus himself pointed to this when he deliberately connected the institution of the Supper with the celebration of Passover. But there is considerable disagreement about the manner in which he did this. Some appeal to the Synoptics and say that on Thursday, the 14th of Nisan, Jesus ate the actual Passover with his disciples and on that occasion instituted the Supper. Others side with John, 12:1; 13:1, 2, 29; 18:28; 19:14, 31, and maintain that on Thursday, the 13th of Nisan, Jesus held an ordinary meal with his disciples and washed their feet, and then on the 14th of Nisan, the actual day of the feast, died as the true Passover lamb. Whether one conforms the Synoptics to John or John to the Synoptics; leaves both unreconciled alongside each other; or, upon the basis of the testimony of the Quartodecimans in the second and third centuries, who for their practice of celebrating the Christian Passover feast in the evening of the 14th of Nisan appealed among others to the apostle John, denies the genuineness of the Fourth Gospel—cf. article “Passah,” Herzog² 11, 270; Meyer on John 18:28; Schäfer, *Das Herrenmahl nach Ursprung und Bedeutung*, 1897, pp. 53–99; Zahn, *Einl. in das N. T.* II 309f.—there is always agreement insofar as Jesus held a meal with his disciples on Thursday evening, died on Friday, and rose on Sunday. Even if this meal had not been the ordinary Passover of 14 Nisan but had been held a day earlier, on Thursday, 13 Nisan, there would still be no objection to assuming that Jesus, who would die the next day and therefore could not eat the Passover with the Jews at the ordinary time, ate it a day earlier and in this way connected the Supper with it. For the latter is raised above all doubt. Both Paul, 1 Cor. 10:16; 11:24, 25, and the Synoptics have Jesus institute the Supper in the closest connection with the Passover. From the Passover table he takes the bread and wine that must serve as signs and seals at the Supper. While he immediately adopts baptism from John, he waits to institute the Supper until the final Passover feast and then causes the covenant meal of the Old Covenant to pass over into that of the New Testament. And all this encounters no contradiction in John, because he is altogether silent concerning the institution of the Supper. Further, the date of Jesus’ death on 14 or 15 Nisan is entirely immaterial to the fact that Jesus died as the true Passover lamb. For not only the Gospel of John brings this out, 19:33, 36; but Paul too expressly says in 1 Cor. 5:7 that our Passover—namely, Christ—has been sacrificed and that therefore believers must purge out the old leaven of sin and, as ἀζυμοι, as new creatures unmixed with unrighteousness, ought to walk. To this is added that the lamb led to slaughter, Isa. 53:7, not improbably contains an allusion to the Passover lamb and is so applied to Christ in the New Testament, John 1:29, 36; Acts 8:32; 1 Pet. 1:19; Rev. 5:6; etc. Just as circumcision was a type of baptism and through Christ’s death passed over into that baptism, so the Passover pointed forward to the Supper and was thereby replaced according to Christ’s command. While the Passover, however, was still in the first place a *sacrificium*, the Supper has entirely lost this character. For the sacrifice brought in the Passover obtained its complete fulfillment in Christ’s death. And it is upon the basis of that once-for-all accomplished and perfect sacrifice that Christ institutes the new dispensation of the covenant of grace and invites and strengthens his disciples at his holy table.
