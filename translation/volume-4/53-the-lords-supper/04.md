## 4.

The Reformation unanimously rejected the Roman doctrine of transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the mass, of *asservatio, adoratio*, and *circumgestio* of the host, of withholding the cup and the use of the Latin language, but it soon diverged in its positive conception of the Supper. Luther taught at first that bread and wine were signs and pledges of the forgiveness of sins, which had been acquired through Christ’s death and was received through faith. But he soon went back on this and, especially from 1524 onward against Carlstadt and Zwingli, maintained on the basis of a synecdochical interpretation of the words of institution that the body of Christ, in accordance with the will and omnipotence of God and its own ubiquity, was really and substantially present in, with, and under the Supper, as his divine nature is in the human and as heat is in iron, and therefore was also eaten bodily by the unworthy, although to their own destruction: Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 163f., 290f.; II 116f., 511f. This doctrine passed into the Lutheran confessional writings, ed. Müller, pp. 41, 164, 248, 320, 365, 499, 538, 645, 779, and was developed more fully by the dogmaticians: Gerhard, *Loc.* XXI; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 176–255; Hollaz, *Ex.*, 1103–1141; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. luth. K.* § 55. But already in the century of the Reformation many held an entirely different opinion. Following Honius—cf. De Hoop Scheffer, *Gesch. d. Kerkherv.* I 86f.—Zwingli understood the words of institution figuratively and explained the little word “is” by “signifies,” just as everyone repeatedly does elsewhere, for example Gen. 41:26; John 10:9; 15:1. Bread and wine in the Supper are therefore signs of and reminders of the death of Christ, and believers, trusting in this, enjoy the body and blood of Christ in those signs. Zwingli decisively opposes the bodily presence of Christ in the Supper, but by this he by no means denies that Christ is spiritually present for faith. On the contrary, Christ is indeed truly enjoyed in the Supper, as John 6 clearly teaches, but Christ is present in the Supper and enjoyed in it in no other way than in the Word and through faith, that is, spiritually. This enjoyment of Christ consists in nothing other than trust in his death. *Spiritualiter edere corpus Christi nihil est aliud quam spiritu ac mente niti misericordia et bonitate Dei per Christum.* And whoever thus enjoys Christ through faith and then comes to the Supper also enjoys him there *sacramentaliter* in the signs of bread and wine. In the Supper we therefore confess our faith and express what we continually possess in and enjoy from Christ through faith; and we do this in remembrance of Christ, for the proclamation of and thanksgiving for his benefits: Zwingli, *Opera* II 1, p. 426; II 2, p. 1f.; III 239, 326, 459; IV 51, 68. A rupture and conflict soon arose between the German and Swiss Reformation, which were not resolved by the religious colloquy at Marburg or by the mediating efforts of Bucer on the Reformed side and Melanchthon on the Lutheran side. They did not understand one another. Both sides acknowledged that Christ, truly, with his divine and human nature, with his own body and blood, was present and enjoyed in the Supper, but they attached different meanings to this “true” presence. When Calvin appeared, reconciliation was no longer conceivable, although in his doctrine of the Supper he adopted a position between and above both parties. For Calvin decidedly stands on Zwingli’s side insofar as he entirely rejects every bodily, local, substantial presence of Christ in the signs of bread and wine. Such a presence conflicts with the nature of a body, with the true humanity of Christ, with his ascension, and with the nature of the communion that exists between Christ and his own and differs as widely as heaven from earth from a useless *oralis manducatio*. But Calvin is not otherwise satisfied by Zwingli; he has two objections in particular to his doctrine of the Supper: 1º that Zwingli allows the gift of God in the Supper to recede too much behind what believers do in it, and thus understands the Supper one-sidedly as an act of confession; and 2º that in eating Christ’s body he sees nothing other or higher than believing in his name, trusting in his death. Calvin therefore goes on to place himself on Luther’s side and says that Christ, although not bodily and locally, is nevertheless truly and essentially present and enjoyed in the Supper with his whole person, also with his body and blood. The difference between him and Luther concerns not the fact but only the manner of that presence. And eating Christ’s body in the Supper is not exhausted in believing, in trusting in his death. Eating is not one with believing, although it always comes about only through believing, but is rather its fruit, just as in Eph. 3:17 the indwelling of Christ in us indeed occurs through faith but is nevertheless distinct from that faith. Calvin is evidently concerned with the *unio mystica*, with the communion of believers with the whole person of Christ. This does not first come into existence through the Supper, for Christ is already the bread of our soul in the Word, but it is nevertheless granted to us *illustrius* in the Supper and sealed and confirmed in the signs of bread and wine. In the Supper there is communion not only with the benefits but also with the person of Christ, and again not only with his divine but also with his human nature, with his own body and blood; and this communion is called eating. This therefore consists neither in a bodily descent of Christ from heaven nor in a *mixtura vel transfusio carnis Christi cum anima nostra*, but in an elevation of our hearts toward heaven, in a union with Christ through the Holy Spirit, in a *communio* with his body, as a result of which Christ *spirat e carnis suae substantia vitam in animas nostras, imo propriam vitam in nos diffundit*: *Inst.* IV, ch. 17, and further *C. R.* 37, 461–524, 681–688; etc.; cf. my essay on Calvin’s doctrine of the Supper in *De Vrije Kerk*, 1887, pp. 459–487. Calvin’s conception is not clear in every respect, especially concerning communion with Christ’s own flesh and blood and the life flowing from it. Utenhove therefore requested him, not without reason, to refrain from more or less obscure expressions when he treated the Supper: Pijper, *Jan Utenhove*, Leiden, 1883, p. 40. And so there was also a difference of expression in the confession and teaching of the Reformed churches and theologians. Some, such as Bucer, sought rapprochement with the Lutheran conception and said that Christ’s body was present *substantialiter, quoad substantiam* in the Supper, M. Vitringa VIII 265, 299. But Calvin’s principal thought, that in the Supper a spiritual communion is exercised through the Holy Spirit with the person and thus also with the body and blood of Christ, and that believers are thereby fed and refreshed unto eternal life, was adopted in various Reformed confessional writings—*Tigur.; Gall.* 36; *Belg.* 35; *Cat. Heid.* 75–80; *Scot.* 21; *Helv.* II 21; *Westmon.* 29—and became common property of Reformed theology: Beza, *Tract. Theol.* I 30, 206, 211, 259; II 121; III 148; Martyr, *Loci Comm.*, 445; Zanchius, *Op.* VII 387; VIII 517; Junius, *Theses theol.*, disp. 52; Ursinus, *Tract. theol.*, 359; A Lasco, *Op.* I 190, 549; Polanus, *Synt. theol.* VI 56; Bucanus, *Instit. theol.*, 649; etc.; cf. M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 266; Heppe, *Dogm.*, 471.

But opposition to this Calvinistic doctrine of the Supper soon arose. The expression in *Conf. Gall.* 36, that Christ nourishes and gives us life *de la substance de son corps et de son sang*, aroused no small objection in the Swiss churches. In the year 1572 they addressed the national synod in France with a request that these words be altered, but found no hearing. Nevertheless, the objection persisted among many, and it increased when Bossuet in his *Exposition de la doctrine de l’église cath. sur les matières de controverse*, 1671, sought support in it for the Roman doctrine of the Supper, M. Vitringa VIII 1, p. 302 sq. Gradually the Zwinglian conception again gained ground, according to which eating was nothing other than believing and communion with Christ nothing other than receiving his benefits: Ostervald, *Verhandeling v. d. geopenb. godsd.*, 1742, p. 385. The weakening of discipline promoted this externalization of the Supper and caused the sacraments to be viewed merely as signs of an external covenant, to which all who lived without giving offense had a right and claim: Swarte, Van Eerde, Janssonius; cf. Ypey and Dermout, *Gesch. v. d. Ned. Herv. Kerk* III 612. Thus the way was prepared for rationalism, which repeated the ideas of Socinians, Remonstrants, Mennonites, and others—cf. M. Vitringa VIII 2, p. 1014 sq.—and saw in the Supper nothing higher than a memorial meal, an act of confession, and a means of moral education: Wegscheider, *Instit.* §§ 178, 179. Through Schleiermacher, however, who rejected not only the doctrine of the Roman Catholics but also that of the Socinians and others and acknowledged those of Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin all as orthodox, there awakened an effort to maintain the Supper as an objective means of grace and to ascribe to it a strengthening of the communion of life with Christ. This was done, however, in very different ways. Some took the Supper as their point of departure as a symbolic action and act of confession, but added that in it believers testified that they received Christ as having died for them: Doedes, *Leer der Zal.* § 144f.; *Ned. Gel.*, 502; *Heid. Catech.*, 352. Others understood it as an act of the church, but one that according to God’s ordinance serves, along an ethical and psychological way, to strengthen faith in the forgiveness of sins and communion with God: Lipsius, *Dogm.* § 853; Biedermann, *Chr. Dogm.* § 927; Pfleiderer, *Grundriss* § 156. Many *Vermittelungstheologen* reject the Lutheran consubstantiation and *manducatio oralis* and approach Calvin’s doctrine when they say that Christ is spiritually present in the Supper, as in the Word, and communicates himself and his benefits (forgiveness, life, spiritual power, salvation) to the believer: Kahnis, *Luth. Dogm.* II 339; Dorner, *Chr. Gl.* II 848f.; Müller, *Dogm. Abhandl.*, 467; Ebrard, *Dogm.* § 545; Nitzsch, *Ev. Dogm.*, 567–569; Schmidt, *Chr. Dogm.* II 471, 478. The old Lutheran doctrine was presented again by Scheibel, Rudelbach, Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 2, p. 260f., and was further supplemented by the Neo-Lutherans with the theosophical conception that through bread and wine Christ nourishes not only the soul but also the body directly, by healing the diseases of the body and strengthening the new human being of the resurrection, which was created in secret through baptism: Martensen, *Dogm.* § 265; Thomasius, *Christi Person u. Werk* II³ 327, 341; Hofmann, *Schriftbeweis* II 2, p. 220; Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 245; Frank, *Syst. d. chr. Wahrh.* II² 290; Rocholl, “Spiritualismus und Realismus,” *Neue Kirchl. Zeits.*, 1898; cf. Luther himself in Köstlin II 163, 516. Finally, in the English church the ritualist party increasingly adopted Rome’s doctrine and practices of the Supper: Wilberforce, *The Doctrine of the Holy Eucharist*, 1853; Pusey, *The Presence of Christ in the Holy Eucharist*, 1853; cf. Ryle, *Knots Untied*, 1886, p. 205; Williams, “The Crisis in the Church of England,” *Presb. and Ref. Rev.*, July 1899. The history of the doctrine of the Supper is treated further in the works on the history of dogma by Hagenbach, Münscher-v. Coelln, Harnack, Schwane, and others; the articles “Abendmahl,” “Transsubstantiation,” and “Messe” in Herzog²; “Abendmahl” and “Eucharistie” in Herzog³; Rückert, *Das h. Ab., sein Wesen u. seine Gesch. in d. alten Kirche*, 1856; Ebrard, *Das Dogma v. h. Ab. u. seine Gesch.*, 2 vols., 1845/6; Stahl, *Die luth. Kirche u. die Union*, Berlin, 1859; Kahnis, *Dogm.* II 360–438; and further literature in Dorner, *Chr. Gl.* II 848; Luthardt, *Komp. d. Dogm.* § 72.
