## 8.

Like baptism, the Supper was instituted only for believers. Jesus ate it only with his disciples. Whether Judas was still present or had left the room before the institution of the Supper cannot be said with certainty. Matt. 26:21–25; Mark 14:18–21; John 13:21–35 give the impression that Judas had gone away before that time, but Luke 22:21–23 relates the disclosure of Judas as betrayer after the institution of the Supper in vv. 19, 20. It is possible, however, that Luke does not adhere to chronological order here. But however this may be, the question has no dogmatic significance. If Judas ate the Supper, then he reclined at table as a disciple of Jesus. That is what he was and presented himself to be; what he inwardly plotted against Jesus in his heart remained his own responsibility, M. Vitringa VIII 347. Likewise, later the Supper was celebrated exclusively by believers within the circle of the congregation, Acts 2:42; 20:7. Unbelievers did have access to the assembly of the congregation in which the Word was ministered, 1 Cor. 14:22–24, but were excluded from that in which the *agapae* were held and the Supper celebrated, 1 Cor. 11:18, 20, 33. So it remained when in the second century the Supper was gradually detached from the *agapae* and took place in the same morning assembly as the ministry of the Word. The first part was accessible to all, but the second part began only when unbelievers, catechumens, excommunicated persons, and so forth had been removed. In this second portion of the worship service the sacraments were administered; and it was an ancient and widely diffused custom that those who were baptized after completion of the catechumenate received the Supper immediately thereafter. When infant baptism came into use, this custom was also followed with children and was in addition urged by the prevailing exegesis of John 6:53, according to which this verse concerned the Supper and this sacrament was thus as necessary to salvation as baptism. In the West, however, this custom again wore away, especially from the twelfth century onward, and was gradually declared unnecessary by different synods; cf. *Trid.* 21, ch. 4. But in the Greek and other Eastern churches it continued and even today the Supper is administered to newly baptized children in the form of a piece of bread dipped in wine, article “Kinderkommunion” in Herzog² 7, 671; M. Vitringa VIII 368, 612. But the magical conception of the Supper had still worse abuses as a consequence. The original simplicity was lost under all kinds of solemn ceremonies. The communicants had to prepare themselves for the Supper not only through self-examination but also through fasting, washing of hands, clothing, and so forth. The bread was received at first with the bare hand, later in a linen cloth or a golden dish, and still later, from the eleventh century onward, with the mouth and in a kneeling posture at the priest’s altar. The consecrated bread was not only consumed by the communicants in the church but also administered to the sick in their homes, given as a *viaticum* to the dying, and regarded as useful for warding off all kinds of disasters and obtaining all kinds of blessings and benefits. The operation of the sacrament extended not only to the living but also to the dead. From ancient times the custom existed of bringing sacrificial gifts not only for oneself but also for deceased relatives on the anniversary of their death and praying for their soul. And when the doctrine of purgatory had been established by Gregory the Great, the Supper understood as a sacrifice of Christ’s own body and blood, and the congregation’s participation increasingly diminished, it was soon established that the Mass could work a reduction of penances and temporal punishments not only for living persons present or absent but also for the deceased in purgatory, M. Vitringa VIII 733; Herzog² 9, 623f. All these corruptions made return to Holy Scripture necessary, according to which the Supper is a meal that cannot exist without guests reclining at table and is intended exclusively for believers. To proceed carefully and do full justice to Scripture in this respect, the Reformed ordinarily posed two questions: 1º who have the right to the Supper and must approach it, and 2º whom the church must admit to the Supper or bar from it, *Heid. Cat.* 81, 82. The first question deals with the duty of the communicants, the second with the duty of the church and its ministers. The answer given to the latter question, and the only answer possible according to Holy Scripture, was that the church had to bar from the Supper all who by their confession and life conduct themselves as unbelieving and ungodly persons. The Supper is a possession of the church, given by Christ to his congregation, and thus to be enjoyed only by members of the household of faith. The unbaptized, unbelievers, heretics, schismatics, public sinners, and excommunicated persons were thereby naturally excluded. But the number of those who had a right to the Supper was restricted much further. First, through the rejection of Mass and purgatory the administration of the Supper for the deceased was also abolished. Nothing of this kind occurs in Scripture in a single word. Paul does indeed speak in 1 Cor. 15:29 of those who had themselves baptized ὑπερ των νεκρων. But even if this passage had to be understood (which has by no means been proved; cf. Cremer³, 156) as meaning that at that time there were Christians who had themselves baptized for the benefit of friends who died unbaptized, even then the apostle uses this practice only as a proof of the resurrection and leaves it without approval or disapproval. At the Council of Carthage in 397 the church decisively condemned baptism for the dead, which was in use among some sects, and can therefore derive no argument from it for administering the Supper for the benefit of the deceased. Second, many Reformed objected to administering the Supper to the sick and dying in a private residence outside the public assembly of believers: Musculus, Bullinger, Beza, Danaeus, Aretius, and others; the Reformed churches of France, Scotland, the Netherlands, and so forth. Others, such as Calvin, Oecolampadius, Martyr, Zanchius, and the churches of England, Poland, Hungary, and so forth, did sometimes permit this. But even then they ordinarily restricted it so that a small assembly of believers had to be present and every occasion for superstition was thereby prevented or avoided, Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 758; M. Vitringa VIII 356; Moor V 660. Third, children too are excluded from the Supper. Trent condemned only the necessity, not the permissibility, of the Supper for children. And among the Reformed, Musculus too took this position in his *Loci Communes*, pp. 471–473. He adduced these grounds for it: that whoever possesses the thing signified also has the right to the sign; that children who, as baptism demonstrates, can receive the grace of regeneration can also without consciousness be nourished in that spiritual life; that Christ is Savior of his whole congregation, including the children, and feeds and gives them all to drink of his body and blood; that the admonition to self-examination in 1 Cor. 11:26–29 was not intended by the apostle as a general requirement. But all these grounds lose their weight over against the following considerations. 1º In the Old Testament there was a great difference between circumcision and Passover. Circumcision was prescribed for all male children, but Passover was celebrated not immediately at its institution but later in Palestine at the temple in Jerusalem; very young children were therefore naturally excluded from it. 2º Likewise there is a great distinction between baptism and the Supper. Baptism is the sacrament of regeneration, in which the human being is passive. The Supper is the sacrament of growth in the communion of Christ, of the nourishment of spiritual life, and presupposes conscious, active conduct in the one who receives it. 3º Christ instituted the Supper in the midst of his disciples, said to them all, “Take, eat, drink,” and presupposes that they accept the bread and wine from his hand. And Paul says that the congregation at Corinth came together to eat and gives no other impression than that only conscious adult persons participated in the Supper. 4º In 1 Cor. 11:26–29 the apostle specifically states the requirement that before the Supper one examine oneself so that one can discern the body of the Lord and not eat and drink unworthily. This requirement is stated altogether generally, addressed to all participants in the Supper, and therefore naturally excludes children. 5º Withholding the Supper from children deprives them of no single benefit of the covenant of grace. This would indeed be the case if baptism were withheld from them. For none can do this except one who believes that children stand outside the covenant of grace. But with the Supper the matter is otherwise. Whoever administers baptism but not the Supper to children acknowledges that they partake of the covenant and all its benefits. He withholds from them only a particular *way* in which the same benefits are signified and sealed, because this does not suit their age. For the Supper gives no single benefit that was not already granted beforehand in the Word and baptism through faith.

This distinction between baptism and the Supper soon made preparation for the worthy reception of the second sacrament necessary. In the apostolic age, when as a rule only adults were baptized, there was not yet such a preparation. Whoever heard and accepted the Word of the gospel was immediately baptized and admitted to the Supper. But when in the following century transitions to Christianity became more numerous but also less reliable, the catechumenate gradually arose, which first had to prepare for baptism and later, after infant baptism became general, for the Supper. In the Roman church this preparation was gradually absorbed entirely into the sacrament of confirmation, which developed out of the laying on of hands originally connected with baptism and united with an anointing. The Reformation rejected this sacrament because it had no ground in Scripture and placed catechesis and public profession in its stead; cf. Höfling, *Das Sakr. der Taufe* II 347f.; Bachmann, *Die Gesch. der Einführung der Confirmation*, Berlin, 1852; Caspari, *Die evang. Confirmation vornehmlich in der luth. K.*, Erlangen, 1890; article in Herzog² 8, 143. The transition from baptism to the Supper was thereby made, and at the same time the church was protected from corruption. Calvin wished a child, when sufficiently instructed in the catechism, publicly to profess his faith in the congregation, in Bachmann, loc. cit., 70. A Lasco desired that children who had become fourteen years old should make profession before the congregation and go to the Supper the following Sunday; but those who lived badly were admonished and finally, if they remained obstinate, cut off from the congregation at eighteen or twenty years of age, ibid., 115. The Dutch church orders likewise prescribe a profession before the church council or in the midst of the congregation and sometimes still speak of a prior examination before the church council. This theory was consistent: the children of believers are baptized as believers, then instructed in the truth, admitted to the Supper upon satisfactory examination and after public profession, or removed from the congregation after repeated admonition in case of unchristian doctrine or disorderly walk. Our ecclesiastical life is still to be ordered according to this doctrine, even though it encounters practical difficulties at every moment. For pietism and rationalism are always inclined to separate what God joined together and, with disdain for the sacrament, to place the emphasis upon personal conversion or ecclesiastical reception or confirmation. But the rule of the covenant is this: the church should educate her youthful members, who were born as children of the covenant and incorporated into her through baptism, to independent, personal profession and upon that ground admit them to the Supper. She does not and cannot judge the heart. Thus while on the one hand she bars from the Supper all who by confession or life conduct themselves as unbelieving and ungodly persons, on the other hand she never ceases the earnest preaching that the Supper is instituted only for those who are displeased with themselves because of their sins, nevertheless trust that these are forgiven them for Christ’s sake, and also desire increasingly to strengthen their faith and amend their life.
