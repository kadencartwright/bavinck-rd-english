## 6.

The Supper is therefore a real meal, but as such it nevertheless has a distinctive spiritual significance and purpose. Christ instituted it not to nourish us physically but spiritually. Before he distributes bread and wine, he blesses both and says that the bread is his body and the wine his blood; as such, as his broken body and shed blood, bread and wine must be taken and enjoyed by his disciples. The *materia sacramenti*, the thing signified in the Supper, is therefore the body and blood of Christ as it was broken and shed in his sacrificial death for his church unto the forgiveness of sins, that is, the crucified and dead Christ with all the benefits and blessings acquired through his death, *ipse Christus cum omnibus suis beneficiis*: Heppe, *Dogm.*, 466, 467. In the moral, rationalistic conception of the Supper this significance does not come into its own. For 1º the Supper is indeed also a memorial meal, but it is this only on the ground that Christ instituted bread and wine as signs of his body and blood. What matters in the Supper in the first place is not what we do but what God does. Above all, the Supper is a gift of God, a benefit of Christ, a means of his grace. If the Supper were nothing but a memorial meal and an act of confession, it would cease to be a sacrament in the proper sense; it could then be called a means of grace only secondarily and indirectly, like prayer. The Supper, however, stands in one line with Word and baptism and must therefore be regarded first of all, like these, as a proclamation and assurance of God’s grace to us. 2º Christ does not elevate bread and wine in general into signs of his body and blood, but does this specifically with respect to that bread and that wine which he holds in his hand and communicates to his disciples. And he does not say that in that bread and wine they must merely see his body and blood, but expressly declares that they must take, eat, and drink both as such. He makes it a meal in which the disciples enjoy his body and blood and thus enter into the most intimate communion with him. That communion does not consist only in their sitting together at one table, but they eat of one bread and drink of one wine; indeed, under the signs of bread and wine the host offers his own body and blood as food and drink for their souls. That is a communion far surpassing that in a memorial meal and act of confession. It is not merely a remembrance of or meditation upon Christ’s benefits, but is a most intimate bond with Christ himself, just as food and drink unite with our body. 3º In the Supper we receive no other or greater benefits, but also no fewer benefits, than in the Word. Now Jesus expressly said, John 6:47–58, that in the Word and through faith we eat his flesh and drink his blood and thus receive eternal life. Although John 6 does not directly treat the Supper, it may and can nevertheless serve to explain this second sacrament. Through the Word and in faith we receive such an intimate communion with Christ, with his body and blood, as arises between food and the one who enjoys it. And this is the teaching not only of John 6 but of all Scripture. The Word does not grant that communion in the literal sense, nor does faith, but God has bound himself to communicate his communion in Christ and all the benefits connected with it to the one who believes his Word. Calvin therefore rightly remarked against Zwingli that eating Christ’s body and drinking his blood is not exhausted in believing. Believing is a means, even a means that is temporary and one day passes into sight, but the communion with Christ that arises through it goes much deeper and endures into eternity. It is a *unio mystica* that can be made somewhat clear to us only under the images of vine and branch, head and body, bridegroom and bride, cornerstone and building; cf. vol. III 556. And it is this *unio mystica* that is signified and sealed in the Supper.

The Christian church has maintained this *unio mystica* in the Supper almost unanimously. Greek and Roman Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed Christians agree with one another in this, that in the Supper an objective and real communication of the person and benefits of Christ takes place to everyone who believes. But they differ greatly among themselves concerning the manner in which that communication occurs. The first-mentioned are not satisfied unless the body and blood of Christ are also physically and locally present in the signs and received and enjoyed by the bodily mouth. The Reformed, however, teach that Christ is indeed truly and essentially communicated to believers in the Supper, but in a spiritual manner and so that he can be received and enjoyed only by the mouth of faith. And for this Scripture supplies abundant proofs. 1º In the words τουτο ἐστι το σωμα μου, the subject τουτο can refer to nothing other than the bread that Jesus holds in his hand. The predicate is το σωμα μου and thereby designates Christ’s own body, which he assumed from Mary and surrendered to death for his own. The copula is ἐστι, which Jesus did not pronounce at all in Aramaic but which in any event joins two disparate concepts, bread and body, and thus cannot be a copula of actual *being*. Here, therefore, ἐστι must have a significative, figurative meaning, for *disparatum de disparato non potest praedicari nisi figurate*. The sentence contains a *tropus*, and this lies not in the subject or the predicate but, as Zwingli correctly saw, in the copula ἐστι, as is so often the case in Scripture, for example Gen. 17:13; 41:26, 27; Ex. 12:11; Ezek. 5:5; Luke 12:1; John 10:9; 15:1; etc.; Gal. 4:24; 1 Cor. 10:4; Heb. 10:20; Rev. 1:20; etc. And that such a *tropus* must be assumed in the words of institution is proved still further by the fact that, according to Luke and Paul, at the second sign Jesus does not say “this wine,” but “this cup is the new testament in my blood.” Even Roman Catholics and Lutherans are compelled to assume a *tropus* here. 2º If the subject τουτο refers not to the natural bread and the natural wine but already to the substance of Jesus’ body and blood, which are hidden under the form of or within the signs of bread and wine, then bread and wine have already been changed into Jesus’ body and blood or have already taken these into themselves before the words “this is my body, this is my blood” are pronounced, and those words lose all the power and value that Roman Catholics and Lutherans ascribe to them. For then transubstantiation or consubstantiation did not come about through those words but already preceded them; and the words on which so much emphasis is placed contain nothing but a declaration of what already exists and came about earlier. It is then difficult, indeed impossible, to say when and how transubstantiation or consubstantiation came about. There is indeed mention of a preceding blessing and thanksgiving, but their content is not stated in a single word; we have absolutely no knowledge of what Jesus said in them and thus also not of what we must say to bring about transubstantiation and consubstantiation. Moreover, when Paul says in 1 Cor. 10:16, “the cup that we bless is communion with the blood of Christ,” he proceeds from the assumption that the cup contains wine and not blood, for otherwise we could not bless it, and that as such, as containing wine, it is through the blessing communion with Christ’s blood. 3º The words Jesus spoke at the institution of the Supper were not intended as a fixed formula. This is evident from the fact that Matthew, Mark, Luke, and Paul transmit them in different readings and that the liturgical usage of the Christian churches displays all kinds of variation. According to the Greek church, the so-called epiclesis, the invocation of the Holy Spirit, belongs essentially to the words of consecration: Schwane, *D. G.* II 810; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Conf.* I 413; while according to Rome transubstantiation comes about through pronouncing the words *hoc enim est corpus meum*, in which the little word *enim* has been inserted arbitrarily and the words “which is broken for you” have been omitted arbitrarily. Still less can it be proved that the words—supposing that it were established which ones in particular had to be used—possess a consecratory, operative, transformative power. For Jesus does not say “this *becomes*,” but “this is my body,” and had therefore already separated the bread from common use and appointed it for a higher purpose through blessing and thanksgiving. 4º When Jesus instituted the Supper, he sat bodily at the table with his disciples. They therefore could not conceive the thought that with their bodily mouths they enjoyed Jesus’ own body and blood, and much less could they eat that body itself and drink that blood. It is no help to say with Philippi that they *über das Mass ihres gewöhnlichen Verständnisses durch den erleuchtenden Geist emporgehoben wurden*, *K. Gl.* V 2, p. 451, or with Hollaz, *Ex. theol.*, 1119, that Jesus sat at the table *naturali modo* but gave himself to be eaten *sacramentaliter*. For not only is none of this stated in Scripture, but the question concerns precisely the manner in which Jesus gave his body and blood to be enjoyed at the first Supper and may not be answered with a *petitio principii*. If the manner in which Roman Catholics and Lutherans, with their transubstantiation and consubstantiation, conceive this enjoyment of Jesus’ body and blood is excluded by the first Supper or can be maintained there only by an appeal to a miracle or by all kinds of evasions for which Scripture offers no ground, then it ought to be abandoned by the Christian who submits to God’s Word. And if no transubstantiation or consubstantiation and no *manducatio oralis* occurred at the first Supper, then it may not be assumed either at the Supper that the Christian church celebrates after Jesus’ death at his command and according to his institution. 5º Just as transubstantiation and consubstantiation conflict with his bodily sitting at the table, so they now conflict with his bodily ascension and his local abode in heaven. For if bread and wine at the Supper are changed into Jesus’ body and blood or take these into themselves, that body must descend from heaven or, according to the Lutheran doctrine of ubiquity—cf. Kübel, article “Ubiquität” in Herzog²—already be present everywhere beforehand. In the latter case, however, an act is still necessary by which the presence of Christ’s body in the Supper is brought about in a special manner, for ubiquity is obviously not sufficient for it. Luther, Brenz, and others therefore said that it is still something else *wenn Gott da ist und wenn er dir da ist. Dann aber ist er dir da, wenn er sein Wort dazu tut und bindet sich damit an und spricht: hie sollst du mich finden*, cited in Kübel, article “Ubiquität” in Herzog² 16, 123, 128. Thus at Rome and with Luther the Word effects such a presence of Christ’s body and blood in the Supper that he is present not only bodily in heaven but also on earth in the signs of bread and wine. And this presence of Christ in the Supper is then further conceived in such a way that Christ as a whole, not only according to his divine but also according to his human nature, is present in every Supper wherever and whenever it is celebrated; that he with his whole divine and human nature is present in every sign of the Supper, indeed in every particle of the bread and in every drop of the wine, *totus in tota hostia et in qualibet parte*. This is an endless multiplication of Christ, which directly conflicts with the teaching of Scripture concerning his human nature, his ascension, and his abode in heaven. For certainly that human nature was glorified at the resurrection and ascension, but was not therefore deprived of its essential properties of finitude and limitation, vol. III 414f. Jesus institutes the Supper precisely in remembrance of himself because he is going away and soon will no longer be bodily with his disciples, as he expressly declares elsewhere too, Matt. 26:11. And at the ascension he went away and was taken up, Acts 1:9–11, into heaven, which is a place, John 14:2, 4; 17:24; Acts 7:56; Col. 3:1; Eph. 4:10; Heb. 7:26, to remain there until his *parousia*, Acts 1:11; Phil. 3:20; 1 Thess. 1:10; 4:16. 6º But even if Christ were bodily and locally present in the Supper, one cannot see why this is necessary or useful. The benefit of the *manducatio oralis* can in no way be demonstrated; cf. Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 516. Suppose that with our bodily mouth we eat Jesus’ own body—what benefit do we have from it? What matters in the Supper, after all, is that our soul, our spiritual life, is nourished and strengthened. And this by its nature cannot occur by eating Christ’s body with the bodily mouth; for what we eat with it partly passes into components of our body and is partly expelled. More recent theologians have therefore conceived the idea that the *manducatio oralis* of Christ’s body planted within us the seed of a new body, a resurrection body. But this conception runs entirely against Scripture and is the fruit of a false theosophy. Yet if this fruit is not connected with eating Christ’s body, no other can be identified. The *manducatio oralis* is useless and empty and at bottom, however much one may dispute it, Capernaistic. The Capernaumites could imagine no eating of Jesus’ flesh other than with the bodily mouth, John 6:41, 52. And although Roman Catholics and Lutherans certainly assume a spiritual eating of Jesus’ body, they nevertheless connect this with, and make it dependent upon, bodily eating, without making the manner of that connection or the nature of that dependence even somewhat clear. Jesus, however, intended in the entire discourse he gave against the Capernaumites nothing other than a spiritual eating, an eating through faith, and did not for a moment mention bodily eating.

All the objections mentioned thus far apply to both Roman Catholic and Lutheran doctrine; against the former the following are added. 7º Transubstantiation is decisively contradicted by the testimony of our senses, by sight and touch, by smell and taste. And our senses have a right to speak here, because bread and wine fall within their reach and can and may be judged by them. If they observe carefully, they are also just as trustworthy here as elsewhere, because otherwise skeptical nominalism stands at the door and all certainty of faith and science disappears. Rome too must therefore yield to their testimony but has devised the claim that the *substantia* changes and the *accidentia* remain the same. How this is to be conceived remains unanswered. At Cana the water became wine, but in such a way that substance and accidents changed. Accidents also cannot be separated from the substance and conceived as resting in themselves, for they then cease to be accidents and themselves become substances. Moreover, in the bread and wine of the Supper all the accidents remain unchanged, both those perceived by smell and taste and those perceived by sight and touch: weight, solidity, color, corruptibility, nutritive power, and so forth—all remains. What then still remains for the substance that can change and has changed? 8º Transubstantiation conflicts with the pair of signs ordained by Jesus at the Supper. Although formerly, too, at times in the administration of the Supper in private homes, to the sick, to prisoners, to anchorites, to *virgines abstemiae*, and in *missis praesanctificatorum*, only the sign of bread was distributed, and conversely to infants, as still today in the Greek church, only the wine was administered, it was nevertheless only from the twelfth century onward that the custom came into vogue of withholding the cup from the laity, and only the Council of Constance in 1415 elevated this custom into ecclesiastical law. Despite the opposition offered to it by the Hussites and the adherents of the Reformation, the Council of Trent too gave its approval to withholding the cup and was led to do so *gravibus et justis causis*, sess. 21. The synod does not enumerate these reasons, but they are nevertheless easy to surmise. Besides the reluctance of some to drink from one cup with others, aversion to wine, the danger of spilling and thus dishonoring the sacrament, and so forth, the Roman Catholic Church was moved to withhold the cup especially by the desire to elevate the priestly estate above the laity and by the conviction that each sign and every part of it was changed into the whole Christ: Oswald, *Die dogm. Lehre v. d. h. Sakr.* I 501. Transubstantiation makes a second sign in the Supper entirely superfluous; in the bread alone and even in its smallest fragment the whole substance of grace is already contained. Through this the Roman Catholic Church comes into conflict with a direct command of Christ, Matt. 26:27, against which its appeal to the conjunction ἠ in 1 Cor. 11:27 is of no avail, and at the same time, by its own acknowledgment, with the custom of the Christian church in the first centuries. It knows how to defend itself only with the claim that it possesses power to proceed as it sees fit in distributing the sacraments, *Trid.*, sess. 21, ch. 2. Conversely, the institution of the Supper under two signs is a strong proof that transubstantiation is not the teaching of Scripture. Together they set the crucified Christ before our eyes and communicate him to believers, not in a bodily but in a spiritual manner, not in and under but simultaneously with the signs; together they form one sacrament, as images and pledges of the one spiritual good, communion with Christ and his benefits. 9º Finally, transubstantiation is further refuted by the idolatrous practices that followed it. Although the mass was prepared by the ideas of sacrifice and priest that were already early connected with the Supper, it is nevertheless essentially built upon the doctrine of transubstantiation developed only in the Middle Ages. And this is presupposed just as much by *asservatio, adoratio*, and *circumgestio*. Through the doctrine of the change of substance, the Supper passed over into the mass and was thereby entirely deprived of its original character. Although the *communio* has continued to exist despite all kinds of restriction, the mass has nevertheless become the center of Roman worship. It is therefore nothing less than the most complete elaboration of the Roman idea that the church with her priesthood is the mediatrix of salvation, the God-man continually realizing himself on earth. In the mass Christ repeats continually and ever anew his sacrifice on the cross; in it he sacrifices himself essentially and truly, although in an unbloody manner, and thereby effects with God that the fruits of his sacrifice on the cross—which were acquired there only altogether generally and in the abstract—are now applied, cf. *Trid.* 22, ch. 1, to all who live in the communion of the church, whether on earth or in purgatory, whether they are present or absent at the mass, whether they desire it for themselves or for others, for spiritual or bodily need, for the forgiveness of sins and for the prevention or averting of illness and accident, drought and flood, war and cattle plague, and so forth. This is what Rome has made of the Supper of our Lord Jesus Christ! The grounds, which surely in so weighty a doctrine as the mass ought to be abundantly present and unshakably firm, are altogether lacking. In Gen. 14:18, where Melchizedek offers Abraham bread and wine for refreshment, not a word is said about a sacrifice, even though it is immediately followed not by “for” but by “and he (Melchizedek) was a priest of God Most High.” Mal. 1:11 perhaps does not even treat the future; but even if this is the case, this passage describes only in Old Testament forms that the Lord’s name will be great among the Gentiles and that incense and a pure offering (מִנְחָה, offering in general) will be brought to him; and in the New Testament these forms are replaced precisely by prayer and by the spiritual sacrifice of believers, Rom. 12:1. At the institution of the Supper Jesus did indeed say, τουτο ποιειτε εἰς την ἐμην ἀναμνησιν, Luke 22:19, but that in doing so Jesus instituted the Supper as a sacrifice and elevated the disciples to priests is not in the least contained in these words, and it is certainly not proved by the fact that the Hebrew עשׂה and the Latin *facere* are sometimes used in the sense of sacrificing. In 1 Cor. 10:21 Paul sets the table of the Lord over against the table of demons; but because the table of demons was an altar, it by no means follows that the table of the Lord is also an altar upon which sacrifice must be offered. These are the principal and strongest proofs Roman Catholics can adduce from Scripture for their doctrine of the mass. And their appeal to the church fathers is little stronger, for in doing so they forget that when the fathers applied the idea of sacrifice to the Supper they attached to it an entirely different sense from that which Rome later connected with it. And over against all these specious arguments stands a series of proofs that set the unscriptural character of the mass in the clearest light. Everything resembling the mass is utterly foreign to the institution and the celebration of the Supper customary in the apostolic church. The eternity of Christ’s priesthood, Heb. 5:6; 7:17, 21–25, and the perfection of his sacrifice on the cross, Heb. 7:27; 9:12, 28; 10:10, 12, 14, make a repetition of his self-sacrifice, even if unbloody, superfluous and unlawful. All the benefits of grace—forgiveness, sanctification, redemption, the whole of salvation—were acquired through the sacrifice on the cross and neither can nor need be supplemented. Indeed, since Christ once sacrificed himself on the cross and surrendered himself to death, he cannot even do this a second time; his sacrifice in death is incapable of repetition, Heb. 9:26–28. His priestly activity does indeed still continue in heaven, but it consists not in any propitiatory sacrifice but in his intercession and appearance before God’s face on behalf of his people, Heb. 7:25; 9:24; and in that intercession and appearance before God’s face, the sacrifice accomplished on the cross always continues to work for the benefit of his own. Because Christ himself lives in heaven to pray for believers and in that intercession causes his sacrifice to continue its work, there is therefore no place for a repetition of his sacrifice on earth, vol. III 419f. His state of exaltation, his elevation above all suffering, pain, and death, and his royal dominion as head of the church directly conflict with a *sacrificium propitiatorium* and *impetratorium* that he would still have to offer on earth every day and in thousands of places. However much Rome may claim that the unbloody sacrifice does not weaken the bloody sacrifice on the cross but makes it effective, in fact it is nevertheless a denial of the one sacrifice on the cross; for a sacrifice that does nothing other than cause the fruits of another sacrifice to be enjoyed is a palpable absurdity. If the sacrifice on the cross is sufficient, others are superfluous; if these are necessary, the first is imperfect. And this is confirmed by Roman practice: the believer’s attention is directed away from Christ and his cross and toward the priest and his mass. For the least grace the Roman Catholic Christian is dependent upon the priest and the church. He cannot do without them for a moment. In theory it is maintained that Christ acquired all grace, but in practice grace is apportioned successively, bit by bit, by the priest. In Rome’s hands the Supper has become a *tremendum mysterium* that keeps believers in a state of immaturity, binds them for their whole life and welfare to the hierarchical priesthood, and causes them to kneel in idolatrous adoration before a god of their own making.
