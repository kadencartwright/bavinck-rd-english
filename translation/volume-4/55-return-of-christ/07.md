## 7.

Only a few passages seem to conflict with this consistent teaching of Scripture and to contain something else. The first passage is Matt. 23:37–39; Luke 13:33–35, where Jesus says to the inhabitants of Jerusalem that their house will be left desolate and that they will not see him until they say: Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord. Here Jesus does indeed express the expectation that the Jews will one day, namely at his return, acknowledge him as Messiah. If a millennium and a conversion of Israel coinciding with it were established from elsewhere, this passage could be explained accordingly. But since this is not the case, not even in Rev. 20, as will appear later, only the acknowledgment of the Messiah by the Jews at Christ’s return for judgment can be meant here. And until then, Jesus expressly says, Jerusalem will be left desolate; thus in any event Jesus does not expect a rebuilding of city and temple before his return. Second, Luke 21:24 must be considered, where Jesus says that Jerusalem will be trampled by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled. The conjunction ἀχρι οὑ does not yet imply that, when the point in time indicated by it arrives, the opposite will occur, namely that Jerusalem will be rebuilt and inhabited by the Jews. But even if this were so, Jesus still does not thereby say that the trampling of Jerusalem will end before his parousia, for immediately after pronouncing judgment upon Jerusalem he proceeds to discuss the signs before and at his return, Luke 21:25f.; the times of the Gentiles continue until his return. Again, if the New Testament taught a twofold return of Christ, this passage could be explained in accordance with it, but it will presently become clear that there is no basis for this in the New Testament. The third text that comes up for discussion here is Acts 3:19–21. There Peter exhorts the Jews to conversion, so that their sins may be blotted out and so that καιροι ἀναψυξεως, seasons of refreshing, may come from the presence of the Lord, and that he, namely God, may send the Christ Jesus appointed for you (the Jews), whom heaven must receive until the times of the restoration of all things. Some think that the seasons of refreshing spoken of here will dawn when the Jewish people is converted and all things are restored to their original purpose in the millennium, and that they will then continue until the second return of Jesus. But there are grave objections to this interpretation. The χρονοι ἀποκαταστασεως παντων can hardly be understood as the restoration of the natural and moral relations that the chiliasts expect in the millennium, for it is clearly stated that these times mark the endpoint of Jesus’ stay in heaven; until then, therefore, Jesus remains at the Father’s right hand, and since Scripture knows only one return of Christ, the times of the restoration of all things coincide with the consummation of the world; moreover, ἀποκαταστασις παντων is far too strong an expression for that restoration of the Jewish kingdom which chiliasm expects. The seasons of refreshing, therefore, are not identical with the times of the restoration of all things but precede them. For Peter indicates a twofold purpose of the conversion of the Jews: that seasons of refreshing may dawn for them and that God may send them the Christ appointed for them. The seasons of refreshing fall before the return of Christ and then refer either to the spiritual peace that follows from conversion and the forgiveness of sins or to particular future seasons of divine blessing and favor. The latter is the most probable because the seasons of refreshing are connected not immediately with the blotting out of sins but with the sending of Christ. And the thought Peter expresses here is then this: Repent, O Jews, for the blotting out of your sins, so that for you also as a people, who delivered up, denied, and killed Christ, vv. 13–15, seasons of refreshing may dawn from the presence of God, and that afterward God may send from heaven that Christ who was appointed in the first place for you and therefore also came first to you, v. 26, in order to restore all things also for your salvation. Whether such seasons will ever dawn for the Jews Peter does not say; that depends on their conversion, and whether this is to be expected is not mentioned here by a single word.

The last passage is Rom. 11:11–32. In Rom. 9–11 Paul treats the tremendous problem of how God’s promise to Israel is to be reconciled with the rejection of the gospel by the overwhelming majority of Israel. In the first place, the apostle answers that God’s promise applies not to the fleshly but to the spiritual seed of Abraham, and he develops this at length in Rom. 9 and 10. And second, he observes that God still always has his elect among Israel and therefore has not cast off that people; he himself is proof of this, and many with him; however many may have been hardened and blinded, the elect have nevertheless obtained salvation, and there is always a remnant according to the election of grace, Rom. 11:1–10. But this hardening, which has come over the greater part of Israel, is nevertheless not God’s final purpose; rather, in his hand it is a means of bringing salvation to the Gentiles, so that they, receiving that salvation in faith, may in turn provoke Israel to jealousy, 11:11–15. After admonishing the believers from among the Gentiles not to exalt themselves because of this, vv. 16–24, Paul develops this thought further and says that hardening has come upon a part of Israel until the *pleroma* of the Gentiles, the full number of those from among them appointed for salvation, has come in. And in this way all Israel will be saved according to God’s promise. Thus, with respect to the gospel, the unbelieving Jews are now indeed hated by God, so that the salvation rejected by them might come to the Gentiles; but with respect to election, they are beloved for the sake of the fathers, for God’s promises are irrevocable. As it has gone with the Gentiles, so it will also go with the hardened Jews; the Gentiles were formerly disobedient and have now received mercy, and so too the Jews are now disobedient so that, through the mercy shown to the Gentiles, they also may receive mercy. For God has shut up all, Gentiles and Jews, under sin so that he might have mercy on them all, vv. 25–32. Most interpreters think that the question whether God has cast off his people, 11:1, is not fully answered by the fact that God always preserves his elect among Israel, who are brought in successively in the course of the centuries, 11:1–10, and they therefore judge that everything following in chapter 11 is not merely a further explanation but a supplement to the answer given in vv. 1–10, a new answer that first fully refutes the objection that God would have cast off his people. By πας Ισραηλ in v. 26, therefore, they understand the people of Israel as a whole, which will be converted in the last days. But however general this interpretation may be, there are weighty objections to it. *a.* If it were the apostle’s intention to give a new, supplementary answer in 11:25–32, he would bring his argument at the end into conflict with its beginning and point of departure. For in 9:6f. he has said that God’s promises have not failed because they apply to the spiritual seed of Abraham and always obtain their fulfillment in this spiritual seed, 11:1–10. *A priori* it is very improbable that Paul would later have returned to this argument and supplemented and corrected it in the sense that God’s promises do not come to their full fulfillment in this salvation of spiritual Israel but are first completely realized when a national conversion of Israel takes place in the last time. *b.* In any event, in chapter 9:1–10:11 not a single word is said about such an expectation for the people of Israel, and there is not a single expression that suggests and prepares for it. And chapter 11:11–24 likewise contains nothing yet that points to such an expectation. Many do indeed understand 11:11–15 in this sense. But even if these words are to be understood not hypothetically, as an element in the argument, but as a description of a fact, they still contain only this thought: Israel’s rejection of Christ has been a great gain for the Gentiles, for through it the reconciliation accomplished by Christ’s death has become the portion of the Gentiles; then Israel’s acceptance by God will be a much greater gain for the Gentiles, for when Israel has reached its *pleroma*, its full number of elect, and the *pleroma* of the Gentiles has also come in, this will result in life from the dead, the resurrection from the dead of the new humanity. To Israel’s ἡττωμα the Gentile world owes its reconciliation mediately; to Israel’s πληρωμα it will one day owe its life from the dead. *c.* If Paul wished to communicate a new fact in 11:25, the manner in which he does so is most surprising. For he does not say: *and then*, *afterward*, namely after the fullness of the Gentiles has come in, all Israel will be saved, but: και οὑτως πας Ισραῃλ σωθησεται, and *in this way* all Israel will be saved. That can mean nothing other than: in the way described in the preceding verses. Immediately before, in v. 25, Paul has said that hardening has come upon Israel always only *in part*, ἀπο μερους. The believers from among the Gentiles could indeed begin to think, as Israel formerly did, that they alone were God’s elect people and that Israel had been entirely rejected. But Paul says that this is not so. No, Israel as such has not been rejected; among them there is always a remnant according to the election of grace; some branches have indeed been broken off, in whose place the wild olive tree of the Gentile world has come, but the trunk of the cultivated olive tree has remained; hardening has come upon Israel only *in part*; while the *pleroma* from the Gentiles comes in, the *pleroma* from Israel is also brought in; and in this way all Israel is saved. *d.* This fact, namely that hardening has come upon Israel only *in part*, Paul calls a μυστηριον, 11:25. Elsewhere he so often calls by this name the fact that the Gentiles are now fellow heirs and fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God, and here he indicates with the same word the fact that the Jews are only partly hardened and that God continually brings many elect from among them into his church. For that *partial* hardening will continue until the *pleroma* of the Gentiles has come in. Never, even to the end of the ages, will God cast off his ancient people entirely; always, alongside a part from the Gentile world, a part from Israel too will be brought to faith in Christ. The Gentiles, but also the Jews, had deserved something altogether different. Yet this is the great mystery: that God is rich in mercy; that from every people, including the people of the Jews who rejected him, he gathers his elect; that he shut up all under sin so that he might have mercy on all. That mystery carries the apostle into rapture and causes him adoringly to worship the depth of God’s wisdom and knowledge, 11:33–36. *e.* Πας Ισραηλ in 11:26, therefore, is not the people of Israel, who will be converted en masse at the end of the days; nor is it the church from Jews and Gentiles together, but it is the *pleroma* that is brought in from Israel in the course of the centuries. Israel, Paul predicts, continues to exist as a people alongside the Gentiles; it will neither perish nor disappear from the earth; it remains until the end of the ages, supplies its *pleroma* for the kingdom of God just as do the Gentiles, and retains its special task and place for that kingdom of God; from *all* peoples and nations and tongues the church of God is gathered. *f.* How great that *pleroma* from Israel will be Paul does not calculate. It is quite possible that the number of the elect from Israel in the last times will be much greater than it was in Paul’s day or in later times or in our day; there is not a single reason to deny this; rather, the spread of the gospel among all peoples gives reason to expect that an ever greater number will be saved both from Israel and from the Gentiles. But this is not what Paul means to say; he does not count but weighs. From the Gentile world will come the full *pleroma*, and also from Israel, and that *pleroma* will be πας Ισραηλ. In that *pleroma* all Israel is saved, just as in the church as a whole all humanity is saved. *g.* A conversion of Israel other than in the way indicated by Paul can also hardly be conceived. For what is a national conversion, and how and when will it take place in Israel? There is of course nothing against the expectation that a very great number from Israel will yet be brought to faith in Christ; rather, the continued existence of the people of Israel, viewed in connection with prophecy, argues for it. But however great this number may be, it remains a remnant according to the election of grace. Even the most ardent chiliast will surely not think that at the end all Jews without exception will be converted. And even if he assumed this, thinking that only in this way would Rom. 11:26 be completely fulfilled, such a national conversion at the end would still not benefit the millions of Jews who, throughout the centuries up to that end time, died in unbelief and hardening. If one really thinks that God’s promise to Israel is truly fulfilled only when not an ἐκλογη from the people but the people itself is brought in, then one comes into conflict with history. Always, throughout all the ages, even in the days of the Old Testament when national Israel was God’s people, it was only a small part of the people that truly served and feared God. And this is the case not only among the Jews but also among the Gentiles. It is always a remnant according to the election of grace that obtains salvation in Christ from among the Christian peoples. Moreover, in Paul’s outline of the future no room remains for such a national conversion of Israel as the chiliasts expect. For he expressly says that hardening has come upon a part of Israel *until* the *pleroma* of the Gentiles has come in and that not *afterward* but *in this way* all Israel is saved, Rom. 11:25, 26. The hardening of a part of Israel therefore lasts until the *pleroma* from the Gentile world has been brought in, and according to the chiliasts the national conversion of Israel must then occur afterward. But is there still an interval of time between the entrance of the *pleroma* of the Gentile world and the end of the ages? If so, are there then still Gentile peoples in that time, and is no one among them converted any longer? The entrance of the *pleroma* of the Gentiles cannot be conceived as temporally preceding the salvation of all Israel. Rom. 11:26 names no new fact that takes place *after* the entrance of the fullness of the Gentiles. But the entrance of the fullness of the Gentiles and the salvation of all Israel run parallel because hardening has come upon Israel only in part. Finally, it should also be noted that even if Paul expected a national conversion of Israel at the end, he still does not mention in a single word a return of the Jews to Palestine, a rebuilding of city and temple, or a visible reign of Christ; in his picture of the future there is no place for any of this.
