## 9.

Now it is very remarkable that this final victory over Satan takes place in two stages. First he is bound for a thousand years and cast into the abyss, and afterward he again deceives the nations, wages war against the church, and is then defeated for good and cast into the lake of fire and sulfur, 20:1–10. Apart from the Old Testament, the advocates of chiliasm find their strongest support in this pericope, while its opponents are considerably perplexed by it and have tried all their exegetical skill upon it. The thought that after the defeat of the world empire one final attack by the nations still had to be repelled was undoubtedly borrowed by John from Ezekiel. This prophet expects that Israel, after it has returned to its land and dwells there securely, will be attacked once more by Gog from the land of Magog, prince of Rosh, Meshech, and Tubal—that is, by the people of the Scythians—in alliance with all kinds of other peoples from the north, east, and south. The attack ends, however, when God himself in his wrath destroys these peoples on the mountains of Israel, chs. 38 and 39. In ch. 38:17 the Lord says that he had already spoken earlier of those peoples through the ministry of his prophets. And indeed earlier prophets had already proclaimed that not only those historical peoples in whose midst Israel lived and with whom it came into contact, but also all the far-distant Gentiles would be judged by the Lord on his day, Joel 2:32; 3:2, 11f.; Mic. 4:5, 11; 5:6–8; Isa. 25:5–8; 26:21; Jer. 12:14–16; 30:23, 24. A similar prophecy occurs very clearly in Zechariah, who in chs. 12–14 depicts how, toward the day of the Lord, Jerusalem will be besieged by the nations and how these will then be judged by the Lord. And Daniel not only sees in Antiochus Epiphanes the embodiment of the world empire hostile to God, but also expects that this power hostile to God will once more exalt itself and so become ripe for judgment, 11:40f. Prophecy thus had a twofold expectation: first, a victory of the people of God over the nations in whose midst it dwelt, and afterward a triumph over the nations that had not yet appeared upon the stage of world history. This double expectation passed into the apocryphal literature, Schürer, *Gesch. des jüd. Volkes* II³, 532, 551, but also into the New Testament. Naturally, the first expectation stands in the foreground. The appearance of Christ arouses the antichristian principle. Jesus speaks of ψευδοπροφηται and ψευδοχριστοι, who will set themselves against him and his kingdom, Matt. 7:15; 24:5, 24; Mark 13:21, 22; Luke 17:23. To temper the impatience of the Thessalonians in their expectation of Jesus’ speedy return, Paul points out in 2 Thess. 2 that this day of Christ will not dawn unless the apostasy first comes and the man of sin is revealed. He cannot yet come now, since there is something restraining him. Even now το μυστηριον της ἀνομιας is indeed at work, but nevertheless the man of sin cannot come until the one who restrains him is taken out of the way. Only then will the ἀνομος be revealed, but he will also immediately be destroyed by Jesus. The Apocalypse sees the antichristian power embodied in the beast from the sea—that is, the Roman empire, which has the city of Rome as its center and a particular emperor as its head—and alongside it in the beast from the earth, that is, the false prophecy that seduces people into paying homage to the world empire and its emperor. In his letters John is the first to call this opponent of Christ by the name αντιχριστος, in 1 John 2:18 probably even without the article; and he sees its essence realized in those who deny in principle the coming of Christ in the flesh, 1 John 2:22; 4:2, 3; 2 John 7. The representations of the antichrist in Scripture therefore differ. Daniel sees his type in Antiochus Epiphanes. Jesus detaches the antichristian principle from the Old Testament opposition between Israel and the nations and sees it embodied in many false Christs and many false prophets who will arise after and against him. Paul has the man of sin arise from a general apostasy and calls him the ἀνομος and the ἀντικειμενος, namely, of Christ, but also depicts him, with traits borrowed from Daniel, as the one who exalts himself above everything called God and everything venerated by human beings, so that he seats himself in God’s temple as a god and displays himself as a god. John in his letters considers the antichrist to have come in the heretics of his day. And the Apocalypse sees his power developing in the world empire supported by false prophecy. From this it appears that the antichrist must not be thought of exclusively as one person or as a group of persons—for example, the heretics of the first centuries, the Roman empire, Nero, the Jews, Muhammad, the pope, Napoleon, and so forth. Scripture clearly teaches that the antichristian power has its history, manifests itself in different ways at different times, and finally develops fully in a general apostasy and breaking of all natural and moral bonds that still restrain such an apostasy today, and then embodies itself in a world empire that enlists the false church in its service and deifies itself in the deification of the head of that empire. Christ himself then puts an end to this antichristian power in its highest and final development by his appearance. But with this the complete victory has not yet been obtained. The antichristian principle can naturally arise only among those nations that have known the gospel and finally rejected it in conscious and deliberate hostility. But there always have been, there still are, and until the end of the days there will be peoples who, like severed branches, stand outside the history and culture of humanity. Jesus does say in Matt. 24:14 that the end first comes after the gospel has been preached in the whole inhabited world as a testimony to all nations. But this prophecy does not imply that Christianity will one day be the dominant religion among all peoples or that it will be known to every human being, one by one. For history teaches that millions of human beings and numerous peoples, also in the centuries after Christ’s coming to earth, have died without having had any knowledge of the gospel. Jesus’ word means only that the preaching of the gospel will penetrate to all peoples and in no way precisely determines the degree to which or the limit as far as which this will happen. The prophecy is also not fulfilled all at once but successively through the course of the centuries, so that many peoples who formerly walked in the light of the gospel were later deprived of it again. While in this nineteenth century the gospel is spreading among the Gentiles, apostasy is increasing rapidly among the Christian peoples. And therefore it is more than probable that toward the time of the parousia many peoples on earth will once again be deprived of the knowledge of Christ. The twentieth chapter of John’s Revelation takes account of this fact. Because it speaks of a thousand-year binding of Satan and of a living and reigning with Christ by the martyrs during that time, many have believed that the so-called thousand-year kingdom is taught here plainly and incontrovertibly. But in fact this is an interpretation of Rev. 20 not according to the analogy of Scripture but according to the analogy of apocryphal literature. Rev. 20 in itself contains nothing of all that belongs to the essence of the chiliastic faith. For 1º not a single word mentions a conversion and return of the Jews, a rebuilding of the city of Jerusalem, a restoration of temple and worship, or an initial renewal of the earth. Rather, all this is excluded. For even if the 144000 in ch. 7 were to be understood as the *pleroma* from Israel and distinguished from those in 14:1, nothing other and nothing more would thereby be meant than that many Jewish Christians too will stand firm in the great tribulation and occupy a distinct place among the multitude before God’s throne. But it is by no means said that they will rise and dwell in Jerusalem. Christians are the genuine Jews, and the Jews who harden themselves in their unbelief are a synagogue of Satan, 2:9. Although the earthly Jerusalem may perhaps still once be called the holy city and the temple there the temple of God, 11:1, 2, that Jerusalem is nevertheless called Sodom and Egypt in a spiritual sense, 11:8. The genuine Jerusalem is above, 3:12; 21:2, 10, and there too is the temple of God, 3:12; 7:15; 11:19, and so forth; and the ark, 11:19; and the altar, 6:9; 8:3, 5; 9:13; 14:18; 16:7. And that Jerusalem descends to earth not already in Rev. 20 but only in Rev. 21. 2º The living and reigning of the believers who remained faithful in the great tribulation takes place not on earth but in heaven. Not a word is said about earth. John sees the angel who binds Satan coming down from heaven, 20:1; the thrones he beholds, 20:4, are in heaven, 4:4; 11:16; and the souls of the martyrs are seen by John here, 20:4, as everywhere else, in heaven, 6:9; 7:9, 14, 15; 11:12; 14:1–5; 18:20; 19:1–8. Believers have already been made kings and priests to God by Christ on earth, 1:6; they are this in heaven, 5:10, and expect that they will one day also be this on earth, 5:10, but this expectation is fulfilled only in the new Jerusalem that descends from above; then they will be kings forever, 22:5. At present, however, in heaven their kingship is temporary; it lasts a thousand years. 3º Neither does John know of a first bodily resurrection that would precede the thousand-year kingdom and a second that would follow it. Nowhere in Scripture is such a first resurrection taught. There is indeed mention of a spiritual resurrection from sin, John 5:25, 26; Rom. 6:4, and so forth. There is also an ἀναστασις ἐκ νεκρων, which refers to individual cases such as the resurrection of Christ, 1 Pet. 1:3; cf. Acts 26:23; 1 Cor. 15:23, or relates only to believers, Luke 20:35, 36; Acts 4:2, but in this case is by no means temporally distinguished by a thousand-year kingdom from the general αναστασις νεκρων, Matt. 22:31; John 5:28, 29; Acts 24:15; 1 Cor. 15:13, 42. Some have indeed thought they found this in 1 Cor. 15:20–28 and 1 Thess. 4:13–18, but wrongly. In 1 Cor. 15:20–28 Paul certainly deals only with the resurrection of believers, while he does not speak at all here, and does not need to speak, of that of the wicked. But of that resurrection of believers he clearly says that it will take place at Christ’s parousia and that afterward the end will come, in which he delivers the kingdom to the Father, vv. 23, 24. From this passage by itself one might indeed infer that according to Paul there is no resurrection of the wicked, but it is impossible to infer that it is separated from that of believers by a thousand-year kingdom. For immediately after the resurrection of believers follow the end and the delivery of the kingdom, because all enemies have been defeated and the last enemy, that is death, has been abolished. Neither does 1 Thess. 4:13–18 say anything about such a first bodily resurrection of believers. In Thessalonica people were anxious about the lot of those who had died in Christ. What the nature of that anxiety was we do not know. The chiliasts believe that the Thessalonians did not doubt the resurrection and eternal life of those who had fallen asleep in Christ, but that they believed in two resurrections, one before and one after the thousand-year kingdom, and were now concerned that the believers who had already died would rise only at the second resurrection and thus have no share in the glory of the thousand-year kingdom. But this opinion is far-fetched and finds no support whatsoever in the text. If there were a first resurrection of believers, one would expect precisely that the church in Thessalonica would not be concerned about the lot of the dead, for then these would share precisely in the privilege of that first resurrection. And if one answers that in Thessalonica they simply did not know that there was a first resurrection of believers, the apostle could simply have told them that in a few words. But he does not do this at all. He does not speak of a first and a second resurrection; he argues only that the believers who are still left alive at Jesus’ coming will have no advantage whatsoever over those who previously fell asleep in Christ. In what respect the Thessalonians believed the latter would be at a disadvantage to the former is unknown to us. But this does not matter either; the fact is established that people in Thessalonica judged in this way. Over against this Paul now says that this is not the case, for through Jesus, who will raise them, God will immediately cause the believers who have fallen asleep to be with him—with Jesus—at his coming, so that he brings them with him, as it were. And the believers left alive will by no means precede them, for the resurrection of the dead comes first, and afterward all believers, both the raised and the transformed, are caught up together in the clouds to meet the Lord. The text therefore contains nothing about a first and a second resurrection. If such a twofold resurrection occurs nowhere in Scripture, one will do well not to find it too quickly in Rev. 20. And in fact it does not occur there either. Verses 4 and 5 say only this: that the souls of the believers who remained faithful in the great tribulation live and reign as kings with Christ for a thousand years, and that this is the first resurrection. John clearly says that he saw the souls, τας ψυχας, of the martyrs, cf. 6:9, and makes no mention of the resurrection of their bodies. Further, he says not that those souls rose or were raised or entered into life, but that they *lived* and that they immediately lived and reigned as kings with Christ for a thousand years. He speaks further of the rest of the dead, οἱ λοιποι των νεκρων, and thus assumes that the believers whose souls he saw in heaven also still belonged in a certain sense to the dead, yet nevertheless lived and reigned. By contrast, he says of the rest of the dead not, as the *Statenvertaling* has it, that they did not live again, but that they *did not* live, οὐκ ἐζησαν. And finally, he adds with emphasis that this living and reigning of the souls of the believers who remained faithful, in contrast to the not living of the rest of the dead, is the first resurrection. One can feel the contrast, as it were: *that* is not the first resurrection that some assumed, already in John’s days, as though a bodily resurrection of believers would precede the thousand-year kingdom; rather, the first resurrection consists in the living and reigning of the believers who remained faithful with Christ in heaven. The believers to whom John writes and who will soon face the tribulation must not think that they will first become blessed at the end of the days. No, blessed are the dead who die in the Lord *from now on*, ἀπαρτι, 14:13. They immediately receive rest from their labors; at their death they are crowned at once; they live and reign with Christ in heaven from the first moment after their death. And therefore they can face the tribulation with courage; the crown of life lies ready for them, 2:10. Here, 20:4, 5, John briefly repeats what he wrote earlier to the seven churches. The promises given there to believers if they persevered to the end all amounted to this: whoever conquers would be crowned. Whoever conquers receives food from the tree of life, 2:7; from the hidden manna, 2:17; receives authority over the Gentiles, 2:26; receives the morning star, 2:28; is clothed in white garments, 3:5; is made a pillar in God’s temple, 3:12; sups with Jesus, 3:20. In one word: “The one who conquers, I will grant him to sit with me on my throne,” 3:21. What John first saw in promise he now sees in ch. 20 in fulfillment. Those who remain faithful unto death immediately live and reign with Christ on his throne in heaven; and *that* is the *first* resurrection. But John adds something more and thereby confirms the explanation given above. Namely, he says: blessed and holy is the one who shares in the first resurrection; over him the second death has no power, but they will be priests of God and of Christ and will reign with him for the thousand years. According to 20:14 the second death is nothing other than being cast into the lake of fire. Whatever may be the lot of the rest of the dead mentioned in v. 5, in any case the believers who remained faithful and who live and reign with Christ are protected from that second death. They already have the crown of life and already eat of the manna of life, and therefore need not fear the judgment that follows later. The one who conquers will not be harmed by the second death that begins at the final judgment, 2:11. If John understood the first resurrection in the chiliastic sense, as a bodily resurrection of believers before the thousand-year kingdom, he would not have needed to give believers such a consolation. He could then have sufficed with saying that they would rise even before the thousand-year kingdom. But no, believers still belong in a certain sense to the dead until the final judgment. Yet there is no cause for alarm: if they persevere to the end, they are immediately crowned and, even though the *first* death still reigns over their bodies, cannot be harmed by the *second* death.
