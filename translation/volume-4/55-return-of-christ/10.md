## 10.

Against this interpretation, however, one may object that John nevertheless clearly speaks of a thousand-year reign of believers with Christ, even if in heaven, and that he places it after the return of Christ, 19:11–16, and the fall of the world empire and of the false prophet, 19:20. Yet this objection does not carry as much weight as it seems. For 1º the placement of the vision in Rev. 20 after that in ch. 19 by itself decides absolutely nothing about chronological sequence. In general, verbal art, unlike painting, can narrate what takes place simultaneously only successively, one thing after another. Scripture forms no exception to this and frequently recounts one after another things that occur alongside one another in reality. Among the prophets it often happens that they see and describe successively what takes place, or will take place, simultaneously or even in an altogether different order. This is especially the case in the book of Revelation, as is increasingly acknowledged. The letters to the seven churches are not descriptions of ecclesiastical conditions that follow one another in that same order. The seven seals, seven trumpets, and seven bowls do not form a chronological series but run parallel and each time bring us to the end, to the final struggle of the antichristian power. And so there is in itself nothing against assuming that what is narrated in Rev. 20 runs parallel to the events of the earlier chapters. 2º It must be acknowledged that in depicting the world empire John has the Roman *imperium* in mind. Prophecy in the Old as well as the New Testament does not move high in the air but stands upon historical ground and sees embodied in the concrete powers in whose midst it lives the struggle of the world empire against the kingdom of God. The book of Daniel, for example, extends to Antiochus Epiphanes and sees in him the personification of enmity against God and his people. And so too John derives the features of his world empire from the Roman Empire of his day. Although everything written previously was also written for our instruction, John’s Revelation is nevertheless in the first place a book of comfort for the persecuted church of his time, to urge it to perseverance in the struggle and to encourage it by the sight of the crown awaiting it. If John personally thought that in the Roman Empire the very last development of the world empire had appeared and that Christ would come within a few years to put an end to it, there would be nothing unusual in this, nothing contrary to the spirit of prophecy. We are bound not to John’s personal opinion but to the word of his prophecy; and prophecy, which casts its light over history, is in turn also explained and disclosed by history. 3º If John himself really believed that the Roman Empire would be destroyed within a few years by the appearing of Christ, then in any case he thought that world history would not yet be finished with this, for in that case there would be no place alongside it for the reign of believers with Christ in heaven, and this reign would first have to begin after that time. But this *zeitgeschichtliche* conception, however much truth it may contain, does not reproduce the complete thought of John’s Revelation. For it is clear that in ch. 19 world history has reached its end. Babylon has fallen, ch. 18; God reigns as king, 19:6; the marriage of the Lamb has come, 19:7–9; Christ has appeared, 19:11–16; the final struggle of all the kings of the earth and their armies has been fought, 19:17–19; the world empire and the false prophet have been destroyed and cast into the lake of fire, which as the second death is opened only after the judgment, 19:20; cf. 20:14; and the rest were killed, 19:21. Thus ch. 19 clearly extends to that same end of the world depicted in 20:10–15. No material remains for a continuation of world history. The *zeitgeschichtliche* exegesis leaves unexplained the origin of the nations that appear in 20:3, 8, or else comes into conflict with 19:17–21. But just as the letters to the seven churches and likewise the seven seals, trumpets, and bowls refer first of all to the conditions and events in John’s days but then have a further scope for the church of all ages and for the whole history of the world, so it also holds for the world empire depicted in Revelation that it has its type in the Roman Empire of the first centuries but does not yet receive its full realization in it. It rises up again and again and must always succumb anew before the appearing of Christ, until at last it exerts its utmost powers, exhausts itself in one final gigantic struggle, and is then destroyed forever by the coming of Christ. 4º If this conception is correct, the vision of Rev. 20 is not intended to tell us what will occur in chronological order after the events of Rev. 19, but has an independent place and informs us of what runs parallel with the foregoing. For there is a twofold end of world history to narrate: one of the historical peoples among whom Christianity appears, and another of the wild peoples who, as Rev. 20:8 so clearly says, live in the four corners of the earth and thus have lived outside the center of human history and culture. Among the former alone could the world empire and false prophecy arise, for antichristianity presupposes acquaintance with the gospel; the latter achieve nothing beyond a savage assault upon the church of Christ. But it is the same Satan who works there and here. Whenever he is driven back and defeated among the culture-bearing peoples, he organizes among the wild peoples a new instrument for the struggle against Christ. First he is cast out of heaven; then he works on earth and establishes his world empire there against Christ; and finally he causes the wild peoples to appear across the breadth of the earth to fight the last battle against Christ. But all this is not in a chronological but in a logical, spiritual sense. 5º The thousand years are a symbolic number, as is now generally acknowledged. They stand over against the few days during which the believers who remained faithful were oppressed and persecuted here on earth, 12:14, but also over against the consummate glory that is eternal, 22:5. They are an indication of the holy, blessed rest of deceased believers in heaven with Christ and at the same time of the longing with which they look forward to the day when their blood will be avenged, 6:9, while on earth the struggle of the world empire and the world of nations against Christ still continues. And when John has then narrated in Rev. 20:1–9 the history of the wild peoples to the same end reached by the history of the culture-bearing peoples in 19:17–21, the thread of both visions is taken up and the very final end of the whole of world history is narrated. There, in 19:21, human beings were killed by the sword of Christ; here, in 20:9, they are consumed by fire from heaven. But after the world empire, false prophet, and Satan have been condemned and cast into the lake, 19:20; 20:10, all the dead rise and are judged according to their works, 20:11–15.
