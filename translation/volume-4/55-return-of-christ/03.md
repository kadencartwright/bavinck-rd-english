## 3.

These messianic expectations of the Old Testament, as everyone immediately sees, bear a very distinctive character: they are limited to a future blessedness on earth. Although in the Old Testament the believer may on a single occasion express his hope that after death he will be received into eternal glory, this expectation is individual and stands by itself. Ordinarily the eye of prophecy is directed toward that future in which the people of Israel under the king from the house of David will dwell safely in Palestine and rule over all the nations of the earth. There is no mention of believers being taken up at the end of the ages into the heaven of glory; blessedness is expected not in heaven but on earth. In connection with this, Old Testament prophecy knows only one coming of the Messiah. It does indeed know that the Anointed One will be born from the house of David when this house has fallen into decay, and that he will share in the suffering of his people, indeed, that as servant of the Lord he will suffer for his people and bear their iniquities. He will be an entirely different king from the rulers of the earth: humble, gentle, doing justice, protecting his people. He will be not only king but at the same time prophet and priest. But Old Testament prophecy never separates the state of humiliation and the state of exaltation in the life of the Messiah; it gathers both into one image. It distinguishes no first and second coming and does not place the latter, which is for judgment, a long time after the first, which extends to salvation. It is one coming in which the Messiah grants righteousness and salvation to his people and brings them to dominion over all the peoples of the earth. The kingdom that he comes to establish is therefore also the consummated kingdom of God. He himself will indeed reign as king over his people, but he is nevertheless nothing more than a theocratic king who does not rule autonomously but realizes God’s government in the absolute sense. Old Testament prophecy makes no distinction between a government of Christ and a government of God. It does not expect that the Messiah from the house of David, after reigning temporarily, will hand his kingdom over to God. It does not regard the future it depicts in the messianic kingdom as an intermediate state that at the end must give way to a government of God in heaven. It regards the messianic kingdom as the final state and clearly has the judgment over the enemies, the repelling of the final assault, the transformation of nature, and the resurrection from the dead precede the establishment and confirmation of this kingdom. And this kingdom is portrayed by all the prophets in paints and colors, under forms and images, all of which are borrowed from the historical circumstances in which they lived. Palestine will be retaken, Jerusalem rebuilt, the temple with its sacrificial service restored, Edom and Moab and Ammon, Assyria and Babylon subjected; all citizens will be granted a long life and peaceful sitting under vine and fig tree. The image of the future is Old Testament through and through; it is determined entirely historically and nationally. But in those earthly, sensory forms prophecy lays an eternal content. The shell becomes the bearer of an imperishable kernel that sometimes breaks through it even in the Old Testament. Return from exile and true conversion coincide; the religious and political sides of Israel’s victory over its enemies are most closely connected. The Messiah is an earthly ruler but also an eternal king, a king of righteousness, an everlasting father for his people, a prince of peace, a priest-king. The enemies are subjected to Israel but in this acknowledge that the Lord is God and serve him in his temple. This temple with its priesthood and sacrificial service is the visible proof that all citizens of the kingdom serve the Lord with a new heart and a new spirit and walk in his ways. And the extraordinary fertility of the land presupposes an entire transformation of nature, the creation of a new heaven and a new earth in which righteousness dwells.

Later Judaism introduced all kinds of modifications into these Old Testament expectations. Deprived of its political dominion and scattered among the peoples, it began increasingly to take account of the future lot of individuals and expanded its horizon to humanity and to the whole world. Israel would indeed one day, on the ground of its own legal righteousness, be brought by the Messiah to political dominion over all peoples; but this messianic kingdom bore a provisional, temporary character and would at the end give way to a kingdom of God, to a blessedness of the righteous in heaven, which was inaugurated by the resurrection of all human beings and by the general judgment of the world. The political and religious sides, which in the prophetic image of the future were most closely united, were torn apart in this way. In Jesus’ day Israel expected a sensory, earthly messianic kingdom whose condition was described in the forms and images of Old Testament prophecy. But these images and forms were now understood literally. The shell was confused with the kernel, the thing with the image, the essence with the form. The messianic kingdom became a political dominion of Israel over the peoples, a period of external prosperity and flourishing. And only at its end, after the general resurrection, did the world judgment take place, in which everyone was judged according to his works and received either blessedness in heaven as reward or torment in Gehenna as punishment for his deeds. In this way the doctrine of Chiliasm arose. A large part of the Jewish apocryphal literature does indeed still remain with the Old Testament expectations. But frequently, especially in the Apocalypse of Baruch and in the fourth book of Ezra, the conception nevertheless occurs that the glory of the messianic kingdom is not the last and highest, but after a definite time—which is repeatedly calculated and in the Talmud, for example, fixed at 400 or 1000 years—will give way to the heavenly blessedness of the kingdom of God. Chiliasm is therefore not of Christian but of Jewish and further also of Persian origin, above, p. 426. It always rests upon a compromise between the expectations of an earthly and a heavenly blessedness and seeks to do justice to Old Testament prophecy in the sense that it regards an earthly messianic kingdom as predicted by it, which after an appointed time will be replaced by the kingdom of God. The strength of Chiliasm may now appear to be the Old Testament, but in fact this is not so. The Old Testament is decidedly not chiliastic. In the messianic kingdom it depicts the consummated kingdom of God, which is without end and endures eternally, Dan. 2:44, and which is preceded by judgment, resurrection, and world renewal. Nevertheless, among the Jews and also among many Christians it found belief and arose repeatedly whenever the world developed its God-hostile power and caused the church to suffer under persecution and oppression. In the earliest period we encounter it in Cerinthus, in the Testament of the XII Patriarchs, among the Ebionites, in Barnabas, Papias, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Apollinaris, Commodianus, Lactantius, and Victorinus. But Montanism urged caution; Gnostics, Alexandrian theologians, and especially Augustine opposed it most strongly; and the changed condition of the church, which had overcome the world power and increasingly regarded itself as the kingdom of God on earth, gradually caused it to die out entirely. It arose anew before and during the Reformation, when many began to regard Rome as the false whore and the pope as the antichrist. It revived among the Anabaptists, the David Jorists, and the Socinians, and since then never died out, although the official churches rejected it. Political disturbances, religious wars, persecutions, and sectarian movements repeatedly gave it new life. Archer, Newton, Joseph Mede, Jane Leade, and many Independents; in the Netherlands by Labadie, Ant. Bourignon, Poiret, and others.
