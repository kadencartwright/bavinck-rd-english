## 11.

The doctrine of Scripture developed thus far clearly brings to light that the course and outcome of world history are entirely different from what human beings ordinarily imagine them to be. If this applies anywhere, then above all it applies with respect to the end of things: God’s ways are higher than our ways and his thoughts higher than our thoughts. Although the kingdom of God is like a mustard seed and leaven and a seed that sprouts and grows tall without human knowledge or intervention, Matt. 13:31, 33; Mark 4:27, it nevertheless does not reach its completion by way of gradual development or an ethical process. Rather, according to the incontrovertible testimony of Scripture, the history of humanity, both among peoples that bear culture and among those without it, issues in a general apostasy and a terrible final struggle of all satanic powers against God and his kingdom. But then the end has also arrived. With the time and the power granted it by God, the world has done nothing other than make itself ripe for judgment, as in the days of Noah; at the height of its power it suddenly collapses at the appearance of Christ. A catastrophe, a decisive act of God, finally ends Satan’s dominion here on earth and brings about the completion of the unshakable kingdom of heaven. Just as in the believer perfection is not the fruit of a slowly advancing sanctification but enters immediately after death, so too the perfection of humanity and the world comes about not gradually but suddenly through the appearance of Christ. Specifically, it is Christ who has been appointed by the Father to bring the history of humanity and the world to an end. And he has been appointed to this because he is the Savior, the perfect Savior. The labor he accomplished on earth is only one part of the great work of redemption he undertook; and the time he spent here is only a small portion of the ages over which he has been appointed as Lord and King. Anointed by the Father from eternity, he began to exercise his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity as soon as sin entered the world; he continued that activity through all the revolving ages; and one day he will complete it at the end of time. What he acquired on earth through his suffering and death he applies from heaven through the power of his word and the working of his Spirit; and what he has thus applied he maintains and protects against all attacks of Satan, in order one day at the end to present it altogether perfect, without spot or wrinkle, to his Father who is in heaven. The return of Christ for judgment is therefore not an arbitrary addition that can be detached from his preceding work and set by itself, but forms a necessary, indispensable component of that work, brings that work to its completion, and crowns it; it is the final and highest stage in the state of his exaltation. Because Christ is the Savior of the world, he returns one day as its Judge; the κρισις he brought about through his first coming he completes at his second coming; the Father gave him authority to κρισιν ποιειν because he is the Son of Man, John 5:27. Eschatology is therefore rooted in Christology and is itself Christology, the doctrine of the final, complete triumph of Christ and his kingdom over all his enemies. With Scripture we can even go back further. The Son is not only *mediator reconciliationis* because of sin, but also, apart from sin, *mediator unionis* between God and his creature. He is not only the *causa exemplaris* but also the *causa finalis* of creation; the world has its foundation and pattern in the Son and therefore also has its goal in him; it was created through him and also for him, Col. 1:16. Because creation is his work, it cannot and may not remain Satan’s spoil; the *Son* is the Head, Lord, and Heir of all things. Comprehended in the Son, gathered under him as Head, the creatures return to the Father, the fountain of all good things, vol. II, 408. The second coming of Christ is thus required by his first; it is included in the first, necessarily flows from it in its time, brings it to its full effect and completion, and was therefore comprehended together with the first coming in one image by Old Testament prophecy. And not only are the second and first comings connected ideally and logically; there is also a real connection between them. Just as the Old Testament was a continual coming of God to his people until he came to dwell bodily among them in Christ, so also the dispensation of the New Testament is a continual coming of Christ to his inheritance in order finally to take permanent possession of it. Christ is not only the one who was to come in the days of the Old Testament and came in the fullness of time, but he is also the coming one, ὁ ἐρχομενος, and the one who will come, ὁ ἐρχομενος ἡξει, Heb. 10:37; cf. Rev. 1:4, 8, and so forth. The second coming of Christ is the complement of the first.
