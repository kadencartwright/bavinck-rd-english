# § 55. The Return of Christ

## 1.

Just as it is appointed for the human being to die once, so too an end must one day come to the history of the world. Not only religion but science too has been convinced of this at all times. A few, such as Aristotle in antiquity and Czolbe, Friedrich Mohr, and others in more recent times, have indeed thought that this world was eternal and had neither beginning nor end. But the untenability of this opinion is now generally conceded; there are many considerations that establish the finite duration of the world beyond all doubt. According to calculations, the earth’s speed of rotation decreases by at least one second in 600,000 years. However little this may be, after trillions of years it nevertheless brings about a reversal in the relation of day and night on earth that puts an end to all life. Further, the earth’s rotation is continually slowed by the influence of ebb and flood tides, since these displace the parts of the earth and reduce the store of kinetic energy; the earth therefore continually approaches the sun and must finally disappear into it. Next, the space in which the planets move is not absolutely empty but filled with ether or rarefied air, which, however slight it may be, impedes motion, reduces the speed of rotation, causes the orbits of the planets to contract, and thus brings them increasingly closer to the sun. Further, the sun too cannot endure forever. Whether it produces its heat through falling meteorites, continual contraction, or chemical processes, it gradually expends that heat, diminishes its volume, contracts, and approaches its end. According to Thomson, the diameter of the sun would decrease by 35 meters annually, and since it had already shone for 20 million years, it could continue to exist for only another 10 million years. Kinetic energy can indeed be converted into heat, but heat can no longer be converted into kinetic energy unless it flows out upon a colder body. Thus, when the temperature is one day the same everywhere, the conversion of heat ceases and the end of things has been reached. The only question, therefore, is which of the two, the sun or the earth, will hold out the longest. If the sun does, the earth is finally devoured by it and everything ends in conflagration; if the earth does, all heat one day ceases and life perishes in the death of freezing. To these are added all kinds of other grounds for the finitude of the world. The water of the earth must continually decrease because of its chemical affinity with minerals; water and oxygen are increasingly bound to solid substances; the products of the earth—coal, wood, peat, foodstuffs—diminish; the earth, however rich it may be, is one day exhausted, and this all the sooner as the human race increases and the danger of overpopulation threatens. From the standpoint of science, therefore, there is absolutely no room for an optimistic expectation concerning the future. Yet many have surrendered themselves to it and dreamed of continual progress and a future paradise of humanity in the *Diesseits*. Humanists and materialists vie with one another in cherishing such illusions, consider their prophecies of material plenty guaranteed by the principle of cosmic evolution, and judge that the happiness of humanity will one day be fully attained through the increase of ideal goods such as science, art, and morality, or through progress in material prosperity, through an abundance of food, shelter, and clothing. Kant, Lessing, Herder, Fichte, Schelling, and others regarded a future as imminent in which the ethical kingdom of God would include all, the *Aufklärung* would be shared by all, and humanity would be the principle of everyone’s life. Even Darwin, at the close of his book on the *Ontstaan der soorten* and in the last chapter of his *Afstamming des menschen*, expresses the hope that the human being, who has already risen so high from his animal origin, is moving toward a still higher destiny in a distant future. In that future, according to Pierson, *Eene Levensbeschouwing*, 269, marriage will no longer be desired by the noblest, but the man will associate with the woman as with his sister and sensual pleasure will no longer be the death of the zest for life; or, according to others, marriage among a highly civilized people will in time assume the form of a double marriage, and two friends will jointly marry two women. Still more extravagant are the expectations of the socialists, these chiliasts of unbelief, who think that in the future state modeled by them all sin and conflict will have disappeared and a carefree, contented life will be the privilege of all. But, as has been said, there is little ground for such expectations. And even if a time of greater prosperity and happiness were to dawn for humanity, what benefit would it bring if all development, as science teaches, must finally perish in death? At the end of his *Kulturgeschichte*, Fr. von Hellwald is unable to give the slightest answer to the question of what purpose everything has served, and what purpose the human being too, with his struggle and striving, his civilization and development, has had. And Otto Henne-Am Rhyn ends his *Kulturgeschichte* with the prediction that all humanity with its culture will one day disappear without a trace: *einst wird Alles, was wir gethan, nirgends mehr aufzufinden sein*. Over against this he can comfort himself only with the thought that it will still take a long time before matters come to that point. Whoever lives without God and without Christ and must expect everything from the *Diesseits*, from immanent cosmic forces, is also without hope in the world. Even civilization cannot be conceived as endless. Billions of years in the past or future of the world may be arbitrarily assumed but cannot be concretely conceived as filled with history. If, for example, humanity were one day one thousand million years old, a textbook of world history that treated one century in ten pages would form no fewer than two hundred thousand volumes, each volume reckoned at five hundred pages; or still twenty thousand volumes if only one page were allotted to each century; or still five hundred volumes if no more than one line were devoted to each century. And so it would be with everything that forms the content of our civilization. The human being and humanity are finite, and therefore their civilization too cannot be conceived as endless. An infinite time is an absurdity for the earth as well as for our race, one even more palpable than the folly of the millions of years known to us from pagan mythologies. From the standpoint of science there is much more ground for accepting the pessimism of Schopenhauer and Ed. von Hartmann, which places the redemption of the world in the combating of the alogical will by logical representation, in absolute *Willensverneinung*, that is, in the annihilation of the world itself. But even then there is not the slightest guarantee that the absolute will does not pass over into another world process and begin all over again without end. Many Greek philosophers held that many other worlds had preceded this one and many others would follow it. The Pythagoreans and the Stoics were even of the opinion that everything would return exactly as it existed in this world and had existed in previous ones. And today too many, for example Haeckel, *Die Welträthsel*, 430, have returned to similar views, although Windelband, *Geschichte und Naturwissenschaft*, Strassburg, 1900, p. 22, rightly calls it a painful thought that *in der periodischen Wiederkehr aller Dinge auch die Persönlichkeit mit allem ihrem Thun und Leiden wiederkehren soll*.
