## 6.

Although this already establishes in general the result that the New Testament is antichiliastic, Kuenen, *Prof.* II, 271, it must nevertheless still be demonstrated in greater detail. Chiliasm includes the expectation that toward the return of Christ a national conversion of Israel will take place, that the Jews will then return to Palestine, and that from there they will rule over the nations under Christ. On this point the chiliasts differ as to whether conversion will precede the return or the return conversion; cf., for example, Guers, *Israels toekomst en herstel*, 171. Because it is difficult to imagine that the dispersed Jews will first be converted one after another and then together form the plan of going to Palestine, some believe that the Jews will first gradually return to Canaan and there later be converted collectively to Christ, while others try to combine both views by supposing that first a large portion of the Jews will move to Palestine and that these, after first restoring city, temple, and worship and then being converted to Christ, will gradually be followed by their other compatriots. And they point out that this expectation is already beginning to be fulfilled. There are already thousands of Jews in Palestine. The Eastern Question is approaching its solution, for Turkey owes its existence solely to the mutual rivalry of the great powers; and once Turkey is destroyed, there is every chance that Palestine will be assigned to the Jews, to whom it rightfully belongs. Further, as appears from the Zionism that has emerged in recent times, a longing is at work in many Jews to return to Palestine and form an independent kingdom there. And finally, the great improvement of the means of transportation, which some regard as already predicted in Nah. 2:3, 4; Isa. 11:16; 66:20, also makes such a return simple and easy. However one may judge these political combinations, the New Testament offers not the slightest support for such an expectation. When the fullness of time had come, the Jews, considered as a people, stood on the same level as the Gentiles; together they were condemnable before God because they sought to establish a righteousness of their own from the law and rejected the righteousness that is from faith, Rom. 3:21. Therefore God sent John to them with the baptism of repentance and thereby had it declared to them that, although circumcised and baptizing proselytes, they themselves stood guilty and unclean before his face and, just as much as the Gentiles, needed regeneration and conversion in order to enter the kingdom of heaven. Through baptism John already separated the true Israelites from the mass of the people. And Jesus continued in these footsteps; he took over John’s baptism and had it administered by his disciples. Like John, he did indeed appear at first with the preaching that the kingdom of God had drawn near. But he understood that kingdom altogether differently from his contemporaries; he meant by it not a political but a religious-ethical dominion and taught that no fleshly descent from Abraham, but only regeneration from water and Spirit, opened access to that kingdom of heaven, vol. III, 232f. In this way he gradually gathered around himself a company of disciples that distinguished and separated itself from the Jewish people. And these were the true ἐκκλησια, the genuine people of God, as Israel ought to have been but now showed itself not to be by its rejection of the Messiah. This separation between the Jewish people and the New Testament ἐκκλησια became increasingly sharp. There were indeed many who believed in Christ, but the people, led by the Pharisees and scribes, rejected him. Though for some appointed for a rising, for many he was appointed for a fall and for a sign that would be spoken against, Luke 2:34. He came to his own, but his own did not receive him, John 1:11. Jesus himself says that a prophet is not honored in his own country, Matt. 13:57. Again and again he experiences that the Jews are unwilling to come to him, John 5:37–47; 6:64. He testifies that they will die in their sins, 8:21; that they are children of the devil, 8:44; plants not planted by the Father, Matt. 15:13, 14; and he sees in their unbelief no accidental, unforeseen circumstance but the fulfillment of prophecy, Matt. 13:13f.; John 12:37f. But not only does Jesus have nothing to hope for from the Jews in the present; in the future too he expects nothing for them. On the contrary, he announces the complete destruction of city and temple, so that not one stone will be left upon another, John 2:18–21; Matt. 22:7; 23:37–39; 24:1f.; Mark 13; Luke 21:6f. At his entry into Jerusalem he weeps over the city, Luke 19:41–44. On the Monday before his death, on the road to Bethany, he curses the fig tree which, in having as yet no fruit but already having leaves, was an image of seemingly pious, self-righteous Israel, and he declared that no one would ever eat fruit from it again, Mark 11:12–14. On the way to his cross he commands the women to weep not for him but for Jerusalem, Luke 23:28. He even preaches that the salvation rejected by Israel will become the portion of the Gentiles. The kingdom of God will be taken away from Israel and given to another people that produces its fruits, Matt. 21:43; the vineyard is leased to other tenants, Matt. 21:41; those found at the outlets of the roads are called to the wedding, Matt. 22:9; the lost son takes precedence over the eldest son, Luke 15. And so he says that many will come from east and west and recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, Matt. 8:10–12; that he has other sheep who are not of this fold, John 10:16; and rejoices that when some Greeks desire to see him, he will now fall into the earth like a grain of wheat and die and thus bear much fruit, John 12:24. After his resurrection he therefore also commands his disciples to preach the gospel to all nations, Matt. 28:18. And we encounter the same judgment concerning Israel in all the apostles. As Jesus’ witnesses they must indeed begin their labor from Jerusalem but then continue it to the uttermost part of the earth, Acts 1:8. Peter therefore immediately brings the gospel to the Jews, Acts 2:14; 3:19; 5:31, but sees in a vision that henceforth no one is unclean and that everyone who fears God is acceptable to him, from whatever nation he comes, Acts 10:35, 43. Paul always begins his preaching with the Jews, but when they reject it he turns to the Gentiles, Acts 13:46; 18:6; 28:25–28. First to the Jew but also to the Greek is the rule he observes on his missionary journeys, Rom. 1:16; 1 Cor. 1:21–24. For both Jews and Gentiles are condemnable before God and need the same gospel, Rom. 3:19f. There is only one way of salvation for all, namely faith, as it was already exercised by Abraham before the law and credited to him as righteousness, Rom. 4; Gal. 3. Those among the Jews who reject Christ are not true, genuine Jews, Rom. 2:28, 29; they are not the circumcision but the mutilation, Phil. 3:2; they are unruly, empty talkers, deceivers of minds, whose mouths must be stopped, Titus 1:10, 11. They killed the Lord Jesus and their own prophets, they persecute believers, they do not please God and are hostile to all people, they prevent the apostles from speaking to the Gentiles, and they fill up the measure of their sins, so that wrath has reached its utmost limit upon them and is now being poured out over them, 1 Thess. 2:14–16. The Jews who slander the church of Smyrna do say that they are Jews, but they are not; rather, they are a synagogue of Satan, Rev. 2:9; 3:9. Genuine Jews, true children of Abraham, are those who believe in Christ, Rom. 9:8; Gal. 3:29, and so forth. Thus the New Testament judges the Jews; the church of believers has in every respect replaced national, fleshly Israel; the Old Testament is fulfilled in the New.
