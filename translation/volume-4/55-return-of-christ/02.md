## 2.

Religion has never reconciled itself to this idea of an endless development or of a total destruction of the world. Various reasons kept it from adopting these philosophical theories. For it is beyond dispute that all such conceptions fail to do justice to the value of personality and sacrifice it to the whole. Further, they misconstrue the significance of the religious-ethical life and place it far below culture. And finally, for the present and the future they build only on the powers immanent in the cosmos and take no account whatsoever of a divine power that governs the world and ultimately, by direct intervention, causes it to answer to the goal that power has set for it. All religions therefore have a different expectation for the future. They all know, in a more or less clear degree, of a struggle between good and evil; they all cherish the hope of the triumph of good, in which the virtuous are rewarded and the wicked punished; and for the most part they also consider that future unattainable except through a revelation of supernatural powers; cf. vol. III, 229. In the Persian religion, at the end of the third world-period, people even expected the appearance of the third son of Zarathustra, Sosiosh, who would inaugurate a thousand-year kingdom of peace and complete the redemptive work of his father; Saussaye, *Religionsgesch.* II, 51; Hartmann, *Rel.* I, 239; Herzog² 11, 239. And among the Muslims, alongside belief in the return of Jesus, the expectation of a Mahdi gradually arose who would lead the faithful back into the golden age of the “four righteous caliphs”; Dr. C. Snouck Hurgronje, *Der Mahdi*, offprint from the *Revue Coloniale Internationale* (1885). In Israel the expectation concerning the future was built on the foundation of the covenant that God had established with Abraham and his seed. This covenant, after all, bears an eternal character and is not nullified by human unfaithfulness, vol. III, 195. Already in the law God repeatedly testifies to the people of Israel that, when they transgress his covenant, he will visit them with the severest punishments but afterward will nevertheless have mercy on them again. When Israel has been scattered among the nations because of its sins and its land has been laid waste, then in that time the Lord will provoke Israel to jealousy by receiving other nations and afterward will convert it and bring it back to its land, bless it with all kinds of spiritual and material blessings, and execute vengeance upon all its enemies, Lev. 26; Deut. 4:23–31; 30:1–10; 32:15–43. After the promise to David’s house that it would endure and its throne be established forever, 2 Sam. 7:16; 23:5; 1 Chron. 17:14, this element acquires ever greater significance in the expectation concerning Israel’s future: that the conversion and restoration of Israel will come about only through the anointed king from David’s line. In prophecy these thoughts are developed more broadly and, notwithstanding the distinctive character they bear in each of the prophets, assume increasingly fixed forms.

In the expectation that the Old Testament cherishes concerning the future of the people of God, the following elements can clearly be distinguished. All the prophets announce 1º to Israel and Judah *a day of judgment and punishment*. The יום יהוה, that is, the time in which the Lord will have mercy on his people and avenge himself on his enemies, was understood by the prophets altogether differently than by the people. The people abused this expectation and thought that YHWH would protect them against every danger regardless of their spiritual condition, Amos 5:18; 6:13; Jer. 29; Ezek. 33:23f. But the prophets said that the day of the Lord would also be a day of judgment for Israel; the people would go into exile and their land be given over to destruction, Amos 2:4f.; 5:16, 18, 27; 6:14, and so forth; Hos. 1:6; 2:11; 3:4; 8:13; 9:3, 6; 10:6; 11:5; 13:12; 14:1; Joel 2:1f.; Mic. 3:12; 4:10; 7:13; Zeph. 1:1–18; Hab. 1:5–11; Isa. 2:11f.; 5:5f.; 7:18f.; Jer. 1:11–16, and so forth. But nevertheless, that punishment is 2º *temporary*. It comes to an end after many days, Hos. 3:4; after a few days, that is, after a short time, 6:2; after seventy years, Jer. 25:12; 29:10; after three hundred and ninety years for Israel and forty years for Judah, Ezek. 4:4f. God chastises his people in measure, Isa. 27:7f.; Jer. 30:11; he leaves them only for a short time; his anger is brief, but his lovingkindness is everlasting, Isa. 54:7, 8. He loves his people with an everlasting love and will therefore have mercy again, Mic. 7:19; Jer. 31:3, 20. He cannot destroy his people, even though he shakes them as in a sieve, Amos 9:8, 9. His compassion is kindled within him, Hos. 11:8. He remembers his covenant, Ezek. 16:60. He will redeem his people, not for Israel’s sake but for his name’s sake, for the sake of his renown among the Gentiles, Deut. 32:27; Isa. 43:25; 48:9; Ezek. 36:22. At the end of the time of punishment, God sends 3º *the Messiah* from David’s house. Obadiah still speaks in general of saviors who protect the escaped community on Zion, vv. 17, 21; cf. Jer. 23:4; 33:17, 20, 21, 22, 26. Amos says that after the judgment upon Israel God will raise up again David’s fallen booth, 9:11. Hosea expects that the children of Israel will be converted and seek the Lord and also David their king, 1:11; 3:5; cf. Jer. 30:9; Ezek. 34:23, 24; 37:22–24. Micah prophesies that Israel will not be delivered from the power of its enemies until the ruler has been born from David’s royal house in Bethlehem, 5:1, 2. That he will come not from Jerusalem but, like David himself, from Bethlehem proves that David’s royal house has lost the throne and fallen into a state of humiliation. Isaiah accordingly says that a shoot will come forth from the felled stump of Jesse, 11:1, 2, and Ezekiel expresses the same thought by saying that the Lord will take a small, tender twig from the topmost shoot of the lofty cedar, 17:22. God will cause him to spring up as a branch for David’s house, Isa. 4:2; Jer. 23:5, 6; 33:14–17, so that in accordance with this he also bears the name Branch, Zech. 3:8; 6:12. Born in Israel’s time of suffering, this Davidic heir will grow up in impoverished circumstances, Isa. 7:14–17. He is a king, but righteous, gentle, humble, and therefore riding on the colt of a donkey, Zech. 9:9. With royal dignity he unites prophetic, Deut. 18:15; Isa. 11:2; 40–66; Mal. 4:5, and priestly dignity, Isa. 53; Jer. 30:21; Zech. 3; 6:13; Ps. 110. The kingdom he comes to establish is a kingdom of righteousness and peace, Isa. 11; 40–66; Mic. 5:9; Pss. 72; 110. He is himself righteousness and salvation for his people and obtains them, Isa. 11; 42; 53; Jer. 23:5, 6; Ps. 72, and so forth. His appearance therefore does not first take place after the day of judgment but precedes it; Judah is delivered only when God grants David a branch, Isa. 9:1–6; 11:1f.; Jer. 23:5, 6; 33:14–17. Among the benefits 4º that will be bestowed on his people through this Anointed One belongs first of all the *return* from the land of exile. Land, people, king, and God belong together; the restoration of Israel therefore begins with return from exile, Amos 9:14; Hos. 11:11; Mic. 4:6; Joel 3:1; Isa. 11:11; Jer. 3:18; Ezek. 11:17, and so forth. According to Isaiah’s depiction, that return will be extraordinarily glorious: the wilderness will blossom like a rose, mountains will be leveled and valleys filled; there will be a prepared highway on which even the blind cannot go astray, 35:1–9; 41:17–20; 42:15, 16; 43:19, 20, and so forth. Both Israel and Judah will share in that return, Amos 9:9–15; Hos. 1:11; 14:2–9; Isa. 11:13; Jer. 3:6, 18; 31:27; 32:37–40; Ezek. 37:17; 47:13, 21; 48:1–7, 23–29. But the return from the Babylonian captivity corresponded to this expectation only to a very limited degree. The postexilic prophets therefore see in that return only a beginning of the fulfillment of the promises, detach their expectation from a return out of exile, and, apart from Zech. 8:13, no longer speak of the ten tribes; those who returned regarded themselves as the representation of all Israel, Ezra 6:17. Indeed, all the prophets understand 5º the return from exile at the same time in an ethical sense, as a *conversion* of Israel. Gathering from the nations and circumcision of the heart go together, Deut. 30:3–6. By no means all will return and be converted to the Lord; many, most, will perish in the judgment that the day of YHWH will also bring upon Israel. The Lord will indeed not utterly destroy the house of Jacob, but he will nevertheless shake it as in a sieve and cause the sinners to die by the sword, Amos 9:8–10. When the Lord restores Israel and Judah, he will first lead them into the wilderness and there enter into judgment with them and purge out the wicked, Hos. 2:13; Ezek. 20:34f. Many men will then fall, so that seven women will take hold of one man, Isa. 3:25–4:1. Destruction is firmly decreed; only a remnant will return, Isa. 4:3; 6:13; 7:3; 10:21; 11:11. The Lord will thresh the children of Israel and then glean them one by one, Isa. 27:12. He will destroy the proud but leave a poor and afflicted people, Zeph. 3:12, and preserve his work alive, Hab. 3:2. One from a city and two from a clan will be brought back, Jer. 3:14; two parts will be cut off, but the third part will be refined, Zech. 13:8, 9. But these survivors will then be a holy people to the Lord, whom he betroths to himself forever, Hos. 1:10, 12; 2:15, 18, 22; Isa. 4:3, 4; 11:9. The Lord forgives all their iniquity, washes them from all their uncleanness, gives them a new heart, pours out his Spirit upon all, causes all idolatry and sorcery to disappear from their midst, and establishes a new covenant with them, Mic. 5:11–14; Joel 2:28; Isa. 44:21–23; 43:25; Jer. 31:31; Ezek. 11:19; 36:25–28; 37:14; Zech. 13:2, and so forth. There will no longer be anyone unclean among them, Isa. 52:1, 11, 12; they are all righteous, Isa. 60:21, who, taught by God, know him, trust in his name, and do no wrong or speak no lie, Isa. 54:13; Jer. 31:31; Zeph. 3:12, 13. Everything there will be holy, even to the bells of the horses, Zech. 14:20, 21. For the glory of the Lord has risen over them, Zech. 2:5; Isa. 60:1, and God himself dwells among them, Obad. 21; Joel 3:17; Hos. 2:22; Zech. 2:10; 8:8, and so forth. For Old Testament prophecy these spiritual benefits include 6º the expectation of *the restoration of temple and worship*. According to Obadiah there will be deliverance on Zion; there dwell the saviors who will protect Israel and judge its enemies, vv. 17, 21. Joel prophesies that the Lord will dwell on Zion, his holy mountain, and that Jerusalem will be a holy place no longer accessible to strangers and enduring forever, 3:17, 20. Amos expects the cities of Palestine to be rebuilt and inhabited and Israel never again to be driven out of them, 9:14, 15. Micah announces that although Zion will be plowed as a field and Jerusalem become a heap of stones, 3:12, nevertheless the mountain of the house of the Lord will be established at the summit of the mountains, that the law will go out from Zion and the word of the Lord from Jerusalem, and that the Lord will dwell on Zion, 4:1, 2, 7; 7:11. The same thought is expressed by Isaiah, 2:2, who further adds that Zion and Jerusalem, kingship and priesthood, temple and altar, sacrifices and feast days, will be restored, 28:16; 30:19; 33:5; 35:10; 52:1; 56:6, 7; 60:7; 61:6; 66:20–23. Likewise Jeremiah expects that Jerusalem will be rebuilt, the Lord’s throne established there, and worship in the temple renewed, 3:16, 17; 30:18; 31:38; 33:18, 21. Haggai predicts that the glory of the second temple will be greater than that of the first, 2:6–10, and Zechariah announces that Jerusalem will be rebuilt and expanded, that priesthood and temple will be renewed, and that God will dwell in Jerusalem in the midst of his people, 1:17; 2:1–5; 3:1–8; 6:9–15; 8:3f. But this image of the future is worked out by none of the prophets in such minute detail as by Ezekiel. After he has said in chs. 34–37 that Israel and Judah will again be gathered by the Lord, accepted as one people under the one shepherd from David’s house as his possession, and endowed with a new heart and a new spirit, and then predicted in chs. 38 and 39 that Israel, having returned to its land, must still endure one final attack by Gog from Magog, in chs. 40–48 he gives an elaborate depiction of the Palestine of the future. The land on the west side of the Jordan will be divided by parallel lines into nearly equal strips. The upper seven will be inhabited by the tribes of Dan, Asher, Naphtali, Manasseh, Ephraim, Reuben, and Judah, and the lower five by Benjamin, Simeon, Issachar, Zebulun, and Gad. Between these upper and lower parts of the land a strip of land is set apart for the Lord. In the middle of this strip, 25000 cubits wide and long, lies a high mountain; and on it is built the temple filled with the glory of the Lord, which measures 500 cubits square and is surrounded by an open space of 500 cubits on every side. Around it the priests, who must all be sons of Zadok, receive their dwelling place to the south and the Levites to the north, 25000 cubits long and 10000 cubits wide, while to the east and west a portion of the holy strip is assigned to the prince. The city of Jerusalem is separated from the temple and lies south of the land assigned to the priests, in a plain 25000 cubits long and 5000 cubits wide. On every side of the city there are three gates in the wall, according to the number of the tribes of Israel. At the great feasts all Israel comes to the temple to sacrifice, but the Gentiles are forbidden access to the temple. If Israel thus lives according to God’s statutes, it will enjoy rich blessing: from beneath the threshold of the temple door flows a stream that continually deepens, makes the land fruitful and even the water of the Dead Sea wholesome; and on its banks stand trees whose fruit serves for food and whose leaves serve for healing.

Alongside these spiritual benefits come 7º all kinds of *material blessings*. Under the Prince of Peace from David’s house Israel will dwell in safety. There will be no more war; bow and sword will be broken, Hos. 2:17; horses and chariots destroyed and fortresses demolished, Mic. 5:9, 10; swords beaten into plowshares and spears into pruning hooks, and all will sit under their vine and fig tree, Isa. 2:4; Mic. 4:3, 4, for the kingdom is the Lord’s and he is their strength, Obad. 21; Joel 3:16, 17. The land will receive extraordinary fertility, so that the mountains drip with sweet wine and the hills flow with milk; a fountain issuing from the house of the Lord will water the arid land and transform the wilderness into an Eden; harmful beasts will be driven away, enemies will no longer rob the harvest, and all the trees, refreshed at the proper time by gentle rain, will bear abundant fruit, Amos 9:13, 14; Hos. 2:17, 20, 21; 14:6; Joel 3:18; Isa. 32:15–20; 51:3; 60:17, 18; 62:8, 9; 65:9, 22; Jer. 31:6, 12–14; Ezek. 34:14, 25, 26, 29; 36:29; 47:1–12; Zech. 8:12; 14:8, 10. There will even be a great transformation in the whole of nature: the animals receive a different nature, Isa. 11:6–8; 65:25; heaven and earth are renewed and the former things are no longer remembered, Isa. 34:4; 51:6; 65:17; 66:22; sun and moon are changed, the light of the moon becomes like the sun and the light of the sun is increased sevenfold, Isa. 30:26; indeed, sun and moon cease, it becomes one continuous day, for the Lord will be an everlasting light, Isa. 60:19, 20; Zech. 14:6, 7. And in the human world too the change will be great. When Israel has been gathered, Palestine will resound with people, Mic. 2:12, 13; the seed of the children of Israel will be like the sand of the sea, and especially that of David’s house and of the Levites will be multiplied, Hos. 1:10; Isa. 9:2; Jer. 3:16; 33:22. Because of the multitude of people and animals, Jerusalem will be immeasurable and will have to be inhabited like unwalled villages, Zech. 2:1–4. This wonderful increase has various causes. Many Israelites, when one part has already been brought back, will come to Jerusalem and desire to share in Israel’s blessing, Zech. 2:4–9; 8:7, 8; Jer. 3:14, 16, 18. Indeed, when the Lord’s messengers make that blessing known among the Gentiles, the latter will bring the Israelites still living among them to Jerusalem in wagons and litters, on horses, mules, and swift animals, Isa. 66:19, 20. Further, the Israelites who have died will also share in those blessings. All Israel can be said to be brought back from death to life, Hos. 6:2; 13:14; Isa. 25:8; Ezek. 37:1–14, but Isaiah, 26:19, and Daniel, 12:2, specifically announce that the slain Israelites also will rise and that at least some will awake to eternal life. And finally, all citizens of the kingdom of God will attain a great age. There will no longer be an infant of only a few days or an old man who does not complete his days, for one who dies as a youth will be a hundred years old, and the sinner who dies a hundred years old will be regarded as having been struck by a curse because of his sin and therefore as having died so early, Isa. 65:20; cf. Zech. 8:4, 5. Nor will there be any more sickness or mourning and crying, 25:8; 30:19; 65:19; indeed, the Lord will destroy death and swallow it up in victory, 25:8. Finally, 8º the *Gentiles* will also share in that blessing of the kingdom of God. Throughout Old Testament prophecy runs the thought that God will avenge the blood of his servants upon his enemies. The prophets therefore announce God’s judgments to various peoples—Philistia, Tyre, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Assyria, Babylon. Yet those judgments do not tend to the destruction but to the preservation of the Gentiles; in Abraham’s seed all the nations of the earth are blessed. With one prophet the political side of this subjection of the Gentiles to Israel comes more to the foreground and with another its religious, spiritual side. But all nevertheless expect the dominion of the Messiah to extend to all peoples; cf. Pss. 2; 21; 24; 45; 46; 47; 48; 68; 72; 86; 89; 96; 98, and so forth. Israel will possess the Gentiles as an inheritance, Amos 9:12; Obad. 17–21. They will indeed be judged, Joel 3:2–15, but whoever calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved, for on Zion there is deliverance, 2:32. The ruler from Bethlehem will be great to the ends of the earth and protect Israel against its enemies, Mic. 5:3f., but the Gentiles will nevertheless go to Zion to learn the Lord’s ways, 4:1, 2. After the Lord has destroyed all the gods of the peoples, Zeph. 2:4–11; 3:8, the coastlands of the Gentiles will bow down before him, and he will give all peoples pure lips to call upon his name, 2:11; 3:9. Ethiopia will bring gifts to the Lord in Zion, Isa. 18:7; Egyptians and Assyrians will serve him, 19:18–25; Tyre will surrender her gain to the Lord, 23:15–18; and he will prepare a rich banquet for all peoples on Zion, 25:6–10. Indeed, the servant of the Lord will also be a light to the Gentiles, make the glory of the Lord known through his messengers also among the peoples of the earth, and also be served by them. The house of the Lord will be a house of prayer for all peoples; all will bring sacrifices there, worship the Lord and call themselves by his name, and pasture Israel’s flock and cultivate its fields, while the Israelites can devote themselves entirely as priests to the service of YHWH, chs. 40–66 passim. When Israel has been restored and Jerusalem is the Lord’s throne, all the Gentiles will be gathered there because of the name of the Lord, bless themselves in the Lord, and glory in him, Jer. 3:17; 4:2; 16:19–21; 33:9. In the end all peoples will acknowledge that the Lord is God, Ezek. 16:61; 17:24; 25:5f.; 26:6; 28:22; 29:6; 30:8f. All the Gentiles will bring their treasures to Jerusalem and fill the house of the Lord with glory, Hag. 2:7–10. They will come and say: let us go to entreat the favor of the Lord; and ten men will take hold of the hem of a Jewish man and desire to go with him because God is with him, Zech. 2:11; 8:20–23; 14:16–19. The people of the saints receive dominion over all the nations of the earth, Dan. 7:14, 27. Cf. the literature listed in vol. III, 232, and, for the Messianic expectations among the Jews in Jesus’ time, Schürer, *Gesch. des jüd. Volkes im Zeitalter J. Chr.*, 3rd ed., II, 496–556.
