## 5.

Already in the Old Testament there are many indications for a different and better interpretation than Chiliasm offers of the prophetic expectations. Even the modern historical view of Israel acknowledges that the Yahwism of the prophets is distinguished from the nature religions by its moral character and gradually gave a spiritual significance to the religious laws and customs among Israel. True circumcision is that of the heart, Deut. 10:16; 30:6; Jer. 4:4. The sacrifices pleasing to God are a broken heart and a contrite spirit, 1 Sam. 15:22; Ps. 40:7; 50:8f.; 51:19; Hos. 6:6; Amos 5:21f.; Mic. 6:6f.; Isa. 1:11f.; Jer. 6:20; 7:21f.; etc. True fasting is loosening the bonds of wickedness, Isa. 58:3f.; Jer. 14:12. For a large part the struggle of the prophets is directed against the external, self-righteous worship of the people. The essence of the future dispensation therefore consists in this, that the Lord will establish a new covenant with his people, that he will grant them a new heart and write his law in it, and that he will pour out his Spirit upon all, so that they love him with their whole heart and walk in his ways, Deut. 30:6; Jer. 31:32; 32:38f.; Ezek. 11:19; 36:26; Joel 2:28; Zech. 12:10. And this future is indeed portrayed in images borrowed from the historical circumstances, so that Zion and Jerusalem, temple and altar, sacrifice and priesthood continue to occupy a large place in it. But 1º it should be remembered that we too do the same and cannot speak of God and divine things, of spiritual and heavenly things, except in earthly, sensory forms. Old Testament worship was instituted by God also for this purpose, so that we could speak truly of heavenly things not in self-made images but in correct images given to us by him himself. The New Testament therefore also adopts this usage and speaks in the future kingdom of God of Zion and Jerusalem, of temple and altar, of prophets and priests. The earthly is an image of the heavenly: *Alles Vergängliche ist nur ein Gleichniss.* 2º It must not be forgotten that all prophecy is poetry, which must be interpreted according to its own nature. The error of the exegesis that formerly prevailed consisted not in its spiritualization as such, but in its desire to transform all the details serving as illustration into a spiritual sense and thereby, just as with Jesus’ parables, frequently losing sight of the principal thought. When it is said, for example, that the Lord will cause a shoot to spring from the felled trunk of Jesse, that he will elevate Mount Zion upon the summit of the mountains, that he will bring back from the exiles one from a city and two from a clan, that he will sprinkle clean water upon all and cleanse them of their sins, that he will cause the mountains to drip sweet wine and the hills to flow with milk, and so forth, everyone senses that he is dealing here with a poetic description that cannot and may not be understood literally. The realistic interpretation here comes into conflict with itself and fails to recognize the character of prophecy. 3º It is also incorrect that the prophets themselves were wholly unconscious of the distinction between thing and image. Not only were the poetic descriptions mentioned above undoubtedly understood by the prophets as images, but by the names Sodom, Gomorrah, Edom, Moab, Philistia, Egypt, Assyria, and Babylon they repeatedly designate the power of the Gentile world that will one day be subjected to Israel and share in its blessings, Obad. 16, 17; Isa. 34:5; Ezek. 16:46f.; Dan. 2; 7f.; Zech. 14:21. Zion is frequently the name for the people, for the congregation of God, Isa. 49:14; 50:1; 51:3; 52:1; 54:1. And although Old Testament prophecy cannot conceive the future kingdom of God without temple and sacrifice, it nevertheless repeatedly goes beyond all national and earthly relations and proclaims that there will no longer be an ark of the covenant, since all Jerusalem is God’s throne, Jer. 3:16, 17; that the kingdom of the Messiah will be eternal and encompass the whole world, Ps. 2:8; 72:8, 17; Dan. 2:44; that all inhabitants will be prophets and priests, Isa. 54:13; 61:6; Jer. 31:31; that all impurity and sin, all sickness and death, will be banished from it, Isa. 25:8; 33:24; 52:1, 11; Zech. 14:20, 21; Ps. 104:35; that it will be established in a new heaven and upon a new earth and will no longer need sun or moon, Isa. 60:19, 20; 65:17; 66:22. Even Ezekiel’s realistic picture of the future contains elements that make a symbolic interpretation necessary: the equal portions assigned to all tribes, although they differ greatly in numerical strength; the measured strips intended for priests, Levites, and ruler; the separation of temple and city; the temple’s elevated location upon a mountain; and the stream that flows from beneath the threshold of the eastern temple door toward the Dead Sea. And finally, its artificial construction and practical impracticability resist a so-called realistic interpretation. Finally, 4º in the exegesis of the Old Testament the question is not whether the prophets were wholly or partly conscious of the symbolic character of their predictions, for even in the words of classical writers there is more than they themselves thought or intended by them. Rather, the question is what the Spirit of Christ who was in them wished to testify and reveal by them. That is determined by the New Testament, which is the completion, fulfillment, and therefore interpretation of the Old, for in the fruit the nature of the tree is revealed. Even modern criticism acknowledges that not Judaism but Christianity is the full realization of the religion of the prophets.

There can surely be no doubt about this, that the New Testament regards itself as the spiritual and therefore as the perfect and true fulfillment of the Old Testament. The spiritualizing of the Old Testament, provided it is understood in the proper sense, is not an invention of Christian theology but began in the New Testament itself. The spiritualized Old Testament—that is, the Old Testament divested of its temporal, sensory form—is the New Testament. The distinctive feature of the old dispensation was precisely that the covenant of grace was represented under visible images and clothed in national, sensory forms. Sin was symbolized in Levitical impurity. Atonement was effected through the sacrifice of a slaughtered animal. Cleansing was foreshadowed in physical washings. Communion with God was bound to going up to Jerusalem. The need for God’s favor and nearness expressed itself in a longing for his courts. Eternal life was conceived as a long life on earth, and so forth. All that was spiritual, heavenly, and eternal was, in accordance with the capacity of Israel, which was placed as a child under the discipline of the law, clothed in earthly shadows. Although the great mass of the people frequently stopped at those external forms, just as many Christians remain attached to the sign in the sacrament, the pious Israelites did penetrate with their hearts to the spiritual kernel hidden in the shell, but even they saw that spiritual reality only in shadow and image. Therefore the New Testament says that the Old was σκια των μελλοντων, το δε σωμα Χριστου, Col. 2:17, ὑποδειγμα και σκια των ἐπουρανιων, Heb. 8:5. The shadow is not the body, but it nevertheless points to the body and falls away when the body itself has come. The New Testament is the truth, the essence, the kernel, the proper content of the Old Testament: *Vetus Test. in Novo patet, Novum Test. in Vetere latet.* Therefore the New Testament so repeatedly speaks of the truth. Over against the law given through Moses stands the truth that came through Jesus Christ, John 1:14, 17. He is the truth, John 14:6; the Spirit he sent is the Spirit of truth, John 16:13; 1 John 5:6; the Word of God he preached is the Word of truth, John 17:17. The good of salvation promised and foreshadowed under the Old Testament has been revealed in Christ as eternal, true reality for all. All the promises of God are yes and amen in him, 2 Cor. 1:20. The Old Testament has not been abolished, but in the new dispensation has come to its fulfillment and still continues in it to come to fulfillment until the *parousia* of Christ. Christ is therefore the true prophet, priest, and king; the genuine servant of the Lord; the true propitiatory sacrifice, Rom. 3:25; the true circumcision, Col. 2:11; the true Passover, 1 Cor. 5:7; the true offering, Eph. 5:2. And his church is the true seed of Abraham, the true Israel, the true people of God, Matt. 1:21; Luke 1:17; Rom. 9:25, 26; 2 Cor. 6:16–18; Gal. 3:29; Titus 2:14; Heb. 8:8–10; James 1:1, 18; 1 Pet. 2:9; Rev. 21:3, 12; the true temple of God, 1 Cor. 3:16; 2 Cor. 6:16; Eph. 2:22; 2 Thess. 2:4; Heb. 8:2; the true Zion and Jerusalem, Gal. 4:26; Heb. 12:22; Rev. 3:12; 21:2, 10. Her spiritual offering is the true worship, John 4:24; Rom. 12:1; Phil. 3:3; 4:18; cf. vol. III 217. All the concepts of the Old Testament lay aside their external, national-Israelite meaning and are revealed in their spiritual, eternal sense. The Semitic no longer needs to be translated by us into the Japhetic, as Bunsen desired. The New Testament itself gave the particularistic ideas of the Old Testament a universalistic, cosmic significance. Entirely wrong, therefore, is the conception of Chiliasm according to which the New Testament with the church from the Gentiles is an intermezzo, a side road entered upon by God because Israel rejected its Messiah, so that the proper continuation and fulfillment of the Old Testament would begin only at the second coming of Christ. Rather, the reverse is true. Not the New but the Old Testament is an interlude. The covenant with Israel is temporary; the law was inserted between the promise to Abraham and its fulfillment in Christ, so that it might increase the transgression and as a tutor lead to Christ, Rom. 5:20; Gal. 3:19. Therefore Paul always goes back to Abraham, Rom. 4:11f.; Gal. 3:6f., and joins his gospel to the promise made to him. Abraham is the father of believers, of all believers, not only from the Jews but also from the Gentiles, Rom. 4:11. The children of the promise are his seed, Rom. 9:6–8. The blessing of Abraham comes to the Gentiles in Christ, Gal. 3:14. Those who belong to Christ are Abraham’s seed and heirs according to the promise, Gal. 3:29. The people of Israel was temporarily chosen in the days of the Old Testament so that salvation might presently benefit the whole world in the fullness of time. Israel was chosen not to the injury but to the benefit of the peoples. From its first beginning the promise to Adam and Noah had a universalistic tendency and, after laying aside its temporary legal form under Israel, revealed this fully in Christ for all nations. The veil has been torn, the dividing wall has fallen, the written code of the law has been nailed to the cross; and now believers from the Gentiles are fellow heirs with those from the Jews, fellow citizens of the saints, members of God’s household, brought near in Christ, and built upon the same foundation of apostles and prophets, Eph. 1:9–11; 2:11–22. The New Testament is therefore no intermezzo, no interlude, no side road, no deviation from the line of the Old Covenant, but the goal intended long beforehand, the direct continuation, the true fulfillment of the Old Testament. Chiliasm, judging otherwise, comes into conflict with Christianity itself. Considered in principle, it is one with Judaism and must come to ascribe to Christianity, to the historical person of Christ, to his suffering and death, a temporary, passing value, and to expect true salvation only from Christ’s second coming, from his appearing in glory. Like Judaism, it subordinates the spiritual to the material, the ethical to the physical; confirms the Jews in their fleshly disposition; excuses their rejection of the Messiah; thickens the veil that lies upon their face when reading the Old Testament; and promotes the illusion that the fleshly descendant of Abraham will still as such possess a prerogative in the kingdom of heaven. Scripture, however, says that the true reading and interpretation of the Old Testament is found in the one who has turned to the Lord Christ, 2 Cor. 3:14–16; that the one who is a Jew inwardly and partakes of the circumcision of the heart is a Jew, Rom. 2:29; that in Christ there is neither male nor female, neither Jew nor Greek, but that they are all one in Christ Jesus, 1 Cor. 12:13; Gal. 3:28; Col. 3:11. The Jew who becomes a Christian was not, but became through his faith, a child of Abraham, Gal. 3:29.
