## 4.

The basic ideas of chiliasm are substantially the same among all its advocates. They amount to this: that a twofold return of Christ and a double resurrection must be distinguished; that at his first return Christ will overcome the antichristian power, bind Satan, raise the deceased believers, gather around himself the congregation—especially the congregation of converted Israel brought back to Palestine—rule the world from that congregation, and cause a period of spiritual flourishing and material prosperity to dawn for his people; and that at the end of that period he will return once more to raise all human beings from the dead, judge them before his judgment seat, and determine their eternal destiny. Yet these basic ideas allow all kinds of modifications. The beginning of the millennial kingdom was determined in different ways. Following the *Epistle of Barnabas*, many church fathers and later also the Cocceians taught that it would begin with the seventh millennium of the world. The Fifth Monarchy Men had it begin after the fall of the fourth world empire. Hippolytus placed its beginning in the year 500, Groenewegen in 1700, Whiston in 1715 and later in 1766, Jurieu in 1785, Bengel in 1836, Stilling in 1816, and so forth. Its duration was fixed at 400 years (4 Ezra), or 500 (*Evang. van Nicodemus*), or a thousand (the Talmud and others), or twice a thousand (Bengel), or only 7 (Darby), or also an indeterminate number of years, so that the number in Rev. 20:2, 3 is interpreted symbolically (Rothe, Martensen, Lange, and others). Some believe that before the establishment of the millennial kingdom there will be no return of Christ (Kurtz), or at least no visible return (Darby), or a return visible only to believers (Irving), and that no resurrection of believers before the millennium ought to be assumed (Bengel). Many do assume that Christ remains upon earth after his first return, but others are of the opinion that he appears only briefly to establish his kingdom and afterward withdraws again into heaven. According to Piscator, Alsted, and others, Christ’s government in the millennium takes place from heaven. The risen martyrs then share in that dominion, either after being received into heaven (Piscator) or while remaining upon earth (Alsted); or all the risen believers share in it, either remaining here upon earth (Justin, Irenaeus, and others) or being borne up into the air to meet Christ at his appearance in the clouds (Irving); or especially the people of Israel share in it. For the chiliasts ordinarily expect a national conversion of Israel, and most imagine that converted Israel will be brought back to Palestine and will there form the principal citizenry of the millennial kingdom (Jurieu, Oetinger, Hofmann, Auberlen, and others). If one assumes that Christ remains upon earth after his first return, the rebuilt Jerusalem is ordinarily designated as his dwelling place, although the Montanists formerly thought of Pepuza and the Mormons now think of their Salt Lake Valley. The restoration of temple and altar, priesthood and sacrifice was as a rule rejected as too clearly conflicting with the New Testament, but it was nevertheless still defended by the Ebionites and, in more recent times, by Serarius, Oetinger, Hess, and others. Very different conceptions are formed of the character and condition of the millennial kingdom. Sometimes it is described as a kingdom of sensual enjoyments (Cerinthus, the Ebionites, and others); at other times it is conceived more spiritually, and all enjoyment of food and drink, all marriage and procreation, are removed from it (Burnet, Lavater, Rothe, Ebrard). Most often the millennium is regarded as a transitional state between the *Diesseits* and the *Jenseits*. It is a kingdom in which believers are prepared for the vision of God (Irenaeus); in which they enjoy rest and peace without yet being entirely delivered from sin and raised above death; in which nature (Irenaeus) and human beings too (Lactantius) will be extraordinarily fruitful; and in which, according to an idea favored in later times, the congregation will above all complete its missionary work among humanity (Lavater, Ebrard, Auberlen, and others). All these modifications amount to just as many objections to chiliasm; it cannot withstand even the testimony of Old Testament prophecy, to which it preferentially appeals. For besides the fact that, as was already said above, the Old Testament sees in the messianic kingdom not a preliminary, temporary condition but the final result of world history, chiliasm is guilty of the greatest arbitrariness in the interpretation of prophecy. It doubles the return of Christ and the resurrection of the dead without the Old Testament knowing anything of this. It lacks every rule and method in interpretation and arbitrarily comes to a halt according to the subjective opinion of the interpreter. All the prophets proclaim equally loudly and powerfully not only the conversion of Israel and of the nations but also the return to Palestine, the rebuilding of Jerusalem, the restoration of temple, priesthood, and sacrificial service, and so forth. And it is nothing but arbitrariness to interpret one feature of this picture literally and another spiritually. It is one picture of the future that prophecy depicts for us. And this picture must either be taken literally as it presents itself—but then one breaks with Christianity and falls back into Judaism—or an interpretation of this picture altogether different from that attempted by chiliasm must be given. Such an interpretation is indicated to us by Scripture itself and must be derived from it.
