## 12.

This ideal and real connection between Christ’s first and second comings also explains the manner in which the New Testament speaks about the time of his parousia. A whole series of passages places this parousia very near. Jesus connects the prophecy of the consummation of the ages immediately with that of the destruction of Jerusalem, Matt. 24:29 and parallels. Paul considers it possible that he and his fellow believers will still live to see the parousia of Christ, 1 Thess. 4:15; 1 Cor. 15:51. And all the apostles say that they are in the last time, that the coming of the Lord is near, and derive from this an exhortation to watchfulness, Rom. 13:11; 1 Cor. 10:11; Heb. 3:14; 6:11; 10:25, 37; James 5:7–9; 1 Pet. 1:6, 20; 4:17; 5:10; 1 John 2:18; Rev. 1:3; 3:11, 20; 22:7, 10, 12, 20. In the interpretation of this New Testament expectation concerning the speedy return of Christ, errors have been made on both sides. The New Testament contains no doctrine whatsoever concerning the time of Christ’s return. It in no way establishes that this return will take place before or immediately after the destruction of Jerusalem. Many have indeed inferred this from Matt. 10:23; 16:28; 24:34; 26:64 and parallels, but wrongly. For it is beyond reasonable doubt that Jesus spoke of his coming in different senses. In John 14:18–24; cf. 16:16–24, he speaks to his disciples of his coming in the Spirit after the day of Pentecost, or, according to other interpreters, of his coming after the resurrection, when for a short time he would again appear to his disciples. In Matt. 26:64 Jesus not only confirmed his Messiahship before the high council with an oath, but also said that he would convince them of it because from now on, ἀπαρτι, they would see him seated at the right hand of God’s power and would see him coming on the clouds of heaven. Such a coming in his glory is also spoken of elsewhere. Matt. 16:28; cf. Mark 9:1; Luke 9:27, establishes this beyond all dispute. Jesus says there that some of those standing around him will not taste death until they have seen the Son of Man coming in his kingdom. Immediately before, he had admonished them to be concerned above all for the salvation of the soul; and he pressed this admonition by saying that the Son of Man would come clothed with divine glory and would then repay everyone according to his works. But it will not even take *that* long, he adds in v. 28, as it were by way of explanation, for even before all those standing around him have died, the Son of Man already comes in his kingdom, ἐν τῃ βασιλειᾳ αὐτου, that is, with the royal power and dignity the Father will give him. For through his resurrection and ascension Christ is appointed by the Father as Head, King, and Lord, Acts 2:33; 5:31; and from that moment onward he continually comes in his royal dignity as his kingdom is established and expanded on earth. And therefore in Mark 9:1 and Luke 9:27 the expression is explained to mean that many will not taste death until they have seen the kingdom of God or have seen it come with power. Matt. 10:23 must be explained in the same way: the disciples would not have completed their circuit through the cities of Israel before the Son of Man would come. Although the coming is not further explained here at all, the parousia nevertheless cannot possibly be meant, since Jesus would then contradict himself; in Matt. 24 he in any case makes his parousia follow the destruction of Jerusalem. How long after this terrible event it would take place is not said by Jesus. He does indeed connect it immediately with Jerusalem’s fall in his prophecy. Translating εὐθεως in Matt. 24:29 as *suddenly* instead of *immediately*, as also advocated by Van Leeuwen, *De Parousie-verwachting in het N. T.*, Utrecht, 1898, p. 37, makes no change in this, for it is clearly added that the signs of the parousia will begin εὐθεως after the tribulation of those days, in those days, after that tribulation, Mark 13:24; cf. Luke 21:25–27. This is confirmed by Matt. 24:34; cf. Mark 13:29; Luke 21:32, where Jesus says that this generation will not pass away until all these things have happened. The words ἡ γενεα αὑτη cannot be understood of the Jewish people but undoubtedly refer to the generation then living; cf. Cremer, s.v. On the other hand, it is clear that the words παντα ταυτα do not include the parousia itself but refer only to the signs that precede and announce it. For after Jesus has predicted the destruction of Jerusalem, the preliminary signs, his return, and even the gathering of his elect by the angels, and thus has actually ended his eschatological discourse, he proceeds in v. 32 to apply it practically and says that, just as the budding of the leaves of the fig tree announces summer, so too παντα ταυτα are preliminary signs that the end is near or that the Messiah is near, at the door. Here παντα ταυτα without any doubt refers to the preliminary signs of the parousia, not to the parousia itself, for otherwise it would be absurd to say that when παντα ταυτα happen, the end is only near. In v. 34 the words παντα ταυτα have the same meaning; and Jesus therefore does not say that his parousia will take place within the lifetime of the generation now living, but that its preliminary signs and announcements, such as would be seen in the destruction of Jerusalem and the events accompanying it, would begin within the lifetime of the generation now living. And Jesus is so certain of this that he says heaven and earth will pass away, but his words will certainly not pass away. For the rest, however, Jesus refrains from every more precise determination of time. His purpose is not to make his disciples know the exact time of his parousia but to spur them to watchfulness. And therefore he does not say when he will come, but what the signs of the times are that announce his coming. Paying attention to the signs of the times is the duty of Jesus’ disciples; calculating the exact time of his coming is forbidden to them and also impossible. The first requires Jesus to cast his light upon the events that will take place; and so he does, just as all the prophets before him and all his apostles after him have done. Therefore, neither does he say that many more centuries would elapse between the destruction of Jerusalem and his parousia; that would immediately have rendered the admonition to watchfulness powerless again. As prophecy does at all times, Jesus announces in the events of his time the approach of the end. And the apostles follow his example and depict for us in heresy and falsehood, in judgments and acts of judgment, in Jerusalem’s fall and Rome’s empire, the heralds of Christ’s return and the initial fulfillment of his prophecy. For all believers at all times ought to live as if the coming of Christ were imminent. *Die Nähe der Parusie ist gewissermaassen nur ein anderer Ausdruck für die absolute Gewissheit derselben*, Baldensperger in Holtzmann, *Neut. Theol.* I, 312. But for that very reason the second thing, that is, calculating the exact time of the parousia, is also unbecoming for Christians. For Jesus deliberately left this wholly undecided. His coming will be sudden, unexpected, surprising, like that of a thief in the night, Matt. 24:43; Luke 12:39; cf. like a snare, Luke 21:35. Many things must happen before the end is there, Matt. 24:6. The gospel must have been preached in the whole world, Matt. 24:14. The bridegroom delays, and the master of the servants departed abroad for a long time, Matt. 25:5, 13, 19. Weeds and wheat must grow together until the day of harvest, Matt. 13:30. The mustard seed must become a tree and the leaven leaven everything, Matt. 13:32, 33. Indeed, when speaking of his parousia, Jesus expressly says that the day and hour of his coming are known to no one, neither angels nor human beings, not even to the Son, Mark 13:32, and testifies even after his resurrection that the Father has placed the times and seasons for the establishment of his kingdom in his own authority, Acts 1:7. And all the apostles speak likewise. Christ comes like a thief in the night, 1 Thess. 5:1, 2; 2 Pet. 3:10; Rev. 3:3; 16:15. He does not appear until the antichrist has first come, 2 Thess. 2:2f. The resurrection takes place in a fixed order, first that of Christ and afterward that of believers at his coming, 1 Cor. 15:23. And that coming delays because the Lord has a different standard for time than we and in his patience wills that all come to repentance, 2 Pet. 3:8, 9.

Holy Scripture speaks as soberly about the manner of Jesus’ return as it does about its time. The second coming of Christ is frequently designated in the New Testament by the name παρουσια, whether absolutely, Matt. 24:3, or further defined as παρουσια του υἱου του ἀνθρωπου or παρουσια του κυριου ἡμων Ιησου Χριστου, Matt. 24:27, 37, 39; 1 Thess. 3:13; 4:15; 5:23, and so forth, or παρουσια της του θεου ἡμερας, 2 Pet. 3:12. Properly speaking, the word does not include the concept of a return, but indicates that after Jesus has been absent and hidden for a time, Acts 3:21; Col. 3:3, 4, and has then come anew, Matt. 16:27; 24:30, and so forth; cf. Luke 19:12, 15, he will again be present and at hand and will then remain present. Therefore it also alternates with ἐπιφανεια, Matt. 24:30; 1 Tim. 6:14; Titus 2:13; ἀποκαλυψις, Luke 17:30; 1 Cor. 1:7; 2 Thess. 1:7; 1 Pet. 1:7, 13; φανερωσις, Col. 3:4; 1 Pet. 5:4; 1 John 2:28. In 2 Thess. 2:8 there is even mention of ἡ ἐπιφανεια της παρουσιας αὐτου. This parousia is a work of God insofar as he will send his Christ and determines the times and seasons for it, Acts 1:7; 3:20; 1 Tim. 6:14–16, but it is also an act of Christ himself as the Son of Man, to whom judgment has been given by the Father and who must reign as king until all enemies have been placed under his feet, John 5:27; 1 Cor. 15:25. Since at his departure from the earth he was received into heaven, at his parousia he comes again from heaven, Phil. 3:20; 1 Thess. 1:10; 2 Thess. 1:7; Rev. 19:11. And just as a cloud received him at his ascension and hid him from the eyes of his disciples, Acts 1:9, so in Old Testament language he is also described as returning on clouds of heaven that bear him toward this earth as a triumphal chariot, Matt. 24:30; 26:64; Mark 13:26; 14:62; Luke 21:27; Rev. 1:7; 14:14. For he does not return in the form of a servant but with great power and with his own and his Father’s glory, Matt. 16:27; 24:30; Mark 8:38; 13:26; Luke 22:27; Col. 3:3, 4; 2 Thess. 1:9, 10; Titus 2:13, as King of kings and Lord of lords, Rev. 17:14; 19:11–16, surrounded by his angels, Matt. 16:27; 25:31; Mark 8:38; Luke 9:26; 2 Thess. 1:7; Rev. 19:14, and by his saints, among whom perhaps those already made blessed are also included, 1 Thess. 3:13; 2 Thess. 1:10; Jude 14. Although because of its unexpectedness his parousia resembles the breaking in of a thief in the night, it will nevertheless be visible to all human beings over the whole earth, be like lightning that flashes from one side of heaven to the other, Matt. 24:27; Luke 17:24; Rev. 1:7, and be announced by the voice of an archangel and the trumpet of the angels, Matt. 24:31; 1 Cor. 15:52; 1 Thess. 4:16. In connection with their doctrine of the ascension, vol. III, 414, the Lutherans said that the return of Christ was subject to no succession of moments but consisted in nothing other than the sudden becoming visible of Christ’s body, which at his exaltation had become invisible and omnipresent. Although it was generally acknowledged that the return of Christ was *visibilis* and *localis*, by this they meant only that through a *singularis Dei dispositio* the human nature of Christ became visible for a time in a definite place for the special purpose of judgment without thereby relinquishing its presence in other places; Gerhard, *Loc.* XXVII, *de extr. jud.*, n. 35; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV, 614; Hollaz, *Ex.*, 1249. But the Reformed ascribed a bodily, local, temporal character to the return of Christ; they even acknowledged that this return, although it would be very sudden, was nevertheless *successiva*, subject to a succession of moments. Even at the highest stage of his exaltation, at the return for judgment, Christ retained his truly human nature; cf. M. Vitringa IV, 160.
