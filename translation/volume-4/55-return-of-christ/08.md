## 8.

In discussing the expectations the New Testament cherishes concerning the future of the people of Israel, it was left undecided whether the New Testament perhaps teaches in passages other than those treated there an intermediate state between this dispensation and the consummation of the ages. If this were the case, it was acknowledged, then Matt. 23:37–39; Luke 21:24; and Acts 3:19–21, although in themselves they gave no occasion whatever for assuming such a transitional period, could nevertheless be understood and explained in that sense. The question now arises, therefore, whether according to Jesus and the apostles the church is to expect a time of power and glory that precedes the general resurrection and the world judgment. If this were so, we would expect it to be mentioned clearly in the eschatological discourse Jesus gave to his disciples during the last days of his life, Matt. 24; Mark 13; Luke 21. But it contains not a word, not even an allusion, concerning such a kingdom. The chiliasts do try to insert their millennium at one place or another in this discourse and say, for example, that the first coming of Christ is mentioned in Matt. 24:27 and the second coming in v. 30, but this exegesis lacks all foundation. In his eschatological discourse Jesus answers two questions his disciples put to him: namely, when the things he had spoken concerning Jerusalem would happen—that not one stone of the temple would be left upon another—and what would be the sign of his coming and of the consummation of the world. Jesus first answers the first question, in such a way that he deals first with the preliminary signs, Mark 13:1–8; cf. Matt. 24:1–8; Luke 21:5–11; then with the fate of the disciples, Mark 13:9–13; cf. Matt. 24:9–14; Luke 21:12–18; and finally with the catastrophe in Judea, Mark 13:14–23; cf. Matt. 24:15–26; Luke 21:20–24. The second question, concerning Jesus’ parousia and the consummation of the world, is answered in Mark 13:24–31; cf. Matt. 24:29–35; Luke 21:25–33. And here Jesus connects his parousia immediately with the destruction of Jerusalem; in the fall of this city he sees the announcement and preparation of the consummation of the world: Matt. 24:29, εὐθεως; Mark 13:24, ἐν ἐκειναις ταις ἡμεραις. He even says that this generation will certainly not pass away until all these things have happened, Matt. 24:34; Mark 13:30; Luke 21:32. However this expectation by Jesus of his speedy parousia, immediately following the destruction of Jerusalem, is to be understood—to which we will return shortly—in any case it is clear that in this discourse there is no place for a thousand-year kingdom. Jesus first enumerates in Mark 13:1–8 some general preliminary signs by which the disciples can see that everything together—namely, the destruction of Jerusalem and the end of the world—is approaching. And these *signa communia* are the appearance of false Christs; wars and rumors of wars; turmoil and uprising among the nations, with earthquakes, famine, and so forth; further, the preaching of the gospel in the whole world as a testimony to all nations, Mark 13:10; and finally, as a prelude to the final drama, what occurs in Judea and Jerusalem, Mark 13:14–23. Then follow the preliminary signs immediately preceding the parousia, the *signa propria*: namely, the darkening of sun and moon, the falling of the stars, and the shaking of the powers in the heavens, Mark 13:24, 25. With the parousia of Christ the judgment, the separation of good and evil, and the consummation of the world are then immediately connected, Mark 13:26, 27. This agrees with what Jesus says in Matt. 13:37–43 and 47–50: the growing together of weeds and wheat and the gathering together of all kinds of fish continue until the consummation of the ages, until the time of the harvest and of the world judgment. Jesus knows only two ages, the present and the coming. In the present age his disciples can expect nothing other than tribulation and persecution and must forsake everything for his sake. Nowhere does Jesus predict for his disciples a glorious future on earth before the consummation of the world. On the contrary, as it went with him, so it will go with his church; a disciple is not above his teacher and a servant not above his master. Only in the coming age do his disciples receive everything back, together with eternal life, Matt. 5:3–12; 8:19, 20; 10:16–42; 16:24–27; 19:27–30; John 16:2, 33; 17:14, 15, and so forth. Accordingly, when the disciples ask Jesus in Acts 1:6 whether he will at this time restore the kingdom to Israel, he does not deny but tacitly concedes that this will one day happen. But he says that the Father has placed the times or seasons for it in his own authority and that the disciples in this time have the calling to act as his witnesses from Jerusalem to the uttermost part of the earth. The whole New Testament, written from the standpoint of the church under the cross, speaks in this same spirit. Believers, among whom there are not many wise, powerful, and noble, 1 Cor. 1:27, can expect nothing but suffering and tribulation here on earth, Rom. 8:36; Phil. 1:29. They are guests and strangers, Heb. 11:13; their citizenship is in heaven, Phil. 3:20; they do not regard the things that are seen, 2 Cor. 4:18, but set their minds on the things above, Col. 3:2; here they have no lasting city but seek the one to come, Heb. 13:14. Yet they are saved in hope, Rom. 8:24, and know that if they suffer with Christ, they will also be glorified with him, Rom. 6:8; 8:17; Col. 3:4. Therefore, with the whole groaning creation, they eagerly reach out toward the coming of Christ and the revelation of the glory of the children of God, Rom. 8:19; 1 Cor. 15:48f., and the suffering of the present time does not compare with that glory, Rom. 8:18; 2 Cor. 4:17. Nowhere in the New Testament does any hope shine through that the church of Christ will one day attain power and dominion here on earth. The highest it envisions is that under kings and all who are in high position it may lead a tranquil and quiet life in all godliness and dignity, Rom. 13:1f.; 1 Tim. 2:2. And therefore the New Testament does not commend in the first place those virtues that equip one for conquering the world, but, while avoiding all false asceticism, Rom. 14:14; 1 Tim. 4:4, 5; Titus 1:15, names as fruits of the Spirit the virtues of love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, self-control, Gal. 5:22; Eph. 4:32; 1 Thess. 5:14f.; 1 Pet. 3:8f.; 2 Pet. 1:5–7; 1 John 2:15, and so forth.

Indeed, it is the consistent expectation of the New Testament that as the gospel of the cross spreads, the hostility of the world will also become manifest. Christ is appointed to be a rising for many but also a fall for many, and to bring their hostile deliberations to light. He came as a crisis into the world, so that those who do not see may see and those who see may become blind, Matt. 21:44; Luke 2:34; John 3:19–21; 9:39; Rom. 9:32, 33; 1 Cor. 1:23; 2 Cor. 2:16; Heb. 4:12; 1 Pet. 2:7, 8. In the last times, in the days preceding the return of Christ, human wickedness will rise to a terrifying height. The days of Noah will return; lust, sensual pleasure, licentiousness, greed, unbelief, pride, mockery, and blasphemy will break out in a dreadful manner, Matt. 24:37f.; Luke 17:26f.; 2 Tim. 3:1; 2 Pet. 3:3; Jude 18. Among the confessors too the apostasy will be great; the deception will become so powerful that, if it were possible, it would bring even the elect to ruin. The love of many will grow cold and vigilance diminish so greatly that the wise virgins fall asleep along with the foolish. It will become such a general apostasy that Jesus can ask whether the Son of Man at his coming will still find faith on earth, Matt. 24:24, 44f.; 25:1f.; Luke 18:8; 1 Tim. 4:1. The book of the Revelation of John agrees with this. The letters to the seven churches do indeed treat concrete conditions as they existed in those churches at the time, and they are first of all addressed to those churches to spur them to watchfulness and prepare them for the coming persecutions and the return of Christ. But they clearly have a much broader scope. The number seven, which always has such great significance in Revelation, already points to this; it is the number of completeness and causes the seven churches selected here from the many churches in Asia Minor to appear as types of the whole Christian church. The letters John addressed to them did not first exist separately and were not each first sent by itself to the respective church, but they belong together, were composed and joined together, and are addressed to the whole church: whoever has ears, let him hear what the Spirit says to all the churches. But although the letters thus undoubtedly have a significance extending much further than the seven churches then existing in Asia Minor and mentioned by name, this significance does not consist in their describing successive periods in the history of Jesus’ church and being a small compendium of the whole history of the church. Rather, they depict ecclesiastical conditions that existed at the time and that are at the same time typical of the whole church of Christ, that can therefore repeatedly occur in the church, and that will especially return at the end of the days. For it is clear that they were all written under the impression of the coming persecution and the speedy return of Christ. They all contain a reference to the parousia and with a view to it urge the churches to watchfulness and faithfulness. They serve to call Christendom, which was becoming increasingly conformed to the world, back to its first love, to awaken it from indifference, and, in view of the crown awaiting it, to gird it for battle and cause it to persevere with unbroken faithfulness unto death. For the day of the Lord approaches. After John has first described the conditions that existed in his time and would exist later, especially toward the end of the world, in the church of Christ, he proceeds to relate what will happen afterward, 4:1. In heaven the Lamb opens the book of God’s decree concerning the end of things, chs. 4 and 5, and in particular an angel shows John what pertains to the very last time, ch. 10, and concerns all the peoples of the earth, 10:11. In turn John now transports us to earth and to heaven. In heaven everything has already been decided and determined; there honor is already brought to God and the Lamb as though the battle had already been fought and the victory won, chs. 4 and 5. There the souls of the martyrs are already clothed in long white robes and wait only for the fulfillment of their number, 6:9–11. There John already sees proleptically the whole multitude of the redeemed standing before the throne, 7:9–17. There the prayers of the saints have already been answered by God, 8:1–4. There too, proleptically, the 144000 who had been sealed, 7:1–8, have already been received, go before the others as firstfruits, 14:1–5, and have obtained victory over the beast and its image, 15:1–4. There the whole multitude of the redeemed already brings glory and honor to God because the marriage of the Lamb has come, 19:1–8. The church on earth therefore need not be afraid of the judgments with which God visits the world at the end. The 144000 servants of God from all the tribes of the children of Israel are sealed beforehand, 7:1–8. The temple and the altar and those who worship there are not handed over to the Gentiles; and the two witnesses who prophesied there are indeed killed but also raised and received into heaven, 11:1–12. And the Christian church, though persecuted by Satan for Christ’s sake, finds a refuge in the wilderness, 12:1–14. In principle the battle has already been decided. For Christ has been received into heaven, 12:5, and Satan has been defeated by Michael and his angels and cast from heaven to earth, 12:7–11. Now he has only a short time on earth, 12:12. But he makes use of that time. He causes the beast to rise from the sea or the abyss, 13:1; 11:7; 17:8, and gives it power and glory. This beast is the Roman empire, 13:1–10; is supported by another beast, the beast from the earth, that is, the false prophet, false religion, the antichrist, 13:11–18; realizes itself fully in one person, who can therefore himself be called the beast, 13:3, 12, 18; 17:8, 10, 11; and has its center in the city of Babylon, that is, Rome, the great prostitute who rules over all peoples, chs. 17 and 18. But all this development of power is vain. Through the opening of the seven seals, the sounding of the seven trumpets, and the pouring out of the seven bowls, God reveals his wrath, visits nature and humanity with his judgments, and prepares the final judgment. First Babylon falls, ch. 18. Then Christ appears, 19:11–16, defeats the beast from the sea and the beast from the earth, vv. 19–21, and soon afterward Satan as well, 20:1–3.
