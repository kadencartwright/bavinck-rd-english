## 10.

The blessedness of communion with God is enjoyed in and heightened through the communion of saints. Already on earth this communion is a glorious benefit of faith. Those who for Jesus’ sake have left house or brothers or sisters or father or mother or wife or children or fields receive already in this life, with persecutions, houses and brothers and sisters and mothers and children and fields in return, Mark 10:29, 30, for all who do the Father’s will are Jesus’ brother and sister and mother, Matt. 12:50. Through the mediator of the New Testament, believers come into communion not only with the church militant on earth but also with the church triumphant in heaven, the assembly of the firstborn, the spirits of the perfected righteous, and even with the many thousands of angels, Heb. 12:22–24. But this communion, though already existing in principle on earth, above, p. 28, will nevertheless be incomparably richer and more glorious when all dividing walls of descent and language, of time and space, have been demolished, all sin and error banished, and all the elect brought together in the new Jerusalem. Then Jesus’ prayer will be fully answered, that all his sheep form one flock under one Shepherd, John 10:16; 17:21. All the saints together will then fully comprehend what is the breadth and length and depth and height of the love of Christ, Eph. 3:18; together they will be filled unto all the fullness of God, Eph. 3:19; Col. 2:2, 10, for Christ, whom God fills, Col. 1:19, in turn fills the church with himself and makes it his *pleroma*, Eph. 1:23; 4:10; cf. vol. III, 417. And reclining with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob at one table, Matt. 8:11, they raise with one voice the song of praise to the honor of God and of the Lamb, Rev. 4:11; 5:12, and so forth. Of the church on earth Scripture says repeatedly that it forms a little flock, Matt. 7:14; 22:14; Luke 12:32; 13:23, which history confirms down to the present day. And even toward the end of the days, when the gospel will have been preached among all peoples, apostasy will increase and the number of the faithful will be small. Old Testament prophecy already announced that only a remnant of Israel would be converted to the Lord and saved, above, p. 429; and the New Testament likewise cherishes the expectation that those who persevere to the end will be few, Matt. 24:13; 25:1f.; Luke 18:8. But on the other hand, Scripture nevertheless frequently speaks in a very universalistic manner. The covenant of grace is made known in Adam to all humanity, Gen. 3:15. The covenant of nature established after the flood embraces all creatures, Gen. 9:9, 10. In Abraham all the families of the earth are blessed, Gen. 12:3. The redemption that will one day be granted to Israel benefits all the Gentiles, above, p. 433. Jesus says that he will give his life as a ransom for *many*, Matt. 20:28, and that *many* will come from east and west and recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, Matt. 8:11. The grace that appeared in him is far more abundant than Adam’s transgression; it comes upon all human beings unto justification of life, Rom. 5:12–20; 1 Cor. 15:22. In this dispensation all things in heaven and on earth are now gathered into one under Christ, Eph. 1:10. And one day at the end every knee will bow before Christ and every tongue confess him as Lord, Phil. 2:10, 11. Then a great multitude that no one can number will stand before the throne and the Lamb, Rev. 7:9; 19:1, 6. They are peoples who are saved and walk in the light of the new Jerusalem, Rev. 21:24, 26; 22:2. And God will then be all in all, 1 Cor. 15:28. In connection with and by appeal to this last series of texts, many have cherished the hope that in the end, if not all creatures, then at least all human beings, and if even this should not be the case, then at least by far the majority of human beings would be saved; hell would not exist at all or would be only a small corner of the universe. They grounded this expectation either on the possibility of also being saved by the works of the law—Pelagians, Socinians, Deists, and so forth—or on the opportunity to hear the gospel and accept it in faith even after death in the intermediate state or even still after the day of judgment—Universalists. These views were discussed earlier and thus need not now be tested against Scripture again; cf. vol. I, 232; II, 318f., 340f., 350f.; III, 390–408; and above, p. 499f. But even among those who maintain the confession that no one comes to the Father except through Christ and that only one name has been given under heaven for salvation, John 14:6; Acts 4:12, there have always been some who believed in the possibility of salvation in this life apart from the preaching of the gospel. They taught this concerning the children of the covenant, concerning all children who die young within and outside the bounds of Christianity, concerning those with intellectual disabilities, the mentally ill, and deaf-mutes who were in fact deprived of the preaching of the gospel, and also concerning some or many Gentiles who in their clear insight and virtuous life gave evidence of true piety. Some church fathers assumed an activity of the Logos in the Gentile world, vol. I, 238. Augustine believed that from the beginning there had always been some not only among Israel but also among other peoples who believed in the Logos and lived piously and righteously according to his commandments, *Ep.* 102; *de civ.* 18, 47, and other passages in Reuter, *Aug. Studien*, 1887, p. 90f. Abelard asserted that Gentiles too could obtain salvation, in Münscher-v. Coelln, *D. G.* II, 147. According to Strauss, *Chr. Gl.* I, 271, Luther once expressed the wish that God might also be gracious to men such as Cicero and Seneca, and Melanchthon wished to leave undecided whether God had perhaps communicated to Solon, Themistocles, and others some knowledge of forgiveness in Christ by a special way. Zwingli spoke more decisively and believed that God also had his elect among the Gentiles, *Chr. fidei expos.*, *Op.* IV, 65. But others left open only the possibility and dared do no more than hope and wish, such as À Lasco, in Kuyper, *Heraut*, 1047; Zanchi, in Shedd, *Dogm. Theol.* I, 436; II, 704; Bilderdijk, *Brieven* V, 81; Kuyper, *Heraut*, 594; cf. 1047; Ebrard, *Das Dogma v. h. Ab.* II, 77; Shedd, *Dogm. Theol.* I, 436; II, 704. This view, however, always remained the view of a few; the churches did not pronounce upon it in their confessions, and most theologians opposed it; cf. literature in M. Vitringa I, 29. People judged somewhat more favorably concerning the salvation of children who die young. Roman Catholics teach that all Christian children who die, baptized *voto* or *re*, are saved, and that all other children who die young suffer in the *limbus infantum* a *poena damni*, not a *poena sensus*; Lombard and others on *Sent.* II, dist. 33. The Lutherans judge concerning Christian children as do Roman Catholics and leave the others to God’s judgment; Gerhard, *Loc.* XVI, § 169; Buddeus, *Inst. theol.* V I, 6. The Reformed inclined to believe that all children born in the covenant of grace and then taken away by death before the years of discretion obtain heavenly blessedness, Canons of Dort I, 17; Voetius, *Disp.* II, 417, although here too many distinguished between elect and reprobate children and did not dare to ascribe salvation with certainty to each of these children individually; Martyr, *Loci Comm.*, pp. 76, 436; Beza, Pareus, Zanchi, Perkins, and others. Concerning children who die young outside the covenant, some judged quite mildly. Junius preferred to conjecture from love that they were saved rather than lost, *Op.* II, 333, and Voetius said: whether they are lost, or whether some among them are elect and are regenerated before their death, *nolim negare, affirmare non possum*, *Disp.* II, 413; cf. further literature in M. Vitringa II, 51, 52, and especially B. Warfield, “The Development of the Doctrine of Infant Salvation,” in *Two Studies in the History of Doctrine*, New York: Christian Literature Company, 1897, pp. 143–239. With Scripture in hand, both in relation to the salvation of the Gentiles and to that of children who die young, we can go no further than to refrain from a decided and definite judgment in either a positive or negative sense. It deserves notice only that Reformed theology is in a much more favorable position on these serious questions than any other. For all other churches can cherish a milder judgment here only when they retreat from their doctrine of the absolute necessity of the means of grace or infringe upon that of the condemnability of sin. But the Reformed, in the first place, did not wish to determine the measure of grace by which a human being could still be bound to God even amid many errors and sins, nor to define the degree of knowledge indispensably necessary for salvation; Voetius, *Disp.* II, 537, 538, 781; Witsius, *Apost. Geloof* II, 2 and 15; Spanheim, *Op.* III, 1291. And in the second place, they maintained that the means of grace were not absolutely necessary for salvation and that God could also regenerate unto eternal life apart from Word and sacraments, Calvin, *Inst.* IV, 16, 19. In the Second Helvetic Confession, art. 1, it reads: *agnoscimus Deum illuminare posse homines, etiam sine externo ministerio, quos et quando velit; id quod ejus potentiae est*. And the Westminster Confession declares in ch. X, § 3, that elect infants who die in infancy are regenerated and saved by the Spirit of Christ, *qui quando et ubi et quo sibi placuerit modo operatur*, and that this also applies to the rest of the elect, *quotquot externae vocationis per ministerium verbi sunt incapaces*. Reuter therefore rightly says, when he has set forth Augustine’s doctrine on this point: *In der That, es lässt sich das Paradoxon rechtfertigen, gerade die partikularistische Prädestinationslehre habe jene universalistisch klingenden Phrasen ermöglicht*, *Aug. Studien*, 92. In Reformed theology even the universalistic texts of Scripture cited above come most fully and beautifully into their own. For those texts are certainly not meant universalistically in the sense that all human beings or even all creatures are saved, nor have they been understood in this way by a single Christian church. All confess without exception that there is not only a heaven but also a hell. At most, therefore, there is a difference over the number of those who are saved and those who are lost. But that cannot be disputed, for that number is known to God alone. To the question, Lord, are there only a few who are saved? Jesus gave only the answer: strive to enter through the narrow door, for many will seek to enter and will not be able, Luke 13:24. This alone is of direct importance to us; the number of the elect need not be known to us. But in any case this stands firm: in Reformed theology the number of the elect need not for any reason or in any respect be conceived as smaller than in any other theology. When it comes down to it, the Reformed confession is larger-hearted and broader in outlook than any other Christian confession. It finds the last and deepest cause of salvation only in God’s good pleasure, in his eternal compassion, in his unfathomable mercy, in the unsearchable riches of his almighty and free grace. What firmer, broader foundation could be found alongside this for the salvation of a guilty and lost human race? Let many then fall away; however moving this may be, in Christ the church, humanity, the world are nevertheless preserved. The organism of creation is restored. The wicked are consumed from the earth, Ps. 104:35; they are cast outside, John 12:31; 15:6; Rev. 22:15. But under Christ all things in heaven and on earth are gathered into one, Eph. 1:10. All things were created through him and for him, Col. 1:16.
