## 9.

The blessings in which the blessed share are therefore not only spiritual but also material or bodily in nature. As wrong as it is, with the pagan peoples and also with some chiliasts, to make the latter the principal component of future blessedness, so one-sided is it also to reckon them indifferent in Stoic fashion or to exclude them altogether from blessedness. Scripture always keeps the spiritual and the natural in close connection. Since the world consists of heaven and earth and the human being of soul and body, holiness and glory, virtue and happiness, the moral and natural orders of the world must also finally be harmoniously united. The blessed will therefore be free not only from all sin but also from all the consequences of sin: from ignorance and error, John 6:45; from death, Luke 20:36; 1 Cor. 15:26; Rev. 2:11; 20:6, 14; from poverty and sickness, sorrow and fear, hunger and thirst, cold and heat, Matt. 5:4; Luke 6:21; Rev. 7:16, 17; 21:4; from all weakness, dishonor, and corruption, 1 Cor. 15:42, and so forth. But the spiritual blessings are nevertheless the foremost and are innumerably many: holiness, Rev. 3:4, 5; 7:14; 19:8; 21:27; salvation, Rom. 13:11; 1 Thess. 5:9; Heb. 1:14; 5:9; glory, Luke 24:26; Rom. 2:10; 8:18, 21; adoption as children, Rom. 8:23; eternal life, Matt. 19:16, 29, and so forth; the vision of and conformity to God and Christ, Matt. 5:8; John 17:24; Rom. 8:29; 1 Cor. 13:12; 2 Cor. 3:18; Phil. 3:21; 1 John 3:2; Rev. 22:4; communion with, service to, and praise of God and Christ, John 17:24; 2 Cor. 5:8; Phil. 1:23; Rev. 4:10; 5:9, 13; 7:10, 15; 21:3; 22:3, and so forth. Because all these benefits are already granted in principle to believers on earth, as, for example, adoption as children, Rom. 9:4; 8:15; Gal. 4:5; Eph. 1:5, and eternal life, John 3:15, 16, 36, and so forth, many have conceived the blessedness Christ grants exclusively as a present one that is increasingly realized only by way of an ethical process; Pfleiderer, *Grundriss*, § 177; Biedermann, *Dogm.*, § 974f.; Scholten, *Initia*, ch. 7. Ritschl and many of his adherents also one-sidedly emphasize the *diesseitige Weltstellung des Menschen*, regard the moral freedom the Christian receives over against the world in faith as the principal benefit, and speak little or not at all of the eternal blessedness Christ prepares for his own in the future; Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* III², 459f., 484f., 534f., 600f. Over against the abstract supernaturalism of the Greek and Roman church, which conceives blessedness exclusively as transcendent and therefore considers the Christian ideal of life here on earth embodied in the monk, this view defends an important truth. Returning to Scripture, the Reformation overcame this supernaturalistic and ascetic view of life in principle. The one who believes has at that same moment forgiveness of sins and eternal life; he is a child of God who serves the Father not as a servant from hope of reward but as a son who fulfills the Father’s will from love and gratitude. And he fulfills that will not by fleeing from the world but by being faithful in the calling entrusted to him here on earth. Living for heaven therefore forms no contrast with living in the midst of the world; precisely in that world Christ preserves his disciples from the Evil One. For the new heaven and earth are built up from the elements of the world that now exists, and the church is restored humanity under Christ as Head. But although blessedness is in a certain sense already the portion of believers on earth, it is nevertheless so only in principle and not in full reality. Believers are saved in hope, Rom. 8:24. Jesus pronounces the poor in spirit, and so forth, blessed because theirs is the kingdom of heaven that will be established on earth in the future, Matt. 5:3–10. Believers are children of God and nevertheless still expect the full realization of that sonship, Matt. 5:9; Rom. 8:23. They have eternal life and nevertheless must still receive it at the resurrection, also according to John, 5:20–29; 6:40, 44, 54. Both are therefore true: that the kingdom of heaven is present and that it nevertheless is still coming. And this double truth determines the whole character of the state of glory. Just as the new heaven and earth are formed from the elements of this world and the church is a re-creation of the human race fallen in Adam, so the life of the blessed hereafter must be conceived in analogy with the life of believers here on earth. On the one hand, it does not consist in a *visio Dei* in the Roman sense, to which human nature can be raised only by a *donum superadditum*; and on the other hand it is also not a slow and gradual development of the Christian life already led by believers here on earth. It is a genuinely natural life but raised by grace to its highest glory and unfolded in its richest beauty; the *materia* remains, but the *forma* differs. Religion, that is, communion with God, therefore occupies the first, central place in it. But that communion will be richer, deeper, more blessed than it ever was or could be here on earth, for it will be disturbed by no sin, broken by no distance, mediated by no nature or Scripture. Now in the mirror of God’s revelation we see only his image; then we see face to face and know as we are known. *Visio, comprehensio, fruitio Dei* constitute the essence of future blessedness. The blessed see God—not indeed with bodily eyes, but nevertheless in a manner that far surpasses all revelation in this dispensation through the means of nature and Scripture. And accordingly they will all know him, though each according to the measure of his capacity, with a knowledge that has its image and likeness in God’s knowledge, direct, immediate, pure and clear; vol. II, 150–155. They then receive and possess everything they expected here only in hope. And thus, beholding God and possessing God, they enjoy God and are blessed in communion with him; blessed in soul and body, in understanding and will. In theology there was a difference over whether blessedness hereafter resided *formaliter* in the understanding or in the will, and thus consisted in knowledge or in love. Thomas said the former, *S. Theol.* I 2, qu. 3, art. 4, and Duns Scotus asserted the latter, *Sent.* IV, dist. 49, qu. 4. But Bonaventure united the two and observed that the *fruitio Dei* was not only a fruit of the *cognitio Dei* but also of the *amor Dei* and had its cause in the union and cooperation of both, *Sent.* IV, dist. 49, p. 1, art. unic., qu. 4, 5; cf. Voetius, *Disp.* II, 1217–1239.
