## 4.

After the resurrection follows the judgment, which in the Old Testament is presented as a victory by the Messiah over all Israel’s enemies, but in the New Testament is described more spiritually as a judicial work of Christ in which he judges and sentences all human beings in accordance with the law God has given them. For the first time Jesus came to earth not to condemn the world but to save it, John 3:17; 12:47. Yet immediately at his appearance he brought about a κρισις whose effect and purpose are that those who do not see may see and those who see may become blind, 3:19, 20; 9:39. As the Son of Man Jesus continually exercises judgment when already here on earth he grants eternal life to those who believe and allows the wrath of God to remain upon those who do not believe, 3:36; 5:22–27. There is therefore undoubtedly an inward, spiritual judgment; a crisis that proceeds from generation to generation; an immanent judgment in the *Diesseits* that is convened in human consciences. Faith and unbelief already bring their fruit and reward with them here on earth; just as faith is followed by justification and peace with God, so unbelief leads to progressing darkness and hardening and to surrender to all kinds of unrighteousness. Indeed, even apart from the opposition between faith and unbelief, virtue and vice each bear their own fruits. Good and evil also have their own reward in natural life, not only in the acquittal or accusation of conscience but also in the external prosperity or adversity frequently connected with them. Moreover, Scripture and history vie with one another in teaching that blessing and curse, mercy and wrath, favors and judgments alternate in the life of human beings and peoples. There is a great truth in the poet’s saying: *die Weltgeschichte ist das Weltgericht*. Yet in this saying truth is mingled with falsehood. It is conceived not theistically but pantheistically and undermines all judgment instead of confirming and upholding it. For if world history is *the* world judgment, it ceases altogether to be a judgment and becomes a natural process that does not concern itself at all with the tremendous opposition of good and evil, and drives this back into the hidden recess of conscience—and even there only for a time. For then there is no longer a God who can make the order of nature serviceable to the moral order, but there is nothing other than a natural power that rules the whole physical world and soon also restricts and causes to disappear that limited territory initially allotted to the moral dominion of the good. For the good is not a power capable of withstanding nature if it does not have its support in an almighty God who is Creator of both the natural and the moral order. Pantheism does indeed continually object that the good must be done for its own sake and not from hope of reward or fear of punishment. But the soul’s longing for the triumph of the good, for the victory of justice, has nothing whatsoever in common with the selfish desire for earthly happiness and sensory satisfaction. On the contrary, although Scripture takes account of the fact that the human being is a sensory being and holds before him a reward great in heaven, that reward is always subordinate to the honor of God’s name and, together with the good works in which believers walk, was acquired by Christ. It is precisely the godly who eagerly look forward to the day when God glorifies his name before the eyes of all creatures and in their cause causes his own to triumph over all opposition. And this longing becomes stronger as the blood that cries for vengeance flows over the earth in a broader and deeper stream, as injustice triumphs, wickedness increases, falsehood prevails, and Satan’s kingdom expands and exalts itself against the kingdom of righteousness. All history cries out for a world judgment; the whole creation groans for it; all peoples testify to it; the martyrs in heaven cry out for it with a loud voice; the church prays for Christ’s coming; and Christ himself, who is the Alpha and the Omega, says: Behold, I come quickly, and my reward is with me, to repay everyone according to his work. Thus, although Scripture, especially in the Gospel of John, recognizes a spiritual judgment continuing throughout history, it nevertheless also speaks everywhere of a final judgment that causes the kingdom of Christ to triumph over all unrighteousness. World history may be a world judgment; *the* world judgment takes place at the end of the days, when Christ comes to judge the living and the dead. Holy Scripture repeatedly ascribes judgment to the Father, Matt. 18:35; 2 Thess. 1:5; Heb. 11:6; James 4:12; 1 Pet. 1:17; 2:23; Rev. 20:11, 12. But he nevertheless exercises this work through Christ, to whom he has given all judgment and whom he has appointed as Judge, John 5:22, 27; Acts 10:42; 17:31; Rom. 14:9, and who therefore will one day summon all human beings before his judgment seat and judge them according to their works, Matt. 25:32; Rom. 14:9–13; 2 Cor. 5:10; 2 Tim. 4:1, 8; 1 Pet. 4:5; Rev. 19:11–21. For Christ is the Son of Man who already brought about a crisis through his appearance, continues it in history, and completes it at the end of the days. The relation to him determines the human being’s eternal well-being or woe; in the judgment of the living and the dead he celebrates his highest triumph and attains the consummation of his kingdom and the complete subjection of all his enemies. Therefore the principal question in the last judgment is also that of faith or unbelief. For faith in Christ is the work of God par excellence, John 6:29; 1 John 3:23. The one who believes does not come into judgment, John 5:24, and the one who does not believe is already judged and remains under God’s wrath, John 3:18, 36. The standard in the final judgment is therefore in the first place the gospel, John 12:48. But that gospel does not stand opposed to the law and cannot even be conceived apart from it; for the demand of faith is itself already grounded in the law, and the gospel is the restoration and fulfillment of the law. Therefore, at the final judgment all the works accomplished by human beings and recorded in the books before God’s face are also taken into consideration, Eccles. 12:14; 2 Cor. 5:10; Eph. 6:8; 1 Pet. 1:17; Rev. 20:12; 22:12. For those works are expressions and fruits of the principle of life dwelling within the heart, Matt. 7:17; 12:33; Luke 6:44, and include everything done by the human being not in the intermediate state but in the body—not only deeds, Matt. 25:35f.; Mark 9:41, 42; Luke 6:35; 14:13, 14; 1 Cor. 3:8; 1 Thess. 4:6, and so forth, but also words, Matt. 12:36, and the hidden counsels of the heart, Rom. 2:16; 1 Cor. 4:5. For nothing remains hidden and everything becomes manifest, Matt. 6:4, 6, 18; 10:26; Eph. 5:11–14; 1 Tim. 5:24, 25. The norm in the final judgment is therefore the whole Word of God in its two parts, law and gospel. Yet Scripture says clearly that account will be taken of the measure of revelation allotted to someone. Those who knew the Lord’s will and did not do it will be beaten with double blows, Luke 12:47. It will be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon on the day of judgment than for Jerusalem and Capernaum, Matt. 10:15; 11:22, 24; Mark 6:11; Luke 10:12, 14; Heb. 2:3. Those who did not hear the gospel are also judged not according to the gospel but according to the law; and the Gentiles, who did not know the Mosaic law but sinned against the law known to them by nature, also perish without that Mosaic law, while the Jews are judged precisely by it, Rom. 2:12. Although Scripture has judgment extend to all human beings without exception, Matt. 25:32; Acts 17:31; Rom. 2:6; 14:10; 2 Cor. 5:10; 2 Tim. 4:1; Rev. 20:12, it nevertheless distinguishes between those nations that knew the gospel and ultimately brought forth antichristianity and those other peoples who never heard of Christ and therefore learn of him for the first time at his parousia, while it further speaks separately of the judgment of the evil angels and of the place the good angels and believers occupy in the final judgment.

Certainly it is difficult to form a somewhat clear conception of that judgment. Without doubt it does not bear an exclusively inward and spiritual character, so that it would take place only in human conscience; rather, it is specifically a judgment that is also carried out outwardly in the sight of all creatures. However much image and reality may flow together, the appearance of Christ, the resurrection, and likewise everything related of the judgment are depicted too realistically for it to be permissible to spiritualize everything. But then the holding of this judgment also requires a place and some span of time. And Scripture gives us reason to think that it has a successive course. The angels accompany Christ at his coming on the clouds in order to assist him in the execution of the sentence; they gather the righteous, separate the wicked from them, and drive them away from before his face, Matt. 13:30, 49; 24:31. Moreover, he is surrounded by the blessed, 1 Thess. 3:13; 4:16; 2 Thess. 1:10; Jude 14; Rev. 17:14; 19:14. After the resurrection of the believers who died and the transformation of those left alive have taken place, they are caught up together in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air, 1 Thess. 4:17. It is not impossible that, just as in Christ’s case resurrection and ascension were distinct and were even separated from one another by forty days, so too the resurrection or transformation of believers at the end of the days does not at once add to them that full glory which will be their portion after the renewal of the world in the new heaven or on the new earth; Lampe and Gerdes in M. Vitringa IV, 143. But however this may be, the resurrection or transformation includes justification for believers, just as it did for Christ. Scripture does indeed say that all human beings without distinction, thus believers too, must appear before the judgment seat of Christ. But it testifies at the same time that the one who believes is not judged and does not come into judgment, for he already has eternal life, John 3:18; 5:24; that believers who died are already in heaven with Christ and clothed with long white garments, 2 Cor. 5:8; Phil. 1:23; Rev. 6:11; 7:9, 14; and that Christ comes to be glorified in his saints and marveled at in all who believe, 2 Thess. 1:10. Before Christ pronounces the sentence upon the evil angels, upon the antichristian world, and upon the peoples without culture, he has already placed the sheep at his right hand and is surrounded by his angels and his saints. This also appears from 1 Cor. 6:2, 3, where Paul expressly says that the saints will judge the world and the angels; M. Vitringa IV, 163. For this statement may not be weakened into an approval by believers of the judgment Christ passes upon the world and angels, but in view of the context specifically indicates that the saints will participate in the judgment of the world and angels. Indeed, Jesus already promised his twelve disciples that they would sit with him on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel, Matt. 19:28; Luke 22:30, and John saw around God’s throne other thrones in heaven occupied by the elders of the church, Rev. 4:4; 11:16; 20:4, 6. For Christ and his church are one; what the world and angels have done against her, he reckons as though it had been done against him, Matt. 25:40, 45; Mark 9:41, 42. This judgment by Christ and his church extends even to the good angels, 1 Cor. 6:3, for the angels are ministering spirits sent out to serve for the sake of those who will inherit salvation and therefore receive a place in the future kingdom of God according to the service they have performed in relation to Christ and his church. In John’s vision Christ, surrounded by his armies, therefore advances to meet the antichristian power, Rev. 19:11–21; the church triumphant shares in his royal dominion, Rev. 20:4–6, and finally with Christ puts an end to all opposition when he judges the nations dwelling at the four corners of the earth, Rev. 20:7–10.
