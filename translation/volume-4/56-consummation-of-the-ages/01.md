# § 56. The Consummation of the Ages

## 1.

With the appearance of Christ upon the clouds begins the יום יהוה, ἡ ἡμερα του κυριου ἡμων Ιησου Χριστου, Matt. 24:36f.; Luke 17:24f.; 21:34; Acts 17:31; 1 Cor. 1:8; 5:5; etc. By this Scripture by no means intends to indicate that everything belonging to the last things—the return, resurrection, judgment, and so forth—runs its course within a period of twelve or twenty-four hours. Under the Old Testament the day of the Lord was that time in which God would come to his people as king in a wonderfully glorious manner in order to deliver them from all their enemies and cause them to dwell with him in Jerusalem in peace and safety. With that coming of God the great turning point arrived at which the old age passed into the new and all conditions and relations in nature and the human world would change completely. Later this was represented by the Jews in such a way that with the day of the Lord the present world-age, עולם הזה, passed over into the future one, עולם הבא, which was then frequently further distinguished into the days of the Messiah, ימות המשיח, lasting three generations or 40 or 100 or 600 or 1000 or 2000 or 7000 years, and the eternity entering thereafter, עולם הבא or עתיד הבא: Weber, *System*, 354. According to the New Testament, the last part of the αἰων οὑτος began with the first coming of Christ, so that we now live in the last days or in the last hour, 1 Cor. 10:11; Heb. 1:2; 9:26; 1 John 2:18; and with his second coming the αἰων μελλων enters, Matt. 19:28; Mark 10:30; Luke 18:30; 20:35; 1 Cor. 15:23; Heb. 2:5; etc.; cf. Cremer, s.v. αἰων. And this αἰων μελλων begins with the ἡμερα του κυριου, that is, the time in which Christ appears, raises the dead, pronounces judgment, and renews the world. This time is nowhere represented in the New Testament as destined to last long. Paul says in 1 Cor. 15:52 that the transformation of those left alive and the resurrection of deceased believers will take place in a point of time, in an instant; cf. 1 Thess. 4:15–17. Resurrection and final judgment are most closely joined, as though into one act, Luke 14:14; 2 Cor. 4:14; Rev. 20:11–13; and the judgment is set on one day, Matt. 10:15; 11:22; etc., indeed even at one hour, Rev. 14:7. But this last designation proves that Scripture does not think of restricting all the events in the *parousia* of Christ to precisely a span of twenty-four hours or sixty minutes. The word ὡρα, originally meaning season, frequently designates a much longer time than an hour of sixty minutes, Matt. 26:45; John 4:21; 5:25; 16:2, 32; Rom. 13:11; 1 John 2:18. The events that must take place in the *parousia* of Christ are also so comprehensive that they will certainly occupy a considerable time. The inventions of this century have reduced distances to a minimum for mutual intercourse, for the exercise of fellowship, and for hearing and seeing what happens at a great distance; and they are probably still only the beginning and prophecy of what will be discovered in following centuries. But however much account ought to be taken of all this in the doctrine of the last things, nevertheless the appearance of Christ so that all see him; the resurrection of all the dead and transformation of those left alive; the pronouncement of judgment upon all human beings according to all their works; and the burning and renewal of the world are such tremendous events that they can take place only over a certain span of time.

The first event that follows the appearance of Christ is the resurrection of the dead. This is not the result of a development of bodies in general, or in particular of the resurrection body implanted in believers through regeneration and sacrament; cf. Kübel, Herzog² 1, 764. Rather, it is the effect of an almighty, creative act of God, Matt. 22:29; 1 Cor. 6:14; 15:38; 2 Cor. 1:9. Specifically, the Father performs this work through the Son, to whom he has given to have life in himself, John 5:28; 6:39, 40, 44; 1 Cor. 6:14; 2 Cor. 4:14; 1 Thess. 4:14; who is the resurrection and the life, the firstborn from the dead, John 11:25; Acts 26:23; 1 Cor. 15:20; Col. 1:18; Rev. 1:5; and who must therefore necessarily bring about the resurrection of his own, John 6:39, 40; 1 Cor. 15:20–23, 47–49. Scripture undoubtedly teaches a universal resurrection, a resurrection not only of believers but also of unbelievers and of all human beings, Dan. 12:2; Matt. 5:29, 30; 10:28; John 5:29; Acts 24:15; Rev. 20:12, 13; and it ascribes this too to Christ, John 5:29. Yet it speaks of this universal resurrection very seldom because it stands in an altogether different relation to Christ than does the resurrection of believers. The resurrection of the dead in general is, after all, only indirectly a fruit of the work of Christ. It became necessary only because temporal death entered; and temporal death was separated from eternal death because God intervened with his grace. The punishment for sin was originally death, death in its full extent and weight. But because God had chosen for himself from the fallen human race a congregation for eternal life, he immediately postponed temporal death in the case of Adam and Eve, allowed them to reproduce from generation to generation, and only at the end of the ages consigns those who are disobedient to his law and gospel to eternal destruction. The universal resurrection therefore serves only to restore in all human beings the bond between soul and body, temporarily broken because grace in Christ intervened, to place them all as *human beings*, soul and body together, before God’s judgment seat, and to cause them to hear the judgment from his mouth. The Father brings about this universal resurrection too through Christ, because he has given not only life but also judgment to the Son, and this judgment must strike the whole human being, both soul and body, John 5:27–29; cf. M. Vitringa IV 149. The resurrection of the dead in general is therefore in the first place a judicial act of God. But this act is full of rich consolation for believers. And therefore in Scripture the resurrection of the congregation everywhere stands in the foreground, so much so that the resurrection of all human beings is sometimes entirely set aside and left unmentioned, Job 19:25–27; Ps. 73:23–26; Hos. 6:2; 13:14; Isa. 26:19, 20; Ezek. 37; Mark 12:25; 1 Cor. 15; 1 Thess. 4:16; Phil. 3:11. This resurrection is the proper, true resurrection and was acquired directly by Christ, for it is not merely a reunion of soul and body but a making alive, a renewal, an immediate entrance into communion with Christ in both soul and body, a being recreated according to God’s image, Rom. 8:11, 29; Phil. 3:21. Therefore Paul makes the resurrection of believers coincide with the transformation of those left alive. The latter have no advantage over the former, for rising precedes being transformed, and together they are then borne up into the air to meet the Lord, 1 Cor. 15:51, 52; 2 Cor. 5:2, 4; 1 Thess. 4:15–17.
