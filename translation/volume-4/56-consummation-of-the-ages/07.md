## 7.

After the final judgment follows the renewal of the world. Some, with Thomas, *S. Theol.*, suppl., qu. 74, art. 7, have indeed placed it before the last judgment, but the ordinary conception is nevertheless that it follows the judgment and begins only when the wicked have already been banished from the earth. Without doubt this order also accords most closely with that in Holy Scripture. In the Old Testament the day of the Lord is indeed preceded by all kinds of dreadful signs, and the judgment upon the nations takes place amid all kinds of terrible events, but the new earth with its extraordinary fertility begins only when the victory over the enemies has been won and the people of Israel have returned to and been restored in their land. Likewise, according to the New Testament, the day of judgment is preceded by many signs, such as the darkening of sun, moon, and stars, the shaking of the powers of heaven, and so forth, Matt. 24:29, but the burning of the earth first takes place on the day of the Lord, 2 Pet. 3:10, and afterward come the new heaven and new earth in which righteousness dwells, 2 Pet. 3:13. When the judgment has been carried out, John sees the new Jerusalem descending from God out of heaven, Rev. 21:1f. In this expectation of a renewal of the world, Scripture takes a position between two extremes. On the one hand, many, such as Plato, Aristotle, Xenophanes, Philo, Maimonides, Averroes, Nolanus, Peyrère, Edelmann, Czolbe, and others, have claimed that this world would continue eternally in its present form. And on the other hand, Origen, the Lutherans, the Mennonites, the Socinians, Vorstius, the Remonstrants, and also some Reformed, such as Beza, Rivetus, Junius, Wollebius, and Prideaux, were of the opinion that it would not merely be changed in form but destroyed in substance and replaced by an entirely new world, M. Vitringa IV, 194–200. But neither of these two views finds support in Scripture. Old Testament prophecy expects an extraordinary change in the whole of nature but nevertheless teaches no annihilation of the present world. The passages in which this latter view is thought to be taught, Ps. 102:27; Isa. 34:4; 51:6, 16; 65:17; 66:22, do describe in very strong terms the change that will begin after the day of the Lord, but nevertheless do not imply an annihilation of the world’s substance. In the first place, the description given there is far too rich in imagery for a literal *reductio ad nihilum* of the whole world to be inferred from it. Further, the perishing, אבד, of heaven and earth, Ps. 102:27, which in itself never indicates an absolute annihilation of substance, is explained by the fact that they will grow old like a garment, change like a robe, fall like a leaf, and disappear like smoke, Ps. 102:27; Isa. 34:4; 51:6. And finally, the word create, ברא, which is used of the new heaven and new earth, Isa. 65:17, by no means always indicates a production out of nothing, but frequently designates such an activity of God whereby he causes something new to emerge from the old, Isa. 41:20; 43:7; 54:16; 57:19. Therefore it also alternates with planting, founding, making, Isa. 51:16; 66:22, and in Isa. 51:16 the Lord can say that he begins that new creation by placing his word in Israel’s mouth and covering it with the shadow of his hand. The New Testament proclaims in the same way that heaven and earth will pass away, Matt. 5:18; 24:35; 2 Pet. 3:10; 1 John 2:17; Rev. 21:1; that they will perish and grow old like a garment, Heb. 1:11; be dissolved, 2 Pet. 3:11; be burned, 2 Pet. 3:10; and be changed, Heb. 1:12. But none of these expressions implies an annihilation of substance. For Peter expressly teaches that the old earth, which arose through the separation of the waters, perished through the waters of the flood, 2 Pet. 3:6, and that the present world will likewise perish, although by virtue of God’s promise no longer through water but through fire. An annihilation of substance in the perishing of the present world can therefore no more be contemplated than in the destruction of the former world in the flood; fire burns, cleanses, purifies, but does not annihilate. The contrast in 1 John 2:17—he who does the will of God remains forever—teaches that the words “the world is passing away with its desire” mean not an annihilation of the substance of the world but a disappearance of the world in its form devastated by sin. Paul therefore also says very clearly that the form, το σχημα, of this world is passing away, 1 Cor. 7:31. Indeed, only such a renewal of the world accords with what Scripture teaches concerning redemption. For redemption is never a second, new creation but a re-creation of what exists. God’s honor consists precisely in this: that he redeems and renews the same humanity, the same world, the same heaven and the same earth that were corrupted and defiled by sin. Just as a human being in Christ is a new creature in whom the old has passed away and everything has become new, 2 Cor. 5:17, so too this world passes away in its present form in order, at God’s word of power, to give existence from its womb to a new world. Just as with the individual human being, so at the end of the days a regeneration also takes place with the world, Matt. 19:28, which is not a physical creation but a spiritual renewal. Cf.
