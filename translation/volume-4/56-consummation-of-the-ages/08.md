## 8.

This renewal of the visible world brings to light the one-sidedness of spiritualism, which confines future blessedness to heaven. With respect to Old Testament prophecy there can be no doubt that it describes blessedness as earthly; it expects that after the great day the people of God will dwell in safety and peace in Palestine under the anointed king from David’s house and will be surrounded and served by the Gentile nations. There is truth in Delitzsch’s words on Isa. 66:24: *Das ist ja eben der Unterschied des A. und N. T., dass das A. T. das Jenseits verdiesseitigt, das N. T. das Diesseits verjenseitigt; dass das A. T. das Jenseits in den Gesichtskreis des Diesseits herabzieht, das N. T. das Diesseits in das Jenseits emporhebt.* But nevertheless they do not do full justice to the New Testament expectation of future blessedness. Undoubtedly there is in the New Testament a spiritualization of Old Testament prophecy. Because Jesus’ coming divides into a first and a second, the kingdom of God is first planted in a spiritual sense in human hearts; and the goods of that kingdom are all inward and invisible—forgiveness, peace, righteousness, eternal life. Accordingly, the essence of future blessedness is also conceived more spiritually, especially by Paul and John, as being always with the Lord, John 12:26; 14:3; 17:24; 2 Cor. 5:8; Phil. 1:23; 1 Thess. 4:17; 5:10; 1 John 3:2. But that blessedness is not thereby confined within heaven. That this cannot be the case is evident in principle already from the fact that the New Testament teaches the incarnation of the Word and the bodily resurrection of Christ, expects his bodily return at the end of the days, and has the bodily resurrection of all human beings, and especially that of believers, take place immediately afterward. All this overthrows the spiritualism that, if it remained faithful to its principle, would be permitted, like Origen, to leave nothing but spirits in an uncreated heaven after the day of judgment. Scripture, however, teaches altogether differently. According to it, the world consists of heaven and earth, the human being of soul and body, and accordingly the kingdom of God also has a spiritual, hidden side and an external, visible side. While Jesus came the first time to establish that kingdom in a spiritual sense, he returns at the end of the days also to give it visible form. Reformation proceeds from within outward; the regeneration of human beings is completed in the regeneration of creation; only then is the kingdom of God fully realized, when it is also visibly extended over the earth. The disciples too understood it in this way when after Jesus’ resurrection they asked him whether he would now restore the kingdom to Israel. And in his answer to that question Jesus does not deny that he will one day establish such a kingdom, but says only that its times have been fixed by the Father and that his disciples now have the calling to be his witnesses in the power of the Holy Spirit to the uttermost part of the earth, Acts 1:6–8. Elsewhere he expressly testifies that the meek will inherit the earth, Matt. 5:5, and presents future blessedness as a banquet at which one reclines with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, Matt. 8:11, enjoys food and drink, Luke 22:30, eats of the new, perfect Passover, Luke 22:16, and drinks of the fruit of the new vine, Matt. 26:29. In this dispensation, until the parousia, the eye of believers is indeed directed upward, toward heaven. There is their treasure, Matt. 6:20; 19:21; there is Jesus, who is their life, seated at God’s right hand, John 14:3; 17:24; Col. 3:1–3; there is their citizenship, while they are strangers here, Phil. 3:20; Heb. 11:13–16; there the inheritance is kept for them, Heb. 10:34; 1 Pet. 1:4. But that inheritance is destined to be revealed. Christ one day returns visibly and then causes the whole church, indeed the whole world, to share in his glory. Not only are believers changed into his image, John 17:24; Rom. 8:17, 18, 29; Phil. 3:21; Col. 3:4; 1 John 3:2, but the whole creation will be freed from the bondage of corruption into the freedom of the glory of the children of God, Rom. 8:21. Earth and heaven are renewed so that righteousness dwells in them, 2 Pet. 3:13; Rev. 21:1. The heavenly Jerusalem, which is now above and was the pattern of the earthly Jerusalem, then descends to earth, Gal. 4:26; Heb. 11:10, 13–16; 12:22; 13:14; Rev. 3:12; 21:2f. This new Jerusalem is not identical with the church, although it can figuratively be called the bride of the Lamb, Rev. 21:2, 9, for Heb. 12:22, 23 very clearly distinguishes between the heavenly Jerusalem and the assembly of the firstborn—the saints of the Old Testament—and of the perfected righteous—Christians who have fallen asleep. The heavenly Jerusalem is a city built by God himself, Heb. 11:10. It is the city of the living God because God is not only its architect but also himself dwells there, Rev. 21:3. The angels are its servants and form the court of the great King, Heb. 12:22; the blessed are its citizens, Rev. 21:27; 22:3, 4. The description John gives of that Jerusalem, Rev. 21 and 22, certainly may no more be taken literally than his preceding visions. This is already excluded by the fact that John represents it as a cube whose length, width, and height are equal—namely, 12000 stadia, that is, 300 German miles—while the height of the wall is nevertheless only 144 cubits, Rev. 21:15–17. In his description John intends not to provide a drawing of the city but to give thoughts and interpret them in images, since the glory of the kingdom of God can be brought to our consciousness in no other way. And he borrows those images from paradise, with its river and tree of life, Rev. 21:6; 22:1, 2; from the earthly Jerusalem, with its gates and streets, Rev. 21:12f.; from the temple, with its holy of holies in which God himself dwelt, Rev. 21:3, 22; and from the whole realm of nature, with all its treasures of gold and precious stones, Rev. 21:11, 18–21. But although it is thoughts that are interpreted in this way through images, those thoughts are nevertheless not imaginations or fictions but descriptions in the *Diesseits* of realities in the *Jenseits*. Whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is of good report in the whole creation, in heaven and on earth, is brought together in the future city of God, but renewed, re-created, raised to its highest glory. The substance for it is present in this creation. Just as the caterpillar develops into a butterfly; as carbon is transformed into diamond; as the grain of wheat, dying in the earth, produces another; as the whole of nature revives in spring and arrays itself in festive dress; as the church is formed from Adam’s fallen race; as the resurrection body is raised from the body that died and was buried in the earth—so one day, through Christ’s re-creating power, the new heaven and earth emerge from the elements of this world purified by fire, radiant in imperishable glory and forever freed from the δουλεια της φθορας. More glorious than this beautiful earth, more glorious than the earthly Jerusalem, more glorious even than paradise will be the glory of the new Jerusalem, of which God himself is the artist and architect. The *status gloriae* will not be a pure restoration of the original *status naturae* but a reformation that, thanks to the power of Christ, causes all ὑλη to pass into εἰδος, all *potentia* into *actus*, and will place the whole creation before God’s face, radiant in unfading splendor and blooming in the spring of eternal youth. Substantially, nothing is lost. Outside, indeed, are the dogs and the sorcerers and the sexually immoral and the murderers and the idolaters and everyone who loves or practices falsehood, Rev. 22:15. But in the new heaven and new earth the world is restored; in the church the human race is preserved; in that church, purchased and gathered by Christ from every nation and language and tongue, Rev. 5:9, and so forth, all the peoples, including Israel, each retain their distinct place and calling, Matt. 8:11; Rom. 11:25; Rev. 21:24; 22:2. And all those peoples bring together in the new Jerusalem whatever glory and honor each has received from God according to its distinct nature, Rev. 21:24, 26.
