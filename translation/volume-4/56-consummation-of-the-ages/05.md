## 5.

The place to which the wicked are consigned after the judgment bears the name gehenna in the New Testament. The Hebrew גֵּי הִנּוֹם was originally the name of the Valley of Hinnom, which lay southeast of Jerusalem and according to Josh. 15:8; 18:16 formed the boundary between two tribes. Under Ahaz and Manasseh this valley became a place for the cult of Molech, in whose honor children were slaughtered and burned, 2 Kings 16:3; 21:6; 2 Chron. 28:3; 33:6; Jer. 32:34, 35. Therefore under Josiah this place was destroyed and declared unclean by the priests, 2 Kings 23:10. Jeremiah prophesied that a terrible massacre of the Israelites would be carried out here and that the name of the Valley of Topheth would be changed into that of the Valley of Slaughter, Jer. 7:32; 19:6. And the apocryphal Book of Enoch expressed the expectation that the wicked would be gathered in this valley for judgment. For this reason the name Gehinnom was later transferred to the place of punishment of the wicked after death. According to others, however, this transfer had another cause. After the Valley of Hinnom had been destroyed by Josiah, it was used, according to the later Jews, for dumping and burning all kinds of refuse. Just as Gan Eden designated the place where the righteous dwelt after death, so Gehinnom became the name of the region to which the unclean and wicked were consigned to suffer punishment in the eternal fire. Fire, after all, had from ancient times been a revelation and symbol of the wrath and indignation of the Lord. Israel’s God is a consuming fire, an everlasting burning, Deut. 4:24; 9:3; Isa. 33:14. He spoke to the children of Israel from the midst of the fire, Deut. 4:12, 33; 5:4, 22–26; 9:10; 10:4; cf. Ex. 3:2. His wrath is a burning fire issuing from his nostrils, Ps. 18:9; 79:5; 89:47; Jer. 4:4. Fire issuing from the face of the Lord consumes the offering, Lev. 9:24. By fire he destroys Nadab and Abihu, Lev. 10:2; the murmurers among the people, Num. 11:1; Ps. 106:18; the Korahites, Num. 16:35; and the companies sent against Elijah, 2 Kings 1:10f. And in fire he will one day come to execute justice upon earth and punish the wicked, Deut. 32:22; Ps. 11:6; 83:15; 97:3; 140:11; Isa. 30:33; 31:9; 66:15, 16, 24; Jer. 4:4; 15:14; 17:4; Amos 1:4f.; Joel 2:30. That fire burns down to the lowest Sheol, Deut. 32:22; it is never quenched, Isa. 66:24, and burns eternally, Jer. 17:4. This conception passed over into the New Testament. Gehenna is the place of punishment of the wicked after the day of judgment, distinguished from ἁδης, φυλακη, ἀβυσσος, see above, p. 374, but identical with the καμινος του πυρος, Matt. 13:42, 50, and the λιμνη του πυρος, Rev. 19:20; 20:10, 14, 15; 21:8. It is the place appointed for the beast from the abyss and for the false prophet, Rev. 19:20; for Satan and his angels, Rev. 20:10; for death and Hades, Rev. 20:14; and for all the wicked, Rev. 20:15; 21:8. And all of these are cast into it after the resurrection, Matt. 5:29, 30; 10:28, and after the final judgment, Rev. 19:20; 20:10, 14, 15; 21:8, while before that time Hades, the prison, φυλακη, 1 Pet. 3:19; Rev. 20:7, or the *abyssus* are their dwelling places, and the punishment of eternal fire or the gloom of darkness is still reserved for them, Matt. 8:29; 25:41, 46; 2 Pet. 2:17; Jude 13. For in that gehenna the eternal, unquenchable fire burns, Matt. 18:8; Mark 9:43, 44, 48; the worm that does not die gnaws, Mark 9:44, 48; and there is eternal torment, Matt. 25:46; 2 Thess. 1:9; Rev. 14:11. It is a γεεννα or καμινος του πυρος, Matt. 5:22; 13:42, 50; 18:9, and at the same time a place of utter and outer darkness, Matt. 8:12; 22:13; 25:30; 2 Pet. 2:17; Jude 13; cf. Deut. 5:22; Ps. 97:2, 3; located outside, Rev. 22:15; in the depths, so that one is cast into it, Matt. 5:29, 30; Rev. 19:20; 20:10, 14, 15; far from the wedding table of the Lamb, Matt. 8:11, 12; 22:13; far from fellowship with God and with Christ, Matt. 7:23; 25:41; Luke 13:27, 28; 2 Thess. 1:9; and in the company of Satan and his angels, Matt. 25:41; Rev. 20:10, 15. The wrath of God reveals itself there in all its terror, Rom. 2:5–8; 9:22; 1 Thess. 1:10; Heb. 10:31; Rev. 6:16, 17, so that gehenna is not only a place of deprivation but also of sorrow and pain, both in soul and in body, a place of κολασις, Matt. 25:46; Rev. 14:10, 11; of κλαυθμος and βρυγμος των ὀδοντων, Matt. 8:12; 13:42; etc.; of θλιψις and στενοχωρια, Rom. 2:9; 2 Thess. 1:6; of ἀπωλεια, Matt. 7:13; Rom. 9:22; Phil. 1:28; 3:19; 2 Pet. 3:7; Rev. 17:8, 11; of φθορα, Gal. 6:8; ὀλεθρος, 1 Thess. 5:3; 2 Thess. 1:9; 1 Tim. 6:9. Gehenna is the domain of the second death, Rev. 2:11; 20:6, 14, 15; 21:8.

Upon this firm foundation of Scripture the doctrine of the eternity of hellish punishment was built in the Christian church; and theology and preaching, poetry and painting, often vied with one another in graphic description and realistic depiction of the pains suffered there in body and soul in the eternal fire. Yet from time to time objections were brought against this doctrine. And since the *Aufklärung* in the previous century caused a milder judgment of sin and crime to arise, abolished instruments of torture, moderated punishments, and everywhere awakened a feeling of humanity, an altogether different view of the punishments of hell also arose, and these were either represented in modified form or entirely rejected by many: Lecky, *Gesch. d. Ursprungs und Einflusses der Aufklärung in Europa*, Leipzig, 1873, I 259f. The grounds upon which the eternity of hellish punishment is opposed always amount to this: *a.* Eternal punishment conflicts with the goodness, love, and mercy of God and makes him into a tyrant who delights in afflicting and tormenting and prepares praise for himself from the eternal groaning of millions of unhappy creatures. *b.* Eternal punishment conflicts with the justice of God, since it bears no relation and is not proportionate to sin, which, however terrible, nevertheless always bears a limited, finite character. It is inconceivable that God, who is perfect love and supreme righteousness, will punish a human child with everlasting torment even if that child had sinned for a thousand years. *c.* Such an eternal punishment is also unimaginable and inconceivable. Scripture speaks of fire and worm and darkness, but these are all images; taken literally, they would exclude one another. But apart from this, what is the value of an eternal punishment that has no other purpose than to torment the sinner eternally? What benefit does it have for the one who undergoes it, since by its very nature it must exclude true repentance and cause him continually to go on sinning? What honor does it bring to God’s name if it does not overcome and destroy sin but perpetuates it and causes it to continue eternally? And how is it possible that under such an eternal punishment the lost continually harden themselves without ever coming to repentance and humbling themselves before God? *d.* Scripture accordingly does not teach an eternal, endless punishment in hell. It does indeed speak of eternal pain and so forth, but eternal there, as elsewhere, does not have the meaning of endless; rather, it designates a duration whose limit eludes observation or calculation. The word αιωνιος designates what extends beyond a longer or shorter αἰων. This is further reinforced by the fact that αἰωνιος, used *in bonam partem* of the goods of salvation, for example of life, chiefly designates an inward quality by which all these goods of salvation are represented as elevated above transience. By contrast, the state of the lost is designated as ἀπωλεια, φθορα, ὀλεθρος, θανατος, which indicates that they cannot continue to exist eternally in this way but must either be completely annihilated or one day be entirely restored. *e.* For the latter Scripture offers hope when it teaches that Christ is a propitiation for the sins of the whole world, 1 John 2:2; Col. 1:19, 20, and that in this way God wills the salvation of all, 1 Tim. 2:4; 4:10. Just as all human beings die in Adam, so they are all made alive in Christ, 1 Cor. 15:22; Rom. 5:18. God now gathers all things together under Christ as head, Eph. 1:10, so that one day every knee may bow before Christ, Phil. 2:10, and God may be all in all, 1 Cor. 15:28. God has shut up all under sin so that he might have mercy upon all, Rom. 11:32.

Upon these considerations the following three hypotheses concerning the end of the wicked are then built, if we leave aside pantheism and materialism, which deny all immortality and eternity. First, there are those who teach that a possibility of conversion remains open not only in the intermediate state until the final judgment, see above, p. 382, but also after it and throughout all eternity. Whether there is therefore a hell and an eternal punishment depends entirely upon the human being and his free will. If he continually resists the call to conversion, he entangles himself ever more firmly and deeply in sin and prolongs his punishment. But since the preaching of faith and conversion never ceases and the human will always remains free, the possibility of eternal punishment in hell becomes highly improbable, and people prefer to flatter themselves with the hope that in the end all come to conversion and enter eternal life. Eternal pain in Scripture therefore means only that those who convert so late will always retain the memory of their obstinate resistance and will eternally rank behind those who believed the gospel in this life. This hypothetical universalism therefore amounts to a continual purification and is a renewal of the doctrine of the transmigration of souls. The only difference is that metempsychosis has this purification take place in the *Diesseits*, while hypothetical universalism places it in the *Jenseits*. This doctrine found acceptance chiefly among the Rationalists in the previous century but is also now defended by many theologians; cf. Wegscheider, *Instit.* § 200; Bretschneider, *Dogm.* II 468f., 581f.; Reinhard, *Dogm.* 706f.; Lange, *Posit. Dogm.* § 131; Dorner, *Gl.* II 972; Nitzsch, *Ev. Dogm.* 624; W. Schmidt, *Christl. Dogm.* II 517; Saussaye, my *Theol. van Ch. d. l. S.* 71–75; H. Ernst, *Geloof en Vrijheid*, 1886, pp. 407–444. Further, in England there are the advocates of the so-called *future (second) probation* or of the *wider hope*, such as Robertson; Maurice, *Theol. Essays*, 1853, p. 442, “the word eternal and the punishment of the wicked”; Thomas de Quincey, *On the supposed scriptural expression for eternity*, 1852; Tennyson, *In Memoriam*; Farrar, *Eternal Hope*, 1878, and *Mercy and Judgment*, 1881, with the literature called forth by these two works; cf. *The wider hope, essays and strictures on the doctrine and literature of future punishment by numerous writers, lay and clerical*, London: Unwin, 1890. In America there are the defenders of the Andover position adopted by the five professors of Andover College—J. W. Churchill, Harris, Hincks, Tucker, and Egbert C. Smyth—who departed from various articles of the confession, including that concerning eternal punishment; cf. *Andover Review*, April 1890, p. 434; etc. This view of continuing conversion and purification naturally leads to the doctrine of the so-called universalists, who think that in the end all creatures will partake of blessedness and glory. What is desired and hoped for there is certainly expected here and proclaimed as dogma. The doctrine of the return of all things into God already occurs in Indian and Greek philosophy, passed from there into Gnosticism and Neoplatonism, and was then first presented in Christian theology by Origen. He does repeatedly speak of an eternal punishment in hell but nevertheless sees in it only a practical doctrine that is necessary for the undeveloped but is conceived altogether differently by the gnostics. According to Origen, all spirits were originally created equal by God, but the acts of free will bring about inequality and cause human souls to be placed in a material world for purification and bound to bodies. But this purification continues after death and after the final judgment until, out of and through the greatest possible diversity, equality again emerges and all spirits return to God in that same condition in which they originally dwelt with him. Since free will, however, always remains the same, it can return from good to evil just as well as from evil to good, and there is therefore a continual alternation of apostasy and restoration of all things, an endless creation and annihilation of the material world; cf. Atzberger, *Gesch. der christl. Eschat.*, 1896, pp. 366–456. This idea of the restoration of all things found resonance in antiquity with Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa, Didymus, Diodorus of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia, and others; cf. Petavius, *de angelis* III 7, 8; in the Middle Ages with Scotus Erigena, Amalric of Bena, and the brothers and sisters of the Free Spirit; after the Reformation with Denck and many Anabaptists, with Jane Leade, J. W. Petersen, Ludwig Gerhard, F. C. Oetinger, Michael Hahn, Jung-Stilling, Swedenborg, and others; and in more recent times with Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.* §§ 117–120 and § 163, *Anhang*; Schweizer, *Gl.* II 577f., 591, 604; Schoeberlein, *Prinzip und System der Dogm.*, 679; Riemann, *Die Lehre von der Apokatastasis*², Magdeburg, 1897; Scholten, *Initia*, 268 sq.; W. Francken, *Geloof en Vrijheid*, 1886; cf. Köstlin, article “Apokatastasis” in Herzog³. Much greater assent, however, was found by a third opinion known by the name conditional immortality. Although earlier theology very frequently spoke of immortality in the spiritual sense as a gift acquired by Christ, almost no one therefore thought of denying the natural immortality of the soul. The Socinians were the first to teach, under the influence of their abstract supernaturalism, that souls were not immortal by nature but first became so in case of obedience through a gift of God. From this it followed that the wicked and the devils, by virtue of a natural perishability, would one day have to cease to exist. Socinus did not yet express this so clearly, but his followers taught without circumlocution that the second death consisted in annihilation; and according to Crell, Schmalz, and others, this occurred not at or soon after death but only after the universal resurrection and the world judgment: Fock, *Der Socin.*, 714f. From the Socinians this doctrine was adopted by Locke, Warburton, Whiston, Dodwell, Walter, and others, and in this century by Rothe, *Theol. Ethik* §§ 470–472, and Weisse, “Ueber die philos. Bedeutung der christl. Eschat.,” *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1836. It began especially to gain currency and find adherents, however, after it was defended in 1875 by Edward White in his *Life in Christ: a study of the Scripture doctrine on the nature of man, the object of the divine incarnation and the conditions of human immortality*, 3rd ed., London: Stock, 1878. This book set many pens in motion and elicited not only serious opposition but also many expressions of agreement. Conditionalism now finds numerous defenders everywhere, such as Schultz, *Voraussetzungen der christl. Lehre von der Unsterblichkeit*, 1861; H. Plitt, *Evang. Glaubenslehre*, 1863, II 413; Weisse, *Philos. Dogm.* § 970; Lemme, *Endlosigkeit der Verdammnis und allgemeine Wiederbringung*, Lichterfelde-Berlin: Runge (lecture delivered 12 Aug. 1898); Charles Byse, *L’immortalité conditionnelle ou la vie en Christ*, Paris, 1880; Petavel-Olliff, *Le problème de l’immortalité*, Paris, 1891; Dr. Jonker, *De leer der condit. onsterfelijkheid*, *Theol. Stud.* I.
