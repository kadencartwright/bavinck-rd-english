## 2.

In this resurrection the identity of the resurrection body with the body that died is preserved. In the raisings that take place in the Old and New Testaments, the body that died is animated with new life. Jesus rises with the same body in which he suffered on the cross and that was laid in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea. When Jesus died, many bodies of the saints were raised and came out of their graves, Matt. 27:52. In the resurrection on the last day, all who are in the graves will hear Jesus’ voice and come out, John 5:28, 29; from the graves, from the sea, from death and Hades the dead return to the earth, Rev. 20:13. And Paul teaches that the resurrection body comes forth from the body that died, just as God raises another from the grain that was sown, 1 Cor. 15:36f. This identity of the resurrection body with the body laid aside at death is of great significance in the Christian religion. For in the first place it thereby stands directly opposed to every dualistic doctrine according to which the body is only an accidental dwelling place or even a prison of the soul. The essence of the human being consists precisely in the most intimate union of soul and body into one personality. The soul belongs by nature to the body and the body to the soul; indeed, although each soul does not itself create the body, it nevertheless has its own body. In the identity of the body, just as much as in that of the soul, the continuity of the individual human being is maintained. And in the second place, redemption through Christ is not a second, new creation but a re-creation. It would have been much simpler if God had destroyed the whole fallen world and replaced it with an entirely new one. But it was his good pleasure to restore the fallen world and to free from sin the same humanity that had sinned. This liberation consists in Christ’s delivering his church from all sin and from all the consequences of sin, and thus also causing it to triumph completely over death. That is the last enemy that must be abolished. And in this the power of Christ comes to expression: that he not only gives his own people eternal life but consequently also raises them on the last day. Regeneration from water and Spirit is completed in the regeneration of all things, Matt. 19:28. Spiritual deliverance from sin is first consummated in bodily deliverance at the end of the days. Christ is a complete Savior. Just as he first appeared to establish the kingdom of heaven in the hearts of believers, so one day he returns to give it a visible form and to bring his absolute power over sin and death incontrovertibly before the eyes of all creatures, to manifestation and acknowledgment. *Leiblichkeit ist das Ende der Wege Gottes.* Care for the dead is directly connected with this. Cremation is not to be rejected because it would set bounds to God’s omnipotence and make the resurrection impossible. But it is nevertheless pagan in origin, was never customary among Israel and the Christian peoples, and conflicts with Christian morals. Burial, by contrast, accords much more with Scripture and confession, history and liturgy; with the doctrine of the image of God, which also comes to expression in the body, and of death as a punishment for sin; with the reverence owed the dead and the resurrection on the last day. The Christian does not preserve corpses artificially, like the Egyptians; neither does he destroy them mechanically, as many now desire, but entrusts them to the bosom of the earth and lets them rest until the day of resurrection; Kuyper, *Ons Program*, 802f.; Sartorius, *Die Leichenverbrennung innerhalb der christl. Kirche*, Basel, 1886.

The Christian church and theology therefore also maintained in the strictest manner the identity of the resurrection body with the body that died. Indeed, it frequently went to the opposite extreme and confessed not only a resurrection of the *flesh* but sometimes taught that the *totalitas materiae* that had belonged to a body would at the resurrection be gathered by God from every corner of the earth and restored to the different parts of the body in the same manner and measure as before; cf. Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* V, 12, 13; Augustine, *Enchir.*, 26; *de civ.* XX, 4, 13 sq.; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 75–86, and so forth. But this conception encounters insurmountable objections. For 1º it leads to all kinds of subtle and curious investigations that have no value for the doctrine of the resurrection. The question then becomes whether hair and nails, blood and bile, *semen* and *urina*, *intestina* and *genitalia* will rise and be formed from the same atoms, numerically and specifically identical, of which they consisted here in the bodies. This conception caused no small perplexity concerning people with disabilities who lacked one or more limbs and concerning children who died young and sometimes even before birth; whether one wished it or not, in all these and similar cases one had to resort to the supposition that the resurrection bodies were supplemented by components that did not formerly belong to them. The resurrection cannot consist in the return and animation of the *totalitas materiae*. 2º Physiology teaches that the human body, like all organisms, is subject to a continual exchange of matter, so that after seven years not a single particle of matter remains of those that previously formed the substance of the body. The substances of which our bodies consist, such as oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, and so forth, are specifically the same as those occurring in other creatures around us, but they change without ceasing; and this change conclusively proves that the identity of bodies cannot consist in their always being composed of numerically the same substances. It is enough that they consist of specifically the same substances. 3º This is reinforced by the many metamorphoses that nature displays in all its kingdoms. Through the action of air, water, and so forth, plants pass into peat and coal, carbon into diamond, clay into claystone, and rock into fertile soil. In the plant and animal kingdoms there is endless variety within the boundaries of the species. And every organism undergoes a series of changes during the time of its existence: the maggot becomes a fly; every larva passes from an undeveloped into a more developed state; the embryo passes through different phases and then comes to an extrauterine existence; the caterpillar becomes a pupa and afterward a butterfly; and so forth. What remains the same under all these transformations we do not know. Matter and form change; there appears to be nothing stable in the whole organism. And yet identity is maintained, which is therefore independent of the coarse mass of matter, its change, and its quantity.
