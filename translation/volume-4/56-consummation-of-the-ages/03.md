## 3.

If we connect these data with what Scripture teaches us about the resurrection, we see the way opened to maintain both the substantial unity and the qualitative difference between the present and the future body. For, strictly speaking, Scripture teaches not a resurrection of the flesh but of the body. From the raisings it reports to us and from the resurrection of Christ, we may indeed infer the essence, but not the form and manner, of the resurrection of the dead at the end of the days. For in all those raisings the body still existed in its entirety, and Christ’s body was not even given over to corruption, Acts 2:31. But the bodies of those who rise at the parousia have been dissolved into their components, scattered in all kinds of ways, and passed into other creatures. There can no longer be talk of flesh in the proper sense, for flesh is always animated; what ceases to be animated and alive thereby also ceases to be flesh and returns to dust, Gen. 3:19. Job may indeed say, even assuming this translation to be correct, that from his flesh he will behold God, 19:26, and after his resurrection Jesus can testify that a spirit does not have flesh and bones as he had, Luke 24:39. But this nevertheless supplies no conclusive proof for the resurrection of the flesh in the strict sense of the word. For the flesh of which Job’s body consisted was indeed the substrate for the resurrection body but did not on that account form its substance. And Jesus rose with the same body in which he had died and which had not even seen corruption; moreover, until his ascension he was in a transitional state, so that he could still partake of food. Yet Paul teaches very clearly that flesh and blood, since they are subject to corruption, cannot inherit incorruption in the kingdom of heaven. Holsten, Holtzmann, and others have inferred from this, entirely wrongly, that according to Paul the body that died does not rise at all and that the actual resurrection already takes place at death. For the apostle expressly confesses his faith in the bodily resurrection and defends it against those in the church at Corinth who denied it both in Jesus and in believers. And he certainly also holds that the same body laid in the grave is raised in the resurrection; cf. Bornhäuser, *Das Recht des Bekenntnisses zur Auferstehung des Fleisches*, Gütersloh, 1899. But at the same time he argues that the resurrection is not a restoration but a reformation. The body rises, but not as flesh and blood, weak, corruptible, mortal, but as a body clothed with incorruption and glory. The body consisting of flesh and blood is indeed the seed from which the resurrection body comes forth, 1 Cor. 15:35–38. Nevertheless, there is a great difference between the two. Already on earth there is much difference in flesh among organic beings and in body among inorganic creatures, vv. 39–41. And likewise there is an important difference between the present and the future body, as the contrast between Adam and Christ also proves, vv. 42–49. The first is a σωμα ψυχικον, consisting of flesh and blood animated by the ψυχη and subject to change; but the latter is a σωμα πνευματικον. It is indeed a true σωμα, yet it is governed no longer by the ψυχη but by the πνευμα; it no longer consists of flesh and blood; it is raised above sexual life, Matt. 22:30, and above the need for food and drink, 1 Cor. 6:13, and in this respect is even distinct from the body the human being possessed before the fall. It is immortal, incorruptible, spiritualized, glorified, 1 Cor. 15:42f.; Phil. 3:21. According to Paul too, therefore, the identity of the resurrection body with the body entrusted to the bosom of the earth is independent of the mass of matter and its continual change. All organisms, and thus also human bodies, always consist of substances that are the same specifically but not numerically. And so too it is by no means necessary for the resurrection body that it consist of precisely the numerically same atoms of which it consisted when it was laid in the grave. But identity does require that in the resurrection body the same organization and form, the same schema and type, be preserved that here stamped the body as the very own body of a particular person. Under the transformations to which all creatures are subject, their identity and continuity are preserved. The human body may decay after death and in its mass of matter be converted into all kinds of other organisms, but something of it remains on earth that constitutes the substrate of the resurrection body. What that is, we do not know and can never discover. But its strangeness disappears as soon as we consider that the very ultimate components of things are completely unknown to us. Every smallest atom is again capable of analysis; chemical analysis continues endlessly but never reaches absolutely simple being. And yet in all organisms, and thus also in the human body, there must be something that preserves its identity throughout the continually proceeding transformation. What absurdity is there, then, in assuming that such an “organic basic form,” such a “schema of individuality,” remains even after the death of the human body to serve as seed for the resurrection body? For according to Scripture this stands firm: the resurrection body is not brought from heaven by the blessed or formed out of spiritual, heavenly elements. The resurrection body comes not from heaven but from the earth; it is not a self-formed product of *pneuma* or *psyche* but arises from the body that was laid in the grave at death. And therefore it is not spiritual in the sense that it would have *pneuma* as its substance, but it is and remains material, even though that matter is organized no longer into corruptible flesh and blood but into a glorified body.
