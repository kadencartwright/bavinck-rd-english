## 6.

If human feeling were to decide the doctrine of eternal punishment, it would certainly be difficult to maintain and would now find only a few defenders. It should be gratefully acknowledged that since the previous century the ideal of humaneness and the feeling of sympathy have powerfully awakened and have put an end to the cruelty that formerly prevailed, especially in the field of criminal law. But no one can be blind to the fact that this humanitarian view also brings with it its own one-sidedness and dangers. The mighty reversal that has taken place can be described in this one sentence: whereas formerly even the insane were treated as criminals, now criminals are regarded as insane. Previously guilt was seen in every abnormality; now all concepts of guilt, crime, responsibility, accountability, and so forth are robbed of their reality; cf. Simons, *Nieuwe richtingen in de strafrechtwetenschap*, *Gids*, April 1900, pp. 48–84. The awareness of right and justice, of transgression and guilt, is weakened in an alarming way as the standard for all these things is relocated from God to the human being and society. With this all certainty and security are gradually lost. For if the interest of society is decisive, then not only is every boundary between good and evil erased, but justice also runs the risk of being sacrificed to power. “It is expedient for you that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation should not perish,” John 11:50, then becomes the language of the highest administration of justice. And the same human feeling that first pleaded for the humane treatment of the criminal does not shrink from subsequently demanding the martyrdom of the innocent; the *hosanna* gives way to “crucify him”; the *vox populi*, which is often wrongly honored as a *vox Dei*, recoils from no atrocity; and while the righteous person still considers how his livestock fares, even the inward parts, the heart, the disposition of the wicked are cruel, Prov. 12:10. There is therefore little foundation to be laid upon human feeling; it may not and cannot be decisive in determining right and law; even if appearances suggest otherwise, it is nevertheless infinitely better to fall into the hand of the Lord than into the hands of human beings, 1 Chron. 21:13. And this also applies to the doctrine of eternal punishments in hell. For 1º it deserves notice that this doctrine, however often it has been worked out far too realistically in church and theology, is nevertheless grounded in Scripture. And no one in Scripture speaks of it more frequently and extensively than our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom no one can deny depth of human feeling and compassion and who was the meekest and humblest among all the children of humanity. It is the highest love that threatens the severest punishments. Over against the blessedness of the eternal life he acquired for his own stands the misery of the eternal destruction he announces to the wicked. Both were enveloped in shadows and presented under images in the Old Testament. But in the New Testament it is Christ who opens the vista both into the depths of the outer darkness and into the dwellings of eternal light. 2º That the punishment in this place of outer darkness is eternal cannot be disputed with Scripture in hand. It is true that αἰωνιος (from αἰων, Hebrew עולם, that is, duration of time, duration of life, course of life, human lifetime, an indefinitely long time in the past or future; the present world-age, αἰων οὑτος; the coming age, αἰων μελλων) very frequently denotes a duration that indeed exceeds human calculation but is by no means endless or eternal. It is also often still used in the New Testament for the entire world-age that had passed before the appearance of Christ, in which the counsel of God was proclaimed through the prophets but was nevertheless not yet fully revealed, Luke 1:70; Acts 3:21; Rom. 16:25; Col. 1:26; 2 Tim. 1:9; Titus 1:2. But in the New Testament the word αἰωνιος serves especially to designate the imperishable nature, elevated above all corruption and destruction, of the benefits of salvation acquired by Christ, and it is then very frequently joined in particular with ζωη. The eternal life Christ gives to everyone who believes already has its beginning here on earth but is nevertheless first revealed perfectly in the future; it belongs essentially to the αἰων μελλων, Luke 18:30; is imperishable, John 11:25, 26; and is called eternal, just as are the οἰκοδομη ἐκ θεου, 2 Cor. 5:1; the σωτηρια, Heb. 5:9; the λυτρωσις, 9:12; the κληρονομια, 9:15; the δοξα, 2 Tim. 2:10; and the βασιλεια, 2 Pet. 1:11; just as God, Christ, and the Holy Spirit are also called eternal, Rom. 16:26; Heb. 9:14; 13:8; and so forth. By contrast, it is said that the punishment of the wicked will consist in το πυρ το αἰωνιον, Matt. 18:8; 25:41; Jude 7; κολασις αἰωνιος, Matt. 25:46; ὀλεθρος αἰωνιος, 2 Thess. 1:9; and κρισις αἰωνιος, Mark 3:29. Like eternal life, eternal punishment is represented by this description as belonging to the αἰων μελλων, in which no change of state is any longer possible. Nowhere does Scripture indicate by any word, or even leave open the possibility, that the state which then begins can still come to an end. And it states positively that the fire there is unquenchable, Matt. 3:12; that the worm does not die, Mark 9:44; that the smoke of torment ascends forever and ever, Rev. 14:11; and that it continues day and night forever and ever, 20:10; and that eternal punishment stands over against the eternal life of the righteous, Matt. 25:46. Unprejudiced exegesis can find nothing here other than an eternal, never-ending punishment. Cf. Cremer, s.v. 3º The state of the lost is described as ἀπωλεια, Matt. 7:13; φθορα, Gal. 6:8; ὀλεθρος, 2 Thess. 1:9; θανατος, Rev. 2:11; and so forth, in agreement with the frequent statement in the Old and New Testaments that the wicked will be eradicated, rooted out, devastated, corrupted, destroyed, cast out, cut off, burned like chaff, and so forth. The advocates of conditional immortality understand all these expressions in the sense of complete annihilation; cf. White, *Life in Christ*, 358–390. But this interpretation lacks all foundation. In Scripture life never means mere existence, and death is never the same as annihilation. The conditionalists cannot deny this even of temporal, bodily death; like the Socinians, they generally assume that the wicked continue to exist even after death, whether in order first to be annihilated by God after resurrection and final judgment or gradually to die away and finally perish physically as well. The latter is an impossible thought both philosophically and scripturally. For sin is not a substance, not *materia* but *forma*, which presupposes existence and does not annihilate what exists but directs it in a wrong direction, turned away from God, vol. III, 81f. And bodily death is not merely a natural consequence but a positive punishment for sin, threatened and executed by God, ibid., 176f. In that death God does not annihilate the human being but temporarily separates soul and body in order to preserve both and reunite them at the resurrection. Scripture clearly and incontrovertibly teaches the immortality of the human being. Conditionalism confuses ethical with physical being when it sees in ἀπωλεια, which is the punishment of sin, an annihilation of the substance of the human being. And just as God does not annihilate the human being in the first death, neither does he do so in the second death. For this is also described in Scripture as torment, Matt. 25:46; weeping and gnashing of teeth, Matt. 8:12; tribulation and anguish, Rom. 2:9; unquenchable fire, Matt. 18:8; the worm that never dies, Mark 9:44; and so forth, all of which expressions presuppose the existence of the lost. Yet their state can be called ἀπωλεια, φθορα, ὀλεθρος, θανατος, because in a moral, spiritual sense they have completely perished and in an absolute sense lack that fullness of life which is bestowed upon believers by Christ. Thus the lost son is called νεκρος and ἀπολωλος, Luke 15:24, 32; the Ephesians in their former state are called νεκροι in their sins and trespasses, Eph. 2:1, alienated from the life of God, Eph. 4:18; those of Sardis are called νεκροι, Rev. 3:1; and so forth, without anyone thinking here of their nonexistence. 4º The advocates of the apocatastasis are guilty of the same failure to recognize the ethical character of sin. The word is derived from Acts 3:21 but, as everyone now acknowledges, by no means contains there what is now meant by it. Scripture nowhere teaches that one day all human beings and even all devils will be saved. It does indeed often speak in very universalistic terms, because the work of Christ is intensively of infinite value and benefits the entire world and humanity in its organic existence, vol. III, 390f. But it decidedly excludes the possibility that all individual human beings, or even the devils, would one day become citizens in the kingdom of God. At all times, therefore, the doctrine of the restoration of all things has been taught only by a few isolated individuals, and even today conditionalism finds more support among theologians than apocatastasis. In fact this doctrine is not of Christian but of pagan origin, and it bears not a scriptural but a philosophical character. Pantheism lies at its foundation and causes all things, just as they proceed from God, successively to return to him. Here God is not a Lawgiver and Judge who will one day judge the world in righteousness but an unconscious, immanent force that propels everything toward the end and one day reunites everything with himself. Here sin is not ἀνομια but a necessary moment in the development of the world. And redemption in Christ is not a juridical restoration and an ethical renewal but a physical process that governs everything. 5º To vindicate eternal punishment, it is therefore necessary above all to acknowledge with Scripture the inviolable righteousness of God and the deeply sinful character of sin. Sin is no weakness, no defect, no temporary and gradually disappearing imperfection, but in its origin and essence it is ἀνομια, transgression of the law, rebellion and enmity against God, negation of his right, his authority, and even his existence. Sin is indeed finite in the sense that it is committed by a finite creature in a finite time, but Augustine already rightly observed that the standard of its punishment is not the duration in which the sin is committed but its inward nature. An hour of thoughtlessness can cause one to weep for years. Sins of a single moment are followed by an entire life of disgrace and punishment. Whoever commits a crime is sometimes punished with death and brought by the earthly government into an irreversible state. God does so too; for what the death penalty is on earth, the punishment of hell is at the final judgment. He judges and punishes sin according to its inward quality. And then sin is infinite in the sense that it is committed against the supreme Majesty, who has an absolute right to our love and our reverence. God is worthy of our obedience and devotion in an absolute, infinite manner; the law in which he demands these is therefore absolutely binding, its binding force infinitely great; and the transgression of that law is thus, considered intensively, an absolute, an infinite evil. Moreover, it is not so much the *diuturnitas peccandi* that comes into consideration here as the *voluntas peccantis, quae hujusmodi est ut semper vellet peccare si posset*, Augustine, *de civ.* 21, 11. Whoever commits sin is a slave of sin and wills and can do nothing other than sin. It is certainly no credit to that person if he is put beyond the opportunity to continue on the sinful path; according to his inward desire he would wish nothing other than to go on living eternally in order to be able to sin eternally. Who then, attending to this sinful nature of sin, would dare to say that God is unjust if he visits it not only with temporal but also with eternal punishments? 6º Ordinarily this argument, derived from the righteousness of God, is therefore advanced only timidly and hesitantly. All the more is eternal punishment regarded as conflicting with the goodness and love of God. If, however, it does not conflict with the righteousness of God, then neither does it, nor can it, conflict with his goodness. There is no choice here. If eternal punishment is unjust, it is thereby judged, and the goodness of God no longer needs to be brought into the matter. But if it accords with God’s righteousness, then the goodness of God remains unimpaired in it; what is righteous is also good. Thus the argument against eternal punishment derived from God’s goodness secretly introduces, in the footsteps of Marcion, a conflict between God’s righteousness and his goodness and sacrifices the former to the latter. A goodness, however, that nullifies righteousness is no longer true, essential goodness. It is nothing other than human weakness and softness and, when transferred to God, a fabrication of the human brain, corresponding in no way to the living, true God who has revealed himself in Scripture and also in nature. For if eternal punishment is incompatible with God’s goodness, temporal punishment is as well. But the latter is a fact that no one can deny. Humanity perishes under God’s wrath and is terrified by his fury. Who can reconcile the suffering of the world with God’s goodness and love? Yet it must be reconcilable with them, for it exists. If the existence of the dreadful suffering in this world may not cause us to doubt God’s goodness, then eternal punishment may not lead us to deny it either. If this world is compatible with God’s love, as it is and must be, then hell is too. For outside Holy Scripture there is no stronger proof for the existence of hell than the existence of this world, from whose misery the features of the image of hell have been drawn. 7º Moreover, the human being who opposes eternal punishment faces the great danger of playing the hypocrite toward God. He presents himself as the loving one who far surpasses the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ in goodness and compassion. That does not alter the fact that this same human being, as soon as *his* honor is assailed, ignites in rage and wishes every evil upon his offender in this life and in the life to come. Envy, hatred, anger, and vindictiveness arise in the heart of every human being against everyone who stands in his way. We seek our own honor but do not concern ourselves with the honor of God; we stand up for our own right but allow God’s right to be trampled underfoot. That is surely conclusive proof that the human being is not a competent judge of God’s words and acts. And yet there is something good even in that defense of one’s own right and honor. However wrongly a human being may apply it, it nevertheless contains the truth that right and honor take precedence over property and life. Even in the sinner a deep sense of right and honor still slumbers. And when that is assailed, it awakens and suppresses all compassion. When a conflict between two persons or between two nations concerns right, each prays with all his heart that God will cause right to triumph and strike its violators with his judgment. All human beings still perceive something of *fiat justitia, pereat mundus* and approve of right triumphing at the cost of thousands of human lives. The day of judgment also concerns right, and not some private right but *the* right above all, right in its entire significance and full scope, the righteousness of God, that God himself as God may be praised forever. 8º There is therefore no doubt that on the day of judgment God, also when he pronounces eternal punishment upon sinners, will fully justify himself before the eyes of all creatures. At present we know in part, and we know the dreadfulness of sin only in part as well. But if already here, when we hear of some atrocities, we consider even the severest punishment not severe enough, what will it be when at the end of the days we receive insight into the depths of iniquity? And in addition we are always one-sided here on earth; the sense of justice and compassion repeatedly come into conflict; in our judgment we are either too lenient or far too severe. But it is not and cannot be so with the Lord our God. In Christ he has revealed his full love, and that love is so great because it has granted a deliverance from the coming wrath and from eternal destruction. The opponents of eternal punishment do not only detract from the condemnability of sin and the severity of divine justice; they also diminish the greatness of God’s love and of the redemption that is in Christ. If the salvation at stake had not been from eternal destruction, the price paid in the blood of God’s own Son would have been far too high. The heaven he acquired for us through his atoning death presupposes a hell from which he delivered us. The eternal life he gave us presupposes an eternal death from which he redeemed us. The favor and good pleasure of God in which he causes us to share eternally presuppose a wrath under which we would otherwise have had to sink eternally. And therefore this Christ will also one day conduct the judgment and pronounce the verdict. A human being, a true, perfect human being who knows what is in a human being, who is the meekest of all human beings, will be the judge of human beings, so righteous that all will acknowledge it, and every knee will bow before him and every tongue confess that Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father. At the end God will be acknowledged as God by all creatures, if not willingly, then unwillingly. 9º This must be enough for us. Investigations into the location and size of hell, the nature of fire and worm, and the psychic and physical condition of the lost lead to no result, because Scripture maintains silence about them. We know only this in addition: that the punishment of hell begins only after the day of judgment; that it is always threatened against those who stubbornly oppose the truth of God, the cowardly and unbelieving and abominable and murderers and sexually immoral and sorcerers and idolaters and liars, Rev. 21:8; and that even then it differs according to the measure of each person’s iniquity. Nowhere does Scripture teach that there is then still a place for conversion and forgiveness. The addition in Matt. 12:32, “neither in this age nor in the coming one,” serves not to establish the forgivability of sin against the Son of Man even in the coming age but to bring to light the absolute unforgivability of sin against the Holy Spirit. In its essence punishment is the maintenance of righteousness and, specifically after the judgment, serves not to purify but to repay each person according to his work. Yet Scripture teaches very clearly that there are degrees in that punishment; the *poena damni* is equal, but the *poena sensus* differs; each person receives according to his works, Matt. 10:15; 11:24; 23:14; 24:51; Luke 10:12, 14; 12:46, 47; 2 Cor. 5:10; and so forth. And in this something of God’s mercy is still expressed, vol. II, 362, 365. All sin stands absolutely opposed to righteousness, but in punishment God nevertheless takes account of the relative difference that exists among sins. Therefore, even if one does not concede with Augustine, *Enchir.* 110; cf. Lombard, Thomas, and Bonaventure on *Sent.* IV 46, *poenas damnatorum certis temporum intervallis aliquatenus mitigari*, nevertheless God’s righteousness manifests itself in eternal punishment in such a way that his goodness and love remain unimpaired and can never rightly be accused. In hell too the saying applies that he does not afflict human beings from his heart, Lam. 3:33; the sorrow he sends is not an object of his delight or that of the blessed but a means to the glorification of his attributes and is thus determined in its severity and measure by this ultimate end.
