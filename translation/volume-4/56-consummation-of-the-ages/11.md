## 11.

Communion with God, enjoyed in the communion of saints, certainly no more excludes all action and all activity in the coming age than in this dispensation. As a rule Christian theology has indeed devoted little attention to this and has spoken chiefly of heavenly blessedness as a knowing and enjoying of God. And without doubt this is also the core and center, the source and power of eternal life. Scripture too offers little data from which to form a clear conception of the activity of the blessed. It describes blessedness more as a resting from earthly labor than as the performance of a new activity, Heb. 4:9; Rev. 14:13. But the rest enjoyed in the new Jerusalem is no more to be conceived as a blessed doing-nothing in the case of God, John 5:17, than in the case of his children. Holy Scripture itself says that eternal life consists in knowing and serving, in praising and glorifying God, John 17:3; Rev. 4:11; 5:8, and so forth. His children also remain his servants, who serve him day and night, Rev. 22:3. They are prophets, priests, and kings who reign on the earth forever, Rev. 1:6; 5:10; 22:5. In proportion as they have been faithful over little on earth, they are placed over much in the kingdom of God, Matt. 24:47; 25:21, 23. Each retains his own personality, for the names of all who enter the new Jerusalem are written in the Lamb’s book of life, Rev. 20:15; 21:27, and each receives his own new name, Isa. 62:2; 65:15; Rev. 2:17; 3:12; cf. 21:12, 14. The dead who die in the Lord rest from their labors, but each is followed by his own works, Rev. 14:13. Families, peoples, and nations contribute their own part to the enrichment of life in the new Jerusalem, Rev. 5:9; 7:9; 21:24, 26. What is sown here is reaped in eternity, Matt. 25:24, 26; 1 Cor. 15:42f.; 2 Cor. 9:6; Gal. 6:7, 8. The great diversity that exists among human beings in all kinds of respects is not destroyed in eternity but cleansed of all that is sinful and made serviceable to communion with God and with one another. And just as the natural diversity in the church on earth is further increased by spiritual diversity, 1 Cor. 12:7f., so this natural and spiritual difference in heaven increases still further because there are different degrees of glory. From opposition to the meritoriousness of good works, some Reformed, such as Martyr, *Loci C.* III, 17, 8; Camero, Schoenfeld, Tilenus, Spanheim—as already Jovinian in the fourth century and later some Socinians and now still Gerlach—denied every distinction in glory hereafter. And it is also true that the same benefits at Christ’s coming are promised to all believers; they all receive the same eternal life, the same dwelling place in the new Jerusalem, the same communion with God, the same blessedness, and so forth. But nevertheless Scripture places it beyond all doubt that within that unity and equality there is very great variation and diversity. Even the parable, Matt. 20:1–16, to which people repeatedly appeal for the contrary, supports such a distinction. For with that parable Jesus intends to teach that many who, in their own and others’ estimation, have labored long and hard will by no means stand behind in the future messianic kingdom those who worked in the vineyard for a much shorter time. The last catch up with the first, for many are indeed called and labor in the service of the kingdom of God, but few enjoy a special distinction for this hereafter and receive a select place. Such a gradual difference in glory is taught much more clearly in other passages of Scripture, especially where mention is made of a reward that will be granted to everyone according to his works. That reward is now kept in heaven, Matt. 5:12; 6:1f.; Luke 6:23; 1 Tim. 6:19; Heb. 10:34–37, and is first distributed publicly at the parousia, Matt. 6:4, 6, 18; 24:47; 2 Thess. 1:7; 1 Pet. 4:13. It is then granted as compensation for what Jesus’ disciples denied themselves and suffered here on earth for his sake, Matt. 5:10f.; 19:29; Luke 6:21f.; Rom. 8:17, 18; 2 Cor. 4:17; 2 Thess. 1:7; Heb. 10:34; 1 Pet. 4:13, and further also as recompense for the good works they performed, such as for the good use of the talents, Matt. 25:15f.; Luke 19:13f.; for love of enemies and disinterested generosity, Luke 6:35; for care of the poor, Matt. 6:1; for prayer and fasting, Matt. 6:6, 18; for serving the brothers, Matt. 10:40–42; for faithful service in the kingdom of God, Matt. 24:44–47; 1 Cor. 3:8, and so forth. That reward will be connected with and proportionate to the works, Matt. 16:27; 19:29; 25:21, 23; Luke 6:38; 19:17, 19; Rom. 2:6; 1 Cor. 3:8; 2 Cor. 4:17; 5:10; 9:6; Gal. 6:8, 9; Heb. 11:26; Rev. 2:23; 11:18; 20:12; 22:12. Blessedness is indeed the same for all, but there is difference in radiance and glory, Dan. 12:3; 1 Cor. 15:41. In the Father’s house, which receives all the children, there are many dwellings, John 14:2; and according to the measure of their faithfulness and devotion, the churches all receive from the King of the church their own ornament and crown, Rev. 1–3. Roman Catholics built upon these statements of Scripture the doctrine of the meritoriousness of good works, *Trid.* VI, can. 31, 32, and the right to special rewards in heaven, which according to Ex. 25:25 are called *aureolae* and are added to the *corona aurea* falling to the share of all, and are granted especially to martyrs, celibates, and teachers; Thomas, *S. Theol.* III, qu. 96; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VII, 7. But this abuse does not remove the truth that there is a distinction in glory according to the works believers performed here on earth. There is no reward to which the human being could lay claim by nature, for God’s law is absolutely binding and does not make the demand for fulfillment depend upon the human being’s free choice. If he has therefore fulfilled the whole law, it nevertheless befits him to say nothing other than that he is an unprofitable servant who has only done what he was obligated to do, Luke 17:10. Every claim to reward can therefore arise only from a covenant, from a sovereign and gracious disposition of God, and is therefore a conferred right, vol. II, 553. So it was in the covenant of works, and so it is still much more in the covenant of grace, vol. III, 563. For Christ accomplished everything: he not only suffered the punishment but also acquired eternal life by fulfilling the law. The eternal blessedness and glory he received was for him the reward for his perfect obedience. But when through faith he grants this righteousness of his to his own and joins eternal life to it, then both—that granted righteousness and future blessedness—are gifts of his grace, which altogether excludes every merit on the side of believers. For believers are God’s workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works that God prepared so that they would walk in them, Eph. 2:10. It is graciously given to them in the cause of Christ not only to believe in him but also to suffer for him, Phil. 1:29; Acts 5:41. Not only in the gift of eternal life to everyone who believes, but also in the distribution of a different measure of glory to those who have brought forth good works from that faith, God crowns his own work. But he also does this so that, as here, so hereafter there would be a rich diversity in the church and in that diversity the glory of his virtues would come to expression. Through that diversity, the life of communion with God, with the angels, and among the blessed mutually increases in depth and intimacy. In that communion each, as in the church here on earth, Rom. 12:4–8; 1 Cor. 12, has his own place and task in connection with his person and character. Although we may not be able to form a clear conception of the activity of the blessed, Scripture nevertheless teaches that the prophetic, priestly, and royal office the human being originally possessed is fully restored in them by Christ. The service of God, mutual communion, and the inhabitation of the new heaven and new earth doubtless offer abundant opportunity for the exercise of these offices, even though its form and manner are unknown to us. But that working is at the same time a resting and enjoying. The distinction between day and night, between sabbath and workdays, has ceased. Time is permeated by God’s eternity; space is full of his presence; eternal becoming is wedded to immutable being. Even the opposition between heaven and earth has disappeared. For everything in heaven and on earth is gathered into one under Christ as Head, Eph. 1:10. All creatures are and live and move in God, who is all in all, who reflects all his virtues in the mirror of his works and therein glorifies himself.
