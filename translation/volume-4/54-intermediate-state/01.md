# Chapter XI. On the Last Things

# § 54. The Intermediate State

## 1.

Just as the origin and essence of things, so also their end is unknown to us. Science gives as unsatisfactory an answer to the question “Where to?” as to the question whence all things are. And yet religion has an urgent need to know something of the destiny of the individual human being, of humanity, and of the world. All peoples therefore have one thought or another about it, and all religions contain a kind of eschatology. There are admittedly still some who claim that belief in the immortality of the soul is originally by no means proper to all human beings and is still absent today, for example among the Veddas in Ceylon, the Indian Seelongs, and others: Büchner, *Kraft und Stoff*, 16th ed., 423; Haeckel, *Die Welträthsel*, Bonn, 1899, p. 223. From the standpoint of evolution, belief in God, in the independent existence of the soul, and in its immortality likewise cannot have constituted an original component of human nature, but must have gradually and contingently arisen and developed through all kinds of circumstances. Veneration of ancestors, love for deceased blood relatives, zest for life and the desire to prolong it, hope for better conditions of life on the other side of the grave, fear of punishment and hope of reward, and so forth, were then the causes that gradually gave rise to belief in immortality. Over against this, however, most and the best students of the history of religions testify that belief in immortality occurs among all peoples and is a component even of the lowest and crudest religions. We encounter it everywhere and at every stage of development where philosophical doubts have not undermined it or other causes pushed it into the background, and everywhere it is also connected with religion: Tiele, *Inl. tot de godsdienstwetenschap*, second series, Amsterdam, 1899, p. 97; Peschel, *Völkerkunde*⁵, 257. One can even say that originally this belief was something very natural. Like the author of the paradise narrative in Genesis, says Tiele, op. cit., p. 197, all peoples proceed from the conviction that the human being is immortal by nature and that it is not immortality that must be proved but death that must be explained. Death appears to be something contrary to nature. Something must have happened through which something so illogical entered the world. The sagas of all kinds of peoples, differing in origin and development, express that idea. There was a time when there was neither sickness nor death on earth. The “simple child of nature” cannot yet even believe in death when he sees it before his eyes. It is a sleep, he says, a state of unconsciousness; the spirit has left the body but can still return. Therefore he waits several days to see whether this may happen. And if the spirit of the dead person does not return, it is held that he has merely disappeared in order somewhere, wherever it may be, to enter another body or to associate with the spirits above the earth. The forms in which this life of the soul after death was conceived were very different and were frequently also combined and mingled with one another. At one time it was thought that after death souls continued to live near the grave and needed the continual care of their blood relatives for their existence, or that in the underworld, in Hades, far removed from gods and human beings, they led a sad, shadowy life. At another time it was believed that, just as the souls of the deceased had sometimes already undergone all kinds of metamorphoses before their dwelling in the human body, so after leaving it they still had to abide for a time in other bodies of animals and human beings in order to purify themselves, attain perfection, and pass into the Deity or an unconscious Nirvana. Or it was taught that immediately after death souls came into divine judgment and, if they had done good, crossed the dangerous bridge of the dead into the land of the blessed, where they lived in fellowship with the gods; or, if they had done evil, plunged into a place of eternal darkness and torment. Cf. the second part of C. W. Flügge, *Gesch. des Glaubens an Unsterblichkeit, Auferstehung, Gericht und Vergeltung*, Leipzig, 1795; Spiess, *Entwicklungsgesch. der Vorstellungen vom Zustande nach dem Tode auf Grund vergl. Religionsforschung*, 1877; Knabenhauer, *Das Zeugniss des Menschengeschlechts über die Unsterblichkeit der Seele*, Freiburg, 1878; Merschmann, *Die Idee der Unsterblichkeit in ihrer gesch. Entw.*, Berlin, 1870; Pfleiderer, *Religionsphilos.*, 1896, p. 625f.; Runze, article “Unsterblichkeit” in Herzog² 16, 189, especially 198f.; idem, *Die Psychologie des Unsterblichkeitsglaubens und der Unsterblichkeitsleugnung*, Berlin, 1894.

The doctrine of personal immortality passed from religion into philosophy. After Pythagoras, Heraclitus, and Empedocles had already led the way, Plato in particular sought to support his religious belief in immortality in the *Phaedo* with philosophical arguments. His proofs amount to this: that the soul, which draws its knowledge of the ideas from recollection, already existed before its dwelling in the body and will likewise continue to exist after leaving that body; that through its contemplative thought of the eternal ideas it is akin to the divine being and through its mastery of the body and its desires is elevated far above the body as something independent and simple; and especially that, as the principle of life and identical with life, it cannot be conceived as lifeless and perishable. With this doctrine concerning the immortality of the soul he then connects all kinds of conceptions concerning preexistence, fall, union with the body, judgment, and transmigration of souls, which for an important part bear a mythical character and certainly were not all intended by Plato himself in a scientific sense. Although other philosophers, such as Democritus, Epicurus, and Lucretius, opposed the immortality of the soul, or, like Aristotle, did not pronounce decisively upon it, Plato’s doctrine had an extraordinarily great influence upon theology and philosophy. The mythical components of preexistence, metempsychosis, and so forth frequently found acceptance among sectarian movements. And under Plato’s influence theology devoted much greater attention to the immortality of the soul than did Holy Scripture. The doctrine of the natural immortality of the soul became an *articulus mixtus*, whose truth was argued even more from reason than from revelation: Tertullian, *de an.* 22; Origen, *de princ.* VI 36; Irenaeus, *adv. haer.* II 34; etc.; among the Reformed, Heppe, *Dogm.*, 166. Nevertheless, a certain difference always remained. The awareness never died out entirely that Holy Scripture always attaches to life and death, besides a physical, also a religious-ethical significance. For Scripture, life is never mere continued existence, and death is never identical with annihilation, but life includes communion with God and death is the deprivation of his grace and favor. Hence the church fathers repeatedly say that Christ came to grant us ἀθανασια, and it can sometimes appear as though they denied the natural immortality of the soul. To this was added that Plato’s doctrine of preexistence, that is, of the soul’s uncreatedness, had to be opposed, and for that reason some were sometimes reluctant to call the soul immortal by nature, since God alone was immortal through himself and the soul could be immortal only through his will: Justin, *Dial.* 5; Theophilus, *ad Autol.* II 27. This must be kept in view in investigating whether there were advocates of conditional immortality among the church fathers. For although an individual such as Arnobius taught an annihilation of evil souls, and although Tatian assumed that at death the soul died with the body in order to rise again at the end of days, the belief was nevertheless general that the soul was immortal by virtue of the nature God had given it: Münscher-v. Coelln, *D. G.* I 333f.; Harnack, *D. G.* I 449; Dr. Jonker, *Theol. Studiën* I 167f.; Atzberger, *Gesch. d. christl. Eschat.*, 118f., 187f., 222f., 338f., 577f. In philosophy too, Plato’s doctrine of immortality retained an important place. Descartes understood spirit and matter, soul and body, as two separate substances, each of which had its own attribute, namely thought and extension, each could exist by itself, and therefore could be united only mechanically. Spinoza accepted these same two attributes but regarded them as modes of appearance of the one eternal, infinite substance, as two sides of the same thing that cannot fall apart but are always together as subject and object, as image and counterpart, as *idea* and *res*. For immortality there was no place in his system, and he had no need of it either, for *quamvis nesciremus, mentem nostram aeternam esse, pietatem et religionem et absolute omnia, quae ad animositatem et generositatem referri ostendimus in quarta parte, prima haberemus*, *Eth.* V 41. The philosophy of the eighteenth century, however, was not favorably disposed toward Spinoza; it bore a deistic character, contented itself with the trilogy of God, virtue, and immortality, and esteemed the last of these three most of all. Following Leibniz, Wolff, Mendelssohn, and others, its truth was argued with all kinds of metaphysical, theological, cosmic, moral, and historical proofs and urged with sentimental reflections concerning a blessed recognition and reunion on the other side of the grave; literature in Bretschneider, *Syst. Entw.*, 824. According to Strauss, the poet’s declaration in Ps. 73:25 was transformed into this other one: *wenn ich nur mein Ich in Sicherheit habe, so frage ich nichts nach Gott und Welt*. Kant, however, put an end to that certainty by demonstrating the inadequacy of all the proofs adduced for the immortality of the soul and considering it acceptable only as a postulate of practical reason. Over against the egoistic wishes of rationalism Schleiermacher set his saying, *wer nicht gelernt hat, mehr zu sein als er selbst, der verliert wenig, wenn er sich selbst verliert*, and knew no other and higher immortality than *mitten in der Endlichkeit eins zu werden mit dem Unendlichen und ewig zu sein in jedem Augenblick*, *Reden über die Religion* II; cf. *Chr. Gl.* § 158, 1. And the idealistic philosophy of Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel left no place at all for the immortality of the soul, although it hesitated to state its inner thought openly on this point. The book by Fr. Richter, *Die Lehre von den letzten Dingen*, 1833, however, brought the consequence of Hegel’s system to light and, despite the opposition of many, paved the way for materialism, which was already loudly proclaimed by Feuerbach and later supported by Vogt, Moleschott, Büchner, Haeckel, and others with so-called natural-scientific arguments. These arguments have made so much impression upon philosophy that it abandons the immortality of the soul altogether—Strauss, *Chr. Gl.* II 738; idem, *Der alte und der neue Glaube*², 123f.; Schopenhauer, *Die Welt u. s. w.* I 330; Hartmann, *Religionsphilos.* II 232—or at most argues for its possibility and speaks only of a hope of immortality—Hoekstra, *De hoop der onsterfelijkheid*, 1867; Rauwenhoff, *Wijsbeg. v. d. godsd.*, 811. Theologians too frequently attach little or no value at all to the proofs for the immortality of the soul: Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 295; Runze in Herzog² 16, 211f.; Frank, *Chr. Wahrheit* II 437f. But over against them there still always stand many men of reputation who consider all, some, or at least a single one of the proofs strong enough to build upon it a firm belief in the immortality of the soul: Weisse, *Philos. Dogm.* §§ 952–972; Fichte, *Die Idee der Persönl. u. d. indiv. Fortdauer*, 1834; idem, *Die Seelenfortdauer u. die Weltstellung des Menschen*, 1867; Göschel, *Von den Beweisen für die Unsterbl. der menschl. Seele*, 1835; article “Unsterblichkeit” in Herzog, 1st and 2nd eds.; Kahnis, *Dogm.* II 485f.; Dorner, *Gl.* II 916f.; Luthardt, *Komp. d. Dogm.* § 75; W. Schmidt, *Christl. Dogm.*, 492f.; Doedes, *Leer v. God*, 248; Van Oosterzee, *Dogm.* § 68; etc.
