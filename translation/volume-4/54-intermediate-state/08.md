## 8.

Whoever loses sight of this teaching of Scripture concerning immortality falls into all kinds of errors. After all, we can form no conception of a pure spirit, of its existence, life, and activity. Of God, who is pure spirit, we are able to speak only in an anthropomorphic manner, in which, moreover, Scripture itself leads the way, vol. II, 65f. The angels are spiritual beings, but they are represented in human form and when they appear frequently assume human bodies, ibid., 435f. And human beings are not only corporeal beings, but all their activities are bound to the body and dependent upon it: not only the vegetative and animal activities but also the intellectual activities of thinking and willing. Although the brain is not the cause of the higher cognitive and appetitive faculties, it is nevertheless their bearer and organ; every disturbance in the brain results in an abnormal operation of the *anima rationalis*. Since the body is not a prison of the soul but belongs to the essence of the human being, we can form no conception of the life and activity of a soul separated from the body and are therefore easily inclined toward surmises and conjectures. Accordingly, three principal hypotheses have been devised to make the existence of souls after death somewhat intelligible.

First, many among the pagans and also among Christians have thought that souls, after being separated from the body, could lead only a life of sleep. Indeed, the change that occurs at death is of extraordinary significance. After all, the entire content of the life of our soul is derived from the external world; all knowledge begins with sensory perception; our whole mode of thought is material; even of spiritual things we speak in words that originally had a sensory meaning. If death, as Scripture teaches, is a sudden, violent, complete, and absolute break with the *diesseitige* world, then there really seems to be no other possibility than that the soul becomes entirely closed to the external world, loses all its content, and as it were sinks back into itself. In sleep the soul also withdraws from the external world and breaks off intercourse with it; but in sleep it does this only in a relative sense, since it remains connected with the body and retains the rich life it has acquired from the world; even in dreams it remains active, though in a confused manner. And nevertheless, what a change sleep already brings about in human life! The cognitive and appetitive faculties cease their activity; consciousness stands still; all sensation and perception cease; only the vegetative life continues its regular work. How much more, then, will every activity of the soul cease when death enters and completely breaks all bonds with this world! Everything thus seems to support the view that after death souls exist in a sleeping, unconscious state. And Holy Scripture, to all superficial appearances, is so far from condemning this doctrine of soul sleep that it instead commends and favors it. For not only in the Old but also in the New Testament it repeatedly calls dying a sleep, Deut. 31:16; Jer. 51:39, 57; Dan. 12:2; Matt. 9:24; John 11:11; 1 Cor. 7:39; 11:30; 15:6, 18, 20, 51; 1 Thess. 4:13–15; 2 Pet. 3:4; etc. Sheol is a land of silence, rest, and forgetfulness, where the dead no longer forever have any share in all that happens under the sun; see above, p. 366. Jesus speaks of the night of death, in which no one can work, John 9:4; and nowhere does Scripture make any mention that those who returned from death to life, such as Lazarus and others, recounted anything of what they had seen or heard in the intermediate state. Yet all these arguments are not able to prove the doctrine of psychopannychy. For 1º it is clear that the soul’s dependence upon the body nevertheless does not exclude its independence. The external world may be the occasion for the awakening of our self-consciousness and the first source of our knowledge; thought may be bound to the brain and have its seat and organ in it; but it is unproved and unprovable that the psychical life of the human being finds its source and origin in physical phenomena. Thinking and knowing are activities of the soul. It is not the ear that hears and the eye that sees, but the psychical “I” of the human being that hears through the ear and sees through the eye; the body is an instrument of the spirit. There is therefore nothing absurd in thinking that, if necessary, the soul can continue its activities without the body. Indeed, whoever would deny conscious life to spirit as such would have to come to regard consciousness and will as impossible also in God and the angels. For although we speak of God in a human manner and frequently represent the angels to ourselves corporeally, they are nevertheless spirits in themselves and yet possess consciousness and will. 2º Scripture teaches as clearly as possible that death is a total break with this entire earthly life and in that respect is sleep, rest, and silence. The state of death is a sleep; the deceased sleeps because intercourse with the *diesseitige* world has ceased. But nowhere does Scripture say that the soul of the deceased sleeps. On the contrary, it always represents the soul after death as more or less conscious; and as revelation progresses, it comes increasingly clearly to light that, while in death all relations to this world are cut off, other relations to another world immediately take their place. The great thought of Scripture, that life is joined to the service of the Lord and death to rejection of him, casts its light also upon the other side of the grave. While the rich man is in torment immediately after his death, the poor man Lazarus is carried into Abraham’s bosom, Luke 16:23. And all believers, who indeed already partake of eternal life here on earth, do not lose it through death, John 11:25, 26, but after death enjoy it much more richly and blessedly in communion with Christ, Luke 23:43; Acts 7:59; 2 Cor. 5:8; Phil. 1:23; Rev. 6:9; 7:9. Dwelling in the body is precisely being away from the Lord, and dying is therefore the way to a closer and more intimate communion with Christ. 3º In addition, it need not surprise us that those who returned to life through resurrection mention nothing of what they saw and heard beyond the grave. For apart from the possibility that they did communicate one thing or another that was not recorded in Scripture, it is most probable that they were neither permitted nor able to tell us anything of their experiences beyond the grave. Moses and the prophets are sufficient for us, Luke 16:29; and after Paul had been caught up into the third heaven, he could say nothing other than that he had heard inexpressible words which a human being is not permitted to speak.
