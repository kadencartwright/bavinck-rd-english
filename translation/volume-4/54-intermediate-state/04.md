## 4.

This doctrine of the Old Testament was not entirely lost in later Jewish literature, but it was nevertheless modified and expanded by all kinds of foreign elements. In general the writings of this category agree in understanding religion more individualistically, under the influence of the idea of recompense introducing a provisional separation between righteous and wicked immediately at death, and giving a more elaborate description of the different places where they reside. Yet they can clearly be divided into two groups, a Palestinian and an Alexandrian. The first-mentioned, to which especially the apocryphal writings of the Maccabees, Baruch, 4 Ezra, Enoch, the Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs, and so forth belong, ascribe only a provisional character to the intermediate state. They too already take up foreign components and teach a certain separation between righteous and wicked immediately at death. The Apocalypse of Enoch places Sheol in the West, describes it as intersected and surrounded by rivers, and distinguishes in it four divisions, two for the good and two for the evil, 17:5, 6; 22:2f. In addition it further assumes a paradise located high above and at the ends of the earth, which became the dwelling place of Enoch and Elijah immediately at their death, 12:1; 87:3; 89:52, and which will also become so for all who walk in their ways, 71:16, 17. But for all the writers of this group the center of gravity nevertheless lies in universal eschatology, in the coming of the Messiah and the establishment of the kingdom of God at the end of the days. Until then the souls of the deceased, even though in different divisions and with provisionally distinct lots, are kept in Hades as in ταμιεια, *promptuaria animarum*, *Apoc. Baruch* 21:23; 4 Ezra 4:35; 5:37. Resting and sleeping, they await the final judgment, 4 Ezra 7:32–35; *Apoc. Baruch* 21:24; 23:4; 30:2. But the writings of the second group, such as the Wisdom of Jesus Sirach, the book of Wisdom, Philo, Flavius Josephus, and so forth, place the emphasis precisely upon individual eschatology and in doing so cause the coming of the Messiah, the resurrection, the final judgment, and the kingdom of God upon earth to recede entirely into the shadows, or do not even speak a word about them. The principal dogma is the immortality of the soul, which according to Philo preexisted, because of its fall was temporarily confined in the prison of the body, and according to its conduct moves after death into other bodies; or in any case immediately after dying receives the definitive decision of its lot, Sir. 1:12; 7:17; 18:24; 41:12; Wis. 1:8, 9; 3:1–10, and goes to the holy heaven or the dark Hades, Josephus, *Bell. Jud.* III 8, 5. Therefore at the time of Christ all kinds of eschatological ideas intersected among the people of Israel. The Pharisees believed in continued existence and provisional recompense after death, but in this also held to the expectation of the Messiah, the resurrection of the dead—if not of all human beings then nevertheless of the righteous—and the establishment of the kingdom of God upon earth. The Sadducees denied the resurrection, Matt. 22:23; Mark 12:18; Luke 20:27; Acts 23:8, and according to Josephus, *Bell. Jud.* II 8, 14; *Ant.* XVIII 1, 4, also recompense after death and immortality. According to Josephus, *Bell. Jud.* II 8, 11, the Essenes assumed that the body was mortal but the soul immortal. Souls originally dwelt in the finest ether but were seized by sensory desire and placed in bodies, from which they are then liberated again by death. The good souls receive a blessed life on the other side of the ocean in a place troubled by no rain, snow, or heat, but the evil must suffer everlasting pains in a dark, cold place. Cf. Gröbler, “Die Ansichten über Unsterbl. u. Auferst. in der jüd. Lit. der beiden letzten Jahrh. v. Chr.,” *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1879, pp. 651–700; Wünsche, “Die Vorst. v. Zustande nach dem Tode nach Apokr., Talmud u. Kirchenvätern,” *Jahrb. f. prot. Theol.*, 1880, pp. 355–383, 435–523; Weber, *Syst. der altsyn. pal. Theol.*, 322f.; Oehler in Herzog¹ 21, 424f.; Runze in Herzog² 16, 193; Atzberger, *Christl. Eschatol.*, 96–156; Schwally, *Das Leben nach d. Tode*, 131–192.

In the footsteps of law and prophets, the New Testament devotes much more attention to general than to particular eschatology. Yet it is incorrect both to maintain with Episcopius, *Op.* II 2, p. 455; Limborch, *Theol. Christ.* VI 10, 4; Oertel, *Hades*, pp. 4–6; Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.*, § 159, 2; Hofmann, *Schriftbeweis* III 462, that Scripture says virtually nothing about the intermediate state or at least contains no doctrine concerning it valid for us, and to hold with Kliefoth, *Eschatologie*, 37, that the New Testament probably says everything about it that can be said. For statements are not lacking that spread as much light upon the intermediate state as we need in and for this life. Even more strongly than the Old, the New Testament brings out that death is a consequence and penalty of sin, Rom. 5:12; 6:23; 8:10; 1 Cor. 15:21. And that death extends to all human beings, 1 Cor. 15:22; Heb. 9:27. Only an individual, such as Enoch, is taken away so that he should not see death, Heb. 11:5. And those who live to experience Christ’s *parousia* are changed at once without the intervention of death, 1 Cor. 15:51; 1 Thess. 4:14–17; cf. John 21:22, 23, so that Christ will judge not only the dead but also the living, Acts 10:42; 2 Tim. 4:1; 1 Pet. 4:5. But that death is not the end of the human being. The soul cannot be killed, Matt. 10:28; the body will one day be raised, John 5:28, 29; Acts 23:6; Rev. 20:12, 13; and believers even partake of an eternal life that cannot die, John 3:36; 11:25. Until the resurrection all the deceased are also, according to the New Testament, in Hades, which is the realm of the dead. In Matt. 11:23 the καταβασις ἑως ᾅδου signifies that proud Capernaum will be most deeply humbled. In Matt. 16:18 Jesus promises his congregation that the πυλαι ᾅδου will have no power over her, that death will not triumph over her. According to Luke 16:23 poor Lazarus is carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom and the rich man immediately through death and burial enters Hades. Here it cannot be proved that Hades is already the same as the place of torment, since the latter is first designated by the further addition ὑπαρχων ἐν βασανοις. Jesus too, as long as he was in the state of death, was in Hades, even though he was not held by it, Acts 2:27, 31; for he descended εἰς τα κατωτερα της γης, Eph. 4:9. And so all the deceased are καταχθονιοι, Phil. 2:10. Not only the wicked but also believers are after death in Hades; they are νεκροι ἐν Χριστῳ, 1 Thess. 4:16; cf. 1 Cor. 15:18, 23. At the resurrection the sea, death, and Hades give back all the dead who were in them so that they may be judged according to their works, Rev. 20:13. Hades follows with and after death, so that death always brings about a relocation into Hades, Rev. 6:8. This conception, that according to Scripture believers too are in Hades from death until the resurrection, is strengthened by the expression ἀναστασις ἐκ νεκρων, Matt. 17:9; Mark 6:14; Luke 16:30; John 20:9; etc.; ἐκ των νεκρων, Eph. 5:14—that is, not out of death but from among the dead, from the realm of the deceased. This common presence in the state of death does not exclude the lot of believers and unbelievers already being very different there. The Old Testament too already expressed this thought, but it meets us much more clearly in the New Testament. According to the parable in Luke 16, poor Lazarus is carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom, which signifies that in heaven, where the angels dwell, Lazarus enjoys blessedness in the proximity and communion of Abraham; cf. Matt. 8:11. Jesus promises one of his fellow crucified that today he will be with him in paradise, Luke 23:43. The word *paradise* is Persian in origin and generally denotes a garden, a pleasure garden, Neh. 2:8; Eccles. 2:5; Song 4:13. The LXX used it as the translation of the garden in Gen. 2:8–15. The Jews used it to designate the place where God grants his communion to the righteous after their death, Weber, *Syst. der alts. pal. Theol.*, 330. Undoubtedly according to the New Testament too paradise, like Abraham’s bosom, is to be conceived as in heaven. Shortly after Jesus had promised the thief that today he would be with him in paradise, he commended his spirit into his Father’s hands, Luke 23:46. In 2 Cor. 12:2, 4, paradise alternates with the third heaven. In Rev. 2:7; 21:2 it denotes the place where in the future God will dwell among his people. In agreement with this, the Gospel of John teaches that believers, who already here on earth possess the beginning of eternal life and have escaped God’s judgment, 3:15–21; 5:24, partake of a communion with Christ that is broken neither by his departure, 12:32; 14:23, nor by death, 11:25, 26, and will one day be completed in eternal togetherness, 6:39; 14:3, 19; 16:16; 17:24. Dying, Stephen prays that the Lord Jesus receive his spirit to himself in heaven, Acts 7:59. Paul knows that the believer partakes of a life exalted above death, Rom. 8:10, and that nothing, not even death, can separate him from the love of God in Christ, 8:38; 14:8; 1 Thess. 5:10. Although he must remain in the flesh a while longer for the sake of the congregations, he nevertheless longs to depart and be with Christ, Phil. 1:23; 2 Cor. 5:8. According to Rev. 6:9; 7:9, the souls of the martyrs are with Christ under the altar of burnt offering standing before God’s throne in the temple of heaven; cf. 2:7, 10, 17, 26; 3:4, 5, 12, 21; 8:3; 9:13; 14:13; 15:2; 16:17; and also Heb. 11:10, 16; 12:23. And just as believers immediately after death enjoy a provisional blessedness with Christ in heaven, so unbelievers, as soon as they have died, enter a place of torment. The rich man was in torment when in Hades he lifted up his eyes, Luke 16:23. Unbelievers who reject Christ remain under God’s wrath and are already judged upon earth, John 3:18, 36, and immediately after death have a judgment to await with all human beings, Heb. 9:27. Yet this place of torment is not identical with the γεεννα or the λιμνη του πυρος, for Gehenna is the place of the unquenchable and eternal fire prepared for the devils, Mark 9:43, 47, 48; Matt. 18:8; 25:41, 46; and the lake of fire is not the present but the future place of punishment for the world empire and the false prophet, Rev. 19:20; for Satan, Rev. 20:10; and for all the wicked, Rev. 21:8; cf. 2 Pet. 2:17; Jude 13. Rather, they are all now kept in a φυλακη, 1 Pet. 3:19, or in the ἀβυσσος, Luke 8:31; cf. Matt. 8:29; Rom. 10:7; Rev. 9:1, 2, 11; 11:7; 17:8; 20:1, 3, for the final judgment and the lake of fire, 2 Pet. 2:17; Jude 6, 13. This distinction in the intermediate state between the good and the evil does not conflict with their all being together in Hades, for all the deceased as such are καταχθονιοι, still belong before the resurrection to the realm of the dead, and only through that resurrection are fully liberated, according to both soul and body, from the dominion of death, 1 Cor. 15:52–55; Rev. 20:14.
