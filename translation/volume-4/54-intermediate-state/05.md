## 5.

In the first period Christian theology confined itself to these simple data of Holy Scripture. The apostolic fathers do not yet have a doctrine of the intermediate state and are generally of the opinion that at death the godly immediately partake of heavenly blessedness and the wicked of infernal punishment. Burnet, in his *Tractatus de statu mortuorum et resurgentium*, 1727, and others after him, such as Blondel, Ernesti, Baumgarten-Crusius, and so forth, did indeed attempt to demonstrate that the oldest Christian writers had the proper blessedness of believers begin only after the world judgment, but they could adduce no conclusive proofs for it, Atzberger, *Gesch. der christl. Eschatologie innerhalb der vornicän. Zeit*, Freiburg: Herder, 1896, pp. 75–99. Only when the *parousia* of Christ did not come as soon as was initially generally expected and different heretics deformed or opposed the doctrine of the last things did people begin more deliberately to reflect upon the intermediate state. Ebionitism attempted to maintain the national privileges of Israel to the detriment of Christian universalism and was therefore generally inclined toward chiliasm. Gnosticism, by virtue of its dualistic principle, rejected the whole of Christian eschatology and had no other expectation than the liberation of the spirit from matter and its reception into the divine *pleroma* immediately at death, Atzberger, ibid., 172–218. Christian theology was thereby compelled to give itself a clearer account of the character of the intermediate state and its connection both with this life and with the final state after the last judgment. Justin already said that after death the souls of the godly resided in a better place and those of the unrighteous in a worse one to await the time of judgment, *Dial. c. Tryph.* 5, and condemned as an unchristian doctrine that there is no resurrection of the dead and that souls are immediately received into heaven at death, ibid., 80. According to Irenaeus, at death the souls of the godly do not immediately enter heaven, paradise, or the city of God, which after the last judgment will be three distinct dwelling places of the righteous, *adv. haer.* V 36, but enter an invisible place determined by God where they await the resurrection and the vision of God that follows. For Christ too first resided for three days where the dead were, *in inferioribus terrae*, to liberate his holy dead from it, and after thus fulfilling the *lex mortuorum* was raised and received into heaven, V 31. There in the shadow of death, in Hades, every human being receives a *dignam habitationem, etiam ante judicium*, the godly probably in Abraham’s bosom, which is thus a division of Hades, II 34. We encounter the same conception of different *receptacula* in Hades, where the deceased await the final decision on the last day, in Hippolytus, Tertullian, Novatian, Commodianus, Victorinus, Lactantius, Hilary, Ambrose, Cyril, and even still in Augustine, *Enchir.* 109, 110; cf. Atzberger, loc. cit., 275f., 301f.; Schwane, *D. G.* II 585. But as the *parousia* of Christ receded into the distant future, it became the more difficult to maintain the ancient conception of Hades and to regard residence there as a brief, provisional, more or less neutral state. An exception was made for the martyrs already at an early date. According to Irenaeus, Tertullian, and others, immediately after their death they had entered heaven and been admitted to the vision of God. Christ’s descent into Hades was accordingly interpreted as having liberated the believers who died before Christ’s sacrifice from the *limbus patrum* and transferred them to heaven. And the doctrine of the necessity and meritoriousness of good works, which increasingly entered the church, naturally led to the thought that those who had dedicated their whole life to God in a special sense were now also worthy of heavenly blessedness immediately at their death. Thus Hades was gradually robbed of its inhabitants. The unbelievers did indeed remain, but this had precisely as its consequence that Hades was increasingly regarded as a place of punishment and identified with Tartarus or Gehenna. Of the Christians only those could still reside for a time in Hades who had not attained so far in sanctification here on earth that at their death they could enter heavenly glory without delay. With this the idea of a purifying fire was gradually connected, first expressed by Origen. According to him all punishments were medicines, φαρμακα, and the whole of Hades, including Gehenna, was a place of purification, *c. Cels.* III 75; VI 25, 26. Specifically, sins were consumed and human beings purified by the πυρ καθαρσιον that at the end of this dispensation would set the world aflame, ibid., VI 12, 13, 21, 64; V 15, 16. Following Origen, later Greek theologians therefore assumed that the souls of many deceased persons still had to suffer pains and could be liberated from them only through the intercessions and sacrifices of the living, *Conf. orth.*, qus. 64–68, but nevertheless objected to a special purifying fire such as the Western church taught. Only at the Council of Florence did they make some concession on this point, Münscher-v. Coelln, *D. G.* II 313; Schwane, *D. G.* II 587; III 486; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Conf.* I 327. In the West, by contrast, the purifying fire of which Origen had spoken was transferred from the final judgment to the intermediate state. Augustine sometimes said that after the general resurrection or at the last judgment some *poenae purgatoriae* were still imposed, *de civ.* XX 25; XXI 24. But he nevertheless ordinarily has the development of the kingdom of God conclude with the last judgment and therefore does not consider it impossible that *nonnullos fideles per ignem quendam purgatorium, quanto magis minusve bona pereuntia dilexerunt, tanto tardius citiusve salvari*, *Enchir.* 69. Caesarius of Arles and Gregory the Great elaborated this in such a way that specifically the *peccata venialia* could be atoned for here or hereafter. And when the ecclesiastical practice, mentioned already by Tertullian, *de monog.* 10, 11; *de exhort. cast.* 11, of making intercessions and sacrifices for the deceased was connected with this doctrine, the dogma of purgatory was complete. Scholasticism gave it broader development, Lombard and others on *Sent.* IV 21; Thomas, *S. Theol.*, appendix, qu. 2; Bonaventure, *Brevil.* VII 2, 3. The Council of Florence in 1439 and Trent, sess. 6, can. 30; sess. 22, ch. 2, can. 3; sess. 25, established it, and later theology gave it an ever greater significance for religious and ecclesiastical life. According to Roman doctrine the souls of the condemned immediately enter hell (*gehenna, abyssus, infernus*), where they are tormented with the unclean spirits in an eternal and unquenchable fire. The souls of those who after receiving baptism were no longer stained by sin or were cleansed from that stain here or hereafter are received into heaven without delay and there behold the face of God, although in different degrees of perfection according to their merits, in Denzinger, nos. 870, 875. Through Christ’s descent into hell, the souls of the saints who had died before that time were also transferred from the *limbus patrum* (Abraham’s bosom) to heaven. Children dying unbaptized, concerning whose lot the church fathers already judged at times more gently and at other times more harshly, are consigned to the *infernus*, where, however, the punishments are very unequal, in Denzinger, ibid.; and according to the most common conception enter a particular division (*limbus infantum*) where they suffer only an *aeterna poena damni* but not a *poena sensus*, Lombard, Thomas, Bonaventure on *Sent.* II, dist. 33; Thomas, *S. Theol.*, suppl., qu. 69, art. 4. But those who, after receiving sanctifying grace in baptism or again in penance, become guilty of *peccata venialia* and were unable to satisfy in this life the temporal punishments determined for them are not pure enough to be admitted immediately to the blessed vision of God in heaven. They enter a place between heaven and hell, not to acquire new virtues and merits but to remove the obstacles lying in the way of their entrance into heaven. For this purpose, in the first moment after dying, through an act of repentance, *per actum peccato veniali contrariae dispositionis*, they are freed from the guilt of venial sins and then still have to bear the temporal punishments that even after forgiveness remain fixed for those sins. Purgatory (Dutch *vagevuur*, from *vagen, vegen*, that is, to cleanse, purify) is therefore no place of conversion, probation, or sanctification, but a place of punishment in which the fire, usually conceived as material, serves *idealiter*, through the representation of pain, to work purifyingly upon the “poor souls.” Moreover, by virtue of the communion of saints the church can come to the aid of these suffering souls and mitigate and shorten their punishment through intercessions, Mass sacrifices, good works, and indulgences. No one indeed knows which particular souls enter purgatory, how long they must remain there, and under what conditions on their part the prayers and sacrifices of the living benefit them; but this uncertainty does no harm to the cult of the deceased. For increasingly the rule is that, apart from a few exceptions such as martyrs and particular saints, the great mass of believers first enter purgatory. In any event, they are far ahead of the living who must still pass through that purgatory on their way to heaven. Poor souls on the one hand, they are nevertheless, considered from another side, blessed souls who with the angels and the blessed are invoked for help by the living in need. Cf. *Catech. Rom.* I, ch. 6, qu. 3; Bellarminus, *de purgatorio*, *Controv.* II 228–269; Perrone, *Prael.* III 309; Möhler, *Symbolik*, § 23; Oswald, *Eschatologie*², 1869, p. 81; Simar, *Dogm.*, § 164; Atzberger, *Chr. Eschatologie*, 269; Deharbe, *Kath. geloofs- en zedeleer* II 328; Jansen, *Prael.* III 975.
