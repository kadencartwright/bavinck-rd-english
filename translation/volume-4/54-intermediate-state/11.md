## 11.

Until now the question was only whether the deceased still stand in any direct intercourse with the earth. Now the question comes under discussion whether Holy Scripture teaches us anything about the new relations and conditions in which the dead find themselves on the other side of the grave. What Scripture tells us about this is not much. Yet already in the Old Testament the lines are present that, when extended, lead to a distinction in the state of the righteous and the wicked after death. The fear of the Lord is the way to life, but the wicked perish and come to an end, above, p. 369. And according to the New Testament the rich man immediately enters a place of torment, which, however, is not yet identical with Gehenna or the abyss. Where this place is to be sought is not mentioned in Scripture. Sheol, Hades, Gehenna, and the abyss are indeed always represented as situated beneath us. But this cannot and may not be understood in a topographical sense. For the concepts above and below, taken locally, are very relative and in this connection have only an ethical significance. We naturally place the kingdom of darkness directly over against that of light and, according to a natural symbolism, seek the former beneath and the latter above us. Every determination of the place of punishment of the deceased—in the earth, under the earth, in the sea, in the sun, in the air, or upon one of the planets—is nothing more than a conjecture. It can only be said that the *Jenseits* is not only a state but also a place, for souls may not be in time and space *circumscriptive*, but they are still much less eternal and omnipresent and must be somewhere and also have a succession of moments of time. Otherwise, it accords better with the few data Scripture offers us to refrain from every determination of the place of punishment of the dead: μη ζητωμεν που ἐστι, ἀλλα πως αὐτην φυγοιμεν (Chrysostom). We know just as little of the condition in which unbelievers and the wicked exist after death until the final judgment. We can say with certainty only that, when God’s wrath already remains upon unbelievers here, it must be felt much more severely immediately after death, since all distraction of earthly life is lacking and bare existence is filled with nothing but the consciousness and feeling of that wrath. The question has been raised, however, whether there will not still exist on the other side of the grave an opportunity for those who here on earth heard the gospel not at all or only very imperfectly to repent and believe in Christ. The first in the Christian church to give an affirmative answer to this were Clement, *Strom.* VI 6, and Origen, *c. Cels.* II 44. They inferred from 1 Pet. 3:18, 19 that Christ, and also the apostles, had proclaimed the gospel to those among the dead in Hades who were receptive to it. Although this view was refuted by Augustine, *de haeresi*, ch. 79, and others, and Christ’s descent into hell was ordinarily understood differently, vol. III, 378f., it nevertheless repeatedly returned and found acceptance especially in this century, in which people acquired a clear awareness of the great multitude and rapid expansion of non-Christians, among very many, above, p. 382. Indeed, it is a fact of the greatest significance that there have been and still are millions of human beings who never had any knowledge of the way of salvation in Christ and thus were also never placed in the position of accepting him with a believing heart or rejecting him with a decided will. These are not to be reckoned among unbelievers in the narrower sense, and Scripture itself says that they are judged according to another standard than Jews and Christians, Matt. 10:15; 11:20–24; Luke 10:12–15; 12:47, 48; John 15:22, 24; Rom. 2:12; 2 Pet. 2:20–22.

Yet it by no means follows from this that there is or must be a preaching of the gospel on the other side of the grave. For 1º Scripture does not speak of it in a single word. Many passages sometimes adduced for it, such as Matt. 12:40; John 20:17; Acts 2:24, 27, 31; 13:29, 30, 34–37; 1 Tim. 3:16, have not the least probative force and do not deal at all with a preaching of Christ in hell. Nor does Ezek. 16:53–63 open a prospect of this. There the Lord promises that despite the abominations Jerusalem committed, which were worse than those of her sisters Sodom and Samaria, he will nevertheless restore her in the end and receive her in grace. But in order to remove all false trust in God’s promise and all self-exaltation in Israel, it is added that the Lord will turn not only the captivity of Jerusalem but also that of Sodom and Samaria, v. 53, so that they too will return to their former state. Some have inferred from this a possibility of repentance in the intermediate state. For Sodom and her sisters—that is, the other cities in the Valley of Siddim—had all long since been destroyed in Ezekiel’s days and thus could not be restored to their former state and received by God in grace unless their former inhabitants came to repentance in Sheol through the preaching of God’s Word. But this thought lies far outside the text. Here the Lord only promises that despite her prostitution he will nevertheless again receive Jerusalem in grace. And not only that, but also Sodom and Samaria, who specifically, as v. 61 shows, are types of all the Gentile peoples, will be restored to their former state. That is, the future will be that Jerusalem is restored and the Gentile cities subject to her. There is no mention of preaching and repentance in Sheol or of a resurrection and return of the former inhabitants. 2º The only passages to which one can appeal with some appearance of right for a preaching of the gospel in Hades are 1 Pet. 3:19–21; 4:6. But these texts too do not contain what people wish to read into them. Even if the subject there were a preaching Christ held after his resurrection to Noah’s contemporaries in Hades, only this fact would thereby be established; it would by no means give the right to teach that in Hades a continual proclamation of the gospel took place to all who did not hear it on earth. For Noah’s contemporaries were precisely not such human beings as never heard the Word of God during their life on earth. In willful wickedness they despised the word of Noah, the preacher of righteousness, and in full consciousness were disobedient to the Lord’s voice. Theirs was thus an altogether special case giving no right to further conclusions. The aorist ἐκηρυξεν also indicates that this preaching by Christ happened only once. Further, this preaching cannot have been a proclamation of the gospel unto salvation. For when one considers how sternly Scripture continually judges all the wicked and how it always describes the human race in Noah’s time as surrendered to all wickedness and unrighteousness, the thought that Christ would have proclaimed the gospel of salvation precisely to them in distinction from so many others becomes absurd. At most, then, it could concern a solemn announcement of his triumph to the inhabitants of the underworld, as the older Lutherans explained the text. Moreover, all kinds of difficulty are connected with such a continual preaching of the gospel in Hades. According to 1 Pet. 3:18, 19, Christ held that preaching specifically after he had been made alive and raised. Did he then go locally with his body to Hades? When did he do this? How long did he reside there? And even granting that all this is possible, however improbable it already is in itself, who then carries on this preaching in Hades after that time and continually now? Is there then also a church in the underworld? Is there a mission, a calling, an ordination? Are they human beings or angels, are they the apostles or other ministers of the Word, who proclaim the gospel there after their death? The doctrine of a *Missionsanstalt* in Hades conflicts with Scripture in all kinds of ways. But as was already demonstrated earlier, vol. III, 422; cf. 379, it also finds no support whatsoever in 1 Pet. 3:18–22. There it is said only that after his resurrection Christ, having been made alive as Spirit, went to heaven and through this ascension preached to the spirits in prison and subjected the angels, powers, and authorities to himself. Neither does 1 Pet. 4:6 concern such a preaching of the gospel in Hades. Already the aorist εὐηγγελισθη suggests not a continual preaching but a definite fact. That proclamation of the gospel took place once, and specifically with the purpose that those who heard it would be judged in the flesh according to the manner of human beings—that is, would die—but would live in the spirit according to the manner of God. Thus the preaching of the gospel preceded their dying. The νεκροι are those who are now dead but heard the gospel during their life. The reason Peter calls these human beings νεκροι lies in the preceding verse. There it was said that Christ stands ready to judge the living and the dead. Now, just as the gospel is proclaimed to the living today, so formerly it was proclaimed to those who are now dead, so that they would indeed still die in the flesh but would nevertheless already now live in the spirit with God. 3º With these objections derived from Scripture the whole doctrine of the preaching of the gospel in the intermediate state already falls. For if Scripture does not contain it, Christian theology is not free to teach it nevertheless. But there are also still many other objections. Granted that the gospel is still preached in Hades, does that preaching go out to all without distinction? Ordinarily people say no and have it brought only to those who did not come to know it here on earth. This is not only in conflict with their exegesis of 1 Pet. 3:18–22, for *if* the subject there is a preaching of the gospel by Christ in Hades, then it takes place precisely to such persons as had indeed heard the gospel through Noah; but it also naturally raises the question whether life here on earth is altogether immaterial for that preaching of the gospel in Hades. Understandably, people as a rule do not dare give a negative answer to this either, for then this life would be entirely without value or significance. Therefore people ordinarily say with Clement and Origen that the gospel in the intermediate state is brought only to such persons as are receptive to repentance, who here on earth through their attitude toward the *vocatio realis* prepared themselves for the believing acceptance of the gospel; cf., for example, Ebrard, *Dogm.*, § 576. In fact, the center of gravity is thereby shifted back to this life, and the proclamation of the gospel in Hades merely brings to light what was already hidden in hearts here on earth. That is, the decision concerning salvation and perdition rests not with the gospel but with the *vocatio realis*, with the law. And this is in essence the same view embraced also by the Pelagians, Socinians, Deists, and so forth—namely, that there are three ways to salvation, the *lex naturae*, the *lex Mosaica*, and the *lex Christi*. 4º To this is further added that the doctrine of a preaching of the gospel in Hades proceeds from all kinds of incorrect presuppositions. Underlying it is the idea that it is God’s intention to save all human beings; that the preaching of the gospel must be absolutely universal; that all human beings personally and individually must be placed before the choice for or against the gospel; that the decision in that choice stands in the human being’s power; that original and actual sins are insufficient to condemn but only decided unbelief toward the gospel makes one worthy of eternal perdition; and so forth. All these presuppositions conflict with decisive statements of Scripture and render the doctrine of gospel preaching in the intermediate state unacceptable. And when, finally, it is asked whether it is not harsh to believe that all who here on earth entirely without their fault did not hear the gospel are lost, the answer must be: *a.* that in this most serious matter not our feeling but God’s Word decides; *b.* that the doctrine of a preaching of the gospel to the deceased does not in the least mitigate this harshness, since it benefits only those who already sufficiently prepared themselves for faith here on earth; *c.* that it increases the harshness, since it does not consider the interest of the millions of little children who die young and in fact places them outside the possibility of being saved; and *d.* that it does not reckon with the sovereignty and omnipotence of God, who can also save without the external preaching of the Word, solely through the inward calling and regeneration of the Holy Spirit.
