## 6.

The Reformation saw in this purgatory a limitation of the merits of Christ and, by virtue of its principle of justification by faith alone, taught that immediately after a *judicium particulare in agone mortis* the human being entered into the blessedness of heaven or the perdition of hell. Luther himself still frequently represented the intermediate state of the pious as a sleep in which they peacefully and quietly awaited the coming of the Lord, Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 568; but later Lutheran theologians almost entirely erased the distinction between the intermediate and final states and said that immediately after death the souls of the pious enjoyed a *plena et essentialis beatitudo* and those of the wicked received a *perfecta ac consummata damnatio*: Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 540, 567; Gerhard, *Loc.* XXVI § 160, 191; Schmid, *Dogm. der ev. luth. K.* § 63. In the main this was also the opinion of the Reformed: *Catech. Heid.* 57, 58; *Ned. Gel.* 37; *Helv.* II 26; *Westm.*, ch. 32; Junius, *Theses theol.* 55, 56; Voetius, *Disp.* V 533–539. But ordinarily they brought out better than the Lutherans the difference that existed in the condition of the dead before and after the last day. Calvin said in his work on the *Psychopannychia* that Abraham’s bosom meant nothing other than that after death the souls of the pious would enjoy full peace, that until the day of resurrection they would nevertheless still lack something, namely *summa et perfecta Dei gloria, ad quam semper aspirant*, and that our salvation thus always remained *in cursu* *usque ad diem illum, qui omnem cursum claudit et terminabit*, *C. R.* 33, 177–232; cf. *Inst.* III 25, 6, and further, though usually expressing themselves somewhat more weakly, *Ned. Gel.* 37; *Synops. pur. theol.* 40, 17; Witsius, *Oec. foed.* III 14, 33; Heidegger, *Corpus Theol.* 28, 38. Others went still further and assumed a definite intermediate state. Lud. Cappellus said that after death the souls of the pious entered a state that could indeed be called blessed in comparison with the state here on earth, but was *longe diversus* from that blessedness which began after the resurrection. For the intermediate state consisted almost entirely *in spe atque exspectatione futurae gloriae, non vero in ipsa gloriae fruitione*. And likewise after death the souls of the wicked entered a state in which they awaited the firmly determined future punishment in anxiety and fear but did not yet suffer that punishment itself, for *exspectatio supplicii non est ipsum supplicium*. William Sherlock, Thomas Burnet, and many other English theologians thought substantially the same, and among the Lutherans Calixtus, Hornejus, Zeltner, Tresenreuter, J. H. Ursinus, and others; cf. M. Vitringa IV 63–69. Indeed, since the previous century all the ideas that had earlier been expressed by pagans and Christians, by philosophers and theologians, concerning the intermediate state returned in Protestant theology. The doctrine of the Roman purgatory was adopted again by many mystics and Pietists, such as Böhme, Antoinette Bourignon, Poiret, Dippel, Petersen, Arnold, Schermer, and others; cf. M. Vitringa IV 81, 82; and further by Leibniz, *Syst. der Theol.*, 1825, p. 340; Lessing, *Erziehung des Menschengeschl.*; J. F. v. Meyer, *Blätter f. höhere Wahrheit* VI 233; Jung-Stilling, *Theorie der Geisterkunde* § 211; Lange, *Dogm.* II 1250f.; Rothe, *Ethik* §§ 793–795; Martensen, *Dogm.* §§ 276, 277; Dorner, *Gl.* II 952f.; Van Oosterzee, *Dogm.* § 142; etc. Following some early Christian writers, the Socinians taught that, just as bodies returned to the earth, so souls returned to God and with him until the resurrection led an existence without perception or thought, without pleasure or displeasure: Fock, *Der Socin.*, 714f. Closely related to this was the doctrine of a sleep of the soul, which had already earlier been advocated by some heretics, later by the Anabaptists, and since the previous century again found acceptance with Artobe, Heyn, Sulzer—cf. Bretschneider, *Dogm.* II 395—with Fries, *Jahrb. f. d. Theol.*, 1856; Ulrici, *Gott und die Natur*², 332f.; and among the Irvingites. By others this doctrine of psychopannychy was modified in such a way that the souls indeed retained an inward consciousness but were shut off from intercourse with the external world: Episcopius, *Op.* II 2, 455; Limborch, *Theol. Christ.* VI 10, 8; J. Müller, *Lehre v. d. Sünde* II 402–408; Martensen, *Dogm.* § 276; Ebrard, *Dogm.* § 570; Dorner, *Gl.* II 952f.; Frank, *Chr. Wahrh.* II 460. Others avoided this doctrine of soul sleep by assuming that, upon laying aside the material covering, souls retained the organic and basic form of the body, or that after death they received a new body composed of the most subtle matter, through which they could remain in communion with the external world: Paracelsus, Helmont, Böhme, Oetinger, Ph. M. Hahn, Swedenborg, Priestley, Schott, Jean Paul; cf. Bretschneider, *Dogm.* II 396, and further Rothe, *Ethik* §§ 111f., 793f.; Hamberger, “Die Rationalität der himml. Leiblichkeit,” *Jahrb. f. d. Th.*, 1863; Delitzsch, *Bibl. Psych.*, 426f.; Martensen, *Dogm.* § 276; Ulrici, *Gott u. die Natur*², 333; Güder, *Lehre v. d. Erscheinung Christi unter den Todten*, 321f.; Splittgerber, *Tod, Fortleben u. Auferst.*³, 45f.; Rinck, *Vom Zustande nach dem Tode*, 1885, p. 114f.; Mühe, *Das enthüllte Geheimniss der Zukunft*³, 14f.; Nitzsch, *Ev. Dogm.*, 619; Grétillat, *Théol. syst.* IV 548; etc. Some have even returned to the ancient doctrine of the transmigration of souls and recommended it in this form: that through continued passages from one human body into another souls gradually partake of perfection—Lessing, *Erz. d. Mensch.*; Schlosser; Ungern-Sternberg; Dr. D. Burger, *De Plat. leer der zielsverhuizing*, Amersfoort, 1877; Carl Andresen, *Die Lehre v. d. Wiedergeburt auf theist. Grundlage*, 2nd ed., Hamburg, 1899, who finds reincarnation taught in John 3:3–17; and others in M. Vitringa IV 95–97; Bretschneider, *Dogm.* II 388; Spiess, *Entw. Gesch. der Vorstellungen vom Zustande nach d. Tode*, 1877, p. 31; Falke, *Die Lehre v. d. ewigen Verdammnis*, 1892, p. 59f. Today the idea of development is so all-controlling that it is also applied to the condition on the other side of the grave. The doctrine of the *limbus patrum* was again adopted by Martensen, *Dogm.* § 277; Delitzsch, *Bibl. Psych.*, 407f.; Vilmar, *Dogm.* II 290; Splittgerber, op. cit., 110f.; Cremer, *Ueber den Zustand nach dem Tode*, 1883, p. 9f. The opinion that in the intermediate state there is still preaching of the gospel and a possibility of conversion is a favorite idea of modern theology: Lange II 1250f.; Rothe §§ 796, 797; Delitzsch 413; Martensen § 277; Dorner II 952f.; Ebrard § 576; Cremer, op. cit., 48, 62f.; Kliefoth, *Eschatologie*, 97–113; Falke, op. cit., 152f.; Krauss, *Lehre v. d. Offenbarung*, 112; Rinck, op. cit., 79–88; Mühe, op. cit., 30f.; Grétillat IV 540, 549; Van Oosterzee § 142; Doedes, *Ned. Gel.*, 521; etc. Many even understand the entire *Jenseits* as an ongoing purification, whose result is that some possibly perish eternally (hypothetical universalism), or that those who persist in evil are finally annihilated (conditional immortality), or that in the end all are saved (apocatastasis); cf. § 56.
