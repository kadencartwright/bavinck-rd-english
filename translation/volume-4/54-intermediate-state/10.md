## 10.

In the third place, there are many who think that after death souls still remain in some relation to earthly life. Among many peoples the thought prevailed that after death souls remained near the grave, and the Jews too believed that for some time after death they continued hovering around the corpse and explained from this that the sorceress at Endor could still summon the spirit of Samuel: Weber, *Syst. der altsyn. pal. Theol.*, 324. The custom was widespread of placing with the dead person in the grave food, weapons, possessions, and sometimes even his wives and slaves; and ordinarily this veneration of the dead was not limited to the day of burial or the period of mourning, but was continued afterward and incorporated into regular private or public worship. Not only the dead in general were venerated, but also deceased blood relatives, parents and ancestors; the fathers and heads of the tribe; the heroes of the people; the rulers and kings of the land, sometimes already during their life; and in Buddhism and Islam also the saints. The cult consisted in maintaining their graves, caring for their bodies (for example by embalming), placing flowers and food upon their grave from time to time, paying homage to their images and relics, arranging meals and games in their honor, sending up prayers to them, and bringing them sacrifices. In doing this people frequently made a distinction, as in Persia, India, and Greece, between the veneration of these deceased human beings and that of the gods, but nevertheless the cult of the dead was a principal component of religion. By venerating them people intended in part to come to their aid, but especially also to ward off the calamity they could cause and to secure their blessing and assistance, whether in an ordinary or an extraordinary way through oracles and miracles; cf. Ch. de la Saussaye, *Lehrb. der Religionsgesch.* I 79–87. From the second century onward all these elements also penetrated Christian worship. Just as the monks in Buddhism and the mystics in Islam, so in the Christian church the martyrs soon became objects of religious veneration. Altars, chapels, and churches were built at the place where they had died or their relics had been deposited. There, especially on the death anniversaries (*dies natales*) of the martyrs, believers assembled to celebrate their memory through vigils and psalm singing, through the reading of the *acta martyrum* and hearing a sermon in their honor, and especially also through celebrating the holy Eucharist. And after the fourth century this cult extended from Mary, the angels, the patriarchs, the prophets, and the martyrs also to bishops, monks, hermits, *confessores, virgines*, and all kinds of saints, and to their relics and images: Schwane, *D. G.* I 389f.; II 620f. In spite of all opposition both within and outside the Roman Catholic Church, it has not only maintained itself to this day but is still increasing in an alarming manner and pushes the worship of the one true God and of Jesus Christ, whom he sent, increasingly into the background. In this cult Rome puts the communion of saints into practice. The one Christian church has three divisions: the *ecclesia triumphans* in heaven, the *ecclesia patiens* in purgatory, and the *ecclesia militans* on earth. The part the *ecclesia patiens* takes in this communion consists in the blessed in heaven coming to the aid of the poor souls in purgatory with their intercessions; the church on earth mitigating and shortening the punishments of those souls through prayers, alms, good works, indulgences, and especially through the sacrifice of the mass; and finally also in the souls in purgatory, who in any event are far ahead of most members of the church militant and therefore may be invoked, helping and strengthening believers on earth through their intercessions. This last element, although increasingly occupying a broader place even in communion with the *ecclesia patiens*, nevertheless forms the principal component of the communion of the church militant with the church triumphant. The blessed in heaven, like the angels, partake of perfect supernatural holiness and are therefore objects of worship and veneration. They do not all share in this holiness to the same degree; like the angels, they form a spiritual hierarchy. Mary stands at the top, and after her follow the patriarchs, the prophets, the apostles, the martyrs, the *confessores*, and so forth. It is a descending order, but in all of them something of the divine virtues shines forth. And everything that has stood or still stands in some relation to the saints shares in this too: their body, limbs, clothing, dwelling, image, and so forth. And to the same degree that something stands nearer to God and shares more in his holiness, it is an object of religious veneration. Thus here too there are all kinds of differences. There is *latria*, which is due to God alone; the human nature of Christ and all its members, for example the sacred heart, is indeed not *per se* and *propter se* but nevertheless *in se* an object of *latria*; Mary is entitled to *hyperdulia*; the saints to *dulia*; their relics to a *cultus religiosus relativus*; and so forth. *Tot sunt species adorationis, quot sunt species excellentiae, adoratio est diversa pro diversitate excellentiae*, vol. II 451f.; III 262, 300. The veneration of the saints generally consists in prayers, fasting, vigils, feast days, gifts, pilgrimages, processions, and so forth, and has as its goal to acquire the favor of God through their intercessions and obtain one benefit or another from him. But this veneration and intercession is not only general but also particular; there are particular saints for particular peoples, families, and persons, and there are special saints for different needs and necessities. Saint George is the patron saint of England, James of Spain, Stephen of Hungary; painters venerate Saint Luke, carpenters Joseph, shoemakers Crispin; Saint Sebastian helps especially in plague, Ottilia with eye disease, Anthony when someone has lost something; even animals have their patron saint—the geese are specially guarded by Saint Gallus and the sheep by Saint Wendelinus; and so forth. Cf. *Trid.*, sess. 25, and other ecclesiastical determinations in Denzinger, 243, 273, 866; etc.; *Catech. Rom.* III 2, qu. 4–14; Jerome, *Contra Vigilantium*, in Migne XXXIII 339–452; Damascenus, *de imaginibus*; idem, *de fide orthod.* IV 15, 16; Lombard and others on *Sent.* IV, dist. 45; Thomas, *S. Theol.* II 2, qu. 83, art. 11; *Suppl.*, qu. 71, 72; Bellarmine, *de ecclesia triumphante sive de gloria et cultu sanctorum*, *Controv.* II 269–368; Oswald, *Eschatologie*², 121–233; Atzberger, *Die christl. Eschatol.*, 263–269; etc. Many of these conceptions have repeatedly returned in Protestant theology too. The Lutherans conceded that the angels and also the saints pray *pro ecclesia universa in genere*, *Apol. conf.* 21; *Art. Smalc.* II 2. Just as Hugo Grotius had earlier defended the invocation of the saints in his *Votum pro pace*, so Leibniz later defended this and even the veneration of images and relics, *Syst. der Theol.*, 1825, pp. 116–195. Ritualism in England moves in the same direction, Ryle, *Knots Untied*, p. 491; etc. Many theologians assume that after death a certain connection between soul and body continues to exist, Beck, *Seelenlehre*, 40f.; Delitzsch, *Bibl. Psych.*, 444f.; that souls still maintain some intercourse with the earth, have knowledge of the most important events, pray for us, and look down upon us in blessing, Splittgerber, *Tod, Fortleben und Auferstehung*, 157f. In the previous century many, such as Swedenborg, Jung-Stilling, and Oberlin, believed themselves to stand in direct intercourse with departed spirits; cf. especially J. C. Wötzel, *Meiner Gattin wirkliche Erscheinung nach ihrem Tode*, Chemnitz, 1804. The possibility of their apparitions was and still is acknowledged by many, such as Kant, *Träume eines Geistersehers*; Lessing, *Dramaturgie*; J. H. Fichte, *Anthropologie*; Jung-Stilling, *Theorie der Geisterkunde*, 1808; Kerner, *Die Seherin von Prevorst*, 1829; Eschenmayer, *Das Mysterium des inneren Lebens, erläutert aus der Gesch. der Seherin von Prevorst*, 1830. Since 1848 spiritualism has been added to this, which intentionally seeks to establish rapport with the spirit world and believes it has received actual revelations (through signs, knocks, writing, psychography), operations (raising and moving furniture, untying knots, playing musical instruments), and apparitions (materializations) of spirits. From the rich literature of spiritualism let only the work of Russian councillor of state Alexander Aksakov be named, which contains all the material and is entitled *Animismus und Spiritismus*, 2 vols., Leipzig: Mutze, 1894.

To begin now with the last, 1º it deserves attention that superstitious practices occur among all peoples, including those with whom Israel came into contact, such as the Egyptians, Gen. 41:8; Ex. 7:11; the Canaanites, Deut. 18:9, 14; the Babylonians, Dan. 1:20; 2:2; etc. They also penetrated Israel and were often practiced extensively, 1 Sam. 28:9; 2 Kings 21:6; Isa. 2:6. Consulting the dead also belonged to these practices, and those who occupied themselves with it were called אֹבוֹת or יִדְּעֹנִים. The word אוב first denotes the divining spirit that dwells in someone, Lev. 20:27; that someone possesses, 1 Sam. 28:7, 8; that is consulted through someone, 1 Sam. 28:8; through whom someone causes a dead person to come up, 1 Sam. 28:9; and that, as the dead were conceived, proclaims oracles in a whispering, mysterious manner, Isa. 8:19; 19:3; 29:4; and subsequently designates the diviner himself, 1 Sam. 28:3, 9; 2 Kings 21:6; 2 Chron. 33:6; LXX ἐγγαστρομυθος, ventriloquist. The other word, ידענים, knowing ones, wise ones, is only a further definition of אֹבוֹת and first designated the divining persons and afterward the divining spirit that was in them, Lev. 19:31; 20:6, 27; Isa. 19:3. This divination could take place in many ways, but among others also through consulting the dead, Deut. 18:11; cf. Stade, *Gesch. des Volkes Israel* I 443f., 505; Schwally, *Das Leben nach dem Tode*, 69f. But law and prophecy declared themselves decisively against it and called the people back to the Lord, to his revelation and testimony, Ex. 22:18; Lev. 19:26, 31; 20:6, 27; Deut. 18:11; 1 Sam. 28:19; Isa. 8:19; 47:9–15; Jer. 27:9; 29:8; Mic. 3:7; 5:11; Nah. 3:4; Mal. 3:5; and the New Testament sets its seal upon this, Luke 16:29; Acts 8:9f.; 13:6f.; 19:13f.; Gal. 5:20; Eph. 5:11; Rev. 9:21; 21:8; 22:15. 2º It is even unprovable that Holy Scripture accepts the possibility of summoning and the appearing of the dead. Resurrections do at times take place through God’s miraculous power, and Scripture acknowledges demonic powers and operations that surpass human ability, Deut. 13:1, 2; Matt. 24:24; 2 Thess. 2:9; Rev. 13:13–15. But nowhere does it teach the possibility or actuality of an apparition of the dead. The only passage that can be adduced against this is 1 Sam. 28, where Saul takes refuge with the sorceress at Endor, for the appearance of Moses and Elijah with Christ on the Mount of Transfiguration, Matt. 17; Luke 9, was effected without human mediation by God alone. But although the rationalistic explanation that sees in this history nothing other than deliberate deceit by the woman must be rejected, an objective, real apparition of Samuel can no more be accepted. For Saul does not see Samuel, v. 14; the woman does see him but is in a hypnotic state, v. 12, and she sees him as he appeared during his life, as an old man wrapped in a prophet’s mantle, v. 14. The woman’s fright, v. 12, therefore had its cause not in her actually seeing Samuel contrary to expectation, but in this, that upon seeing Samuel in her hypnotic state she also immediately recognized Saul the king and feared him. After Saul has been brought under the impression that a subterranean spiritual being, אלהים, had come up from the earth and Samuel himself had appeared, the latter speaks to Saul in and through the woman and announces judgment to him. There is nothing in 1 Sam. 28 that goes beyond the known phenomena of hypnotism and somnambulism and cannot be explained in the same way. 3º There are, however, many who think that precisely from the phenomena of hypnotism, somnambulism, spiritualism, and so forth, they must infer the operation of spirits. But this hypothesis appears for the present to be by no means justified. Apart from the many frauds that have occurred in this domain, what is related of the appearance and operation of spirits is so childish and insignificant that the involvement of the spirit world by no means needs to be assumed for it. This does not deny that all kinds of phenomena occur that have not yet been explained; but they are all of such a kind (such as the sudden knowledge and speaking of foreign languages, clairvoyance, hypnosis, suggestion, second sight, premonition, knowledge of what is happening elsewhere at the same moment, telepathy, and so forth) that they become no clearer whatever through the hypothesis of the appearance of spirits. When we further consider that the human being in his perceptions is bound to and limited within a certain number of ether vibrations, so that some alteration in these would display to him an entirely different image of the world, and that he himself possesses a deep and rich life of the soul that appears only to a very small extent in self-consciousness—cf. my *Beginselen der Psychologie*, 70f., 78f.—then within the bounds of the *Diesseits* such a vast domain still lies open for occultism that for the present we do not need to take refuge in an influence of the spirit world. Cf. Kirchner, *Der Spiritismus, die Narrheit unseres Zeitalters*, Berlin: Habel, 1883; Ed. v. Hartmann, *Der Spiritismus*, Leipzig: Friedrich, 1885; idem, *Die Geisterhypothese des Spiritismus und seine Phantome*, ibid., 1891. Furthermore, 4º the whole of Scripture proceeds from the thought that death is a total break with life on this side of the grave. The dead do indeed retain memory of what happened to them here on earth. The rich man and poor Lazarus know who and what they were here and in what surroundings they lived, Luke 16. At the final judgment human beings are conscious of what they did on earth, Matt. 7:22. Their works follow those who have died in the Lord, Rev. 14:13. What we have done here on earth becomes our moral possession and accompanies us into death. There is also no doubt that the dead recognize those whom they knew on earth. The subterranean inhabitants mockingly greet the king of Babylon, Isa. 14; the mighty heroes address Egypt’s ruler and people from the midst of Sheol, Ezek. 32; the rich man knows Lazarus, Luke 16. The friends whom we acquire here by doing good will one day receive us with joy into the eternal tabernacles, Luke 16:9. But otherwise Scripture always represents the communion with this earth as totally broken at death. The dead have no further part in all that happens under the sun, Eccles. 9:5, 6, 10. Whether their children come to honor or fall into poverty, they do not know, Job 14:21. Abraham does not know the children of Israel and Jacob does not acknowledge them, and therefore they cry out to the Lord, who is indeed their Father, Isa. 63:16. Nowhere is there mention of intercourse of the dead with the living; they belong to another realm, which is totally separated from the earth. Nor does Heb. 12:1 teach that the cloud of witnesses sees and observes us in our struggle. For the μαρτυρες there are not eyewitnesses of our struggle but witnesses of faith who serve for our encouragement. 5º Therefore there is also no place for the invocation and veneration of the saints. In itself there is nothing strange or improper in the thought that angels and the blessed intercede for human beings on earth; an interest in the history of the church militant, a general intercession, was also repeatedly accepted by Protestants. But it is all the more remarkable that Scripture, which so frequently mentions the intercession of human beings on earth and specifically recommends and prescribes it, Matt. 6:9f.; Rom. 15:30; Eph. 6:18, 19; Col. 1:2, 3; 1 Tim. 2:2, and moreover teaches that God frequently spares others for the sake of the elect and at their intercession, Gen. 18:23f.; Ex. 32:11f.; Num. 14:13f.; Ezek. 14:14, 20; Matt. 24:22; etc., never speaks a single word about an intercession of the angels and the blessed for those living on earth. With respect to the intercession of angels, this was demonstrated earlier, vol. II 449f., and of the intercession of the blessed the Roman Catholics themselves concede that it does not occur in Scripture; cf., for example, Oswald, *Eschatologie*², 132. Only 2 Macc. 15:12–14 mentions an intercession by Onias and Jeremiah for their people in a dream vision to Judas and proves only that the Jews at that time were convinced of the intercession of the blessed dead. 6º There is still less ground for the invocation and veneration of the saints. Holy Scripture indeed says that believers on earth may request one another’s intercession, Num. 21:7; Jer. 42:2; 1 Thess. 5:25, but nowhere speaks of a request to the dead for their intercession. And angels and human beings expressly repel from themselves all religious veneration, which belongs to God alone, Deut. 6:13; 10:20; Matt. 4:10; Acts 14:14; Col. 2:18, 19; Rev. 19:10; 22:9; cf. vol. II 451f. Nor is there mention of the veneration of relics. Although God sometimes performs miracles through them, 2 Kings 13:21; Matt. 9:21; Luke 6:19; Acts 5:15; 19:12, they may not be objects of veneration, Deut. 34:6; 2 Kings 18:4; 2 Cor. 5:16. Oswald, op. cit., 143, therefore also reckons the invocation and veneration of the saints among the *Traditionsdogmen*. Even if a general intercession of the saints for believers on earth is conceded, it by no means follows that they may be invoked and venerated for this purpose. For a request for someone’s intercession is indeed in itself by no means unlawful and repeatedly takes place among believers. But such a request always presupposes some means of communication and must be capable of being conveyed orally or in writing. And precisely that is lacking here and is also in direct conflict with the teaching of Scripture concerning the condition of the dead. Rome therefore does not dare to say that the invocation and veneration of the saints is commanded and necessary, but declares only *bonum atque utile esse, suppliciter eos invocare*, *Trid.*, sess. 25. Theology is altogether unable to make clear how the saints acquire knowledge of our prayers and advances all kinds of conjectures. Some think that the prayers are communicated to them by the angels, who repeatedly come here to earth, or that the saints, like the angels, can move with miraculous speed and are *quodam modo ubique*. Others are of the opinion that the saints are informed of the content of our prayers by God himself, or behold in the divine consciousness all things that they need to know. And there are also some who say that it is not necessary for them to know everything, provided they have a wholly general knowledge of our needs; or that we need not concern ourselves with the manner in which they acquire knowledge of our prayers: Thomas, *S. Theol.* II 2, qu. 83, art. 4; *Suppl.*, qu. 72, art. 1; Oswald, *Eschatologie*², 139. To this is added that Roman Catholics by no means know with certainty which of the dead are in heaven and belong to the perfected saints. The pious of the Old Testament were at first in the *limbus patrum* and were indeed brought into heaven by Christ, but stand too far from us to be invoked much by us, Oswald, op. cit., 132, 167. Of some pious persons in the New Testament, such as Mary and the apostles, and also of the later martyrs, Rome indeed assumes that they have been received into heaven. But these are only a few, and error is not excluded here. In earlier times it was the voice of the people that ascribed to a deceased person the predicate of holiness; and it occurred that men who possessed this predicate lost it again, as, for example, Clement of Alexandria through Benedict XIV. To prevent these errors, therefore, since Alexander III and Innocent III the ecclesiastical declaration of someone’s holiness, that is, canonization, became a right of the apostolic see. Yet here again it is a question whether the pope is infallible in this canonization or not. And even if this may be the case, by its nature the pope makes sparing use of it. By far the most saints are invoked and venerated without it being accurately known whether they abide in heaven or in purgatory. One must therefore be content with a moral conviction, further remember that a possible error entails no harmful consequences, and for safety’s sake simply extend the invocation also to the “poor souls” in purgatory, as increasingly occurs in practice, Oswald, op. cit., 148, 164. 7º At Rome the invocation of the saints is by no means any longer merely a request for their intercession (*ora pro nobis*), but has gradually passed over into an adoration and veneration. The saints are objects of a *cultus religiosus*, even though this is called not *latria* but *dulia*. There is no doubt that we would have to show respectful homage to the angels and the blessed if we met them and had some intercourse with them, vol. II 455. But precisely the latter does not occur. And therefore every invocation of angels and the blessed issues in a religious veneration that is not justified by the name *dulia*, ibid., 451f. On the path Rome treads with this veneration of the creature, there is no stopping. Holiness is conceived by Rome as a *donum superadditum*, as something substantial that can be communicated to all creatures in different degrees and in the same degree may then be religiously venerated. Insofar as a person or thing shares in divine holiness, it is entitled to a *cultus religiosus*. First of all, therefore, Mary, the apostles, the martyrs, and the saints share in it, but further all persons and things that have come into contact with these or still stand in relation to them, and thus also relics, images, dwelling places, and so forth. According to this principle every creature can be religiously venerated, *quod et quatenus respectum habeat ad Deum*, even down to the hands of the soldiers who seized Jesus and the lips of Judas that kissed him: Voetius, *Disp.* III 880, 896. In any event, it is impossible to see why the saints who are on earth are not already invoked and venerated by Roman Catholic Christians, and among them especially the pope, who is the saint par excellence. *Es spricht an sich nichts dagegen, dass auch die Heiligkeit auf Erden religiös veneriert werde. Wäre man also von der Gottseligkeit einer Person vollkommen überzeugt, so dürfte sie an sich wohl eine Verehrung geniessen, wie sie den Heiligen im Himmel zukommt. In einzelnen Fällen mag es privatim geschehen sein und geschieht vielleicht noch*, Oswald, op. cit., 157. What Oswald still adduces against it is derived from utility and shows that at Rome the veneration of living saints, specifically of the pope, is only a question of time. The communion of saints degenerates into a mutual veneration that pushes the mediator of God and human beings into the background.
