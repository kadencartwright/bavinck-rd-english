## 2.

The proofs for the immortality of the soul derived from history and reason give no conclusive certainty but are nevertheless not without significance. In the first place it is already significant that belief in immortality occurs among all peoples, at every stage of development. The *consensus gentium* here is as strong as with belief in God, Cicero, *Tusc.* I 3. The various considerations from which belief in immortality has been derived—such as fear of death and thirst for life, experiences of dream and ecstasy, the enigma of death and the impossibility of imagining an absolute annihilation of the human being’s thinking essence, fear of punishment and hope of reward; cf. Herzog² 16, 206–211—can indeed support and confirm belief in immortality *a posteriori*, but they give no satisfactory explanation of its origin. Even where such considerations are lacking or regarded as worthless, belief in immortality nevertheless occurs. Among many people the desire to continue existing is frequently weaker than the desire that existence might end with death. Hope of reward does not explain the belief in those who have died to all selfishness and found the highest blessedness in communion with God. The thought of recompense is foreign to conceptions of continued existence as a shadowy life of shades. The enigma of death leads only in rare exceptions to conclusions concerning the immortality of animals and plants. And experiences of dream and ecstasy do not extinguish the awareness of the essential difference existing between these phenomena and dying. Rather, with this belief in the immortality of the soul, as with belief in the existence of God, we are dealing with a conviction obtained not from reflection and reasoning but preceding all reflection and arising spontaneously from human nature. It is self-evident and natural and is found everywhere that philosophical doubts have not undermined it. With consciousness of one’s own independent, individual existence awakens also that of personal continuance. Self-consciousness—not the abstract self-consciousness with which psychology deals, but the self-consciousness of the human being as a personal, independent, rational, moral, religious being—everywhere and always includes belief in immortality. Therefore this belief is no mere wish or desire, no conclusion from premises, but a powerful, ineradicable testimony of human nature itself that maintains itself against all reasoning and opposition. The so-called proofs for immortality are nothing other than different attempts made by this belief along the way of reasoning to give itself some thoughtful account of itself, without in reality ever depending upon or making itself dependent upon them. In this lies their strength and at the same time their weakness. They are testimonies to, not grounds for, the belief; knowledge remains far behind faith.

The *ontological* proof, which concludes from the idea of immortality to its truth, no more bridges the chasm separating thought from being than does the ontological proof for the existence of God, vol. II, 53. It formulates only the awareness that belief in immortality in the human being is no arbitrariness or accident but is given with his nature and in a moral sense is necessary for him. The human being does not derive the idea of immortality from the world around him, for this preaches nothing to him but transience and death; rather, it is pressed upon him by his own nature. Just as God does not leave himself without witness but speaks to us from all the works of his hands, so from his own being the conviction presses itself upon the human being that he does not perish like the animals of the field. And this is what the ontological proof intends to demonstrate. It does not cross the boundary from thought to being but gives expression to the universality, necessity, and priority of belief in immortality. The *metaphysical* proof goes a step further and concludes from the nature of the soul to its immortality. But it can do this and does it in different ways. One can point out that the soul, as the principle of life and identical with life, is invulnerable to death; or that, as shown by the unity of self-consciousness, it forms an indivisible, simple unity, lacks every composition, and is therefore susceptible to no dissolution; or that under all exchanges of matter and all changes of the body, again as shown by self-consciousness, it continually remains identical with itself and thus enjoys an independent existence and life apart from the body. Along these different ways one can then attempt to reach the conclusion that the soul is immortal. But very serious objections have been brought against this argument. Even though the soul is an active, living principle, it is never identical with life itself. God alone is life itself; he alone is immortal, 1 Tim. 6:16. If the soul continues to exist, this can happen only through God’s omnipresent and almighty power. The soul is a creature and thus limited, finite, relative, never entirely free from all passivity and composition, all change and alteration. Indeed, we see before our eyes that it changes, increases or decreases in knowledge and power, depends upon the body, and undergoes all kinds of influences. And the subjective unity and identity of the self by no means proves the objective unity and simplicity of the soul, or, if it did prove this, would also prove the immortality of plants or at least of animals, as this was accordingly accepted consistently by Leibniz, Bonnet, Bilderdijk, and others. But over against these objections stands the incontrovertible fact that life cannot be explained from mechanical metabolism and points back to a principle of its own. *Omne vivum ex vivo* is even today the last word of science. And what applies to life in general applies even more strongly to conscious life. The most primitive sensation is already separated by an unbridgeable chasm from every nerve vibration. With it we enter an altogether new, higher world that differs essentially from that of sensory, tangible, weighable, and measurable things. That life, and thus also conscious life, is bound to the sensory world and most closely united with it was known long ago and can truly not be called a discovery of more recent science. But that it has its cause in the sensory world has indeed been repeatedly asserted but so far proved by no one. The metaphysical proof retains its value insofar as it concludes from distinctive psychic phenomena to an independent spiritual principle distinct from matter. Yet the objection always remains that one can reason and conclude in the same way with plants or at least with animals and that their immortality is nevertheless implausible. Therefore the *anthropological* proof must next be added to the metaphysical, concluding from the distinctive character of the human being’s psychic life to a spiritual existence distinct from that of animals and plants. The soul of the animal, although also simple and independent over against the exchange of matter, is directed toward the sensory; it is confined within the finite; it lives in the present; it is so bound to the body that it cannot exist outside it. But the human being has not only sensation and perception but also understanding and reason. Through thought he rises above the sensory, material, finite world; he elevates himself to the ideal, the logical, to the true, good, and beautiful, which cannot be seen with the eyes and touched with the hands. He seeks an enduring, eternal happiness, a supreme good that this world cannot grant him, and through all this is a citizen and inhabitant of another, higher kingdom than that of nature. The rational, moral, religious consciousness of the human being indicates a psychic existence that rises above the visible world. What by virtue of its nature seeks the eternal must be destined for eternity. To this is further added the *moral* proof and the *proof from recompense*, which demonstrate the disharmony existing in this life between *ethos* and *physis* and from it conclude to another life in which the two are reconciled. Let no one object against this that the proof rests upon egoism and that virtue bears its reward and sin its punishment within itself, Spinoza, *Eth.* V 41, 42; Strauss, *Gl.* II 706f. For the godly of all ages knew well that God had to be served for himself and not for any reward; cf. also Calvin, *Inst.* III 2, 26; 16, 2. But they nevertheless maintained that if they hoped in Christ only in this life, they would be the most miserable of all human beings, 1 Cor. 15:17, 19, 30, 32. For the satisfaction of a selfish desire is by no means at stake here, but no less is involved than the dominion and triumph of justice. The question underlying the moral proof is this: in the end, will good or evil, God or Satan, Christ or Antichrist prevail? History gives no conclusive answer to this. From the standpoint of the *Diesseits*, no satisfactory explanation of the world is possible; then there is all too much ground for pessimistic despair. And therefore the sense of justice, which the righteous God himself planted deep in the human heart, demands that justice be restored at the end of the days, that there be harmony between virtue and happiness, sin and punishment, that truth eternally conquer falsehood and light darkness. Although it has rightly been said that *le néant fut toujours l’horizon des mauvaises consciences*, even those who have nothing good to hope for from a life after this life are convinced by their sense of justice of the necessity of this restoration of justice. If justice does not triumph in the end, then there is no justice. And if God does not finally prove to be the conqueror of Satan, life is not worth living. Not an egoistic wish but a deep sense of justice, thirst for harmony, longing for the perfect glorification of God, in whom holiness and blessedness are one, find expression in the moral proof. Even art prophesies such a future when it presents the ideal to us in visible form. All these proofs, and still more those derived from the human being’s capacity for perfection, his moral personality, the many uninhabited stars, spiritualistic apparitions, and so forth, are not proofs in the sense that they silence all contradiction, but they are nevertheless testimonies and indications that belief in immortality arises altogether naturally and spontaneously from human nature itself. Whoever denies and opposes it does violence to his own nature. *Der Gedanke an die Unsterblichkeit ist schon der erste Akt der Unsterblichkeit*, von Baer in Splittgerber, *Tod, Fortleben und Auferstehung*³, 1879, p. 93.
