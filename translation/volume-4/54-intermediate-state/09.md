## 9.

Others are of the opinion that after death souls receive a new corporeality and thereby can again enter into communion with the external world, above, p. 381. For this view appeal is made to the fact that no conception can be formed of the life and activity of the soul without a body, and further to those passages of Scripture that appear to ascribe a certain corporeality to the souls of the deceased. The inhabitants of the realm of the dead are described exactly as they appeared on earth. Samuel is represented as an old man clothed with a robe, 1 Sam. 28:14; the kings of the Gentiles sit upon thrones and go to meet the king of Babylon, Isa. 14:9; the Gentiles lie there as uncircumcised, Ezek. 31:18; 32:19f. Jesus still speaks of eyes, fingers, and tongue among the deceased, Luke 16:23, 24. Paul expects that if the earthly house of the tabernacle is destroyed, he will have a building from God and will be not unclothed but further clothed, 2 Cor. 5:1–4. And John saw a great multitude standing before the throne and the Lamb, clothed with long white garments and with palm branches in their hands, Rev. 6:11; 7:9. But 1º nothing can be inferred from this manner of speaking of Holy Scripture for the corporeality of souls after death. For it cannot speak of God and the angels, of souls in the realm of the dead, of joy in heaven and pain in hell otherwise than in human language, under images borrowed from earthly conditions and relations. But alongside this it nevertheless clearly and decisively declares that God is spirit and that the angels are spirits, and thereby supplies the rule according to which all these anthropomorphic manners of speaking must be understood. And it does the same in relation to the deceased. It cannot speak of them otherwise than as human beings of flesh and blood, but says alongside this that while their body rests in the grave they are souls, spirits, Eccles. 12:7; Ezek. 37:5; Luke 23:46; Acts 7:59; Heb. 12:23; 1 Pet. 3:19; Rev. 6:9; 20:4. We must hold to these clear statements. Whoever nevertheless ascribes a kind of body to souls must also come to conceive, with the theosophists, of God and the angels as in a certain sense corporeal. 2º The strongest passage speaking for a *Zwischenleiblichkeit* of souls is 2 Cor. 5:1–4. But upon sound interpretation this text too loses all its probative force. For there is no disagreement about the principal thought Paul expresses here. The apostle knows that when his earthly body is dissolved, he has a building from God. Yet he groans and is burdened in this body, because he dreads death, and would therefore prefer not to be unclothed from this body but to be clothed at once, according to both soul and body together, with the heavenly dwelling, so that what is mortal might be swallowed up by life. But although this is his dearest wish, he knows that after the breaking of this earthly body, even though he is unclothed from the body (the reading in v. 3, εἰ γε και ἐκδυσαμενοι, deserves preference, in my opinion, above εἰπερ or εἰ γε και ἐνδυσαμενοι), he will nevertheless not therefore be found naked but will dwell with the Lord, vv. 1, 3, 8. If this is the principal thought, however, then the dwelling from God cannot be understood as the resurrection body and still less as an intermediate body. For Paul desires precisely to be clothed with that dwelling without dying, while he retains the earthly body. The resurrection body does not stand alongside the earthly body and is not drawn over it, but comes forth from it by God’s word of power or, in those who remain alive, passes into it by transformation, 1 Cor. 15:42, 51. An intermediate body is mentioned even less, since Paul would then know no fewer than three bodies that would successively be put on one over the other. Holtzmann, *Neut. Theol.* II 199, therefore rightly says: *von einem Zwischenleib redet man am besten gar nicht mehr, Paulus kennt zwei, nicht drei* σωματα, which then, however, must not be set dualistically after and alongside each other. For this reason the οἰκοδομη ἐκ Θεου can be nothing other than heavenly glory conceived as a place and at the same time as a garment, the eternal light in which God himself dwells, 1 Tim. 6:16, which is from God, made without hands, from and in heaven, and into which believers are transferred at death or at the resurrection; cf. Col. 1:12; John 14:2; 17:24. Finally, 3º the corporeality ascribed to souls after death is a concept of which nothing definite can be conceived and concerning which opinions therefore also diverge very widely. From his trichotomist standpoint Delitzsch assumes that the soul fulfills this service of intermediate body for the spirit. For him the soul stands between spirit and matter; it is the life principle of the body derived from the spirit, the bodily external clothing of the spirit and yet again the immaterial inward side of the body. Güder teaches that the power which organized our earthly body is preserved and on the other side of the grave forms a new body from the elements present there. Splittgerber says that the organic basic form of the body accompanies the soul and in the intermediate state gives it an imperfect, provisional corporeality. Rinck is of the opinion that the *Nervenleib*, a subtle inward body that is the bearer of the life of the soul, accompanies the soul after death and in the regenerate is clothed by the Spirit of God and formed into a *Zwischenleib* through the irradiation of the glorified body of Christ, while in the wicked it becomes increasingly permeated by sin and darkness, and so forth. But whatever one says about it, it remains equally unclear. We know nothing other than spirit and matter. An *immaterielle Leiblichkeit* is a contradiction imported at an unfortunate hour from theosophy into Christian theology, and it attempts in vain to reconcile the false dualism of spirit and matter, thesis and antithesis, through an inconceivable synthesis; cf. vol. II, 193, 537.
