## 7.

The history of the intermediate state proves that it is difficult for the theologian and the human being in general to remain within the bounds of Holy Scripture and not to be wise above what one ought to be wise. The data Holy Scripture contains about the intermediate state are sufficient for life, but leave unanswered many questions that can arise in the curious understanding. If one nevertheless wishes to resolve them, one can do nothing but enter the path of conjecture and runs the danger of nullifying divine testimony by inventions of human wisdom. This is immediately evident already in speaking about death and immortality. Philosophy deals with these in an altogether different way than Holy Scripture. The former considers death something natural and believes that immortality—that is, the continued existence of the soul—is sufficient. But Scripture judges altogether differently. Death is not natural but has its cause in the transgression of God’s command, Gen. 2:17; in the devil, insofar as through his temptation he caused the human being to fall and die, John 8:44; in sin itself, since it works dissolvingly upon the whole of human life and as it were produces death from itself, James 1:15; and in God’s judgment, since he pays sin with death, Rom. 6:23. And in Scripture that death is never identical with annihilation, with nonbeing, but always consists in the breaking of harmony, in being cut off from the different relations of life in which a creature is placed according to its nature, in a return to the elemental, chaotic being underlying the whole cosmos, at least logically. According to Herbert Spencer, life consists in the continual adaptation of inward to outward relations, vol. I, 416. Although the essence of life is by no means explained by this definition, it is nevertheless true that life is richer as the relations in which it stands to its environment are more numerous and healthier in nature. Therefore the highest creature is the human being. By virtue of his creation he stands in relation to nature and the human world, visible and invisible things, heaven and earth, God and angels. And he lives if and insofar as he stands in the right, God-willed relation to this whole environment; cf. Drummond, *Das Naturgesetz in der Geisteswelt*, p. 121f. Death, therefore, in its essence and full scope is disturbance, the breaking of all these relations in which the human being originally stood and still ought to stand. Its cause therefore is and can be no other than sin—that is, disturbance of the right relation to and breaking of the communion of life with God. Sin not only has death in this sense as its consequence but coincides with it. Sin is death, death in the spiritual sense. Whoever commits sin stands at that same moment over against God, is dead to God and divine things, has no delight in the knowledge of his ways, and turns away from him in enmity and hatred. And because this relation to God, this being created in his image and likeness, is no *donum superadditum* but belongs to the human being’s essence and bears a central character, the disturbance of this relation must work destructively upon all other relations in which the human being stood to himself, his fellow human beings, nature, angels, and the whole creation. According to its nature sin ought properly to have had the full, complete death as its consequence at the same moment it was committed, Gen. 2:17, a return of the whole cosmos to its chaotic existence.

But God intervened and broke the power of sin and death. It is true that, as Schelling said, an irrational remainder underlies everything that exists. Everything left to itself passes over into dissolution. Nature that is not cultivated grows wild; the human being who is not educated degenerates; the people standing outside civilization becomes corrupted. By nature everything within and outside the human being stands in enmity over against everything else. Understanding and will, conscience and inclination, duty and desire, soul and body, the human being and his neighbor, the human, animal, and angelic worlds—all are at war with one another and exist in a state of dissolution and decline. Yet God intervened with his grace: first with his common grace to restrain the power of sin and death, then with his special grace to break and overcome them. Bodily death is not only postponed and a human existence and development made possible by all kinds of measures, but through his cross Christ in principle gains the victory over sin and death and brings life and immortality to light, Rom. 5:12f.; 1 Cor. 15:45; Heb. 2:14; 2 Tim. 1:10; Rev. 1:18; 20:14, so that whoever believes in him has eternal life and will never die, John 3:36; 5:24; 8:51, 52; 11:25. It is now this life and this immortality that stand in the foreground in Holy Scripture. Immortality in the philosophical sense, the continued existence of the soul after death, has subordinate value for it. Scripture does not deny it, but neither does it deliberately teach it, and was least of all given, as deism believed, to make this immortality known to us as one of the most important religious truths. For this truth is sufficiently known to the human being by nature. What Scripture had to teach us was this, that bare existence, mere being without more, is not yet life as befits and suits human beings. It is not so on this side, and much less on the other side, of the grave. Here on earth the life of the human being, even of the one who lacks communion with God, still stands in many relations and thereby receives some content and value. But when all this falls away and all these bonds are broken, life sinks back into a poor, empty, contentless, shadowy existence. The Old Testament ordinarily views the *Jenseits* from this side. Dying is an exit from this life, a breaking of *all* bonds with *this* world. In relation to life on this side, death is a nonbeing, a resting, a sleeping—in a word, a being completely dead to the whole rich, joyful life on earth. The dead no longer forever have any share in all that happens under the sun, Eccles. 9:5, 6. In the concept of Sheol, negation of this earthly life and activity stands in the foreground and forms, if not its only, nevertheless its principal component. Whether another life now takes the place of this entirely broken earthly life in Sheol and the deceased there enter into new relations on another side is expressed in the Old Testament only a few times, when the eye of faith of the godly penetrates through the shadows of death to eternal life in communion with God. At the standpoint of Old Testament revelation it was enough that the great thought was carried into human consciousness that true life is found only in communion with God. The anguish of hell as well as the joy of heaven remained shrouded in mist for the believer of the old day. Only when Christ had died and risen was life in incorruptibility brought to light. Christ did not acquire or reveal immortality in the philosophical sense, the continued existence of souls after death. Rather, he again filled the human life here and hereafter, emaciated and emptied by sin, with the positive content of communion with God, with peace and joy and blessedness. Death is no longer death for the one who is in Christ Jesus but a passage to eternal life, and the grave a consecrated resting place until the morning of the resurrection.
