## 13.

Although there is no place for the veneration of the saints or for intercession for the departed, there nevertheless is and remains an unbreakable communion between the church militant on earth and the church triumphant in heaven. When believers on earth became Christians, they came to the heavenly Jerusalem, which is above and is the mother of all; to the many thousands of angels who serve and praise God there; to the assembly of the firstborn, that is, of the saints of the Old Testament, who are enrolled in heaven and have received citizenship there; to the spirits of the righteous, that is, of the Christians who have already fallen asleep and have attained perfection, consummation; to Christ, the mediator of the New Testament; and to God, the judge of all creatures, Heb. 12:22–24. This communion does not imply that direct intercourse must exist between the members of the church militant and the church triumphant. For although such intercourse is also absent among different human beings and peoples in distinct times and places, humanity is nevertheless one organism, sprung from one blood. The personal association that every believer has here on earth is limited to a few persons, but nevertheless he is a member of the one holy, catholic, Christian church. The unity that binds all believers together, both the dead and the living, lies in Christ and, through him, in communion with the same Father, in possession of the same Spirit, and in participation in the same goods of the covenant. The love that remains even when faith and hope disappear keeps all believers united with Christ and with one another. And on our part that love expresses itself in this: that we remember with reverence the saints who have gone before us; that we speak worthily of them; that we imitate them in faith and good works and, spurred on by their example, run with patience the race set before us; that we feel ourselves to be one with them and live in the expectation of going to them; and that with them and all creatures we magnify the name of the Lord. Among the forms in which the communion of the church militant with the church triumphant manifests itself, the hope of reunion occupies a large place. Rationalism made dreadful abuse of it; it seemed as though the blessedness of heaven lay not in communion with Christ but in the sentimental enjoyment of one another’s presence. Nevertheless, there is a good and true thought in it. The hope of reunion beyond the grave is perfectly natural, genuinely human, and also in agreement with Holy Scripture. For Scripture teaches no bare immortality of shadowy souls but the eternal life of individual human beings. Regeneration does not erase individuality, personality, and character but sanctifies them and places them in the service of God’s name. The church is the new humanity, which bears within itself every kind of shade and distinction and manifests the richest diversity in unity. The joy of heaven therefore lies first of all in communion with Christ, but subsequently also in the communion of the blessed with one another. And just as this communion on earth, though always defective here, does not infringe upon believers’ communion with Christ but rather confirms and enriches it, so it is also in heaven. Paul’s highest desire was to depart and be with Christ, Phil. 1:23; 1 Thess. 4:17. But Jesus himself presents the joy of heaven under the image of a banquet where all recline with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, Matt. 8:11; cf. Luke 13:28. The hope of reunion is therefore not wrong in itself, provided only that it remains subordinate to the longing for communion with Christ. And, on the other hand, it is no absurd thought that the blessed in heaven long for the believers who are on earth. After all, they retain the memory of the persons and conditions they knew on earth, Luke 16:27–31. The souls under the altar cry out for vengeance for the blood that was shed, Rev. 6:10. The bride—that is, the entire church in heaven as well as on earth—prays for the coming of the Lord Jesus, Rev. 22:17. Although Scripture gives us no right to believe that the blessed in heaven know everything that happens here on earth, it is nevertheless probable that they know at least as much about the church militant on earth as it knows about them. And that little, added to the knowledge they possess from memory and that is perhaps repeatedly enlarged by communications from angels and the newly departed, is sufficient to make them think continually with interest about this earth and the mighty struggle being fought here. Added to this is the fact that the state of the blessed in heaven, however glorious, still bears a provisional character for various reasons. For at present they are only in heaven and confined to that heaven, and are not yet in possession of the earth, the inheritance of which has been promised to them along with that of heaven. Further, they are deprived of the body, and this bodiless existence is not, as dualism must suppose, a gain but a loss, not an increase but a diminution of being, since the body belongs to the essence of the human being. And finally, the part cannot be perfect without the whole; only in communion with all the saints is the fullness of Christ’s love known, Eph. 3:18; the one group of believers cannot attain consummation without the other, Heb. 11:40. Therefore, among the blessed in heaven there is still room for faith and hope, for longing and prayer, Rev. 6:10; 22:17. Like believers on earth, they reach out toward the return of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, and the restoration of all things. Only then is the end attained, 1 Cor. 15:24. This thought stands so much in the foreground in Scripture that the intermediate state contracts into a brief span of time that is not taken into account at all in the final judgment. Nowhere is it said that what the deceased perform in that intermediate state will also be judged on the last day before the judgment seat of Christ. The judgment concerns exclusively what was done in the body, whether good or evil, 2 Cor. 5:10; the *judicium universale* is to that extent identical with the *judicium particulare*. From this, however, it still cannot be inferred with Kliefoth, *Eschatologie*, 61–66, that souls after death live outside time and space and are deprived of all development or progress. For although there is certainly no development like that on earth, and still less can one think of a possible change for better or worse, nevertheless a true existence and life of souls without activity is impossible unless one regards them as sunk in unconscious sleep. For the dead remain finite and limited beings and cannot exist otherwise than in space and time. The dimensions of space and calculations of time are doubtless entirely different beyond the grave from what they are here on earth, where miles and hours are our standard. But the souls who dwell there do not become eternal and omnipresent like God; like the angels, vol. II, 438, they must have an *ubi definitivum*, cannot be in two places at once, and are always somewhere in a definite place—in paradise, in heaven, and so forth. Likewise, they are not raised above every form of time, that is, above every succession of moments, for they have a past that they remember, a present in which they live, and a future that they approach. The rich man knows that his brothers are still alive, Luke 16:28; the souls under the altar look forward to the day of vengeance, Rev. 6:10; the bride longs for the coming of Christ, Rev. 22:17; those who have come out of the tribulation serve God day and night, Rev. 7:15; and those who have worshiped the beast have no rest day or night, Rev. 14:11.

Now if souls exist in some form of space and time, they also cannot be conceived as without all activity. Jesus does indeed say that in the night of death no one can work, John 9:4, and heavenly blessedness is frequently presented in Scripture as rest, Heb. 4:9; Rev. 14:13. But just as it is not contradictory that God rested from his work of creation, Gen. 2:2, and yet always works, John 5:17, or that Christ had completed his work on earth, John 17:4, and yet prepares a place for his own in heaven, John 14:3, so neither does the one exclude the other: that believers rest from their labors and yet serve God in his temple. Their work on earth is finished, but for that reason they still have other works to do in heaven. Scripture teaches this clearly. Those who have fallen asleep in the Lord are with Jesus, Phil. 1:23, stand before the throne of God and of the Lamb, Rev. 7:9, 15, cry out and pray, praise and serve, Rev. 6:10; 7:10, 15; 22:17. Indeed, if they possess consciousness and know God, Christ, the angels, and one another, they thereby of themselves exercise activities of understanding and will, increase in knowledge, and are confirmed in love. If Paul can say that believers on earth, by beholding the glory of the Lord in the mirror of his word, are transformed into his image from glory to glory, 2 Cor. 3:18, how much more will that be the case when they are admitted into his immediate presence and see him face to face? There is no change of state; there is also no development in the earthly sense, not even sanctification as in the church militant, for holiness itself is the portion of all. But just as Adam before the fall and Christ as a human being, though perfectly holy, could nevertheless increase in grace and wisdom, so in heaven there is a continuing confirmation of state, an ever greater conformity to the image of the Son, a never-ending growth in the knowledge and love of God. And in this everyone has his own task and place. Roman Catholics assume that after death the saints of the Old Testament sojourned in the *limbus patrum* and were first delivered from it by Christ in his descent into hell; and at the same time they believe that children who die unbaptized are received neither into hell nor into heaven but into a separate *receptaculum*, the *limbus infantum*. But there is no ground in Scripture for either of these *receptacula*. It goes without saying that whoever loses sight of the unity of the covenant of grace and conceives of the benefits acquired by Christ as a new substance that did not previously exist must have the saints of the Old Testament wait in the *limbus patrum* for this acquisition and communication of Christ’s benefits. But whoever acknowledges the unity of the covenant and conceives of the benefits of Christ as God’s gracious favor, which in view of Christ could already be distributed before his suffering and death, has no need of a *limbus patrum*. The way to heavenly blessedness was the same under the Old as under the New Testament, although there is a difference in the light by which believers then and now walk; cf. vol. III, 196f., 211f. Likewise, beyond the grave there is no place for a *limbus infantum*. For children of the covenant, baptized or unbaptized, enter heaven when they die; and so little has been revealed to us about the fate of the others that we do best to refrain from a definite judgment; cf. B. B. Warfield, “The Development of the Doctrine of Infant Salvation,” in his *Two Studies in the History of Doctrine* (New York, 1897). Nevertheless, in the *limbus patrum* and *limbus infantum* lies this true thought: that there are different degrees both in the punishment of the wicked and in the blessedness of the saints. There is distinction of rank and activity in the world of angels. There is diversity among all creatures and it is richest among human beings. There is a difference of place and task in the church of Christ; here on earth every believer is given his own gift and assigned his own task. And at death each person’s works follow the one who falls asleep in the Lord. Doubtless this diversity is not erased in heaven but, on the contrary, is cleansed of all that is sinful and multiplied in the richest manner, Luke 19:17–19. Yet this difference in degree takes nothing away from the blessedness each enjoys according to his measure. For all dwell with the same Lord, 2 Cor. 5:8, are received into the same heaven, Rev. 7:9, enjoy the same rest, Heb. 4:9, and find their joy in the same service of God, Rev. 7:15.
