## 3.

However much value the indications offered by nature and history for belief in the immortality of the soul may have, Scripture adopts a position with respect to this doctrine that at first acquaintance can only cause surprise. The immortality of the soul appears to be of the greatest significance for religion and life, and Scripture never mentions it in so many words; it never announces it as a divine revelation or places it anywhere in the foreground, and much less does it ever make any attempt to demonstrate its truth or maintain it over against its opponents. It is therefore understandable that earlier and later many have claimed that the doctrine of the immortality of the soul did not occur at all in the Old Testament, or at least in its oldest books, and was first introduced into Israel from outside; cf. Oehler, article “Unsterblichkeit” in Herzog¹ 21, 409f.; Himpel, *Die Unsterblichkeitslehre der A. T.*, 1857; Atzberger, *Die christl. Eschatologie*, Freiburg, 1890, p. 17. Gradually, however, this view was abandoned, and today it is universally acknowledged that Israel, like all peoples, did indeed believe in continued existence after death. Stade, *Gesch. des Volkes Israel* I 387–427 and *Ueber die altt. Vorstellungen vom Zustande nach dem Tode*, 1877; Oort, “De doodenvereering bij de Israelieten,” *Theol. Tijdschr.*, 1881, pp. 358–363; and especially Fr. Schwally, *Das Leben nach dem Tode nach den Vorstellungen des alten Israel u. s. w.*, Giessen, 1892, have even sought to demonstrate that in ancient times the dead were venerated among Israel as among the other peoples and were therefore undoubtedly thought to exist. They derived their proofs for this from the ritual customary at a death, such as tearing garments and wearing mourning dress, covering face and head, laying aside ornaments, special treatment of the hair and self-mutilation, casting dust and ashes, not washing or anointing, fasting and holding a meal, taking up lamentations and bringing sacrifices, all of which customs could, they claimed, be explained only from an earlier veneration of the dead. But Schwally, op. cit., 75, must himself acknowledge, *dass in der Zeit, als Israel in die Geschichte eintritt, die animistische Naturreligion im Princip bereits überwunden ist*. And against his derivation of the mourning customs from an original animism, Joh. Frey, *Tod, Seelenglaube und Seelenkult im alten Israel*, Leipzig, 1898, raised such serious objections that the hypothesis of an original cult of the dead among Israel can be made plausible only by other and new proofs. Yet it is clear that in Israel there was a great distinction between the folk religion, which contained all kinds of superstitious and idolatrous elements, and the worship of YHWH advocated by Moses and his followers. Yahwism on the one hand opposed, prohibited, and eradicated that folk religion, but on the other hand also allowed various religious conceptions and customs that were not wrong in themselves quietly to continue, or adopted and sanctioned them; cf. Wildeboer, *Jahvedienst en volksreligie in Israel*, 1898. In his revelation to Israel God connected with the historical circumstances under which it lived; grace did not destroy nature but renewed and sanctified it. So it also went with the popular belief in continued existence after death. Already the custom of burial and the great significance attached to it is a proof of that belief. Cremation of bodies was not indigenous to Israel; it took place only after the execution of the death penalty, Gen. 38:24; Lev. 20:14; 21:9; Josh. 7:15, 25. Nothing can be inferred from 1 Sam. 31:12 and Amos 6:10, since the text may be corrupt or otherwise reports only isolated cases; and 2 Chron. 16:14; 21:19; Jer. 34:5 treat only the burning of aromatic spices at burial. Burial, however, was highly valued and is therefore repeatedly mentioned separately in the Old Testament. To remain unburied was a great disgrace, 1 Sam. 17:44, 46; 1 Kings 14:11, 13; 16:4; 2 Kings 9:10; Ps. 79:3; Eccles. 6:3; Isa. 14:19, 20; Jer. 7:33; 8:1; 9:22; 16:6; 25:33; Ezek. 29:5. A dead person no longer belongs in the land of the living; his unburied corpse arouses revulsion; shed blood cries out for vengeance, Gen. 4:10; 37:26; Job 16:18; Isa. 26:21; Ezek. 24:7, since the blood is the seat of the soul, Lev. 17:11; and therefore the dead must be covered, hidden, withdrawn from sight. Through death all souls enter the realm of the dead, Sheol, שׁאול, a word of uncertain derivation which according to some comes from שׁאל, to ask, demand, or also exact, bring to a decision, and according to others from שׁעל‏‎, ‎‏שׁול, to be slack, hang downward, sink: Delitzsch, *Neuer Comm. über die Genesis*, 444; Atzberger, *Christl. Eschat.*, 24. This Sheol is situated in the depths of the earth, so that one descends into it, Num. 16:30; Ps. 30:4, 10; 55:16; Isa. 38:18; it belongs to the lower places of the earth, Ps. 63:10; Ezek. 26:20; 31:14; 32:18; it lies still below the waters and the foundations of the mountains, Deut. 32:22; Job 26:5; Isa. 14:15, and is therefore repeatedly intensified by the attribute תַּחְתִּית, lowest, Deut. 32:22; Ps. 86:13; 88:7. Sheol therefore also stands in close connection with the grave or pit, קֶבֶר or בּוֹר. The two are not identical, for the dead who are not buried are nevertheless in Sheol, Gen. 37:33, 35; Num. 16:32; but just as body and soul form the one human being and are thought still to stand in some reciprocal relation after death, so grave and Sheol cannot be conceived apart from each other. Both belong to the lower places of the earth, are represented as the dwelling of the dead, and repeatedly alternate with one another. Sheol is the one great grave that encompasses all the graves of the dead, the realm of the dead, the underworld, and is therefore often wrongly translated as hell in our Dutch States Translation. For Sheol is the place where all the dead without exception come together, 1 Kings 2:2; Job 3:13f.; 30:23; Ps. 89:49; Isa. 14:9f.; Ezek. 32:18; Hab. 2:5, and from which return is possible only through a miracle, 1 Kings 17:22; 2 Kings 4:34; 13:21. The realm of the dead is, as it were, a city furnished with barred gates, Ps. 9:14; 107:18; Job 17:16 (“to the bars of the underworld my hope descends”); 38:17; Isa. 38:10; Matt. 16:18, and by its power, Ps. 49:16; 89:49; Hos. 13:14, holds all human beings captive as in a prison, Isa. 24:22. Sheol is an eternal house, Eccles. 12:5. The enemies of Israel who have been cast into it cannot rise again, Isa. 26:14. The one who descends into the grave does not come up again, Job 7:9, 10; 14:7–12; 16:22. This realm of the dead therefore stands directly over against the land of the living, Job 28:13; Prov. 15:24; Ezek. 26:20; 32:23f. The dead are indeed conceived as existing and living; they are frequently represented and described as they appeared here on earth and are therefore also recognized by one another and moved at meeting one another, 1 Sam. 28:14; Isa. 14:9f.; Ezek. 32:18f. There is also mention of innermost chambers lying deep within Sheol, Prov. 7:27; Ezek. 32:23, and to this extent a distinction exists among the dead, since each one is gathered to his fathers, Gen. 15:15; Judg. 2:10, or to his people, Gen. 25:8, 17; 35:29; 49:29, and the uncircumcised are laid together, Ezek. 32:19. But otherwise Sheol is always described from its negative side, in contrast to the earth as the land of the living. It is the region of darkness and the shadow of death, Job 10:21, 22; Ps. 88:13; 143:3; the place of destruction, indeed destruction itself, אֲבַדּוֹן, Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Ps. 88:12; Prov. 27:20; without order, that is, without fixed contours and clear distinctions, Job 10:22; a land of rest, silence, and forgetfulness, Job 3:13, 17, 18; Ps. 115:17, where God and human beings are no longer seen, Isa. 38:11; God is no longer praised and thanked, Ps. 6:6; 115:17; his virtues are no longer proclaimed, Ps. 88:6, 12, 13; Isa. 38:18, 19; and his wonders are no longer beheld, Ps. 88:11, 13; where the dead know nothing at all, perform no more work, make no more calculations, possess no more wisdom or knowledge, and have no part whatever in all that happens under the sun, Eccles. 9:5, 6, 10. They are רְפָאִים, from the adjective רָפֶה, slack, Job 26:5; Prov. 2:18; 9:18; 21:16; Ps. 88:11; Isa. 14:9; weakened, Isa. 14:10; without strength, Ps. 88:5.

This whole conception of Sheol was formed from the standpoint of this earthly existence and applies only in contrast to the wealth of life the human being enjoys here on earth. Death is then indeed a breaking of all earthly bonds, a being dead to the rich life on earth, a resting, sleeping, being still, a nonbeing in relation to the things on this side of the grave. The condition in Sheol is not an annihilation of existence but nevertheless a terrible diminution of life, a deprivation of all that constitutes the joy of life in this life. There is no place in the Old Testament for a conception that allows only the body to die and consoles itself with the immortality of the soul. The whole human being dies when at death the spirit, Ps. 146:4; Eccles. 12:7, or the soul, Gen. 35:18; 2 Sam. 1:9; 1 Kings 17:21; Jonah 4:3, goes out from the human being. Not only his body but also his soul is in the state of death and belongs to the underworld; therefore one can also speak of a dying of the soul, Gen. 37:21; Num. 23:10; Deut. 22:20; Judg. 16:30; Job 36:14; Ps. 78:50, and of defilement through contact with the soul of a dead person, that is, with a corpse, Lev. 19:28; 21:11; 22:4; Num. 5:2; 6:6; 9:6, 7, 10; Deut. 14:1; Hag. 2:13. Just as the whole human being was destined for life in the way of obedience, so through his transgression he also falls entirely, both soul and body, under death, Gen. 2:17. This thought had to be deeply impressed upon the consciousness of humanity; and in antiquity all peoples also realized that death is a punishment, that it is something contrary to nature, in conflict with the essence and destiny of the human being. The revelation God gave to Israel therefore also connects with this; it allows this thought to remain and takes it over, just as it takes over so many customs and ceremonies (sacrifice, priesthood, circumcision, and so forth). It only cleanses it of the impure elements that gradually became connected with it among the peoples, such as self-mutilation, Lev. 19:28; 21:5; Deut. 14:1, and consulting the dead, Lev. 19:31; 20:6, 27; Deut. 18:10, 11. But revelation does something else and something more. It not only maintains and strengthens the antithesis that exists between life and death, but introduces into this life itself a still sharper antithesis. For this life is not true life, because it is a sinful and unclean life, afflicted by suffering and destined for death. It first becomes life in the true sense and first acquires an essential content of life through the service of YHWH and in communion with God. Entirely in agreement with the dispensation of the covenant of grace at that time and with the election of Israel as the people of God, the Old Testament conceives the connection between piety and life in such a way that the former receives its fruit and reward in a long life on earth, Ex. 20:12; Deut. 5:16, 29; 6:2; 11:9; 22:7; 30:16; 32:47; etc. Into the universally known, natural antithesis of life and death another moral, spiritual antithesis weaves itself, namely that between a life in the service of sin and a life in the fear of the Lord. Death is connected with evil, life with good, Deut. 30:15. Those who have wanted forcibly to find the philosophical doctrine of the immortality of the soul in the Old Testament have not understood the revelation of God to Israel and have read Western, rationalistic ideas into the religion of the Eastern people. Pfleiderer, *Religionsphilos.*, 626, says much more truly: *was man oft für eine Schwäche der prophetischen Jahvereligion Israels gehalten hat* (namely, that the hereafter occupies so small a place in it), *ist in Wahrheit ihre auszeichnende Stärke gewesen; der lebendige Gott, der in geschichtlichen Thaten sich offenbart, hat nichts gemein mit den Schatten des Scheol*. The God of Israel is not a God of the dead but of the living. Therefore, the expectation of pious Israel was directed almost exclusively toward the earthly future of the people, toward the realization of the kingdom of God. The question concerning the future of individual persons in Sheol receded entirely into the background. God, people, and land were inseparably connected with one another, and the individuals were incorporated into that covenant and reckoned accordingly. Only when Israel after the exile becomes a religious congregation and religion becomes individualized does the question of each person’s future lot press itself into the foreground. The spiritual antithesis that revelation had woven into the natural one continued to work; the distinction between righteous and wicked increasingly replaced that between Israel and the nations and was continued also on the other side of the grave. The data for this were, indeed, already present in the revelation of earlier times. The human being who serves God continues to live, Gen. 2:17; life is connected with keeping his commandments, Lev. 18:5; Deut. 30:20; his Word is life, Deut. 8:3; 32:47. In Proverbs, life is indeed frequently understood as length of days, 3:2; 3:16; 10:30, but it is nevertheless remarkable that it usually brings death and Sheol into connection only with the wicked, 2:18; 5:5; 7:27; 9:18, and in contrast ascribes life almost exclusively to the righteous. Wisdom, righteousness, and the fear of the Lord are the way to life, 8:35, 36; 11:19; 12:28; 13:14; 14:27; 19:23. The wicked person is overthrown when calamity befalls him, but even in his death the righteous person still retains trust and comfort, 14:32. Blessed is the one who has YHWH as his God, Deut. 33:29; Ps. 1:1; 2:12; 32:1, 2; 33:12; 34:9; etc., even in the severest adversity, Ps. 73:25–28; Hab. 3:17–19. By contrast, the wicked perish and come to an end even though they temporarily enjoy so much prosperity, Ps. 73:18–20. From this standpoint the pious expect not only deliverance from oppression and adversity in time, but with the eye of faith also repeatedly penetrate to the other side of the grave and expect a blessed life in communion with God. The passages ordinarily adduced for this—Gen. 49:18; Job 14:13–15; 16:16–21; 19:25–27; Ps. 16:9–11; 17:15; 49:16; 73:23–26; 139:18—are of uncertain interpretation and according to many refer only to temporal deliverance from death. But even if this were the case, the entire Old Testament teaches that God is Creator of heaven and earth, that his power knows no bounds, and that he also possesses absolute dominion over life and death. It is God the Lord who gave the human being life, Gen. 1:26; 2:7, and who still creates and sustains every human being, as everything that exists, Job 32:8; 33:4; 34:14; Ps. 104:29; Eccles. 12:7. He sovereignly connects life with his law and appoints death as the penalty for its transgression, Gen. 2:17; Lev. 18:5; Deut. 30:20; 32:47. He dwells in heaven but is also present by his Spirit in Sheol, Ps. 139:7, 8. Sheol and Abaddon lie naked and open before the Lord, just like the hearts of the children of men, Job 26:6; 38:17; Prov. 15:11. The Lord kills, preserves alive, and makes alive; brings down into Sheol and brings up from it again, Deut. 32:39; 1 Sam. 2:6; 2 Kings 5:7. He has ways of escape from death, can deliver when death already threatens, Ps. 68:21; Isa. 38:5; Jer. 15:20; Dan. 3:26; etc.; can take Enoch and Elijah to himself without death, Gen. 5:24; 2 Kings 2:11; and can cause the dead to return to life, 1 Kings 17:22; 2 Kings 4:34; 13:21. He can nullify death and triumph completely over its power through the raising of the dead, Job 14:13–15; 19:25–27; Hos. 6:2; 13:14; Isa. 25:8; 26:19; Ezek. 37:11, 12; Dan. 12:2.
