## 12.

The state of deceased believers who have not yet attained perfect holiness here on earth is conceived by Rome as a cleansing of souls through the punishment of fire. The idea of such a state of purification is pagan in origin and occurred especially in two forms. The doctrine of the transmigration of souls, which is found among the Indians, Egyptians, Greeks, Jews, and others, holds that before the soul entered the human body it had already dwelt in other bodies and that after leaving the human body it enters new organisms in order to purify itself and finally attain perfection. This doctrine, however, conflicts too greatly with Scripture ever to have gained assent within the bounds of Christianity except among a few sects and individual persons. For it proceeds from the idea that souls preexist, originally possessed no body, and are indifferent to all bodies. Further, it conflicts with the doctrine of the redemption accomplished by Christ and regards purification and perfection as the human being’s own work. And finally, it does not make clear at all how souls, by repeatedly passing into other bodies, could be freed from sins and led to holiness; cf. M. Vitringa IV 87–97; Moor II 1081; Bretschneider, *Syst. Entw.*, 846; Spiess, *Entwicklungsgesch. der Vorst. v. Zustande nach dem Tode*, 31, 558; J. F. von Meyer, *Blätter für höhere Wahrheit*, 1830, I 244–299; and so forth. The other conception exercised more influence upon Christian theology: after death souls must still be cleansed for a time by all kinds of punishments before they can obtain a share in the highest blessedness. In Parsism we find the belief that after the general resurrection there follows a three-day purification in molten metal, which is mild for the good but very painful for the wicked, Saussaye, *Rel. Gesch.* II 51. The Jews taught that only the perfectly righteous went immediately to heaven; the others were sent to Gehinnom, which according to some was a purgatory for all human beings but in any case for the Jews, Weber, *Syst.*, 327. Since Origen this conception also penetrated among Christians and there led to the doctrine of the Roman purgatory or of a period of purification accepted by many Protestants; see above, p. 381. At first, this idea has much appeal. For at the moment of their death all believers are still tainted with sin; even the holiest possess only a small beginning of perfect obedience. Further, this sin that clings to believers resides not in the body but in the soul, which therefore cannot enter heaven unless it has first been freed not only from the guilt of sin but also completely cleansed from its stain. It is difficult to conceive how this cleansing could suddenly be brought about through or at death. For not only does sanctification proceed slowly in this life, but in every sphere we see no sudden transitions, only gradual growth and development. Everything therefore favors the view that the souls of believers must undergo a purification after death and only thereafter be received into heaven and admitted to the vision of God.

But whatever human reasoning may commend such a purgatory, the first and in itself already decisive objection is that Scripture nowhere speaks of it. Roman Catholics do indeed cite some texts, but not one proves what it is called upon to prove. Matt. 5:22 says not a word about a purgatory but does speak of Gehenna; cf. vol. III, 99. To think of purgatory in connection with the φυλακη in Matt. 5:25 is arbitrary. Rather, it is an image of Gehenna, for whoever enters it has first been condemned by the judge and never again has an opportunity to pay the debt and leave the prison; the ἑως in v. 26 indicates an unattainable terminus; cf. 18:30, 34. In Matt. 12:32 Jesus says that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit will be forgiven neither in this age nor in the age to come. The words “nor in the age to come” serve only to reinforce the unforgivability of blasphemy against the Holy Spirit and therefore by no means need to presuppose that some sins can still be forgiven after this life. But even if this were so, the text would still prove nothing for the doctrine of purgatory, for here the forgiveness of sin is in view, whereas purgatory is precisely not a place for forgiveness but only a place for satisfying temporal punishments; and the text speaks of forgiveness in the age to come, that is, the time after the parousia, while purgatory belongs to the time before that parousia and ceases to exist at the last judgment. According to 1 Cor. 3:12–15, the work of the ministers of the church will have to endure the test on the day of Christ’s parousia. The one who has built gold, silver, and precious stones upon the foundation of Christ—that is, the one who has performed good, sound work in his office and ministry—will indeed be tested in his work, but because that work proves able to withstand the fire of judgment, he will receive a reward. The one, by contrast, who has built wood, straw, and stubble upon the foundation, which cannot withstand the fire, will be punished and lose his reward, even though he himself is saved through the fire of judgment. Thus there is indeed mention here of an *ignis revelatorius*, v. 13, an *ignis probatorius*, v. 13, and an *ignis exarsorius*, v. 15. But Paul places the fire of judgment at Christ’s coming and therefore leaves no room precisely for a purgatory that would now cleanse believers and end before the last judgment. There are no other texts to which Rome can appeal for the doctrine of purgatory even with some semblance of right. Only one passage in the Apocryphal books of the Old Testament, namely, 2 Macc. 12:42–46, teaches that the Jews of that time considered offerings and prayers for those who had died in sins necessary and good, something that is, moreover, also known from elsewhere. But it deserves all the more attention that this popular belief existing among the Jews is neither mentioned nor, much less, approved in either the Old or the New Testament. 2º The doctrine of purgatory is most intimately connected with that of justification. Rome understands justification as the infusion of supernatural, sanctifying grace, which then enables the human being to do good works and thereby merit eternal life. This grace, however, is capable of increase and decrease. Whoever loses it through mortal sin and then dies is lost. Whoever at the hour of death has brought it to perfection by keeping the *praecepta* and *consilia* immediately enters heaven. But whoever still has to make satisfaction for the guilt and temporal punishment of a venial sin, or whoever, after recovering in penance the *gratia infusa* lost through a mortal sin, is nevertheless still in arrears at death in paying the temporal punishments, is sent to purgatory and remains there until he has paid the last penny. At Rome justification, sanctification, and glorification are the work of the human being himself, even though on the basis of supernatural grace infused into him. Once he has received this grace, he must make himself worthy of eternal life and the blessed vision of God in heaven according to a *meritum ex condigno*; and if he does not advance so far here on earth, then, just as the pagans imagined, he must continue the work begun in the hereafter until he has attained perfection. But from Scripture the Reformation once again came to know the justification of the sinner through faith and therefore had to reject the purifying fire. Christ accomplished everything: he not only bore the punishment but also by his keeping of the law acquired eternal life for us. And all those benefits that Christ acquired through his suffering and death and that lie fully and perfectly prepared in him immediately become a person’s portion when he truly believes. Whoever believes *has* eternal life. In justification the human being is credited not only with the merit of Christ’s passive obedience but also with that of his active obedience. In that benefit he receives not only forgiveness and freedom from punishment. Nor is he thereby merely restored to the state of Adam before the fall, who had to keep the law and merit eternal life with the power granted him. Rather, on the ground of Christ’s perfect obedience he also immediately has a right to eternal life. The holy works Christ accomplished are imputed to him. He does not have to merit eternal life by fulfilling the law but does good works from the principle of eternal life already granted him in faith. Sanctification here is therefore no self-preparation for heaven, no self-perfection, but nothing other than the unfolding in the believer of what he already has in Christ, a walking in the good works God prepared in Christ, Eph. 2:10. God therefore does not need to wait for any further good works before he can receive the believer into heaven, for in Christ that heaven is immediately opened to everyone who believes. Whoever believes has forgiveness and eternal life, is “ripe” for heaven, and needs to pass through no purgatory either here or hereafter. Even the suffering that he often still has to bear here on earth, even as a result of sin, is no punishment, penance, or payment of a demand of the law, but a fatherly chastisement that serves for his training. 3º The only question from the standpoint of the Reformation is therefore this: when does the believer enter into full possession of the benefits of Christ granted to him? Whoever believes immediately receives these in the juridical sense. In Christ he has a right to all the goods of the covenant, to the whole of salvation. But while on earth he does not yet enter into full possession of this salvation. When, then, does this take place? When does the believer cease to be a pilgrim and arrive in the fatherland? To this Scripture has only one answer: at death. Nowhere does it represent the godly as still being tormented after death by punishment or suffering for sin. The godly always express their certain expectation that death marks the end of their pilgrimage and their entrance into the eternal, blessed life of heaven, Ps. 73:23, 24; Luke 23:43; Acts 7:59; 2 Cor. 5:1; Phil. 1:23; 2 Tim. 4:7. After death there is no more sanctification, but a state of holiness begins in which the spirits of the perfected righteous, Heb. 12:23, are clothed in long, white robes and stand before the throne and before the Lamb, Rev. 7:9, 14. It is related of the modest de Sacy of Port-Royal that he always stood in the fear of God and therefore did not dare to hope for immediate blessedness after death but, dying, cried out, *o bienheureux purgatoire!* But such a state of mind is entirely foreign to the godly of the Old and New Covenants and can be explained only by the fact that someone who looks to himself fails to look to the finished work of Christ. 4º The way in which the state of holiness begins immediately at the death of the believer can of course neither be comprehended nor clearly described. Regeneration and sanctification, which the Holy Spirit works here on earth, are also mysteries. But without doubt death serves as a means in this. Not in the sense of Platonic dualism, as though liberation from the body were by itself already the sanctification of the soul, for sin has its seat precisely in the soul. Nor in the sense of sentimental rationalism, which treats death as a messenger of peace and has the human being become an angel through it, for death in itself is a revelation of God’s wrath and the wages of sin. Death does serve as a means, however, in the sense of Holy Scripture, which sees in the believer’s death a dying to sin. For every chastisement serves for the benefit of believers, so that they may partake of divine holiness, Heb. 12:10. Whoever, like Christ, suffers in the flesh on account of sin ceases from sin, 1 Pet. 4:1. But this applies especially to death. Ethical death—that is, dying to sin in fellowship with Christ—results in a person’s being justified from sin and dead to sin and henceforth living to God in Christ, Rom. 6:6–11; 8:10; 1 Pet. 2:24. And this ethical death is completed in physical death, Rom. 7:24; 2 Cor. 5:1; Phil. 1:21, 23. Death is a tremendous change, a breaking of all ties with this earthly life and an entrance into a new world with wholly different relations and conditions. There is nothing strange in the fact that God uses death, as he does all suffering, to sanctify the believer’s soul and cleanse it from every stain of sin. The objection that such a sanctification is mechanical and occurs in a leap does not hold, for death is the greatest leap a person can make, a sudden transfer of the believer into Christ’s presence and thereby a complete decay of the outer person and a total renewal of the inner person. 5º In addition, the doctrine of purgatory does not make this sanctification of the believer any more comprehensible. For first, Roman theology too must assign to death a critical significance similar to that assigned by Protestant theology. Purgatory is not a place where sins are still forgiven but only a place where remaining temporal punishments can be paid. Whoever, therefore, has committed *peccata venialia* and received no forgiveness for them in this life must receive that forgiveness at death. And so the Roman theologians too teach that the soul dying in venial sins immediately receives forgiveness of guilt in death, in order then to satisfy in purgatory the temporal punishments appointed for them, Oswald, *Eschat.*² 87. Next, it is impossible to see how purgatory effects the sanctification of souls. Apart from the fact that purgatory is usually described by Roman Catholics as a material fire that can therefore operate upon the soul only *idealiter*, the question naturally arises how pain by itself can sanctify. This would indeed be possible if repentance, humiliation, conversion, faith, love, and so forth might be worked in the soul through that torment. But this may not be assumed from the Roman standpoint. For purgatory is no *Missionsanstalt*, no institution for conversion, no school for sanctification, but a place of punishment where only temporal punishments are paid. Thus, on the one hand, the poor souls can no longer sin and incur new guilt, and, on the other hand, neither can they improve themselves, for every improvement includes merit at Rome, and in purgatory merit can no longer be earned. No good conception can therefore be formed of the state of the poor souls in purgatory. If they are still to be thought of as more or less stained by sin, then from the Roman standpoint it is incomprehensible that they cannot still sin and even completely lose again the grace they have received. If this is excluded, then the souls are in themselves pure and holy and have only some temporal punishments still to bear that they could not satisfy on earth, in which case it is again incomprehensible that perfectly righteous persons can still be temporarily excluded from heaven and handed over to the torment of purgatory. And in both cases it remains a riddle how purgatory can be an *ignis purgatorius*; it is nothing other than an *ignis vindicativus*. Oswald, loc. cit., 116, rightly says that this purifying character of purgatory belongs to the *quaestiones difficiliores*! Finally, there are still various questions that the doctrine of purgatory leaves unanswered. According to Roman belief, the godly of the Old Testament went to the *limbus patrum*. Is this limbus to be conceived as a purgatory, or was no purgatory necessary for them? And how are those purified who die a short time before the parousia and therefore can no longer enter purgatory, since it ceases to exist with the end of this world? The souls of those who died in earlier centuries fare much worse than those who enter purgatory later, for the possible duration of torment in purgatory becomes shorter and shorter. How can this be reconciled from the Roman standpoint with God’s justice and with the souls’ need for purification? If one says that as the end of the world approaches sanctification is shifted increasingly to the suffering of the present time and to death, then one undermines the doctrine of purgatory in a troubling way and approaches the standpoint the Reformation took over against this Roman doctrine. 6º If the doctrine of purgatory is untenable, then every offering and prayer for the departed naturally also falls away. Veneration of the dead through offerings and prayers was customary among the pagans. Intercession for the departed later came into use among the Jews, 2 Macc. 12:40–45, and has remained so to the present day, Schwally, *Das Leben nach dem Tode*, 188–190. In the Christian church the custom soon arose of wishing the dead *pax, lux, refrigerium* and commemorating them in prayers and at the celebration of the Supper. In the first period this took place with respect to all who had died in the Lord without distinction, and these *oblationes* and *sacrificia* were celebrated only *ob commemorationes eorum*, Cyprian, *Ep.* 12, 2; 39, 3; *Const. Ap.* VI 30; VIII 13, 41–44. But gradually a distinction was made between those souls who were immediately received into heaven and others who still had to remain in purgatory for a time. Communion with the former was then increasingly exercised through invocation and veneration, and communion with the latter through intercessions, good works, indulgences, and masses for souls; cf. *Trid.*, sess. 22, chs. 2, 3, and sess. 25; Bellarminus, *de purg.* II 15–18; Perrone, *Prael.* VI 289; VIII 29; Simar, *Dogm.*, 900; Jansen, *Prael.* III 1030; and so forth. In the Old Catholic sense—as a prayer to God that he might increase the blessedness of those who had fallen asleep in Christ and accept their prayers for the living, and at the same time as a commemoration of and exercise of communion with the departed—intercession for the dead was also approved by the Greeks, M. Vitringa VIII 509; many Anglicans, ibid., 515; the Lutherans, ibid. IV 79; Grotius, ibid., 80; and many more recent theologians: Frantz, *das Gebet für die Todten in seinem Zusammenhang mit Kultus und Lehre nach den Schriften des h. Augustinus*, Nordhausen: Büchting, 1857; K. M. Leibbrand, *Das Gebet für die Todten in der evang. Kirche zulässig und recht*, Stuttgart, 1864. But the Reformed rejected intercession for the departed because their lot was irrevocably decided at death, Suicerus, s.v. ταφη; Vossius, *Disp. de orationibus et oblationibus pro defunctis*, *Op.* VI 458 sq.; Rivetus in his polemic against Grotius, *Op.* III 962, 1026; Moor V 30–32. In neither the Old nor the New Testament is a word said about such intercession. The only passage to which one can appeal is 1 Cor. 15:29, where Paul mentions those who had themselves baptized ὑπερ νεκρων. It cannot be inferred from this, however, that such baptism was undergone by the living so that it might benefit the dead. For there is no evidence whatever that such a practice occurred in the time of Paul or later. Tertullian and others do report that this custom was found among the followers of Cerinthus and Marcion, but first, the accuracy of this report is by no means beyond all doubt, and second, it would follow that it was a heretical practice that never existed or gained acceptance in the Christian church. Whoever wished to let this text serve as proof of the right to pray for the dead would first have to begin by baptizing living persons for the benefit of the dead. But Paul also does not say that the living had themselves baptized for the dead so that this baptism might benefit the dead. Paul presents the dead as the motive why the living had themselves baptized. Because those who had fallen asleep in Christ would rise, living believers had themselves baptized on their account, for their sake. The apostle expresses no other thought than this: baptism presupposes faith in the resurrection of Christ and of believers; take away the resurrection and baptism becomes an empty ceremony. Intercession for the departed therefore finds not the least ground in Scripture, as Tertullian, moreover, already acknowledged. For after speaking in *cor. mil.*, ch. 3, of various ecclesiastical practices and among them also of offerings for the departed, he added in ch. 4: *harum et aliarum ejusmodi disciplinarum, si legem expostules scripturarum, nullam invenies; traditio tibi praetenditur auctrix, consuetudo confirmatrix, et fides observatrix*; cf. also Bellarminus, *de missa* II, ch. 7; Oswald, *Eschat.*, 95. Because there is no *praeceptum Patris*, one must content oneself with the *institutio matris*, that is, of the church, which thus once again comes to stand alongside and above God’s Word. Since, then, intercession for the departed cannot stand before Scripture, the question of its usefulness and comfort is no longer relevant. Yet these too are difficult to demonstrate. For although it appears beautiful that the living can help the departed through their intercessions and can make amends for ways they may have wronged them during their life, in reality this ecclesiastical practice directs Christian piety onto a wholly wrong path. Contrary to Matt. 8:22, it makes it appear as if care for the dead were of higher value than love for the living. It ascribes to one’s own works and prayers a meritorious, satisfactory power that exerts its effect even on the other side of the grave and can there benefit the dead. It is built upon and conducive to the doctrine of purgatory, which on the one hand, especially among the rich, nourishes carelessness and on the other perpetuates believers’ uncertainty. It weakens in Christian consciousness faith in the sufficiency of Christ’s offering and intercession. Cf. against purgatory: Calvin, *Inst.* III 5; Polanus, *Synt. Theol.* VII 25; Chamier, *Panstr. Cath.*, Tom. III, lib. 26; Amesius, *Bellarminus enervatus*, Tom. II, lib. 5; Voetius, *Disp.* II 1240; Forbesius a Corse, *Instruct. hist. theol.*, lib. XIII; Gerhard, *Loc.* XXVI 181 sq.; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 555; Kliefoth, *Eschatologie*, 82f.; Charles H. H. Wright, *The intermediate state and prayers for the dead, examined in the light of Scripture and of ancient Jewish and Christian literature*, London: Nisbet, 1900.
