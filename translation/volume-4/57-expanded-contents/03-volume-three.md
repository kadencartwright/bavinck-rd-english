# Volume III

## Chapter VI. On the World in Its Fallen State

**§ 35. Providence, pp. 1–34.** The work of creation passes over into the work of providence, which is clearly taught in Holy Scripture but is also known in part from nature, 1. Yet the Christian doctrine of providence differs from that of philosophy, even though it borrowed from philosophy a name not used in Holy Scripture. For *providentia*, carefully distinguished as *executio ordinis* from the *ratio ordinis* belonging to the counsel of God, is no *nuda praescientia* but an almighty and omnipresent act of God, 2, which avoids alike fate and chance, pantheism and deism, 3; as preservation maintains both the connection with and the distinction from creation, 4; as concurrence safeguards the creatures’ own nature with its *causae secundae* precisely through their absolute dependence on God, 5; and as government leads the whole creation, even through the fall into sin, to the end fixed by God, 6.

**§ 36. The Origin of Sin, pp. 34–72,** according to Holy Scripture lies not in God, although he created the possibility of sin, but in the voluntary transgression of God’s commandment, to which the human being in turn was enticed by the fallen angel, 1. Although such a fall is not wholly unknown outside special revelation, people have ordinarily tried to explain sin otherwise, either deistically from the will that always remains free and innocent, as Pelagius also did, 2, 3, or pantheistically from the nature of things—namely, from human sensuality, from the cosmos, or from God, 4. All these explanations, however, are unsatisfactory: those from the ever-free will and from sensuality, 5, as well as those from the world and from God, 6. God’s providence does indeed extend over sin, and not with a *nuda* but with an *efficax permissio*. But sin, in which the *materia* and the *forma* must be carefully distinguished, nevertheless does not have its *causa efficiens* in God, 7. He willed that it should exist, but he did not will it as sin or in the same manner as he wills the good and as human beings will it, 8. The origin of sin lies in the will of the creature, which was created mutably good and with the possibility of sinning and in an incomprehensible manner made that possibility pass into actuality, 9. This fall was not eternal and did not occur at the moment of creation, but, whether a longer or shorter time after creation, first among the angels and afterward among human beings, 10.

**§ 37. The Essence of Sin, pp. 72–104.** The first sin already enclosed all others within itself and disclosed its true nature, 1, which according to the names and description of sin in Holy Scripture consists in ἀνομια and thus has its standard in God’s law, 2. Accordingly, against all movements that saw in sin a substance or a necessary, transitory moment of development, the Christian church conceived it as a *privatio*, 3, which can therefore exist only in the good, must be regarded as a *deformitas*, and has the will as its proper subject, 4. Although one in essence, sins nevertheless differ in degree. There are demonic and human sins, 5, and the latter differ according as they are committed with more or less knowledge and intent, against God or a human being, and under aggravating or mitigating circumstances. They can therefore also be classified in different ways, but the Roman Catholic distinction between *peccata mortalia* and *venialia* must be rejected, 6, and blasphemy against the Holy Spirit bears a special character, 7.

**§ 38. The Spread of Sin, pp. 104–154.** Scripture and experience both teach that sin is altogether universal and thus extends over all human beings and, in each individual human being, over his entire nature, and does so by virtue of their descent from the first human being, 1. The Christian church built upon this the doctrine of original sin, which was denied by Pelagianism, 2, and weakened by Semi-Pelagianism, 3, but was especially developed and defended by Augustine and, after being misconstrued by Rome, 4, was recovered by the Reformation, 5. For a correct understanding, original sin must be distinguished into *peccatum originans* and *peccatum originatum*; the former is the imputation of Adam’s sin to all his descendants on the ground not only of their physical but also of their federal unity, 6. The latter is a consequence and in a certain sense a punishment of the former, consisting in total moral corruption, 7, which becomes the lot of all human beings not through imitation but through generation, as the more recent doctrine of heredity also acknowledges to a certain extent, 8, including Mary but not Christ, 9, and which in each individual has affected soul, spirit, body, and all faculties and powers and makes that person incapable of all spiritual good, 10.

**§ 39. The Punishment of Sin, pp. 155–186.** The punishment God threatened for the first sin has been postponed and modified through intervening grace. Yet it is nevertheless executed in part, even already on earth, for the restoration and maintenance of God’s justice, 1, and always consists in a certain suffering that is imposed upon the transgressor and makes him bow beneath justice, 2. It includes guilt, that is, liability to punishment, 3; pollution, that is, moral corruption, 4; suffering in body and soul, 5; death, that is, separation of soul and body, 6; and the dominion of Satan, 7.

## Chapter VII. On Christ

**§ 40. The Covenant of Grace, pp. 187–228.** Grace begins immediately after the fall, mingles even in the punishment for sin, and immediately assumes the character of a covenant, 1, which is then established more formally with Noah, Abraham, and Israel at Sinai and gradually comes to light ever more clearly as a divine disposition, as a διαθηκη, 2. This covenant idea passed from Scripture into theology and was defended there, in order to maintain the unity and the distinction between the Old and New Testaments, first against Gnosticism and Judaism, 3, and later, especially by the Reformed, against Anabaptism, and was brought to richer development, 4. This doctrine of the covenant of grace is therefore of the greatest significance for church and theology. It rests upon the *pactum salutis*, 5; is connected with the *foedus gratiae* in the broader sense, which was established with the whole fallen world, 6; maintains the unity and the distinction in the dispensation of grace under the Old and New Testaments, 7; is pure grace and rests secure in God alone, so that, although incorporating the broken covenant of works, it is nevertheless distinct from it and is also not identical with the *pactum salutis*, 8; maintains God’s sovereignty in the work of redemption and at the same time, although knowing no demands in the proper sense, does justice to the rational and moral nature of the human being; and finally allows election to realize itself through the generations, through the organism of humanity, 9.

**§ 41. The Person of the Mediator, pp. 228–302.** The covenant of grace has in Christ a mediator of reconciliation, who himself through his Spirit bore advance witness in Israel, with its institutions and offices, to his own person and work, 1, and then in the fullness of time appeared on earth, established the kingdom of heaven, and revealed himself as Son of God and Son of Man, both in an altogether unique sense, 2. Gathering the data of Scripture and avoiding Ebionitism and Gnosticism alike, the Christian church soon came to confess the personal unity of the two natures in Christ, 3, which was nevertheless conceived differently in the Greek and Roman Catholic churches and in the Lutheran and Reformed traditions, 4, and was opposed by all kinds of movements on the right and the left, especially also in more recent theology (Kant, Schleiermacher, Ritschl, and associates), 5. This is not surprising, because the person of Christ, though not the starting point, is nevertheless the center of Scripture, religion, and dogmatics. The incarnation, which *a priori* is unacceptable from a pantheistic and deistic standpoint, presupposes the Trinity and specifically eternal generation within it, for the person of the Son became human, 6. It further presupposes creation, even though it is not immediately given with creation and the doctrine of an incarnation apart from sin must be rejected, 7. Finally, it presupposes revelation in Israel, with the election and preparation of Mary as the mother of Jesus, even though her immaculate conception is not taught in Scripture, 8. Yet although Christ in his incarnation joins himself to the preceding revelation, he is no product of the past but existed personally from eternity and was and remained true God, 9, who assumed human nature through conception by the Holy Spirit and birth from Mary, 10, 11, and who therefore, in opposition to Docetism, was truly human and, in opposition to Apollinaris, fully human, 12, so that God and human being, two distinct natures, are united in him undivided and inseparable (against Nestorius), unchanged and unmixed (against Eutyches), 13. This union can therefore be conceived only as personal, as the union of the person of the Son with an impersonal human nature, 14, and necessarily entails the *communicatio idiomatum*, the *communicatio apotelesmatum*, and the *communicatio charismatum*, which was developed especially in Reformed theology in order to maintain the truly human nature of Christ, 15, and finally also the *honor adorationis*, 16.

**§ 42. The Work of the Mediator, pp. 302–424.** Among all peoples there are holy persons, prophets, kings, and especially priests, who through sacrifices bring about and maintain communion with God, 1. In Israel too a priesthood was chosen and sacrifices were prescribed by God that clearly had a typical significance and pointed to the true sacrifice of the servant of the Lord, who would be the true priest and at the same time the true prophet and king, 2. According to the New Testament, Jesus, the son of Mary, is this servant of the Lord, who in his threefold office accomplishes the great work of God: as prophet he proclaims the law and the gospel; as priest, through his self-surrender, which fulfills all the sacrifices of the Old Testament, he acquires the whole of salvation for his own; and as king he applies it to them, 3. This work of Christ was described in different ways in Christian theology, sometimes more as illumination or as the communication of new life, and then again as ransom from the power of Satan or as satisfaction of God’s justice. The last conception gained acceptance especially through Anselm and, although significantly modified, passed into Protestant theology, 4. But it was opposed not only by all kinds of mystical movements but also by many rationalistic ones, and especially by the Socinians, who advanced everything that could be said against the doctrine of satisfaction, 5. In more recent philosophy and theology it was therefore entirely rejected or reconstructed in a mystical or ethical sense, 6. Jesus is already designated by his very names as the mediator between God and human beings, a mediator according to both his natures, who was anointed by the Father from eternity as prophet, priest, and king and also exercised these offices in the days of the Old Testament, 7, so that the doctrine of the *munus triplex* is wrongly opposed by Ritschl and others, 8. As such, Christ is a revelation of God’s love but at the same time of his righteousness, which does not conflict with love, 9, and which, in connection with God’s holy nature, the terrible character of sin, and the inviolable character of the moral law, makes satisfaction necessary, 10. The Socinians and their associates did indeed oppose this necessity, but thereby created a false antithesis between justice and grace, 11. The sacrifice of satisfaction that Christ brought consisted in his perfect obedience, which must not be conceived exclusively, as was formerly often done, among others by Piscator, as passive, but also includes the active, 12, and which may not be understood exclusively, as commonly in more recent theology, as active, but also includes the passive, 13. This entire obedience bears the character of a *satisfactio*, and specifically of a *satisfactio vicaria*, 14, which requires further explanation and defense against all kinds of objections, 15, and whose possibility is opened by way of the covenant, 16. Christ accomplished this work in the state of humiliation, from his conception through the descent into hell, 17; through it acquired the whole of salvation with all the benefits included under it—reconciliation (ἱλασμος and καταλλαγη), justification, sanctification, and glorification—which therefore cannot be restricted to any single concept of *satisfactio*, *meritum*, *Erlösung*, or *Versöhnung*, 18; and acquired these benefits not for all creatures or human beings, as the universalists asserted—an assertion the Reformed generally opposed, 19—but for his church, as Scripture clearly teaches, 20, even though the work of Christ has significance for the whole creation, 21. This work, accomplished in the state of humiliation, Christ executes and applies in the state of exaltation with its various stages—a state that was first of all the reward to Christ himself for his labor, 22, but also benefits his church, for even in heaven he remains its prophet, priest, and king until he has brought about the whole re-creation, 23.

## Chapter VIII. On the Benefits of the Covenant

**§ 43. The Order of Salvation, pp. 425–485.** Whereas in the pagan religions the human being must work out his own salvation, in Holy Scripture it is God himself who seeks the human being, graciously imparts his benefits to him in the way of the covenant, and then on that ground obligates him to a new obedience, 1. When Jesus appears with the gospel of the kingdom, he sets no demand other than faith and conversion, which are themselves again gifts of God’s grace. For it is he himself who through his death acquired all the goods of the kingdom for his own and now from heaven imparts them to his own through the Holy Spirit as the worker of faith and of a new life, 2. This doctrine of grace was soon weakened in Christian theology, especially by Pelagius, for the sake of freedom of the will, 3, and, notwithstanding Augustine’s powerful defense and despite the maintenance of *gratia praeveniens*, 4, did not receive its due even in Roman Catholic theology because of the Semi-Pelagian direction it increasingly took, 5. The Lutheran Reformation resisted this but nevertheless soon relapsed into synergism, 6. The doctrine of grace, also in the application of salvation, was maintained much better by the Reformed, 7, but alongside them there nevertheless arose the movements of mysticism and rationalism, of antinomianism and neonomianism, which identified the application of salvation with its acquisition or detached it from the acquisition altogether, 8, 9, and which continue to operate in more recent philosophy and theology, 10. The order of salvation, however, which indicates the way by which the sinner comes into possession of Christ and his benefits, is kept pure only when it avoids the two dangerous reefs of nomism and antinomianism, 11, and, on the foundation of the Trinitarian confession, sees in the application of salvation a work of the Holy Spirit that is indeed distinguished but not separated from the acquisition by Christ, since the Holy Spirit takes everything from Christ, 12. The benefits that Christ acquired and applies through the Holy Spirit can all be comprehended under the name grace, but this is conceived altogether differently by the Reformation than by Rome, 13, and contains many particular benefits that were gradually worked out more broadly in dogmatics but can be reduced to three groups, 14.

**§ 44. Calling and Regeneration, pp. 485–511,** form the first group. Just as God brings about creation through Word and Spirit, so he also brings about re-creation. In a certain sense he already calls through nature (*vocatio realis*), but nevertheless especially through his word (*vocatio verbalis*), 1; this calling is universal, sincere, and of great value, 2. But the *vocatio verbalis* by itself is not sufficient and must therefore, on the ground of Holy Scripture, be distinguished into an *externa* and an *interna*, 3, and in the latter sense stands in the closest connection with regeneration, which in the narrower sense is the infusion of the principle of the new life, 4, and, without detracting from the human being, cuts Pelagianism off at the root and maintains grace undiminished also in the application of salvation, 5.

**§ 45. Faith and Justification, pp. 511–552.** The spiritual human being, implanted in regeneration, comes in due time through the illumination of the Holy Spirit to faith, which, though a gift of God, is neither a substance foreign to the human being nor a supernatural addition, but in accordance with the new life binds him to the word of Christ, 1. For in Scripture it bears a twofold character: it is an acceptance as true of the apostolic testimony concerning Christ, and it is a personal trust in Christ as one mighty to forgive sins and grant all the benefits of grace; and these two stand in an inseparable connection with each other, 2. Although they are frequently separated from each other in doctrine and practice, they must always go together, since on the one hand there is no participation in Christ’s benefits except after and through communion with his person, and on the other hand this communion comes about only by means of the apostolic testimony, 3. One of the most glorious fruits of that faith is justification, which according to Scripture is not an ethical but a juridical act, 4, and has its ground not in a human being’s own righteousness, an ἰδια δικαιοσυνη, but in a δικαιοσυνη θεου that is revealed in Christ and lies ready, is imputed to the human being by God out of grace, and on the human being’s part is received by faith, 5. This doctrine of *justitia imputata* is indeed strongly opposed, but this opposition proceeds largely from an altogether incorrect conception of the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, 6. This justification takes place in a certain sense in the decree, in the resurrection of Christ, and in the *vocatio interna*, but according to the consistent representation of Holy Scripture nevertheless occurs especially from and through faith and precisely as such stands over against justification from works, 7. It includes not only the complete forgiveness of all sins, of both guilt and punishment—which, however, does not nullify the daily confession of guilt and prayer for forgiveness—but further adoption as children and the granting of the right to eternal life, 8.

**§ 46. Sanctification and Perseverance, pp. 553–572.** Sanctification follows justification and is essentially distinct from it but may not be separated from it for a single moment, for Christ gives himself to his own not only objectively but also subjectively (*unio mystica*), 1. This sanctification is in the first place a work of God, consisting not only in external separation from the world but also in internal renewal by the Holy Spirit, without, however, attaining its completion in this life, whether all at once or gradually; but further it is also a work of believers, insofar as on the ground of the benefits received they are called to sanctify themselves and to produce good works as fruits of their faith, 2. Likewise, the perseverance of the saints is an act and a gift of God, but one that realizes itself through believers themselves, in the way of the means, and therefore does not exclude their own activity but does offer them rich comfort and certainty, 3.
