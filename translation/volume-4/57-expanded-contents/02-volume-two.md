# Volume II

## Chapter IV. On God

**§ 20. The Incomprehensibility of God, pp. 1–24.** Dogmatics lives in mystery, for it always deals with God, who according to Holy Scripture is incomprehensible and cannot even be named by any name, 1. This incomprehensibility of God was also acknowledged outside the domain of special revelation, 2; was adopted in Christian theology by the church fathers, 3, 4, the scholastics, 5, and the Reformers, 6; and in more recent philosophy was replaced by the absolute unknowability of God, 7, 8. Although there is certainly no adequate knowledge of God, the grounds advanced by this agnosticism, 9, are untenable, since agnosticism would make all speech by us about God and all revelation by God to us impossible, 10. Denial of the knowability of God implies that God has not willed and has not been able to reveal himself, and that we even lack a ground for assuming his existence, 11.

**§ 21. The Knowability of God (*cognitio Dei insita*), pp. 24–44.** God is knowable insofar as he reveals himself. That he reveals himself is established according to Scripture and nature and is confirmed by all human beings; there are scarcely any atheists, 1. The naturalness, universality, and necessity of the knowledge of God led many to belief in innate concepts, 2, 3. But Christian theology rejected this doctrine—not only the fathers and scholastics, 4, but also Lutherans and Reformed, 5—since it led to rationalism or mysticism, 6. Yet this did not deny that the human being brings with him the *potentia* and *inclinatio* to arrive of himself, without scientific reasoning, at some knowledge of God, 7.

**§ 22. The Knowability of God (*cognitio Dei acquisita*), pp. 44–62.** The *cognitio Dei insita* is increased by the *cognitio Dei acquisita* obtained by way of reflection and reasoning, 1. Both belong to *theologia naturalis*, which is not a product of human reason but is incorporated into Scripture and inferred from nature by the believer, 2. Holy Scripture does not prove God’s existence but testifies to it, 3. The so-called proofs for God’s existence, partly overestimated and partly underestimated, 4—namely, the ontological, 5, cosmological, 6, teleological, 7, and moral proofs, 8—are wrongly called proofs but are *signa* and *testimonia* that God does not leave himself without witness to the human being, 9.

**§ 23. The Names of God, pp. 62–78.** Everything made knowable of God through his revelation is comprehended by Holy Scripture under the names by which God is named (*nomen inditum*) on the ground of his revelation (*nomen editum*), 1. All those names are anthropomorphic, 2, and formed according to the analogy in creatures, 3, but rightly so, since God himself reveals himself in and through creatures and can be named in no other way, 4. They therefore give no adequate knowledge, but neither do they give an untrue knowledge, 5; rather, they give an ectypal, analogical knowledge of God, 6.

**§ 24. Classification of the Names of God, pp. 78–102.** Holy Scripture equally honors all God’s virtues and does not know the distinction between essence and attributes, 1, which later arose in theology and led to God’s essence being described as the Absolute, or as Sovereign, or as Father, and so forth, 2–4, and the attributes being separated from it, 5. But in God there is no distinction between essence and attributes, 6, although the attributes differ mutually *ratione ratiocinata*, 7. The attributes were classified in many ways, 8, but all those classifications agree substantially and are burdened by the same objections, so that a good classification is difficult to find, 9.

**§ 25. The Proper Names of God, pp. 102–115,** taken so in the broader sense, are those by which God is named and addressed, such as θεος, *deus*, God, 1; in Holy Scripture, El, Elohim, Elyon, Adonai, 2; El Shaddai, YHWH, 3; YHWH Zebaoth, 4; κυριος, πατηρ, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, 5.

**§ 26. The Essential Names of God, pp. 115–227,** which must be treated before the doctrine of the Trinity, include first those by which God is designated as the *Being* in the absolute sense: namely, independence, 1; immutability, 2; infinity, which in turn includes eternity, 3, and omnipresence, 4. Next there are names that make God known to us as the Living One, as *Spirit*: namely, simplicity, 5; spirituality, 6; invisibility, 7. Further, there are names that describe the divine essence as perfectly self-conscious, as *Light*: namely, knowledge of himself and of the world—in connection with which *praescientia* and *scientia media* are discussed—8; wisdom—ideas in God—9; truthfulness, 10. Then there are names that point us to the ethical nature of God’s essence and make him known as the *Holy One*: namely, goodness—lovingkindness, mercy, patience, grace, love—11; holiness, 12; glory, 13; righteousness, 14. And finally there are names in which God meets us as absolute power, as *Sovereign*: namely, the will, which is distinguished as *propensio Dei in se ipsum* (*necessaria*) and as *propensio in creaturas* (*libera*), 15; in the latter sense is to be conceived as free but not as sheer arbitrariness, 16; further divides into *voluntas beneplaciti* and *signi*, 17; and finally is to be honored as omnipotence, 18.

**§ 27. The Personal Names of God, pp. 227–312,** make known to us within the one divine essence the self-distinctions that already occur in the Old Testament—Elohim or YHWH brings everything about through Word and Spirit—1, but are revealed much more clearly in the New Testament, especially also in opposition to the false doctrine of intermediate beings in Jewish philosophy, 2, 3. The New Testament teaches not only that everything in nature and grace comes about through Father, Son, and Spirit, 4, but also makes known to us something of the relations among these three persons when it calls the Father in a metaphysical sense the Father of Christ, 5; Christ in a special sense the Word, the Son, the Image of God, 6; and the Spirit in a particular sense the Spirit of the Father and of the Son, 7. These data of Holy Scripture led the church to the confession of the Trinity, a doctrine first developed by Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Origen, 8, then especially worked out and defended by Athanasius, 9, and Augustine, 10, but strongly opposed by Arianism and Sabellianism, 11, and the movements connected with them, 12, 13. The terms ordinarily used with this dogma—essence, persons, Trinity, and so forth—are indeed not taken from Scripture but are not therefore impermissible, 14. The word *essence* designates the Deity, the divine nature, as it exists in the persons, 15. The three independent *modi subsistendi* in the one divine essence are called *persons*, 16; they differ from that essence not *re* but nevertheless *realiter* and are mutually distinguished in names, personal properties, and manner of working, 17. The first person has *paternitas* as his personal property, 18. The second person is the Son, generated from the essence of the Father by spiritual, simple, eternal generation, 19. The third person is the Spirit, whose personality and deity, though acknowledged in the church later than those of the Son, are of the highest importance for doctrine and life, 20, and whose personal property is the εκπορευσις not only from the Father, 21, but also from the Son, *Filioque*, 22. This ontological Trinity is reflected in the economic Trinity, which brings to expression in revelation not only the unity but also the distinction of the three persons, 23; but although it has very often been attempted, it is difficult to elucidate by images or prove by reasoning, 24. Nevertheless, it is of the greatest significance for the doctrine of God, creation, and re-creation, indeed for the whole Christian religion, 25.

**§ 28. The Counsel of God, pp. 313–385,** which, as embracing the *opera essentialia ad intra*, indicates the connection between God and his works (*opera ad extra*), occurs in the Old Testament especially as a fact in history, 1, and in the New Testament clearly proves to be eternal, personal, and related to the destiny hereafter, 2. But in the Christian church the doctrine of predestination was weakened by the Greek church, by Pelagianism and Semi-Pelagianism, 3, by the Roman church, 4, and even by the Lutherans, 5, while the Reformed indeed maintained it but differed over the method of treatment, 6, and over the infralapsarian or supralapsarian order of the decrees, 7; and many others—Socinians, Remonstrants, and so forth—denied it, converted it into a philosophical determinism, or modified it in a Pelagian or Semi-Pelagian sense, 8. Now God’s counsel is nothing other than his simple and eternal decree concerning everything that will exist or happen in time, 9, embracing first the *providentia* in the sense of *ratio ordinis rerum*, 10, and next *praedestinatio*, which was exchanged by Pelagianism for *praescientia*, 11, and is opposed especially on three points, 12. Among the Reformed there was a difference over its infralapsarian or supralapsarian order, 13, both of which have something for and against them, 14, and neither of which offers the true solution, 15. *Reprobatio*, which repeatedly occurs in Holy Scripture, decidedly belongs in a certain sense to predestination, 16, but nevertheless belongs to it in another sense than election, 17, in which predestination attains its end and comes to its full reality, 18; for election has Christ and his church as its object and the honor of God in the salvation of the fallen world as its goal, 19.

## Chapter V. On the World in Its Original State

**§ 29. Creation, pp. 386–424,** is the beginning of the execution of God’s counsel. Its doctrine occupies a broad place in Holy Scripture, 1, and can be understood only by faith, so that the Gentiles did not know it and philosophy ordinarily rejected it in order to explain the world pantheistically or materialistically, 2. Pantheism and materialism, however, are both incapable of making known to us the origin of things, 3. The Christian church therefore held to the doctrine of Holy Scripture, 4, and taught a creation from nothing, 5, brought about by the triune God, Father, Son, and Spirit, 6, and specifically having the Son as mediator, 7. It did not take place in time but coincided with the creation of time, 8, and had its cause neither in God’s overflowing fullness of being nor in his poverty and need, 9, but in God’s will, which in it aims at the glory of his name, 10. As a product of God’s will, the world is one whole containing the greatest diversity within itself.

**§ 30. The Spiritual World, pp. 424–456,** includes the beings ordinarily bearing the name angels. Their existence is assumed in almost all religions but has nevertheless also been repeatedly rejected, 1. Though philosophically and historically unprovable, it is nevertheless important for religion, though not a central dogma, 2. Scripture has no single common name for them but names different classes, 3. Yet they are one in this: that they are all created, 4; spiritual, incorporeal, 5; rational, 6; moral beings, 7; essentially distinct from the human being, who is the image of God, 8; called to an extraordinary and ordinary service in the kingdom of heaven that, however, includes no personal protection and intercession, 9; and gives no claim to religious veneration, 10.

**§ 31. The Material World, pp. 456–489,** remains unexplained in its origin outside Holy Scripture, 1, but according to Gen. 1:1 arises by creation from nothing (*creatio prima*) and is prepared in the six days (*creatio secunda*), 2, in which a definite order can be observed, 3. This hexaemeron was frequently treated at length in Christian theology and modified without objection according to the prevailing view of the world—Ptolemaic, Copernican—4. But the hypothesis of Kant and Laplace, 5, as well as that of developmental periods of the earth built upon its strata, are difficult to reconcile with Scripture, 6. Attempts at reconciliation cannot be called successful, 7, although they also contain good elements, 8. But a correct interpretation of the creation narrative, 9, and a clean sifting between the certain and uncertain in geological science nevertheless bring about a provisional agreement, 10.

**§ 32. The Origin of the Human Being, pp. 490–507,** is described differently in the first and second creation narratives but according to both accounts is due to God’s creative omnipotence, 1. The Christian church maintained this divine origin of the human being against every materialistic, evolutionary explanation in earlier and later times, 2, and in this also found support among many scientists who opposed Darwinism, 3. Further, between Holy Scripture and contemporary science not only the origin of the human being is in dispute, but also the age, 4, unity, 5, and cradle of humanity, 6.

**§ 33. The Essence of the Human Being, pp. 508–545,** according to Holy Scripture lies in the fact that he is the image of God, 1. The content of this image was understood differently in the Christian church. Gradually, especially two conceptions came to stand over against each other: the naturalistic, 2, which is equally contradicted by history, Scripture, and sound reasoning, 3; and the supernaturalistic, which came to development in the Roman doctrine of the *donum superadditum*, 4, but rests on a false conception of the human being and his destiny, 5. The Reformation therefore taught that the image of God was essentially proper to the human being, 6, not as a substance but as a quality belonging to the essence of the human being, 7. For the whole human being is the image of God, as the Reformed acknowledged over against the Lutherans with their distinction between the image of God in the broader and narrower senses: according to soul and spirit, which are not two substances, and according to all their faculties, 8; according to the virtues of knowledge, righteousness, and holiness (*justitia originalis*), and even in a certain sense according to the body, 9. Among the works of creation, human nature is the highest revelation of God, 10.

**§ 34. The Destiny of the Human Being, pp. 545–571.** Although Adam was God’s image, according to Holy Scripture he did not immediately possess the highest and was therefore placed in the covenant of works, 1. Christian theology did not fully acknowledge this truth and alternately estimated Adam’s state too low or too high. But the Reformed incorporated it into the doctrine of the *foedus operum*, 2, a doctrine that, though developed too scholastically, nevertheless contains a profound thought, since it maintains religion as communion with God, 3; fully takes account of Adam’s state before the fall, 4; first allows the riches of the image of God to unfold in the human race, 5; and therefore also explains why Roman Catholics and Reformed, in distinction from Lutherans, were generally adherents of creationism, 6, 7.
