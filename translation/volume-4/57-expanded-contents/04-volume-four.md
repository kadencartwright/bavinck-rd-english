# Volume IV

## Chapter IX. On the Church

**§ 47. The Essence of the Church, pp. 1–59.** The communion of those who share in Christ and his benefits bears the name of the church, which finds its analogies among the pagan religions and was prepared in the Old Testament, 1, but in the proper sense was first established in the days of the New Testament, 2. After the apostolic age it quickly developed into a catholic, visible, infallible, hierarchical institute of salvation, 3, so that reformation was urgently needed. Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin brought this about, each in his own way, 4, but on the left and right rationalism and mysticism falsified the concept of the church, so that even now, despite many attempts at restoration, it suffers from great confusion, 5. The name the church bears already designates it as an assembly of Christian believers, and Scripture places this in the clearest light in all kinds of ways and under all kinds of images, especially also through what it says concerning the communion of saints, 6. As such it can be taken in a broader and narrower sense (*ecclesia generalis, triumphans, militans, universalis, nationalis, particularis*). But according to its idea it never includes unbelievers, even though on earth they are always present in the church, 7. Neither is it to be described as an assembly of *praedestinati, perfecti, communicantes, vocati,* or *baptizati*, but as an assembly of believers having both a visible and an invisible side. The visible and invisible church are therefore not two churches; they are not equivalent to the circle of unbelievers and believers in the church, nor to the church as institute and as organism, but are two sides of the same church, 8. The marks of the church are not the 15 *notae* Bellarmine gives, 9, but the Word of God, administered and confessed in preaching, sacrament, confession, and life, and wrongly rejected from the Roman side, 10. For although this mark entails the possibility of great schism and division among the churches, Rome cannot prevent these either; and although the pluriformity of the church is sinful and painful on the one hand, it nevertheless includes a blessing on the other, 11. In accordance with this, the attributes of the church—unity, holiness, catholicity, apostolicity, imperishability, and infallibility—have a different meaning among us than at Rome, 12.

**§ 48. The Government of the Church, pp. 59–131.** The church is inconceivable without government and therefore has also always had a government. It was always at the same time institute and organism, 1, both in the Old and in the New Testament. The apostolate especially is strong proof of the institutional character of the church; to this office were added those of evangelists and prophets, while Peter was *primus inter pares* among the apostles, 2; and later those of ruling and teaching elders and deacons, 3. After the apostolic age this aristocratic-presbyterial church government soon passed into a monarchical-episcopal one, which still rules in many churches, and at Rome into a papal church government, which drew its consequence in the declaration of the pope’s infallibility, 4. Both these forms of church government, however, conflict with the teaching of Holy Scripture concerning the relation of clergy and laity, the episcopate, the apostolate, and Peter’s primacy, and further also with the earliest testimonies of the church, 5. Opposition to the Roman hierarchy brought many to reject all church government, but this too contradicts Scripture, which ascribes a government of its own to the church. Hence the Reformed maintained it in its distinction from political power, 6. For Christ is the sole head of the church, both of the local church and of all churches together. He pours out his gifts in it, anoints all as prophets, priests, and kings, 7, and further institutes different offices that are not the organ of the congregation but derive their authority from Christ and are obtained by way of vocation, examination, and ordination, 8. These offices have indeed been arbitrarily increased and changed in character in the church, but they are two, respectively three, in number—teaching and ruling elders, and deacons.

**§ 49. The Power of the Church, pp. 132–192.** Like the state, the church first arose after sin and was gradually distinguished more clearly from the state. Even in Israel the two, though closely united, were not one, 1, and in the New Testament Christ gave his church a power essentially different from that of the civil government by its spiritual character, 2. At Rome, however, this spiritual power of the church degenerated into a juridical, coercive power that, in contrast with the caesaropapism of the Greek church, subjects all domains of life, including that of the state, to itself and makes them serviceable, 3, and culminates in the pope’s *plena et suprema potestas*. This power, specifically the infallibility of the pope, however, not only finds no support in Scripture but is also in itself indefinite, ungrounded, in conflict with the episcopate, and antichristian, 4. The Reformation therefore unanimously rejected this power but diverged again insofar as Luther and Calvin judged differently concerning confession, the presbyterate and discipline, and the relation of ecclesiastical and secular power. In the centuries after the Reformation the distinction between these last two powers increasingly passed into an at least relative separation, 5. Yet it is beyond doubt that Christ granted his church a power having its own origin, organ, nature, and goal, and equally misconstrued by Romanism and Anabaptism. This power of the church is threefold, 6: first, *potestas docendi*, that is, the power to preach, explain, defend, and confess the Word of God, 7; second, *potestas gubernationis*, that is, the power to govern the congregation, 8, and exercise discipline, 9; and third, *potestas misericordiae*, that is, the power to show mercy to the distressed, 10. This power comes together in the consistory in the local church and, for multiple churches, in the broader assemblies, which are already of ancient date and are necessary and good for the welfare of the churches, 11. With this power the church stands independently and freely in the world, but serves it with the goods Christ grants her, 12.

## Chapter X. On the Means of Grace

**§ 50. The Word, pp. 193–215.** In distributing his grace Christ makes use of means that were wrongly misconstrued by mysticism and rationalism and wrongly conceived in an absolute sense by Rome, but were restored to their proper value by the Reformation, specifically by Calvin, 1. In the first place the Word of God belongs to these means of grace; it also works outside official administration, 2, and as a means of grace is to be distinguished into law and gospel, 3. This distinction, however, is not an irreconcilable opposition, as antinomianism teaches, nor an essential identity, as nomism maintains, for the two differ in principle but nevertheless always go together in preaching, 4. Likewise the Word as a means of grace is neither to be separated from the working of the Spirit nor identified with it. The Holy Spirit works neither *sine verbo* nor *per verbum* but *cum verbo*, 5.

**§ 51. The Sacraments, pp. 215–252.** The second means of grace is the sacrament. The name does not occur in Scripture, was first customary for all kinds of ecclesiastical acts and ceremonies, and only in the Middle Ages was restricted to seven ceremonies. The doctrine of the sacraments too was first worked out by scholasticism and displayed an increasingly great deviation from Scripture, 1. For Rome understands by the grace communicated through the sacrament only *gratia sanctificans* and detaches the sacrament from the Word and likewise from faith. The Reformation rejected these errors but nevertheless presented in the persons of Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin different conceptions that divided the churches and, despite the weakening of the significance of the sacraments by mysticism and rationalism, cause church and theology to diverge down to the present day, 2. Although the name sacrament does not occur in Scripture, it is not therefore to be rejected. Neither is treating the general doctrine of the sacraments before baptism and the Supper to be disapproved, since thereby the difference in conception comes clearly to light and error is more easily recognized. According to Scripture the sacraments are signs and seals of God’s grace and at the same time acts of confession of faith, 3. As visible signs and seals they indicate and confirm the invisible grace granted by God in Christ—that is, the *same* grace also offered in the Word, though in another manner, 4. The connection between sign and thing signified is not physical, corporeal, local, but a connection of relation brought about by the word of institution and, although spiritual in nature, nevertheless objective and real, 5. Therefore the sacraments also do not work *ex opere operato* but presuppose faith in the recipient, which does not detract from their objectivity and leaves their value unimpaired, 5. The number of sacraments is not seven but only two, but these two equal Rome’s seven in value, 6.

**§ 52. Baptism, pp. 252–299.** Prepared in the Old Testament by circumcision, baptism was first administered to the Jews by John at God’s command. This baptism of John does not differ essentially from Christian baptism but was taken over by Jesus and before his ascension extended to all peoples as a sign and seal of the forgiveness of sins and of incorporation into Christ and his church, 2. After the age of the apostles it was soon administered with the trinitarian formula, conceived as a *mysterium* of initiation, surrounded with all kinds of ceremonies, and increasingly represented as magical in its working. According to Rome, through the intimate connection of sign and thing signified it grants *ex opere operato*, besides a *character indelebilis*, sanctifying grace that delivers from all guilt, punishment, and stain of sin, 3. The Lutherans taught a similar close union of the water of baptism with divine grace and, at least in children, had regeneration come about through baptism; but the Reformed maintained that baptism, as a sacrament, also presupposed faith in children and rested upon the foundation of the covenant of grace. But this covenant was increasingly externalized; and rationalism and mysticism so misconstrued the value of baptism that in more recent times various attempts have been made to maintain baptism’s objective character, 4. Scripture speaks only of the baptism of adults and clearly teaches that it was instituted only for believers and may be administered after confession. The sign in baptism is water, into which the baptized person is immersed or with which he is sprinkled, 5. This water becomes a sacrament through the word of institution, which in Scripture is not intended as a formula but was nevertheless soon applied as such in the church, and which brings about between sign and thing signified a real but not a physical and local union. The benefits of baptism are principally forgiveness and regeneration, 6. The children of believers also belong among those who have a right to baptism. This right was argued in different ways over against the opponents of infant baptism, 7, and rests on many scriptural grounds, 8. The minister of baptism is Christ, who in it makes use of human beings, specifically ministers; has it administered in the public assembly of the congregation; at no fixed times; in the case of infant baptism, in the presence of the parents; and to all persons who can still be reckoned as belonging within the circle of the covenant, 9.

**§ 53. The Lord’s Supper, pp. 299–352.** The second sacrament is the Supper, which in the Old Testament was typified and prepared by the Passover, 1, and in the New Testament instituted by Christ as a sign and seal of communion in his sacrifice on the cross, 2. This Supper soon became the center of Christian worship and was gradually, especially since scholasticism, conceived as an unbloody sacrifice of the body and blood of Christ to which communion is subordinate, 3. The Reformation indeed unanimously rejected this Roman doctrine but diverged widely in its positive exposition of the doctrine of the Supper. Luther, Zwingli, and Calvin each presented a conception of his own that, modified and abandoned after them, nevertheless continues to exist down to the present day, 4. The names for the Supper are very numerous, but the best is Holy Supper or meal of the Lord, since it is a true meal with Christ as host, the ministers as his servants, with bread and wine as food and drink, in the first period connected with an ordinary meal, to be celebrated at a table and not upon an altar, 5. But this meal has a spiritual meaning; it is not merely a memorial meal or an act of confession but an exercise through faith of communion with the crucified Christ. Christ is indeed not physically and locally present in the signs, for transubstantiation and consubstantiation are both excluded for many scriptural and natural reasons, 6. Nevertheless, this communion with Christ in the Supper is objective and real, yet in such a way that it presupposes faith on the side of the recipients and precisely thereby is strengthened as communion in the person and benefits of Christ, 7. Like baptism, this Supper too was instituted only for the church, specifically for baptized and living believers who have confessed their faith and have not been barred by the church on account of doctrine or life, 8.

## Chapter XI. On the Last Things

**§ 54. The Intermediate State, pp. 353–422.** All religions contain a certain eschatology and specifically teach the immortality of the soul; and philosophy repeatedly adopted this latter doctrine, 1. The proofs adduced for the immortality of the soul have no compelling force over against unbelief but are nevertheless important as testimonies and justifications of faith, 2. Scripture, already in the Old Testament, also contains the doctrine of immortality, as its representation of Sheol clearly proves; but it adds the much richer revelation that true life is obtained and enjoyed only in communion with God, which triumphs over death and grave, 3. Later Jewish literature failed to do justice to this revelation, but the New Testament brings out still better than the Old Testament that death is a punishment for sin, that through Christ believers receive a life raised far above death, and that although after dying they belong in a certain sense to the realm of the dead, they nevertheless immediately enter a state different from that of unbelievers, 4. At first the Christian church remained with these few data of Scripture concerning the intermediate state, but devoted more attention to it as the return of Christ delayed and gradually came to assume five *receptacula* beyond the grave, among which purgatory especially is significant for the intermediate state, 5. The Reformation rejected this doctrine but soon saw all kinds of views concerning the intermediate state arise, such as those of a provisional state, a purgatory, a sleep of the soul, a certain corporeality of souls, transmigration of souls and continued purification, *limbus patrum, limbus infantum*, and possible conversion after death, 6. Scripture contains little concerning the intermediate state because, although assuming the immortality of the soul, it especially wishes to make known the new life Christ has brought to light and without which human life already here on earth, and especially hereafter in Sheol, loses its content and value, 7. The gaps Scripture leaves in our conception of the intermediate state may not be filled with human conjectures such as the sleep of the soul, 8; the corporeality of souls after death, 9; or the continuing intercourse of the dead with the living, which then entails the sacrifice of the Mass, intercession, veneration of saints, necromancy, and the cult of relics, 10. Little can be said of the place of the intermediate state, but it is certain that at death a different state immediately begins for believers and unbelievers, in which no change through conversion, as many now believe, is any longer possible, 11. Neither is the state of believers who have died to be conceived as a purgatory, for according to the teaching of Scripture they enter heaven with Christ immediately after death. Although there can therefore be no question of a veneration of the saints or an intercession for the departed, 12, a communion of the church triumphant with the church militant nevertheless remains. The blessed in heaven too are not made perfect without us; they look longingly toward the parousia of Christ and are not raised above all space, time, and activity, 13.

**§ 55. The Return of Christ, pp. 422–481.** Just as an end comes to the life of the individual human being, so too to that of humanity and the world. Science confirms this, 1; religions universally cherish this expectation; and Old Testament prophecy proclaims at the end of this dispensation the establishment of the messianic kingdom, which, rich in spiritual and material blessings, will extend to the ends of the earth, 2. The Old Testament describes in this messianic kingdom not an intermediate period but a final state, but later Jewish literature began to distinguish between a preceding messianic kingdom and a following kingdom of God and thus caused the doctrine of chiliasm to arise, which was also adopted by many Christians, especially in times of persecution and oppression, 3. Although agreeing in some principal matters, the chiliasts nevertheless conflict greatly with one another and all make themselves guilty of great arbitrariness in interpreting Scripture, 4. The Old Testament itself already offers a better interpretation of prophecy than chiliasm provides us, and the New Testament regards itself as the spiritual and therefore true fulfillment of the Old Testament, 5. But not only does chiliasm conflict with this principal thought of Scripture; it also conflicts with many other data, especially with the consistent expectation of the New Testament concerning the Jewish people, 6, which is not contradicted by a few passages, especially Rom. 11:11–32, 7. Further, the New Testament nowhere expects a state of glory and honor for the church of Christ in this dispensation, but rather increasing persecution and oppression, to which the one return of Christ first puts an end, 8. There is indeed mention in Rev. 20 of a thousand-year binding of Satan. But this chapter actually contains nothing of all that essentially belongs to chiliasm, any more than other passages of Scripture, 9. And the events related in Rev. 20 do not fall chronologically after those of the preceding chapters but run parallel with them. They are intended to depict for us the end of the peoples without culture, just as the preceding chapters did with respect to those nations in whose midst the gospel has been preached and thus the antichristian power can develop, 10. Christ, therefore, to whom as Son of Man judgment has been given, ends world history through his second coming, 11. The time of that return is not determined in the New Testament, although its preliminary signs begin already with the fall of Jerusalem. And Scripture is equally sober in describing the manner in which that return takes place, 12.

**§ 56. The Consummation of the Ages, pp. 481–529.** With the return of Christ begins the Day of the Lord, whose duration cannot be determined. The first event that then takes place is the resurrection of the dead, 1, in which the identity of the resurrection body with the body that died remains preserved, 2, 3. Then follows the judgment, which extends over all human beings and angels and over all their thoughts, words, and deeds, 4. The place to which the wicked are consigned and suffer eternal punishments is Gehenna. The eternity of punishment in hell has indeed often been contested on all kinds of grounds and alternately replaced by the doctrine of hypothetical universalism, of the restoration of all things, or of conditional immortality, 5. But it is contained in Scripture and required by the nature of sin and divine justice, although it does not exclude very different degrees in its application, 6. After the final judgment follows the renewal of the world, which consists not in an annihilation of its substance but in a re-creation of its *forma*, 7. This renewal of the world proves that blessedness is to be conceived not exclusively as heavenly but also as earthly, 8, and embraces not only spiritual but also material blessings, both of which indeed begin here but are first completed in eternity. Communion with God, which is the essence of future blessedness and is enjoyed with both understanding and will, 9, is heightened through the communion of saints, whose number, even though it does not include all human beings and there is little to say about the salvation of Gentiles and children who die young, will nevertheless form a multitude that no one can number, 10, and that itself includes every kind of diversity of personality, gifts, reward, and activity, 11.
