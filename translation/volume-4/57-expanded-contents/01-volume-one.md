**§ 5.** *History and Literature of Dogmatics*, pp. 51–139. Scientific theology, which does not yet occur among the Apostolic Fathers 1, becomes necessary for self-defense among the Apologists after the faith is attacked by Lucian, Celsus, Porphyry, Julian, and the Gnostics 2, and then diverges in the direction of the North African, Asia Minor, or Alexandrian school 3, and acquires a firm foundation in the controversy against Monarchianism in the 3rd century 4. Establishment and defense of the Trinitarian and Christological dogma in the East from the 4th to the 8th century 5. Dogmatic development in the East culminates in John of Damascus 6, and then does continue but remains for the most part unknown 7. From the beginning, already in Tertullian, Cyprian, and Irenaeus, dogmatics in the West bears a character of its own, although it is also dependent upon the East 8, and receives its most outstanding interpreter in Augustine 9. Gregory conducted church and theology over to the new peoples, who at first simply adopted both (Isidore, Alcuin) 10, but then became independent and produced a theology of their own in scholasticism 11. Scholasticism passed through three periods 12 and had mysticism alongside it 13. At the end of the Middle Ages it declined and was exposed to much criticism, but it recovered after the Reformation 14 and flourished especially in Spain 15. In the 18th century Roman Catholic theology came under the influence of Rationalism 16 and remained partly under it even in the 19th century (Hermes, Günther); nevertheless, it succeeded in freeing itself again and reappeared independently as traditionalism, romanticism, mediating theology, and especially Thomism 17. The Lutheran Reformation received its first dogmatician in Melanchthon and its consensus in the *Form. Conc.* 18, was developed scholastically in the 17th century 19, but weakened in the 18th century by Pietism, Moravianism, and Rationalism 20, 21, and in this century aligned itself especially with the various systems of philosophy: with Kant 22, Jacobi 23, Schleiermacher 24, Hegel 25, and Schelling 26, or appeared as confessionalism, biblical theology 27, or neo-Kantianism 28. The Swiss Reformation, which differs in principle from the German 29, received its dogmatic system from Calvin and gained entrance into France, the Netherlands, England, Scotland, and Germany 30. Toward the end of the 16th century the scholastic method arose in Reformed theology and brought it to great flourishing in various countries 31, but it declined through Anabaptism, Arminianism, Cartesianism, and Cocceianism 32, Amyraldism, Independency, the Baptist movement, Quakerism, and Deism 33. Tolerance, neology, supernaturalism, and neonomism now came to the fore 34. But over against the negative tendencies in our country—supernaturalism, Groningen theology, and modern theology—there came a revival of positive theology through the Secession and the Réveil, the Utrecht and ethical-irenic tendencies, and the awakening Calvinism 35, a revival that also occurs elsewhere but ordinarily bears a less decisively Reformed character: Germany 35, Switzerland and France 36, England and Scotland 37, America 38, 39.

### Chapter I. Principia in General

**§ 6.** *Significance of the Principia*, pp. 140–145. The name ἀρχη, *principium* 1. *Principium essendi* 2. *Principium cognoscendi*, *externum* and *internum* 3. Unity and distinction of these three *principia* 4.

**§ 7.** *Principia in Science*, pp. 145–170. *Rationalism*, which runs through the whole history of philosophy 1, derives all knowledge from the subject 2, but misconstrues the *princ. cogn. externum* and falls into idealism and illusionism 3. *Empiricism* seeks the source of knowledge in sensory perception 4, but misconstrues the *princ. cogn. internum* and leads to materialism 5. *Realism*, by contrast, proceeds from the general, natural certainty concerning the objectivity and truth of knowledge 6, acknowledges both the *princ. cogn. internum* and the *externum* 7, has all knowledge begin with sensory perception 8, but ascribes to the understanding the capacity to penetrate from the *phaenomena* to the *noumena* 9, because the reason within us and the reason outside us together have their origin in the Logos 10.

**§ 8.** *Principia in Religion*, pp. 171–214. The *essence* of religion, whose name is of uncertain derivation 1, is according to Holy Scripture, as *religio objectiva*, identical with God’s revelation and, as *religio subjectiva*, with the disposition to walk in his ways 2. This distinction is indispensable for determining the essence of religion. As *religio objectiva*, all religions contain the elements of tradition, dogma, *ethos*, and *cultus* 3. And as *religio subjectiva* they are *pietas*, which has *cultus* as its fruit 4. This *religio subjectiva*, which is a *habitus*, was corrupted by sin, is renewed by regeneration, and according to Holy Scripture consists in faith 5, which manifests itself in a *cultus* according to God’s will 6. The *seat* of religion has been investigated especially since the essence of religion was wrongly placed solely in the *religio subjectiva* 7, 8; it is not exclusively the understanding 9, for religion and science are distinct 10; nor the will 11, for religion and morality are two different things 12; nor feeling 13, for religion is something other than art 14; but the whole human being, who must serve God wholly, always, and everywhere 15. The *origin* of religion is not fear, priestly deception, ignorance, animism, and so forth 16, nor the urge to maintain oneself over against the world 16, 17. The historical and psychological methods are insufficient to explain the essence and origin of religion 18. Religion is explicable only when the existence of God and the truth of his revelation are accepted 19.

### Chapter II. External Principle

**§ 9.** *General Revelation*, pp. 215–244. Revelation is a religious concept that can be maintained only in theism 1, and consists generally in an act of God by which he places the human being in a religious relation to himself 2. The distinction between natural and supernatural revelation derives already from the earliest ecclesiastical writers, but at Rome it became a rational antithesis 3, which was indeed modified by the Reformation but nevertheless lived on in Socinianism and elsewhere and undermined the whole concept of revelation 4. Scripture makes no conceptual distinction between natural and supernatural revelation, since all revelation acknowledges the personal existence of God exalted above nature and differs only in the means through which it comes to us 5. Supernatural revelation is not identical with immediate revelation, for all revelation occurs mediately, but differs from natural revelation in the ways by which it comes to us. Therefore the distinction between general and special revelation deserves preference 6. General revelation is insufficient because it contains nothing of God’s grace in Christ 7, but is nevertheless of great value for the pagan world 8, 9, and also for the church.

**§ 10.** *Special Revelation*, pp. 244–295. Like all religions, Christianity appeals to a special revelation, φανερωσις, ἀποκαλυψις, which occurs in three ways 1: through theophany 2, prophecy (God’s speaking, lot, Urim, dream, vision, inward illumination) 3, and miracles 4. Holy Scripture thereby makes special revelation known to us not as a mere communication of doctrine, or as religious inspiration, or as an infusion of new life, or as God’s becoming conscious in the human spirit 5, but as a system of words and deeds of God that concentrate in the person of Christ and, after his appearance and after the completion of Scripture, continue and are completed in modified form in the dispensation of the Holy Spirit 6. In this rich sense, however, it is misconstrued both by the abstract supernaturalism of Rome and others 7, 8, and by rationalistic naturalism 9, which is nevertheless insufficient to explain the phenomena of revelation 10 and has its ground in a pantheistic or materialistic monism 11. But this monism is contradicted both by a correct view of nature 11 and by Scripture; in its worldview special revelation occupies an appropriate, harmonious place 12.

**§ 11.** *Holy Scripture*, pp. 295–362. Special revelation, which is history and as such passes away, is extended to humanity and the world by appearing in the form of Scripture 1. Scripture is therefore neither all of revelation nor merely the record of revelation, but the final act, the capstone of revelation 2. For revelation as a whole falls into two dispensations: the first brings about the incarnation of the Logos and the inscripturation of his Word; the second applies the treasures of Christ and of his testimony in the church, which therefore continually stands in a living, organic relation to that Scripture 3. The divine inspiration of that Scripture has always been confessed by the church, from the earliest times 4, through scholasticism 5, by Roman Catholics 6 and the Reformers 7, but has also frequently been opposed from without and then weakened into a dualistic or dynamic inspiration or denied altogether 8. Scripture, however, clearly teaches its own inspiration, both in the Old Testament 9, in the New Testament concerning the Old 10, and also in the New Testament concerning itself 11, and therefore presents itself as the Word of God 12. This self-testimony of Scripture is unimpeachable 13; it distinguishes the inspiration by which Scripture arose from every other inspiration of genius, from God’s immanent activity in the world, from revelation, and from regeneration, even though it is related to all these 14. It is a speaking of God through prophets and apostles, not, however, in a mechanical but in an organic sense 15, and it cannot be restricted to the animation of the persons or to the inspiration of the chief matters 16. The objections raised against this inspiration are undoubtedly connected in part with the hostility of the human heart 17, and are diminished in part by an organic conception 18 and by a clear description of the purpose for which Scripture was given 19.

**§ 12.** *Attributes of Scripture*, pp. 363–415. The doctrine of the attributes of Scripture developed in the conflict with Rome, which defines the relation of Scripture and church altogether differently from the Reformation 1, 2. The *authority* of Scripture rests at Rome upon the church 3, and according to the Reformation, without misconstruing the pedagogical significance of the church, upon Scripture itself 4. Although the *auctoritas historiae et normae* must be distinguished in that authority 5, its divine character must nevertheless not be exchanged for a human, historical, moral authority 6. For in religion a divine authority, which is nevertheless altogether different from force, is indispensable 7. The *necessity* of Scripture is denied by Rome, by many mystical tendencies 8, and also by Schleiermacher 9, but nevertheless stands firm, since the church has always rested upon an unwritten or written Word of God 10, falls into error and falsehood without such a Word 11, and in this dispensation continually remains in need of it 12. The *clarity* of Scripture is likewise rejected by Rome for the benefit of the church 13, but, properly understood, is grounded in Scripture itself, was generally acknowledged by the ancient church 14, and is of the greatest significance for maintaining the freedom of the Christian 15 and the *facultas S. Scr. se ipsam interpretandi* 16. Finally, the *sufficiency* of Scripture was contested by Rome in favor of tradition 17, which in the end retains no other criterion for its truth than the papal pronouncement 18 and subjects Scripture to the church, that is, to the pope 19; it was again maintained by the Reformation over against tradition 20, since it is taught by Scripture itself and accords with the nature of the present dispensation 21, without thereby denying the relative right of tradition 22.

### Chapter III. Internal Principle

**§ 13.** *Significance of the Internal Principle*, pp. 416–421. A subjective principle must correspond to the objective principle; in each person, from childhood onward, it assumes a definite form in accordance with the religion into which he was born 1, and in its deepest ground it almost entirely eludes all investigation 2. Therefore many and various grounds are also named for faith (historical-apologetic, speculative, ethical-practical), but these misconstrue the independence of religion and the work of the Spirit 3.

**§ 14.** *The Historical-Apologetic Method*, pp. 423–431, was applied especially by Rome 1 and by various supernaturalistic tendencies 2, but, although a believing apologetics has a right to exist, is theoretically and practically untenable 3.

**§ 15.** *The Speculative Method*, pp. 431–444, followed by Hegel 1, by the *Vermittlungstheologie* 2, and in modified form by Hofmann and Frank 3, did offer important advantages over the apologetic method, but fell into the error of ancient Gnosticism and sacrificed being to thought, history to the idea 4. Frank’s attempt to derive the objective truth of the *dogmata* from the subjective certainty of the Christian was likewise unsuccessful 5 and rests upon an incorrect *Erkenntnistheorie* 6.

**§ 16.** *The Ethical-Psychological Method*, pp. 444–465, although customary from ancient times, came into honor through Pascal and Vinet 1. It is related to Kant’s moral proof 2, and in this form finds support among many in this country 3, and especially also in Ritschl and Lipsius, *c. s.* 4, 5. But although religion cannot lack the element of consolation, the experience of the heart 6, no more than religious valuation, can be proof of the truth of religion 7, and it moreover leads to an untenable dualism between believing and knowing 8.

**§ 17.** *Faith*, pp. 465–484. Since the *principium internum* must correspond to the *princ. ext.*, it cannot lie in the natural human being but only in the human being enlightened by God’s Spirit, so that the dogmatician thereby adopts a subjective standpoint 1. Scripture names faith as this *princ. internum*, an act of consciousness related to faith in the natural sphere 2, but nevertheless clearly to be distinguished from it 3 and also not to be identified with *nudus assensus* 4. That faith brings a certainty of its own 5, which alongside scientific and moral certainty has a right to exist 6.

**§ 18.** *The Ground of Faith*, pp. 484–509. Since this faith can be worked by no proofs or arguments but only by God’s grace 1, Rome thereby adopts in principle the same subjective standpoint as the Reformation 2. The *testimonium Sp. S.* is the final ground of faith, as the Reformation, especially Calvin, acknowledged but Rationalism wrongly opposed 3. For it accords with the testimony that our spirit in general gives to the truth 4, corresponds to special revelation in Christ, and maintains its autopisty 5. Further, it does not exclude argumentation, is strong as the testimony of the whole of Christendom, presupposes regeneration, and arises from it spontaneously and freely 6. Nor does it relate one-sidedly to the authority of Holy Scripture, but stands in connection with the whole life of faith, gives subjective assurance of all God’s truth and therein also of the *divinitas* of Holy Scripture 7, and is not deprived of its truth by the mutual differences among Christians 8.

**§ 19.** *Faith and Theology*, pp. 509–532. Although certain of the truth in faith, the Christian church nevertheless always strove, despite all opposition, for a scientific theology 1, which, notwithstanding its many deviations, has a right to exist and without which revelation, religion, the history of dogma, and the church are in danger of being misconstrued 2. This theology is a fruit of the thinking consciousness of the church and needs the assistance of philosophy 3. Faith is therefore distinct from theology, as was also formerly assumed, as appears from the doctrine of *fides implicita* 4, the distinction between *theologia infusa* and *acquisita*, and that between fundamental and nonfundamental articles 5. But their affinity is as great as their difference 6. And since theology has its origin not in faith as such but in believing thought, developed reason and thus philosophy are indispensable for its cultivation 7, even though the knowledge theology provides never becomes comprehension and ends in wonder at the mystery 8.
