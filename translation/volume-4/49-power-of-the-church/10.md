## 10.

In the third place, Christ is also priest and still always exercises this office from heaven in his church through intercession and blessing. Just as he teaches his own as prophet and governs them as king, so as priest he shows them the riches of his mercy. When he was on earth, he went about the cities and villages, not only teaching in the synagogues and preaching the gospel of the kingdom but also healing every disease and every infirmity among the people, Matt. 9:35. And this was not an incidental and accidental activity but a principal component of the work that the Father had commissioned him to do, Matt. 8:17; John 5:36; 9:3, 4; etc. The fullness of his power and the riches of his mercy were revealed in it; the works of sin and Satan were destroyed by it; the consequences of sin in the physical world were initially removed by it; and these works culminated and received their seal and completion in the resurrection, which was the victory over death and the beginning of the renewal of all things. Accordingly, when he sends out his disciples, he gives them not only the commission to preach the gospel but just as firmly and emphatically to cast out unclean spirits and to heal every disease and every infirmity, Matt. 10:1, 8; Mark 3:15; Luke 9:1, 2; 10:9, 17. The disciples fulfilled that commission not only during Jesus’ sojourn on earth but also after his ascension, Acts 5:16; 8:7; etc. Indeed, according to Jesus’ own promise, Mark 16:17, 18, in the first period believers were granted many extraordinary gifts of healing and workings of powers, Acts 2:44, 45; 4:35; Rom. 12:7, 8; 1 Cor. 12:28. But as it went with the gifts of teaching and the gifts of government, so it also went with those of mercy. The extraordinary condition of the church gradually became normal. And although the gifts were not suppressed or destroyed, they were nevertheless gradually connected more and more with the office. Teaching was entrusted to the *didaskalos*, government to the presbyter, and likewise the ministry of mercy to the deacon, Acts 6. And the gifts themselves, though remaining gifts of the Holy Spirit, acquired a simpler and more ordinary character. Rome indeed claims that the power of miracles continues with her, vol. I 270, but more beautiful than the miracles of which she boasts are the works of mercy that bear a powerful witness to her faith and love. For when the diaconate in the Christian church gradually changed character entirely, the treasure of love and mercy that Christ pours out in his church manifested itself richly in private benevolence. Although the ordering of the ministry of mercy in Rome leaves much to be desired, she nevertheless occupies first place among the Christian churches in works of love. For although the Reformed Church restored the office of deacon, she did not properly order its place and ministry or bring its activity to development. This development, toward which the need of the times now presses, can take place in the main only in this direction: 1º that the office of deacon be honored more than hitherto as an independent organ of Christ’s priestly mercy; 2º that love and mercy be acknowledged and practiced as the preeminent Christian virtues; 3º that deacons be charged to move all members of the church, especially the wealthy, in the name of Christ to mercy, and to warn and guard them against the sin of greed, which is a root of all evil; 4º that the diaconate not extinguish private benevolence but awaken, regulate, and guide it; 5º that the bearers of this office, when necessary in large churches, make use of the help of deaconesses, in the same way as the other two offices make use of catechists and comforters of the sick; 6º that they distribute their gifts in Christ’s name as taken from the tables of the Lord, upon which they have been laid by the church and given to Christ himself, Matt. 25:40; 7º that they extend their help to all the poor, the sick, strangers, prisoners, persons with intellectual disabilities, the mentally ill, widows, orphans—in a word, to all the wretched and needy who are in the midst of the church—and meet them in their suffering with word and deed; 8º that the ministry of mercy receive a much larger place on the agenda of all ecclesiastical assemblies than has hitherto been the case; 9º that deacons be delegated with ministers and elders to the broader assemblies of the churches and receive a deciding vote in all matters touching the ministry of mercy; 10º that at these assemblies the ministry of mercy be ordered according to general principles, with due regard for the differences in the circumstances of the churches; that general needs be undertaken jointly; and that it be extended from the local church to the support of other churches and further also to assistance for poor and oppressed fellow believers abroad; and 11º that this diaconal labor be maintained in its independence and not be absorbed into or mingled with the work of the *inneren Mission*, or with state poor relief, which bear an altogether different character.
