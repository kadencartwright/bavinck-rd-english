## 2.

When Jesus, coming to his own, was not received by his own, he organized his disciples into an ἐκκλησια, which, hoping and suffering, was to await his second coming and the victory over all his enemies. This church displays some similarity to the synagogue in organization and worship, yet it is a still freer and more independent organization of the new life that Christ had brought to light. For there can be no doubt that Christ founded such a church and entrusted a certain power to it. He himself speaks of it as built so firmly upon a rock that the gates of hell will not prevail against it, Matt. 16:18, and furthermore he gives to that church offices, ministries, institutions, and gifts, Rom. 12:6f.; 1 Cor. 12–14; Eph. 4:11, all of which indicate that it has an existence of its own—free and independent—and an autonomous organization. But this power that Christ grants to his church bears a distinctive character. It consists in nothing other, but also in nothing less, than the power of the keys, which Christ first bestowed upon Peter, Matt. 16:19. After Peter had first been called a rock, on account of his confession of Jesus’ Messiahship, upon which Christ would build his church, Christ then appointed him in verse 19 as οἰκονομος of the kingdom of heaven and handed over to him the keys of that kingdom. Keys are a sign of dominion, Isa. 22:22; Luke 11:52; Rev. 1:18; 3:7; 9:1; 20:1, and here they designate Peter’s power to open and close the kingdom of heaven, that is, to determine who will enter it and who will not. His judgment, after all, will also be valid in heaven; whatever he shall bind or loose (release, set free) will also be so reckoned by God. In these words Peter is not granted legislative power, but he is granted a judging or judicial power; because he is a confessor of Jesus as the Christ, in that confession he has a standard for determining what does and does not belong to the church and thus what will and will not enter the kingdom of heaven. In Matt. 18:18 the church in general receives the power to regard an impenitent person as a Gentile and a tax collector, for its judgment is valid in the heavens. And in John 20:23 all the apostles, on the basis of the gift of the Spirit bestowed on them in verse 22, receive the power to forgive or retain people’s sins. From this it is clear that the power of the church, and specifically of Peter and the apostles, consists in pronouncing a judicial judgment upon people’s relation to the kingdom of heaven according to the standard of the confession of Jesus as the Christ and the gift of the Holy Spirit. This power is repeatedly defined more precisely in the New Testament. It is not an authoritarian, independent, sovereign dominion, Matt. 20:25, 26; 23:8, 10; 2 Cor. 10:4, 5; 1 Pet. 5:3, but a διακονια, λειτουργια, Acts 4:29; 20:24; Rom. 1:1, etc.; it is bound to Christ, who has all power in heaven and on earth, Matt. 28:18, who is the only head of the church, Eph. 1:22, and who as such distributes all gifts and offices, Eph. 4:11; bound to his Word and Spirit, through which Christ himself governs his church, Rom. 10:14, 15; Eph. 5:26; and exercised in his name and power, 1 Cor. 5:4. It is therefore indeed a power, a real and comprehensive power, consisting in the administration of Word and sacrament, Matt. 28:19; in opening and closing the kingdom of heaven, Matt. 16:19; in forgiving or retaining sins, John 20:23; in exercising discipline over the members of the church, Matt. 18:18; Rom. 16:17; 1 Cor. 5:4; 2 Thess. 3:6; Titus 3:10; 2 John 10; 2 Tim. 2:17; Heb. 12:15–17; Rev. 2:14; in discerning all things, 1 Cor. 2:15; in teaching, comforting, admonishing, and so forth the brothers, Col. 3:16; in employing gifts for the benefit of others, Rom. 12:4–8; 1 Cor. 12:12f.; in performing miracles, Mark 16:17, 18, etc. But all this power is spiritual and moral in nature, essentially distinct from every other power that God has given to human beings over other human beings or over other creatures in family, society, state, art, and science. For Jesus appeared in no other capacity than as the Christ, as prophet, priest, and king; he held no other office and discharged no other function. He was not a head of a household, a scholar, an artist, or a statesman; he honored all the ordinances and works of the Father and came only to destroy the works of the devil, 1 John 3:8. His kingdom did not have its origin in this world, John 18:36. And therefore he recognizes every civil authority, the high priest, the Sanhedrin, Herod, Pilate, and so forth; he pays the tax, Matt. 17:24; refuses to be an arbiter between two brothers disputing over an inheritance, Luke 12:14; commands that Caesar be given what is his, Matt. 22:21; rebukes John when he wishes to call down fire from heaven, Luke 9:55, and Peter when he cuts off Malchus’s ear, John 18:10; and forbids his disciples to fight with the sword for his name and cause, Matt. 26:52. The gospel of Christ never takes up the struggle against nature as such; it did not come to condemn the world but to save it, John 3:16, 17. It leaves family, marriage, the relation of parents and children, of masters and servants, and of government and people untouched; it finds nothing objectionable in itself and every creature good if it is received with thanksgiving and sanctified by the Word of God and by prayer, 1 Tim. 4:4. It lets everyone remain in the calling in which he was called, 1 Cor. 7:12–24; 1 Thess. 4:11; teaches respect for the civil government, Rom. 13:1; 1 Tim. 2:2; 1 Pet. 2:13; and even allows slavery to remain, 1 Cor. 7:22; Philem. 11. Even when it commands obedience to God rather than to human beings, it preaches only passive resistance, Acts 4:19; 5:29. But nevertheless, though averse to every revolution, it is all the more intent on reformation. It never takes up the struggle against nature as such, but everywhere and always, in every domain and into the most hidden recesses, it does take up the struggle against sin and falsehood. And so it preaches principles that work their way through everywhere, not by a revolutionary but by a moral and spiritual path, and reform and renew everything. While, according to Jesus’ command, it must be preached to every creature, Mark 16:15, it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes, Rom. 1:16; a sharp two-edged sword that penetrates to the division of soul and spirit, Heb. 4:12; leaven that leavens everything, Matt. 13:33; a principle that recreates everything; a power that conquers the world, 1 John 5:4.
