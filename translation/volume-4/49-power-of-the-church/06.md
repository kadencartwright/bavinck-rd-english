## 6.

That Christ granted a certain power to his church on earth is hardly open to doubt. Already in general it is an undeniable truth that nothing can exist without order and rule, that essence without form is inconceivable, and that a proper ὑλη in every place and every domain is nothing but a philosophical abstraction. A household cannot exist without a head, a people without a civil government, an association without a governing board, an army without a general, and so forth; anarchy is impossible. To say that Christ founded a church without any organization, government, or power is a claim that arises from mystical-philosophical principles but takes account neither of the teaching of Scripture nor of the reality of life. The question that divides is therefore not properly whether the church of Christ, in order to exist, needs a certain power and government, for all agree with that, whether they have the congregation give itself this government or assign it to the civil government. Rather, the disagreement concerns whether Christ himself in his Word—naturally not in every kind of detail but in principles and main points—granted his church a power and government which therefore also constitutes, and may constitute, an article of our faith and a part of our confession, *Ned. Gel.*, arts. 30–32. But this disagreement too is decided by Holy Scripture as firmly and clearly as possible. Christ indeed said that his kingdom is not of this world, but he is not a spiritual king in the sense that he is absolutely unconcerned with the external and earthly. On the contrary, he assumed the full human nature and came into the world not to condemn it but to save it. He planted his kingdom in that world and took care that it can exist there, propagate and expand itself, and work renewingly as leaven upon every domain of life. His work was to destroy the works of the devil everywhere and to bring the justice and honor of God to acknowledgment; intensively, his reconciling and redeeming activity extends as far as sin has devastated and corrupted everything. Therefore, he does not merely bring some individuals to faith by his Spirit so that they might then freely unite and serve one another with the gifts of the Spirit they have received. Rather, he founds a congregation, a church, and from the outset organizes it in such a way that it can exist, propagate and expand itself, and fulfill its task on earth. For the sake of clarity, a distinction may and must be made between the essence and the government of the church. But this distinction may never be understood as if believers had originally been deprived of every government and power. On the contrary, the preceding section brought to light that from the first moment of her existence after the fall the church possessed a certain organization: first in the patriarchal families, then in the people of Israel, and since Christ’s coming to earth in the various extraordinary and ordinary offices he instituted in his congregation, Mark 3:14; Luke 10:1; Acts 20:28; 1 Cor. 12:28; Eph. 4:11. Every office, however, includes a power, right, and authority. There are admittedly many gifts in the congregation which, bestowed by the Holy Spirit, reveal themselves as διακονιαι of Christ and as ἐνεργηματα of God the Father and serve for the mutual edification of the congregation, 1 Cor. 12:4f. Nevertheless, Christ joined to the offices he instituted in his congregation a special power, ἐξουσια, consisting in the preaching of the gospel, Matt. 10:7; Mark 3:14; 16:15; Luke 9:2; etc.; in the administration of the sacraments, Matt. 28:19; Mark 16:15; Luke 22:19; 1 Cor. 11:24–26; in the performance of all kinds of miracles, Matt. 10:1, 8; Mark 3:15; 16:18; Luke 9:1; 10:9, 19; etc.; in retaining or forgiving sins, Matt. 16:19; 18:18; John 20:23; in shepherding the flock, John 21:15–17; Acts 20:28; in exercising discipline, Matt. 18:17; 1 Cor. 5:4; in serving tables, Acts 6:2; and in the right to live from the gospel, Matt. 10:10; 1 Cor. 9:4f.; 2 Thess. 3:9; 1 Tim. 5:18. This description Scripture gives of the power of the church indicates not only its indubitable existence but also its complete independence and distinctive character over against every other power in the world. There are many kinds of power and authority on earth, in the household, society, state, art, science, and so forth. But ecclesiastical power is essentially distinct from all these and fully independent over against them. For all that other power comes from God as Creator of heaven and earth, Rom. 13:1, but this ecclesiastical power has its origin directly in God as the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, 1 Cor. 12:28; Eph. 4:11; Acts 20:28, and is therefore entirely free and independent with respect to every other earthly power. Whoever, with Caesaropapism or Erastianism, curtails and limits this power of the church and assigns it to the civil government detracts from the honor of Christ and infringes upon the rights and freedoms granted to the church. This power of the church must remain independent over against every other earthly power, because it is wholly distinctive, cannot be taken over or exercised by any other power, and would thus be robbed of its nature and destroyed by such a transfer. For all the power Christ gave to his church—the ministry of Word and sacrament, exercise of discipline, service of tables, and so forth—has, in addition to an origin of its own, an organ of its own, a nature of its own, and a goal of its own. It is bound to offices which Christ alone instituted in his congregation, for which he alone grants and can grant the gifts, and to which he alone calls and sends. No one takes this honor to himself except one called by God, Rom. 10:15; Heb. 5:4. Further, this power is spiritual. That does not mean that it is invisible and wholly inward, for Christ is indeed a spiritual king but rules over both soul and body; his Word and sacrament address the whole human being; the ministry of mercy even has especially to relieve bodily needs. But when the power of the church is called spiritual, this signifies that it is granted by the Holy Spirit of God, Acts 20:28; can be exercised only in the name of Christ and the power of the Holy Spirit, John 20:22, 23; 1 Cor. 5:4; extends exclusively over human beings as believers, 1 Cor. 5:12; and works and can work only in a spiritual, moral manner—not with coercion and punishment in money, property, or life, but through conviction, faith, goodwill, freedom, love, and thus with spiritual weapons alone, 2 Cor. 10:4; Mark 16:16; John 8:32; 2 Cor. 3:17; Eph. 6:7; etc. Finally, this power also has a goal of its own. Although it also brings an aggravation of judgment for unbelievers, it extends to salvation, to edification and not destruction, to the perfecting of the saints and the building up of Christ’s body, Matt. 10:13; Mark 16:16; Luke 2:34; 2 Cor. 2:16; 10:4, 8; 13:10; Eph. 4:12; 6:11–18; etc.; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* IV 783. Through all this ecclesiastical power is generically distinct from every political power. Already under the Old Testament, state and church, although closely joined, were nevertheless not one and the same. But Christ expressed much more clearly the distinction between his kingdom and the kingdoms of the world, Matt. 22:21; John 18:36. He himself refused every earthly power, Luke 12:13, 14; John 6:15, and forbade his disciples everything that resembled worldly dominion, Matt. 20:25, 26; 1 Pet. 5:3. There are accordingly all kinds of differences between church and state and the power of each: not only in origin, as was already observed above, but also, further, in organs, for the offices in Christ’s congregation are all διακονιαι, but the political government is sovereign and, although God’s servant, nevertheless has the right and power to issue laws and demand submission to them; in kind and nature, for the power of the church is spiritual, but the power of the political government is natural, earthly, worldly, extends over all subjects without any qualification other than that they are subjects, and regulates only their earthly interests; in goal, for ecclesiastical power extends to the building up of the body of Christ, but political power has its destination in this life and strives for the *bonum naturale et commune*; in means, for the church has no weapons other than spiritual ones, but the civil government bears the sword, possesses the right over life and death, and may demand obedience by coercion and force. Therefore, as impermissible as it is on the one hand to assign ecclesiastical power to the civil government, so sinful it is on the other hand to change ecclesiastical power into a political power. Romanism and Anabaptism are both guilty of this, because both proceed from the antithesis between nature and grace. Anabaptism makes that antithesis absolute and thereby destroys nature; Rome conceives it relatively and suppresses nature. In the Middle Ages, when the Roman church possessed sole dominion, this tendency appeared more clearly; but in principle she has not changed and is still driven by the same desire to free the clergy as much as possible from political subjection; to draw all kinds of civil matters within her circle and subject them to her judgment; to shine with external splendor and pomp, expand possession of capital and real estate, and exercise political influence at the courts; to claim for the pope, upon the basis of Matt. 28:18 and according to the theory of the two swords, if not direct then nevertheless indirect power over the whole world; and so forth; cf. Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 115. Yet the Roman church is not only continually intent upon making all earthly political power subservient to herself; worse still is that she deprives ecclesiastical power itself of its spiritual character and changes it into political dominion. First, this becomes manifest in the fact that the Roman church ascribes to herself—that is, to the pope—the highest legislative power. Formerly this power was still limited by Scripture and tradition, by bishops and councils; the government was a monarchy tempered by aristocracy. But since the promulgation of the dogma of infallibility, this relation has been reversed. In a formal sense the pope is an absolute monarch. By virtue of the claimed assistance of the Holy Spirit, he infallibly determines what must be believed and done. There is no higher appeal. What he binds or looses is bound or loosed in heaven; what he says has as much authority as if it were spoken by Christ himself. The dogmas and laws he promulgates bind the conscience and oblige to faith and obedience on pain of eternal perdition. From the civil government there is an appeal to God, but the sovereignty of the pope is the very highest; God himself speaks through his mouth. Second, the Roman church ascribes to herself—that is, to the pope—the highest judicial power. Ecclesiastical power is twofold, *potestas ordinis* and *potestas jurisdictionis*; the *potestas docendi*, even when it is named separately, properly belongs to the *potestas jurisdictionis*. This implies that at Rome the ministry of Word and sacrament is no proclamation of the gospel but a legal act and judicial pronouncement. All baptized persons belong to the pope not in a moral, spiritual sense but *rechtens*, in a juridical sense, with an immutable and inalienable right. They are his sheep, whom he may if necessary bring back into the sheepfold by force, even if the circumstances of the time perhaps prevent him from doing so. *Nach dem Rechte der katholischen Kirche gehören eigentlich alle Getauften zur Kirche, also auch zur Parochie, aber in Ermangelung von Zwangsmitteln fehlt gegenüber Andersgläubigen die Durchführbarkeit dieses Anspruchs*, Vering, *Lehrb. des kath. orient, u. prot. Kirchenrechts*³, 603. And all members of the Roman church come under preaching and to the sacrament of penance to hear their judgment. The confessional is a tribunal, the priest a judge. After hearing the accusations the penitent brings against himself, the priest pronounces the sentence. He binds and looses, not deprecatively and conditionally but, by virtue of the official spirit dwelling in him, peremptorily and absolutely; as he judges, so God judges in heaven. Third, Rome—that is, the pope—lays claim to the highest executive and coercive power. The distinction between ecclesiastical and civil punishment has no value for Rome. If the church judges it useful and is able to do so, she applies the latter just as well as the former. It is true that she did not execute the death penalty, for *ecclesia non sitit sanguinem*. But otherwise she left no means untried to compel disobedient children to submission. And Rome was inventive. Monetary penalty, penance, prison, inquisition, rack, assassination, ban, interdict, release of subjects from obedience to their ruler, and so forth—all have rendered service. That was, and in principle still is, Rome’s conception of ecclesiastical power.

Over against this, the Reformers once again conceived the *potestas ecclesiastica* in the sense of Scripture as a spiritual power. Thus the *potestas docendi*, the ministry of Word and sacrament, naturally came to the foreground. The Lutherans even, at least in practice, let the whole of ecclesiastical power be absorbed into it; they had only *pastores*, no presbyters and deacons. But the Reformed also restored these offices and therefore included the *potestas jurisdictionis* alongside the *potestas docendi*. The word *jurisdictio*, however, found no general approval, although Calvin, *Inst.* IV 11, adopted it. Coccejus, on 1 Cor. 5, rejected it; Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* 15, 75f.; 16, 70a, said that, speaking purely and precisely, there was no *jurisdictio* in the church and that in the ecclesiastical sphere the word could be understood only *analogice*. All acknowledged that the *jurisdictio* in the church was of an altogether different kind than that in the state and bore a spiritual character, Voetius, *Pol.* IV 798; and many avoided the word and preferred to speak of *potestas gubernans, ordinans, disciplinaris*, and so forth. Further, some distinguished a twofold but others a threefold power. The power of the church was in fact not exhausted in the ministry of Word and sacrament and in the exercise of discipline; she also had the right and authority, in the interest of order, to make laws and take measures of every kind. Thus alongside, and frequently between, the *potestas docendi (doctrinae, scientiae, dogmatica, ordinis)* and the *potestas disciplinae (critica, jurisdictionis, correptionis)* there came to stand the *potestas directionis (regiminis, ordinis, diatactica, legislativa)*: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 8, 1; Bucanus, *Inst.*, 519; Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* 16, 70; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 118; Vitringa IX 1, p. 457. It is remarkable that in connection with this power of the church the diaconate was never brought up. Yet in it too Christ gave his church a power that is of the greatest significance. A threefold power is therefore to be distinguished in his church in connection with the offices of pastor, presbyter, and deacon, and further in connection with the threefold office of Christ, the prophetic, royal, and priestly office: the *potestas docendi*, the *potestas gubernans* (of which the *potestas disciplinae* is a part), and the *potestas misericordiae*.
