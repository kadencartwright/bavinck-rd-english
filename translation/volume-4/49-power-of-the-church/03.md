## 3.

This apostolic doctrine of ecclesiastical power continued to be acknowledged in the Christian church for a considerable time. At first the thought did not arise that the poor, small congregation would one day become a world church that prescribed laws to rulers and nations. All that was desired was to be permitted to lead a peaceful and quiet life under the pagan government in all godliness and dignity. But when the church attained prestige and dominion, its power too came to be conceived altogether differently. The development of episcopacy and tradition, of the ideas of priest and sacrifice, entailed that ordination was regarded as a sacramental act which, performed by the bishop, communicated the official spirit and granted the right and competence to carry out ecclesiastical ceremonies. And although the power of the keys, granted to Peter in Matt. 16:19, was in the first period understood of the forgiveness of sins through combination with Matt. 18:18 and John 20:23—Cyprian, *de unit. eccl.* 4; *Ep.* 75, 16—it gradually acquired a juridical character, especially through the sacrament of penance. According to Rome, therefore, the power of the church is twofold: *potestas ordinis* and *potestas jurisdictionis*, of which the latter is in turn distinguished into *jurisdictio fori interni (sacramentalis)* and *fori externi (legifera, judiciaria*, and *coactiva)*: Thomas, *S. Theol.* II 2, qu. 39, art. 3; *Catech. Rom.* II 7, 6; *Conc. Vat.*, ed. Lacensis, col. 570; Klee, *Kath. Dogm.* I² 162; Dieringer, *Kath. Dogm.*⁴ 619, 715; Liebermann, *Inst. Theol.* I⁸ 290; Simar, *Dogm.*, 593; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 1, p. 317f.; Schell, *Kath. Dogm.* III 1, p. 396; Jansen, *Prael. theol.* I 380 sq.; etc. For a correct understanding of this power ascribed by Rome to the church, the following must be kept in view. 1º The *potestas docendi* is indeed sometimes treated separately by later theologians and naturally, according to Rome, belongs to the church as well. But properly it is a part of the *potestas jurisdictionis*. The *Catech. Rom.* II 7, 7 might suggest that the *potestas docendi* belongs under the *potestas ordinis*, because it says that the latter contains not only the power to administer the Eucharist, *sed ad eam accipiendam hominum animos praeparat et idoneos reddit*; but *Conc. Vatic.* IV, chs. 3, 4 expressly brings the *magisterium* under the *potestas jurisdictionis*. At Rome the ministry of the Word is a judicial pronouncement, culminating in the infallible decisions of the pope. It is not preaching but a promulgation of dogmas which as such bind the conscience, oblige to faith—that is, to *assensus*—and, if necessary, can be imposed by coercion: Richter-Dove-Kahl, *Kirchenrecht*, 305; Achelis, *Prakt. Theol.* I² 79. 2º The *potestas ordinis*, the power to administer the sacraments, can be obtained only through the *sacramentum ordinis* conferred by the bishop, which communicates the official spirit and impresses a *character indelebilis*, and is therefore incapable of being lost, Thomas, *S. Theol.* II 2, qu. 39, art. 3. Even heretics and schismatics who were once ordained at Rome by the bishop retain this power; it therefore also stands by itself and is wholly independent of the ministry of the Word. At Rome the *sacerdotium* can also exist without preaching the gospel. *Si quis dixerit . . . sacerdotium . . . non esse potestatem aliquam consecrandi et offerendi verum corpus et sanguinem Domini et peccata remittendi et retinendi, sed officium tantum et nudum ministerium praedicandi evangelium, vel eos, qui non praedicant, prorsus non esse sacerdotes, anathema sit*, *Conc. Trid.*, sess. 23, *de sacr. ordinis*, can. 1. 3º In agreement with this, at Rome the forgiveness of sins is not granted in the preaching of the Word, which has merely preparatory significance, but in the sacrament, which contains grace within itself and infuses it into the recipient *ex opere operato*. Specifically, it is communicated in baptism and, for sins committed after baptism, in the sacrament of penance. Discipline was, in fact, very strict in the first Christian church. When experience taught that by no means all who had become Christians walked worthily of their calling and that they frequently fell into sins of the flesh and, during the persecutions, especially into denial of the gospel, the admonitions increased to confess sins in the midst of the congregation, especially before participating in the Supper: *Did.* 4:14; Clement, *1* and *2 Cor.*, passim. A prolonged conflict arose over the return of such fallen and excluded persons into the bosom of the church. Montanism, Novatianism, and Donatism wanted to close the door of the church to such fallen persons, particularly to those who had become guilty of denial, murder, or sexual immorality, and to leave them to God’s grace; cf. Heb. 6:4–6; 10:26; 1 John 5:16. But Hermas, *Mand.* IV 1, already said in the case of adultery that one repentance was possible. In Tertullian’s time a distinction was already made between venial and mortal sins. Those who committed the former, even if they were temporarily excluded from the congregation, underwent only a *castigatio*, not a *damnatio*, Tertullian, *de pudic.* 7. Those guilty of the latter, especially idolatry, adultery, or murder, were nevertheless, upon repentance, admitted again as it were into the forecourt of the church. The development of the catholic idea of the church (as an institute of salvation), the concentration of all ecclesiastical power in the bishop, and the doctrine of the meritoriousness of good works (*satisfactio, meritum*) favored the readmission of fallen and excommunicated persons by means of penance. Callistus, the bishop of Rome, proclaimed in 217 that he also forgave the sins of sexual immorality in those who repented. A council at Carthage in 252 allowed all the fallen, in case of penitence, reconciliation or absolution, Cyprian, *Ep.* 57, although one who returned still had to pass through different stages before being received into the full communion of the church (προσκλαυσις, ἀκροασις, ὑποπτωσις, συστασις). And in 316 the Donatists were condemned who maintained that no excommunicated person, or person worthy of excommunication, could administer the sacrament: Moeller-v. Schubert, *Kirchengesch.* I 96, 133, 278, 298, 358, 415, and the literature cited there. In penance, which gradually came to be understood as a sacrament and consisted of three parts (*confessio oris, contritio cordis*, and *satisfactio operis*), the church created for herself the possibility of receiving again into her full communion all who had fallen into lesser or greater sins after baptism. After baptism she was a *secunda post naufragium tabula*, *Trid.* XIV, ch. 2. At Rome all discipline was absorbed into this systematized penance, and this penance became an *actus judicialis*, a tribunal in which the priests sit as *praesides* and *judices*, hear the confession of the *mortalia crimina*, determine the penalty casuistically according to the standard of the *libri poenitentiarii*, and in the name of Christ grant forgiveness of sins (absolution) not conditionally and declaratively but absolutely, categorically, and peremptorily. This juridical character of penance is also evident in the fact that this sacrament may be administered only to those who are baptized, since the church has jurisdiction over none but those who through baptism stand under her power; that believers may receive this sacrament only from the hand of that priest whose *subditi* they are according to ecclesiastical—that is, papal—ordinance; and that higher clergy, bishops, and so forth, and especially the pope, reserve to themselves certain scandalous cases in which they alone can judge and decide, as, for example, when the popes applied the ban and interdict to rulers and lands in the Middle Ages: *Conc. Trid.* XIV; *Cat. Rom.* II 5, 32 sq. 4º In order to exercise this jurisdiction *in foro interno*, the Roman church further claims to possess the *potestas jurisdictionis in foro externo (potestas regiminis)*, distinguished into *potestas legislativa, judiciaria*, and *coactiva*. In order that the church might be faithful to her calling, Christ first of all gave her a legislative power. She may bind and loose, forbid or permit, impose or annul moral obligations; and all that she determines is valid in heaven. It is just as if God himself commands it; it therefore binds consciences and obliges to unconditional obedience, Matt. 16:19; 18:18; John 20:21, 23; Acts 15:27–29, 41; 1 Cor. 11:4–7; 14:26; 2 Cor. 8; 10:6, 8; 1 Tim. 3; Titus 1:5; Heb. 13:7, 17. This legislative power of itself includes the judicial, since the former could not exist without the latter. Christ gave this power to the congregation in Matt. 18:15–17, and the apostles exercised it, Acts 5:1–10; 1 Cor. 5:3, 11–13; 1 Tim. 5:19, 20. And finally the church also has an executive and coercive power and can impose not only spiritual penalties, as Donatists, Waldenses, Albigenses, and others maintained, but also temporal and corporal penalties, and that not only upon the authority of or through the state, but also herself, by her own authority and directly. Rome grounds this power upon Matt. 16:19; 18:18; 28:19; 1 Cor. 4:18–21; 5:4–5; 2 Cor. 10:6, 8; 13:2, 3; 1 Tim. 1:20; has repeatedly taught it expressly, Denzinger, *Enchir. symb. et defin.*, nos. 1367, 1546, 1572; *Conc. Vatic.*, coll. Lac. VII 570, 577; and has also frequently applied it; cf. Perrone, *Prael. Theol.*, Louvain, 1843, VII 275; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 1, p. 322; Jansen, *Prael. theol.* I 390; etc. 5º Finally, Rome teaches that this ecclesiastical power, essentially distinct from every earthly power, is fully independent and sovereign. She does say that with respect to Christ this power is a ministering one, a *ministerium*; but over against every earthly authority and power she is entirely independent. With this doctrine of the independence of ecclesiastical power she took an altogether different path from the church of the East. There, through Constantine, Theodosius, and Justinian I, the church increasingly became an organ within the state. The emperor could not yet do whatever he wished with the church, for he was bound to dogma and was no αρχιερευς but only εὐσεβης, protector of orthodoxy; nevertheless, just as he was the governing head of the state, so he was also the governing head of the church. This conception still prevails today in the Russian church. In 1721 Peter the Great placed the supreme administration of the church in the hands of a permanent Holy Synod, which is bound to the czar through the intermediary of the procurator. Although the power of the czar is in many respects limited and weakened in comparison with that of the Byzantine emperors, it is nevertheless he who governs the church through the Synod, regulates the religious affairs of his people, and determines the measure of freedom for his Roman Catholic and Protestant subjects. In Russia orthodox dogma is still formally constitutional law and heresy a crime against the state; cf. Pobedonoszew, *Streitfragen der Gegenwart. Autor. Uebersetzung*, Berlin, 1897; Herzog² 5, 425f.; Kattenbusch, *Vergl. Confessionskunde* I 374–393. Thus, while Caesaropapism developed in the East, the church in the West, organized in the pope, knew how not only to maintain her independence against the state but frequently also to extend her supremacy over it. In Charlemagne the empire became a Christian, a Roman institution, and from that time onward was frequently subordinate to the pope. And this was not only practice but increasingly became theory as well. According to Rome, state (family, society, art, science, all that is earthly) and church stand related as nature and grace, flesh and spirit, *bonum naturale* and *bonum supernaturale*, the temporal and the eternal, the earthly and the heavenly. Just as the moon receives its light from the sun, so rulers owe their temporal power to the pope, who as vicar of Christ possesses all power in heaven and on earth (Alvarus, Pelagius, and others); or at any rate the pope, as head of Christendom, also possesses *summa potestas disponendi de rebus temporalibus omnium Christianorum*, Bellarminus, *de Rom. Pontif.* V 6, 7. Even a temporal territory for the exercise of his sovereign power is absolutely necessary for him. Although the state is free and independent within its own domain, it is nevertheless less than the church, bound to her pronouncement and subject to the church wherever the spiritual intervenes in the natural. The state must be Christian, that is, Roman Catholic, may acknowledge none other than the Roman church as the true church, and is obliged, if the church desires it and does not do so herself, to persecute and punish heretics. Pontif.* V; *de membris eccl.* III; Hergenröther, *Kath. Kirche und Christl. Staat in ihrer geschichtl. Entw.*, 1872; Hammerstein, *Kirche und Staat*, Freiburg, 1883; Stöckl, *Lehrb. d.
