## 9.

Ecclesiastical discipline, *potestas disciplinae*, also belongs especially to the work of the overseers. Hebrew has for discipline the word מוּסָר, which properly means *adstrictio, constrictio* and is translated in Greek by νουθετημα, διδασκαλια, νομος, σοφια, and in the New Testament especially by the word παιδεια. Like the Dutch word *tucht*, from *tien*, to draw, both words indicate in general that something young, tender, small, and weak is carefully nurtured. But because in general, and especially among human beings, this nurture must always at the same time counteract abnormal development, the word discipline acquires the additional meaning of correction, chastisement, and punishment. The words almost never denote instruction and teaching alone—cf., however, Acts 7:22; 22:3—but always an upbringing and instruction that acts correctively and chasteningly. Thus God brings up his children, Heb. 12:5–11, and Christ his congregation, Rev. 3:19, by means of Scripture, which is useful προς διδασκαλιαν, προς ἐλεγμον, προς ἐπανορθωσιν, προς παιδειαν την ἐν δικαιοσυνῃ, 2 Tim. 3:16. And Christ also instituted such discipline in his congregation. In the Old Testament there was not yet a proper ecclesiastical discipline, even though Adam was banished from paradise and the uncircumcised, lepers, and unclean were barred from the sanctuary in Israel, Lev. 5f.; Ezek. 44:9, for atonement always existed for unintentional sins, extirpation was the penalty for sins committed with a high hand, and the ban was at the same time a civil penalty. Only when Israel became a congregation did the exclusively ecclesiastical penalty arise, separation from the congregation of believers, Ezra 10:8; and this ban is still applied by the Jews in some cases, Gunning, *De Chasidim*, p. 55. Perhaps in connection with this synagogal discipline, Christ ordained discipline in his congregation. In Matt. 16:19 he gives the keys of the kingdom of heaven to Peter, in Matt. 18:18 to the congregation, and in John 20:23 to the apostles, so that on the basis of the confession of Christ and under the guidance of the Spirit they possess the power to bind and loose, to retain or forgive someone’s sins. Only because Christ gives this power to his congregation is she authorized to exercise discipline. Then in Matt. 18:15–17 he indicates how this discipline must be exercised. God does not will that one of the little ones who believe in Jesus should perish, Matt. 18:1–14. Thus if someone has been offended or unjustly treated by his brother, he must attempt to win him, first by personal rebuke, then by rebuke before two or three witnesses, and afterward by rebuke on behalf of the whole congregation. Only if all this is of no avail may he, the offended one (σοι, v. 17, in the singular), regard him as a Gentile and a tax collector; he has then tried everything with him and is free of his blood. Such a judgment then has force in heaven. This is the ordinary path discipline must follow in the congregation according to Jesus’ command. But this must indeed be distinguished from the discipline God himself, Christ, and also the apostles sometimes exercise in his name and power. God can visit sins in the congregation—for example, unworthy participation in the Supper—with sickness and death, 1 Cor. 11:30. Ananias and Sapphira fell dead at Peter’s feet because of their lying against the Spirit of God, Acts 5. Paul punished Elymas with blindness, Acts 13:11. In 1 Cor. 5 Paul commands the congregation, while assembled together with his spirit—which had already pronounced judgment upon the incestuous man in their midst, v. 3—and thus joined with the power of the Lord Jesus Christ, to deliver such a person to Satan in Christ’s name, so that he may be stricken by him in his body and yet thus be saved in spirit on the day of Christ. In this Paul rebukes the Corinthians, v. 2, for not having removed him from their midst earlier, and thus presupposes that they had the right and obligation to do so. And precisely because they had not done this but had borne with the sinner and thus become participants in his sin, he now considers a radical measure necessary. He himself, as apostle, has already pronounced the judgment for his own part and now requires that the congregation, in full assembly, immediately and without further admonition, according to the authority now granted it by the apostle—indeed, according to the power of Christ himself—judge the evil person in his name, and not merely place him outside the congregation, as was required of it in v. 2, but specifically deliver him to Satan for bodily affliction. Thus the subject here is not ordinary excommunication, as, for example, in Matt. 18:17, but a special apostolic exercise of power. This is also evident from 1 Tim. 1:20; 2 Tim. 2:17, where Paul as apostle, entirely alone and without the congregation, in the same manner delivers Hymenaeus and Alexander to Satan so that they might be disciplined not to blaspheme any longer. There is therefore a great difference between these extraordinary penalties and the ordinary exercise of discipline entrusted to the congregation. Paul dealt with the latter in 1 Cor. 5:2 and more fully in vv. 9–13. There he says that in an earlier letter, which thus preceded this “first” letter, he admonished them not to associate—that is, have intercourse—with sexually immoral persons. But the Corinthians had misunderstood this and inferred from it that they might have no dealings whatever, not even civil ones, with sexually immoral, greedy, rapacious, and idolatrous persons outside the congregation. But that was not the apostle’s intention. That would have been an impossible demand, equivalent to their having to go out of the world. He had only desired that they should have no dealings with a sexually immoral person and so forth if such a person was called a brother and was a member of the congregation. Indeed, in that case he desires that they should not even share a meal with such a fallen brother—not go to eat with him or invite him to eat, not associate with him amicably and fraternally—but that, while leaving those outside to God’s judgment, they remove from their midst such an evil person moving within their circle; cf. 2 Cor. 2:5–10. Elsewhere Paul similarly speaks of the right and duty of the congregation to watch out for and turn away, ἐκκλινειν, from those who cause divisions and offenses, Rom. 16:17; in Jesus’ name to withdraw, separate, στελλεσθαι ἀπο, from every brother who walks disorderly, 2 Thess. 3:6, 14; and after a first and second—that is, after repeated—admonition, to withdraw from and have nothing to do with (παραιτεισθαι, to free oneself by entreaties from something or someone, let go, dismiss) a heretical person who (as a member of the congregation, or perhaps also from outside) breaks the unity of faith in the congregation, Titus 3:10. John says the same. If someone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine, you may not receive him into your house as a brother, associate with him amicably and fraternally, or greet and welcome him as a brother, 2 John 10. Finally, in Rev. 2:2 the congregation at Ephesus is praised because it does not tolerate the evil, while in Rev. 2:14, 20, 24 those at Pergamum and Thyatira are rebuked because they tolerate heretical teachings and pagan abominations. Cf. Meyer, “Die Lehre des N. T. von der Kirchenzucht,” *Zeits. f. kirchl. Wiss. u. kirchl. Leben*, 1881.

This doctrine of Holy Scripture was applied most purely in the exercise of discipline by the Reformed churches. According to it, 1º the objects are not impersonal things, writings, buildings, or lands, but always persons; and not human beings in general, for God judges those who are outside, 1 Cor. 5:13; not deceased persons, not a class or group of people, but always definite individual persons who are members of the congregation either by baptism alone or also by profession of faith. 2º The causes of discipline are not all kinds of weaknesses that occur in believers, nor even such terrible sins as the Christian civil government punishes—although the church then follows and her discipline is not superfluous, Calvin, *Inst.* IV 11, 3; Bucanus, 539—but such sins as cause offense in the midst of the congregation and are not punished, or only very lightly punished, by the civil government, Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 6, 8. 3º Among these sins to which ecclesiastical discipline applies, a distinction must be made between secret and public sins. The former are treated according to the rule of Matt. 18 and first acquire the character of public sins when private admonitions are of no avail and thus the whole congregation, or its representation in the church council, becomes involved. 4º In the case of these sins made public by obstinacy and those public from the outset by their character (for example, murder and adultery), the procedure is as follows. As soon as the transgressor shows sincere penitence, all ecclesiastical discipline in the narrower sense ceases. The Supper may then still be withheld so that the offense may be removed from the congregation and the sincerity of the confession of guilt may come to light, but discipline is no longer in question. Whoever confesses his sin finds mercy with God and thus also with his congregation. Discipline leading to excision always begins only after impenitence and obstinacy have become evident. So that the congregation may be fully convinced of this and not lightly proceed to remove the evil person from her midst, the church council begins with admonitions. If the transgressor hardens himself against them, the first thing that follows, together with withholding the Supper, is the announcement of the sin without the sinner’s name in the midst of the congregation; thereafter the announcement of the sin with the sinner’s name, but not without a well-grounded judgment from the classis; next the announcement that, if he continues to persist, he will be cut off; and finally the excision itself with the form for excommunication. The time that must elapse between all these admonitions and disciplinary measures cannot be fixed, since it is connected with the nature of the sin, the conduct of the transgressor, and the offense within and outside the congregation. 5º The penalties the church applies here are purely spiritual. They do not, and may not, consist in monetary fines, corporal chastisement, branding, torture, imprisonment, infamy, exile, capital punishment, and so forth, as Rome maintains, nor in the dissolution of familial, civil, and political relations, as the Anabaptists taught; nor yet in exclusion from public worship, as the Christian church applied this in the first period. For the weapons of the congregation are not carnal but spiritual and therefore mighty before God, 2 Cor. 10:4. Rather, the discipline of the congregation is a serious test of whether someone who has misbehaved and hardens himself against every admonition still can and may be regarded as a brother. Excommunication is therefore ultimately nothing other, but also nothing less, than a renunciation of fraternal association and fraternal communion; a withdrawing by the congregation; a final, sorrowful letting go of the person who presented himself as a brother but proved not to be one. It is no delivery to Satan, which occurs in the New Testament only as an apostolic act; no damnation or curse; no ἀναθεμα, which in the New Testament is never used of ecclesiastical discipline, not even in Rom. 9:3—cf. Cremer, s.v.—but only, and nevertheless no less than, a solemn declaration by the congregation in Jesus’ name that the sinner has been publicly revealed as not being a sincere brother in Christ, and thus a placing of him outside the congregation and her communion so that God alone may judge him. 6º Excommunication is a final remedy so that the one removed from fraternal association may come to repentance. Even apostolic delivery to Satan still had this meaning, 1 Cor. 5:5; 1 Tim. 1:20. Although the congregation may regard the expelled person as a Gentile and a tax collector because she has expended every effort upon him without fruit; although she had to expel him so as not herself to have communion with his sins, 1 Cor. 5:6, 7; 11:30; nevertheless the hope remains that he may still be a brother who will be brought back from his error through admonition, 2 Thess. 3:14. 7º Therefore readmission into the congregation always remains possible, Matt. 16:19; 18:18; John 20:23; 2 Cor. 2:5–10; but prior public confession is then necessary, which in all other cases may be required only with all caution and according to the judgment of the whole church council.
