## 8.

Christ is not only a prophet; he is also king, who still continually governs his congregation personally from heaven. Yet he makes use of human beings in doing so and thus to that extent gave his congregation a *potestas gubernationis*. In a broader sense this is to be understood as all the guidance and care that believers together exercise with respect to one another. In the congregation the word of Cain does not apply: Am I my brother’s keeper? All are members of one another, suffer and rejoice with one another, and have the ability and calling mutually to teach, admonish, comfort, and edify one another, Rom. 15:14; Col. 3:16; 1 Thess. 5:11. There are gifts of leadership and government that Christ distributes to the congregation through the Holy Spirit and that he does not nullify through the offices but keeps on the right track, Rom. 12:8; 1 Cor. 12:28. And among those gifts love stands foremost, causing one person to prefer another in appreciation and esteem, Rom. 12:10; Phil. 2:3; 1 Pet. 5:5. Yet as king of his church Christ also instituted in the presbyterate a specific office through which he governs his congregation. This governing, however, bears a spiritual character, since Christ is king in the kingdom of grace, and is called a ποιμαινειν in Scripture, John 21:15–17; Acts 20:28; 1 Pet. 5:2. Everything suggestive of earthly power and political dominion is excluded from it, 2 Cor. 1:24; 1 Pet. 5:2, 3. In a broader sense this ποιμαινειν also includes the work of the teacher, but there is nevertheless a great difference between the public proclamation and the personal, individual application of the Word, between tending the flock in general and caring for each of the sheep in particular. Believers are indeed called all to watch over one another so as to stir up love and good works, Heb. 10:24, but so that not a single sheep of the flock might remain uncared for, Christ has nevertheless entrusted the feeding of the sheep to a specific office. That he thereby met an essential need of his congregation is apparent from the fact that, when the presbyterate gradually disappeared, a human substitute for this official ministry was instituted in confession. Confession therefore undoubtedly contains something good, James 5:16; Calvin, *Inst.* III 4, 13, but it cannot compare with the well-ordered ministry of the presbyterate. For it introduces an impermissible coercion of conscience, makes believers dependent upon the priest’s absolution, imposes in the confession of all particular sins—specifically the mortal sins—a duty impossible to fulfill, makes grace and salvation uncertain and unstable at every moment, compels a casuistic and quantitative treatment of sin and punishment, and gives occasion for all kinds of immoral practices. Scripture therefore nowhere speaks of such compulsory confession. Rather, what it tells us in example and precept is this: that Nathan goes to David, Elijah to Ahab, and Isaiah to Hezekiah to admonish them personally concerning their sins; that Christ traverses the land preaching and blessing, that he knows all his sheep by name and lets none be lost, John 10:3, 28; that he entrusts to Peter and to all the apostles not only the tending of the flock but also the feeding of the sheep, John 21:15–17; that he commands his disciples to preach the gospel in cities, villages, and houses, Matt. 10:11, 12; that Paul visits the brothers in every city, Acts 15:36, strengthens the congregations, Acts 15:41, and publicly and from house to house, δημοσιᾳ και κατ’ οἰκους, preaches repentance toward God and faith in Christ, Acts 20:20, 21; cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 1, 22. Indeed, the need for such continual spiritual care is implicit in the condition of the church of Christ in this dispensation. Even once the congregation has been planted, it is not immediately perfect. On the contrary, it has conflict within and without, is exposed to all kinds of attacks from sin and falsehood, and at every moment runs the danger of straying to the right or to the left. The congregation is a field that must continually be weeded, a tree that must be pruned in its time, a flock that must always be guided and pastured, a house that must continually be built, a bride who must be prepared to be presented to her husband as a pure virgin. There are the sick, the dying, the tried, the sorrowful, the embattled, the assailed, the doubting, the fallen, the prisoners, and so forth, who need teaching and instruction, admonition and comfort. And even apart from this, the congregation ought to grow in the knowledge and grace of the Lord Jesus Christ. The children must become young men, the young men men, and the men fathers in Christ, and for this they need guidance and care. The teachers too are weak and sinful human beings and ought to stand under oversight. If the council of elders and the assembly of neighboring churches do not exercise it, the congregation becomes the plaything of the pastor, or else a superintendency or episcopate becomes necessary. In a word, the teachers sow the Word; the elders seek the fruit.
