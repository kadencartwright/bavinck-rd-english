## 4.

This power, ascribed by Rome to the church, culminates and also finds the guarantee of its existence and continuance in the power of the pope. According to *Conc. Vatic.* IV, chs. 3, 4, this power bears the following characteristics. 1º It is not merely a *primatus honoris*, nor only an office of supervision and direction, but an authority of legislation, government, and jurisdiction independent of the bishops, a *potestas jurisdictionis*. 2º It is not an extraordinary, temporary power, but an ordinary, permanent power that God gives him and that he can exercise at all times, not merely in a few extraordinary cases. 3º It is immediate, both in origin, since Christ gives it, and in its exercise, since the pope can exercise it not only through the bishops but also by himself or through his legates, without asking anyone’s permission or authorization, and can therefore communicate directly and freely with all bishops and believers. 4º It is not a limited power, but a *plena et suprema potestas*, extending extensively over the whole church, containing intensively all the power necessary for the direction and government of the church, and absolutely sovereign, subject not to laity, bishops, or council, but to God alone. 5º All members of the church, whether individually or together, and all bishops, each by himself or assembled in synod and council, owe the pope absolute obedience, not only in matters of faith and morals but also in those of the discipline and government of the church. *Haec est catholicae veritatis doctrina, a qua deviare, salva fide atque salute, nemo potest.* 6º One part of this power is the teaching office, concerning which it is established that the pope, when he speaks *ex cathedra*, is infallible through divine assistance.

After all that was said earlier about the teaching of Scripture and the earliest ecclesiastical testimonies, it requires no further demonstration that this entire papal system rests on an unscriptural foundation. The *consensus patrum*, namely after Irenaeus, the teaching and practice of popes and councils, and the agreement of later theologians, as these are set forth more fully in Roman Catholic dogmatics, for example in Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 323f., cannot make good this deficiency. However much this papal edifice, through its strict unity, often fascinates many Protestants too, it is religiously and ethically weak in the same measure as it is politically and juridically imposing. For 1º the nature and character of infallibility are inadequately defined. Rome has not dared to go so far as to ascribe to the pope the same infallibility as to the apostles. This certainly lay, and still lies, along the line of development. One would expect that the apostles had communicated apostolic authority to the *episcopi* whom they appointed, and Peter in particular to the bishop at Rome. But this is not so. The pope is infallible, yet not through inspiration but through the assistance of the Holy Spirit, through a special care of God by which the church is guarded from error and preserved in the truth. And his infallibility does not consist in his receiving new revelations and being able to propound a new doctrine, but consists only in his being able faithfully to preserve and interpret the revelation handed down by the apostles. Nor is it to be understood in the sense that the words spoken *ex cathedra* by the pope are literally God’s Word, but only that they contain it substantially: Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 220–245; Jansen, *Prael. theol.* I 616. 2º Although infallibility is a special gift, it nevertheless does not belong to the pope at all times: not in his person, nor as author, orator, judge, legislator, administrator, or temporal ruler; not as bishop of Rome, metropolitan of the ecclesiastical province of Rome, or patriarch of the West; but only as pope, as head of the whole church. Yet no unanimity prevails concerning this. Especially in view of the case of Pope Honorius, earlier theologians and even Innocent III, cited in Schwane, *D. G.* III 535, conceded that the pope could fall into heresy *privatim*, and then *jure divino ex ipso facto* was deposed as pope (Paludanus, Turrecremata, Alphonsus de Castro, Sylvester, and others), or could be deposed by a judicial sentence of a council (Cajetanus, Canus, and others). This, however, had its problematic side and endangered the pope’s unlimited sovereignty and inviolability. Others, therefore, such as Pighius, Bellarmine, Suarez, and others, were better able to accept the probable and pious opinion that divine providence would also preserve the pope personally from heresy: Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 257; Scheeben-Atzberger, *Dogm.* IV 1, p. 450. 3º The Vatican Council says, in an expression perhaps first used by Melchior Canus, cited in Schwane, *D. G.* IV 302, that the pope is infallible when he speaks *ex cathedra*. This appears to draw a boundary, but in practice it is a very unusable criterion. For the system demands that no one can determine whether a pope has spoken *ex cathedra* except the pope himself. And so a pope always has it in his power to reject his own pronouncements or those of other popes by saying that they were not spoken *ex cathedra*, or to declare them binding by saying that they were. He can even say later that he or a predecessor, though thinking that he had spoken *ex cathedra*, had not actually done so. 4º The infallible teaching office is one component of the *plena et suprema potestas jurisdictionis in universam ecclesiam*. The Vatican Council, sess. IV, ch. 3, does not, it is true, expressly declare that the pope is infallible at all times in exercising this entire power. But it does say concerning this power: *cujuscumque ritus et dignitatis pastores atque fideles, tam seorsim singuli quam simul omnes, officio hierarchicae subordinationis veraeque obedientiae obstringuntur, non solum in rebus, quae ad fidem et mores, sed etiam in iis, quae ad disciplinam et regimen ecclesiae pertinent*. Thus whether the pope is fallible or infallible in all this, everyone without distinction, without any right of criticism (*neque cuiquam de ejus licere judicare judicio*), must obey the pope unconditionally, and that on pain of no less than the salvation of their souls. 5º In *Conc. Vatic.*, sess. IV, ch. 4, infallibility is indeed expressly ascribed to the pope only when he speaks *ex cathedra* and, as shepherd and teacher of all Christians, *doctrinam de fide vel moribus ab universa ecclesia tenendam definit*. But it can by no means be inferred from this that he is not infallible otherwise. How else could absolute and unconditional obedience to the pope also be demanded in ch. 3 in matters of discipline, government, and jurisdiction? But however this may be, in matters of faith and morals the pope is certainly infallible. And the Roman Catholic theologians elaborate further what belongs to these. The pope is infallible when he treats the truths of revelation in Scripture, the truths of tradition, the truths of the divine institutions, the sacraments, the church, ecclesiastical organization and government, and the truths of natural revelation. But even with this, the boundary of his infallibility has by no means yet been reached. In order to be infallible in all this, the theologians say, he must also be infallible in judging the sources of the truths of faith and in their interpretation, that is, in establishing the authority of Scripture, tradition, councils, popes, the *patres*, and theologians; in making use of and applying natural truths, representations, concepts, and expressions; in judging and rejecting errors and heresies; even in determining the *facta dogmatica*; in the prohibition of books; in matters of discipline; in the approval of orders; in the canonization of saints; and so forth. Faith and morals encompass almost everything, and all that the pope says about them is infallible. The term *ex cathedra* in fact draws virtually no boundary whatever. For this expression surely does not signify that only those pronouncements are infallible in which *ex cathedra* is literally mentioned, since then all earlier papal determinations would be excluded from it. It therefore indicates only something substantive. But who then determines whether the pope speaks *ex cathedra*? In practice and by the people his pronouncement will always be understood as infallible, if only from fear that one might perhaps reject an infallible pronouncement. And theoretically only the pope can say infallibly when he speaks *ex cathedra*, infallibly: Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 554–654. Moreover, why is the pope infallible? Because he himself declares it? But that is a *circulus vitiosus*. Because the council declares it? But the council is fallible, also in this declaration. Where, then, is the certainty so highly extolled for the Roman Catholic Christian? 6º The Vatican Council drew up the result of a long historical process. In the first Christian period all apostles, all churches, and also all *episcopi* were equal to one another in rank; at most there was a *primatus honoris*, but in no respect a *primatus jurisdictionis*. Gradually, however, the church and the bishop at Rome learned how to subject all other churches and bishops to themselves. Yet the independence of the latter within their own sphere was long preserved to a certain degree. Toward the end of the 13th century, the controversy arose over the relation between episcopal and papal power: Schwane, *D. G.* III 549. Some then still sought to maintain the independence of the former in the sense that the bishop, though subordinate to the pope, had nevertheless received from God, *ex jure divino*, the power belonging to him within his own sphere and was merely designated by the pope as the bearer of that power. So taught Henry of Ghent; Alphonsus de Castro, bishop of Bruges, † 1558; Vitoria, 1480–1544, father of neo-scholasticism at the University of Salamanca; Petrus Guerrero, bishop of Granada; and many other Spanish and French bishops at the Council of Trent, who wanted the following words included in the 7th canon of the 23rd session: *episcopos jure divino institutos presbyteris esse superiores*, Schwane, *D. G.* IV 292f. The fiercest debates arose at the council over this question; they continued throughout the winter of the year 1562 and beyond the middle of the following year. On 15 July 1563, episcopal power was indeed more precisely defined in can. 6–8, but the question whether it was *ex jure divino* or *ex jure ecclesiastico* was deliberately left undecided. On the other side, after all, stood a strong party headed by Lainez, the Jesuit general, which claimed that the bishop indeed received the *potestas ordinis* immediately from God, but obtained the *potestas jurisdictionis* only by free transfer on the part of the pope; the latter was thus to that extent *de jure ecclesiastico* and could be restricted, altered, or taken away by the pope at will, for the pope had his full power over the whole church solely and immediately from God. After Trent this opinion gained ground more and more through the influence of the Jesuits; it gained the victory over Gallicanism and was elevated to a dogma at the Vatican Council. It is indeed said in sess. IV, ch. 3 that the power of the pope makes absolutely no encroachment upon the *ordinaria ac immediata potestas jurisdictionis* of the bishops, but rather confirms, strengthens, and maintains it. Yet the pope has full and entire legislative, governmental, and judicial power over the whole church; without anyone’s intervention or mediation he can communicate freely (*communicare*) with all the shepherds and flocks of the whole church; and all, without distinction and unconditionally, are subject to him. Bishops, councils, the entire church, and all believers are fallible in themselves and infallible only with and through him. The pope is the root, stability, and foundation of the unity, authority, and infallibility of bishops, councils, church fathers, theologians, all believers, and the whole church. He alone receives all power, authority, and infallibility directly from God. A single expression still recalls the old catholic conception, as, for example, when *Conc. Vatic.* IV, ch. 4 says that the pope *ea infallibilitate pollere, qua divinus Redemptor ecclesiam suam in definienda doctrina de fide vel moribus instructam voluit*; but immediately the very inference drawn from this is that the *definitiones* of the pope are infallible *ex sese* and not *ex consensu ecclesiae*. 7º This power of the pope goes still further. Although the popes down to the 8th century were subjects of the Roman Empire and their spiritual office by no means included the possession of temporal power, the thought nevertheless soon arose in the Roman Catholic Church that the pope, in order to be able to remain independent in the spiritual domain, also had to be sovereign in the temporal domain. And after the abolition of the Papal States in 1870, this idea came still more to the foreground and was expressed with greater emphasis. Pius IX and Leo XIII did not fail to declare repeatedly that the pope as universal bishop could not be the subject of a particular ruler or bear a particular nationality: Jansen, *Prael. theol.* I 657; Hase, *Prot. Polem.* 254; and their pronouncements bind Roman Catholic believers. Even if the idea of an ecclesiastical state, of a priestly state, is entirely out of date; even if the existence of such a state detracts from the unity of Italy; even if the pope cannot be both ecclesiastical head and temporal sovereign without church or state or both suffering harm thereby; even if spiritual power has absolutely no need of political sovereignty; even if the pope for centuries had no political territory and after the otherwise in itself unlawful seizure of the Papal States in 1870 did not lose influence, any more than the German bishops did after they ceased to be princes of the empire; even if the pope’s independence over against the king of Italy is more than adequately guaranteed by the guarantee of 13 May 1871 and by the power of Roman Catholic rulers and peoples—all this matters little: Rome does not abandon the demand that the pope once again become a temporal ruler. Yet this is still only a small part of the whole demand. Appealing to Matt. 28:18 and Luke 22:38 and following Boniface VIII in the bull *Unam Sanctam*, many Roman Catholics have gone much further still and have said that the pope is the actual sovereign of the entire world and transfers temporal power at his pleasure to princes and kings as his *ministri* and *vicarii*. This, however, was far too extreme for many. They disputed the view that the pope is sovereign over the unbelieving part of the world, for Christ entrusted to Peter only the care of the sheep, and those outside God judges. Nor was the pope a temporal ruler over the Christian nations, for nowhere was such political power assigned to the pope, and Christ gave Peter only the keys of the kingdom of heaven. The pope does not even have a *temporalis jurisdictio* or temporal power *directe* or *jure divino*, for Christ is a spiritual king and has a spiritual kingdom. But although these thus rejected direct temporal power, they nevertheless continued to speak of a *potestas indirecta* and ascribed to the pope not merely a *potestas directiva* in temporal affairs, but also, in the interest of the kingdom of God, a *summa potestas disponendi de rebus temporalibus omnium Christianorum*, for tending the sheep also requires power over the wolves. Temporal power, after all, is subject to the church as the body is to the spirit; unbelieving rulers who lead their subjects into heresy may be resisted and deposed; Christian rulers as such are subject to Christ and must promote the faith and protect the church, just as many kings acted in the days of the Old Testament and in the history of the church: Bellarmine, *de Rom. Pontif.*, lib. V. But even with this theory of *potestas indirecta*, the pope retains the right, in the interest of the kingdom of God, to demand unlimited obedience from all rulers; in case of disobedience to depose them and release their subjects from their oath of obedience; to assign non-Roman Catholic peoples and lands to Roman Catholic rulers; to declare political laws and rights null and void; and so forth. Even though many Roman Catholics today give the appearance that all these rights belonged to the popes only temporarily and incidentally in the Middle Ages, the Syllabus of 1864 expressly declares in no. 23 that popes and councils never exceeded the limits of their power, arrogated to themselves the rights of rulers, or ever erred in a decision on matters of faith and morals. The exercise of these rights may be suspended by circumstances, but there is no doubt that the rights themselves are inalienable. Rome does not change. Cf. Fr. v. Schulte, *Die Macht der römischen Päpste über Fürsten, Länder, Völker und Individuen*, 3rd ed., Giessen, 1896. 8º From all this the all-controlling place that the pope occupies in the life of the Roman Catholic Christian is evident. The Roman Catholic Church is a monarchy, a kingdom, a state with a spiritual ruler at its head. From the days of Augustine onward, the church is represented by preference as a state and a kingdom in which all *dogmata* have the force of laws and rights that bind human beings on pain of the salvation of their souls. Boniface VIII therefore said of the pope that he *jura omnia in scrinio pectoris sui censetur habere*, cited in Schulte, op. cit., 66. The government in this state is absolutely monarchical; after the council of 1870 it is not even, as was formerly said, tempered by the aristocracy of the bishops, for the bishops are fallible and derive their power from him. Indeed, according to the express determination of the Vatican Council, the pope can communicate directly with all shepherds and flocks and therefore, bypassing the bishop altogether, directly appoint or dismiss every priest and every chaplain, decide every lawsuit, chastise every layperson, and so forth; the bishops are in principle utterly deprived of sovereignty within their own sphere. Nor is this monarchical government of the pope any longer constitutional in principle, for Scripture and tradition are subject to his infallible interpretation; *la tradizione son io*, Pius IX therefore said to Cardinal Guidi, cited in Schulte, op. cit., 80; the pope determines, if necessary, what the doctrine of Scripture and tradition is. Through his infallibility the pope is the sole, absolute sovereign in the Roman Catholic Church, the source of all ecclesiastical and, even directly or indirectly, all temporal power. Therefore, since the 9th century, in distinction from all other bishops, he is called *Papa*, Schwane, *D. G.* I 543; not merely successor and vicar of Peter but *vicarius Christi, vicarius Dei*, Schwane III 536, 538; *pater spiritualis omnium patrum, imo omnium fidelium, hierarcha praecipuus, sponsus unicus, caput indivisum, pontifex summus, fons et origo, regula cunctorum principatuum ecclesiasticorum*, Bonav., *Brevil.* VI 12. The pope is the church, is Christianity, is the kingdom of God itself. *Le pape et l’église, c’est tout un*, said Francis de Sales. *Ubi papa, ibi ecclesia.* The primacy of the pope is *summa rei christianae*, Bellarmine, *de Rom. Pontif.*, in the preface. Without the pope there is no church, no Christianity: Veuillot, cited in Hase, *Prot. Polemik*⁵ 187. Subjection to the pope is necessary for salvation for all human beings (Boniface VIII). The pope is the mediator of salvation, the way, the truth, and the life. All that is still lacking is that he be worshiped, but even that is a question of time: Harnack, *D. G.* III 652.
