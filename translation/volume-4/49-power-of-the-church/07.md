## 7.

The *potestas docendi* has its origin and ground in the prophetic office for which Christ was anointed and which he himself still always exercises through his Word and Spirit. He has not transferred it to any human being, nor appointed any pope or bishop, pastor or teacher, as his agent and representative; rather, he is still always our supreme prophet, who from heaven teaches his church through Word and Spirit. Yet in this he ordinarily makes use of human beings as his organs, not only of officebearers in the narrower sense but of all believers and of each one of them according to the grace given to him. The church herself is a prophetess, and all Christians share in the anointing of Christ and are called to confess the name of the Lord. The office does not suppress the gifts but only guides them. There are many *charismata* that belong to the church’s *potestas docendi*: wisdom, knowledge, prophecy, and so forth, 1 Cor. 12:8f. Christ instructs and teaches through the father in the family, through the teacher at school, through the presbyter during home visitation, and through all believers in their fellowship with one another and their interaction with others. But he does so especially and in a distinct way—officially, with an expressly granted commission and authority, in the public assemblies of the people of God—through the minister of the Word. By the *potestas docendi* is now understood chiefly this official ministry of the Word. The independence of this ministry must be maintained in two directions. First, over against the Roman Catholic Church, which subordinates the Word to the sacrament, the homilist to the liturgist, preaching to worship, and the *potestas docendi* to the *potestas jurisdictionis*. According to Scripture, after all, the Word comes first, and the sacrament is added to it as an appendage and seal; there is no sacrament without the Word, though there is a Word without sacrament. The sacrament follows the Word. The one who ministers the Word therefore need not always administer the sacrament, 1 Cor. 1:14–17, but he can and may administer it, and then too he is a minister of the Word, of the visible Word that is added to the audible Word. Second, this official ministry of the Word is independent over against all instruction in the Word that occurs among believers themselves or toward those outside, and is even essentially distinct from the application of the Word that the presbyter must make when visiting members of the church. Certainly the official ministry of the Word in the assemblies of the church can and may also be understood as shepherding the flock. Scripture leads us in this. The Lord is the shepherd of his people, Ps. 23:1; 80:2; Isa. 40:11; 49:10; Jer. 31:10; Ezek. 34:15. Christ is called the shepherd of the flock, Ezek. 34:23; John 10:11, 14; Heb. 13:20; 1 Pet. 2:25; 5:4; Rev. 7:17. And under him as the ἀρχιποιμην, 1 Pet. 5:4, his ministers too bear the name of shepherds, ποιμενες, *pastores*, Isa. 44:28; Jer. 2:8; 3:15; 23:1f.; Ezek. 34:2f., etc.; John 10:2; 21:15–17; Acts 20:28; 1 Cor. 9:7; Eph. 4:11; 1 Pet. 5:2; cf. the Form of the Reformed Churches for the Ordination of Ministers of the Word. But since the two activities of shepherding and teaching, of governing and laboring in the Word and doctrine, were separated and each received an organ of its own, Eph. 4:11; 1 Tim. 5:17, the name teacher has become the characteristic title of the minister of the Word. By his preparation and training, by his complete dedication to labor in the Word, by the right to live from the gospel, and by his official administration of Word and sacrament in the assembly of believers, he is distinguished from the overseer, the ruling elder, who is specially charged with ποιμαινειν, Acts 20:28; 1 Pet. 5:2. Yet this teaching may not be understood in an intellectualistic sense. Rather, with the Form mentioned above, it is to be interpreted thus: the ministers shall present the Lord’s Word thoroughly and sincerely to their people and apply it, both in general and in particular, for the benefit of the hearers, by instructing, admonishing, comforting, and rebuking according to each one’s need, proclaiming repentance toward God and reconciliation with him through faith in Jesus Christ, and refuting with Holy Scripture all errors and heresies that contend against this pure doctrine. More particularly, this *potestas docendi* includes the right and duty of the church: 1º to provide for the training of her prospective ministers or to supervise that training carefully; to call, examine, send, ordain, and support her ministers; through their ministry to have the Word of God preached both to believers and unbelievers; and thus to establish, extend, and propagate the church of God among the human race; 2º to administer the Word of God through the office in different forms according to each one’s need, specifically in the form of milk to the young and of solid food to the mature members of the church, yet always in such a way that the full counsel of God, the whole wealth of his Word, is unfolded and is developed and applied in keeping with the needs of every people and land, of every century and age, of every church and of all believers in particular, Isa. 3:10, 11; 2 Cor. 5:20; 1 Tim. 4:13; 2 Tim. 2:15; 4:2; 3º to preserve, translate, and interpret the Word of God according to the rule of faith, and to defend it against every assault of falsehood, 1 Tim. 1:3, 4; 2 Tim. 1:13; Titus 1:9–11, 13, 14, and thus to build up the church upon the foundation of apostles and prophets, Eph. 2:20, and cause her to be a στυλος και ἑδραιωμα της ἀληθειας, 1 Tim. 3:15, that is, a pillar and foundation that bears the truth, displays it before everyone’s eyes, and makes it known to all.

From this there follows directly the authority of the churches to confess the truth that they believe and to maintain it in their midst as a confession. Against this the Remonstrants, in the preface to their Confession and Apology, the Baptists, Congregationalists, Quakers—cf. Schaff, *Creeds* I 834, 852, 864—and many others have objected that the formulation of binding confessions conflicts with the sufficiency of Scripture, destroys Christian freedom, introduces an intolerable tyranny, and cuts off further investigation and continuing development. Scripture, however, clearly imposes upon the churches the duty to be a pillar and foundation of the truth and to confess it before all people, to avoid those who depart from the doctrine of the truth, and to maintain the Word of God over against all its opponents. Almost from the beginning, that is, from the beginning of the second century, the church has been a confessional church, which had its unity in the rule of faith common to all, that is, in the baptismal confession, in the original and later somewhat expanded Apostles’ Creed, and which thereafter was repeatedly compelled in the course of the centuries by heresy and slander to develop the truth more fully. Cf. Zahn, *Glaubensregel und Taufbekenntniss in der alten Kirche*, in his *Skizzen aus dem Leben der alten Kirche*, 1898, p. 238–270; idem, *Das apost. Symbolum*, 1893; Kattenbusch, *Das apost. Symbol* I, 1894; Kunze, *Glaubensregel, H. Schrift und Taufbekenntniss*, Leipzig, 1899. Nor can a church exist without a rule of faith in a world full of falsehood and deception; without a fixed confession, as history teaches especially in this century, it becomes prey to every kind of error and confusion and is subjected to the tyranny of prevailing tendencies and opinions. With such a confession the church does not detract from the perfection of Holy Scripture either, but expresses nothing other than what is contained in that Scripture. The confession does not stand alongside, much less above, but far beneath Holy Scripture. Scripture alone is αὐτοπιστος, binding unconditionally to faith and obedience, and immutable; but the confession always is and remains subject to examination and revision by Scripture. It is not a *norma normans* but at most a *norma normata*, not a *norma veritatis* but a *norma doctrinae in aliqua ecclesia receptae*: subordinate, fallible, human work, an imperfect expression of what the church has received from Scripture into her consciousness as divine truth and now confesses on the authority of God’s Word over against every error and falsehood. Nor does the church compel anyone with this confession or constrain investigation, for she leaves everyone free to confess differently and to understand the truth of God in another sense. She listens attentively to objections that may be brought against her confession on the ground of God’s Word and examines them as her confession itself requires. She only refuses, and must refuse, to lower herself to a *debating society* or philosophical association in which what counts as truth today was falsehood yesterday, for she is like a rock, not a wave of the sea, a pillar and foundation of the truth. Cf. vol. I 25, and further (Dunlop), *A collection of confessions of faith, catechisms, directories, books of discipline etc. of publick authority in the church of Scotland*, I, Edinburgh, 1719, preface, pp. V–CXLIV.
