## 11.

This power granted by Christ to his congregation is brought together in the local church in the church council. According to the New Testament every local church is independent, an *ecclesia completa*, and therefore, just as much as the church in its entirety, bears the name temple of God, 1 Cor. 3:16, 17; 2 Cor. 6:16; bride, 2 Cor. 11:2; or body of Christ, 1 Cor. 12:27. Believers do not stand by themselves but form a unity, and likewise the officebearers in a local church do not remain loosely alongside one another but unite in a council of the church. Traces of this are already present in the New Testament. In Jerusalem, after being incorporated into the congregation by baptism, the believers assembled from time to time, persevering in the apostles’ teaching, in fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers, Acts 1:14; 2:41f.; 5:12; etc. And they stood under the leadership of the college of apostles, who were soon assisted in this by the presbyters, Acts 6:2; 15:2, 6, 22. All kinds of circumstances—the gifts of the Holy Spirit, especially those of teaching, prophecy, and glossolalia; the assemblies of believers; the ministry of Word and sacrament; the collection of offerings; the care of the poor; and so forth—naturally made leadership and regulation, and with them also counsel and deliberation, necessary. At first the apostles provided as much as possible for all those needs, took measures, and made regulations, Acts 15:28f.; 1 Cor. 11:4–6, 34; 14:27f.; 16:1; 1 Tim. 3; etc. For everything in the congregation of Christ had to happen decently and in order, in peace and for edification, 1 Cor. 14:26, 33, 40. But when the office of elders was instituted, they were charged with the leadership and government of the congregation, and among themselves they soon formed a college, πρεσβυτηριον, 1 Tim. 4:14. Under the leadership of such a college, however, in the first period the congregation enjoyed a great measure of independence; it was regularly consulted in important matters. In Acts 1 the disciples assemble to choose an apostle; in Acts 6 the congregation chooses the deacons; in Acts 15 it attends the meeting of apostles and elders; in 1 Cor. 5 it exercises discipline. The first synods were assemblies of the local church. But all the local churches together also form a unity. Together they too bear the singular name ἐκκλησια; they all stand under the apostles, to whom the leadership and government of the whole church is entrusted; they are one with one another in Christ, thus one in doctrine, faith, baptism, and love; they greet one another, Rom. 16:16; 1 Cor. 16:20; 2 Cor. 13:12; serve one another with gifts of love, Rom. 15:26; 1 Cor. 16:1; 2 Cor. 8:1, 4; 9:1; Gal. 2:10; and have one another read the letters they receive from the apostles, Col. 4:16. It thus lay in the nature of the case that these congregations, which were spiritually one, would eventually deliberate with one another about matters of common concern. The first example of this occurs in Acts 15, on the occasion of the question whether the Gentiles could be saved without circumcision. The congregation at Antioch sent Paul and Barnabas and some others to Jerusalem to exchange views concerning this important question with the apostles and elders there and to come to agreement. The apostles and elders therefore held an assembly with these delegates from Antioch, 15:6, which perhaps was also attended by the congregation, 15:12, 22 (in v. 25, however, και οἱ before ἀδελφοι should probably be omitted). After much ζητησις, investigation and disputation, not merely advice was given, but a decision was taken in the Holy Spirit that bound the brothers in Antioch, Syria, and Cilicia; it was communicated to them by letter and was further explained orally by Judas and Silas in an assembly of the congregation, Acts 15:22–31. All these assemblies reported in the New Testament were assemblies of the local congregation, only in Acts 15 attended by delegates from elsewhere. This custom was followed later, already in the second century as well. In important affairs, such as the appointment and deposition of a bishop, excommunication, absolution from mortal sins, and so forth, not only did the presbytery give its guidance but the congregation also gave its consent. Cyprian still says that from the beginning of his episcopate onward he did nothing without the counsel of his presbytery and the consent of the congregation, *Ep.* 14, 4. Therefore at the synods of the second and third centuries not only bishops but also presbyters, deacons, and ordinary congregational members were present. Even the Council of Nicaea was attended not only by bishops but also by presbyters, deacons, and members, who participated in the debates. And in the first period the delegates invited from neighboring congregations to congregational assemblies were by no means bishops alone, but could also be presbyters, deacons, or other members of the congregation. But the development of the hierarchical idea entailed that the congregation’s consent was sought less and less, that presbyters and deacons were detached from the congregation and changed into counselors and assistants of the bishop, and that synods were gradually held only by bishops. Further, in the second and third centuries all congregational assemblies attended by delegates from neighboring congregations were equal in rank. There was not yet a hierarchy of ecclesiastical assemblies; there were not yet provincial, metropolitan, or ecumenical councils. All assemblies of the churches took place in the name of Christ, made decisions in the Holy Spirit, and were valid for the whole of Christendom (*concilium universale, catholicum*). But in this too a change occurred. Already in the third century specifically provincial synods—that is, assemblies of bishops in a particular province—were held here and there. In the fourth century, as a consequence of the great controversies that divided the church, synods of bishops from different provinces assembled. And although the Council of Nicaea was by no means a representation of the whole of Christendom, since it was attended by only a few bishops from the West, it was nevertheless convened from all directions by the emperor. Thus a ranking of provincial, national, patriarchal, and ecumenical councils gradually arose, Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 247–344. But the characteristic mark of an ecumenical council is difficult to identify. It cannot lie in the fact that it was convened by the pope, for from the fourth to the tenth century it was convened by the emperor; nor in the universal validity and great significance of its decisions, for the canons of ecumenical councils have frequently been rejected and those of provincial synods accepted; nor yet in the fact that an ecumenical council represents the whole of Christendom, for this has by no means always been the case with the so-called councils. Toward the end of the Middle Ages the theory did indeed arise that a council was ecumenical and infallible only when it consisted of delegates from all the churches. But this theory was of revolutionary origin, led in practice to all kinds of difficulties, and was never accepted by Rome either. For Rome a council is ecumenical only when its decisions have been approved by the pope and thereby acquire an infallible character binding the whole of Christendom: Bellarminus, *de conciliis et ecclesia*, books I, II; Heinrich, *Dogm.* II 476f.; Scheeben, *Dogm.* I 230f.; Vering, *Kirchenrecht*, 613f.; etc.

In the Protestant churches synodal church government first developed on French soil. Synods did arise in the Lutheran church, but these consisted only in assemblies of pastors. In 1528 Zwingli instituted synods at Zurich, which were convened by the council, consisted of the ministers of city and country and some members of the council, and above all had to consider complaints against the doctrine and life of the ministers: Mörikofer, *Ulr. Zwingli*, Leipzig, 1869, II 118f. Calvin similarly stipulated in the *Ordonnances ecclesiastiques* that the ministers had to assemble every three months to supervise one another’s doctrine and life, and in addition introduced an annual visitation in 1546, Kampschulte, *Joh. Calvin* I 408. In 1526 Franz Lambert drafted for Hesse a church order that included both congregational assemblies and synods consisting of ministers and delegates appointed by the congregations, but this church order did not go into effect, Lechler, *Gesch. d. Presb. n. Syn. Verf.*, 14f. A synodal church government arose first in France, where the churches expanded greatly and, through the need for unity, first met in synod at Paris on 26 May 1559 and united in a common confession and church order, Lechler, ibid., 69. It is remarkable here that the general synod arose first, that it introduced the provincial synods, and that later, in 1572, the classis was inserted between these and the church councils, ibid., 81; cf. likewise in Scotland, 97. Such a synodal church government was then later introduced in other Reformed churches as well, in Poland, Bohemia, Hungary, Germany, the Netherlands, Scotland, England, America, and so forth. But opposition soon arose from two sides. The Remonstrants, aligning themselves with Zwingli and Erastus, assigned ecclesiastical power to the civil government and inferred from this that synods were indeed permissible but not commanded and were not necessary to the being or well-being of the church; and that when they were held, the right to convene them, to appoint delegates, to determine the agenda, and to preside belonged to the civil government: Uitenbogaert, *Tractaat van ’t ampt ende auctoriteit*, etc., 1610, p. 107f.; cf. Limborch, *Theol. Chr.* VII 19. And under the influence of Anabaptist error the Independents went still further, regarded every group of believers as independent, and rejected every binding classical or synodal connection: Robinson in Kist and Royaards, *Ned. Arch. voor Kerk. Gesch.* VIII, 1848, p. 371f.; Neal, *Historie der rechtz. Puriteinen* II 1, p. 96. The grounds that can be adduced against synodal church government are indeed not devoid of weight. For the local churches in the New Testament are all fully independent with respect to one another; there is not a word about a legal, binding classical or synodal connection. Such a connection also seems entirely in conflict with the independence of the local churches, because it introduces assemblies that stand above the local churches and act authoritatively toward them, and thus again introduces an impermissible hierarchy and tyranny into the church of Christ. To this is added that the history of synods does not always testify favorably to their usefulness and frequently represents them as a cause of all kinds of conflict and division, so that Gregory of Nazianzus could already say, μηδεμιας συνοδου τελος εἰδον χρηστον, and the proverb not unjustly reads, *omne concilium parit bellum*. Over against this, however, other considerations clearly bring to light the necessity and utility of synods. 1º In the New Testament there is not yet a classical or synodal connection among the churches, but at that time there was also no need for it, since the apostles themselves were living, assisted the congregations with counsel, and also cared for them through evangelists as their representatives. 2º Already then the congregations were connected to one another in all kinds of ways by spiritual bonds and received the right not only to assemble themselves but also to send delegates to other congregations and request a decision there in certain disputes. Acts 1, 6, 15, 21 show that synods in a wholly general sense are *juris divini permissivi*. 3º Synods are not absolutely necessary *ad esse ecclesiae*, nor are they specifically commanded by God’s Word, but they are permissible and necessary *ad bene esse ecclesiae*. 4º The necessity lies in this, that the unity of doctrine, discipline, and worship to which the congregation is called; the order, peace, and love she must preserve; and the common interests entrusted to her (such as the training, calling, and sending of ministers; mission among the Gentiles; support for needy churches; and so forth) can be properly served only by means of synods. 5º Synods are not a pedestal for but an undermining of every hierarchy. They uphold the independence of the local churches and preserve them from disorder, division, the hierarchy of the pastor, and domination by individual members. They confirm the freedom of the individual members by giving them support in the connection with other churches and allowing appeal to broader assemblies. 6º Nor are they a cause of division and conflict, but a means of bringing disputes that continually arise in the church here on earth concerning doctrine, discipline, and ministry to a decision in a peaceful manner, through careful investigation and ample discussion. 7º In order to fulfill their purpose, synods must always be assemblies of churches whose members (pastors, presbyters, deacons, or ordinary members) are delegates of churches and bound by letters of instruction from churches; which are convened by the churches themselves and not by civil government, pope, and so forth, and led by ecclesiastical persons chosen for this purpose; and which freely and independently, without interference from the civil government, judge and decide ecclesiastical matters. 8º The ecclesiastical assemblies (local, classical, provincial, general, ecumenical) do not differ essentially from one another. One assembly is not *per se* higher, weightier, less exposed to error, or more assured of the guidance of the Holy Spirit than another. For every church and every group of churches is independent over against the others, and all are bound in the same measure to the Word and share in the promise of the Spirit. In ecclesiastical assemblies it is not representatives of the people but ecclesiastical officebearers who come together, called on Christ’s behalf to govern his church. Thus they are distinguished not by power of another or higher kind, but only by the greater amount of power brought together and extending over a wider domain. 9º The authority of all ecclesiastical assemblies is no other than that of the churches themselves; it is subject to the Word of Christ. Christ is the only one who has authority in the churches and in their different assemblies; his Word alone decides; only what seems good to the Holy Spirit in and through the members is binding in the church of Christ. But the church cannot enforce even these decisions, taken according to God’s Word and in the Holy Spirit, otherwise than through moral means. She has no ruling, coercive power, but only a serving one. Cf.
