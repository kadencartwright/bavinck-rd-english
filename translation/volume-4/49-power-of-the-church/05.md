## 5.

The Reformation opposed this degeneration of ecclesiastical power. It again confessed that the church was a *communio sanctorum* and that she had received from Christ a power essentially distinct from that in the state. *Christi Reich ist nicht ein leiblich oder weltlich, irdisch Regiment, wie andere Herren und Könige auf Erden regieren, sondern ein geistlich, himmlisch Regiment, das da gehet nicht über zeitlich Gut, noch was dies Leben betrifft, sondern über Herzen und Gewissen, wie man vor Gott leben soll, seine Gnade erlangen*, Luther in Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 464, 488; cf. further Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 486f., 553f. Calvin likewise distinguished between church and state as between soul and body, the future and the present life, and ascribed to the church offices, power, and jurisdiction of her own, *Inst.* IV 1–11, 20; cf. Lobstein, *Die Ethik Calvins*, 1877, p. 115f. The power of the church therefore did not consist in some *corpus juris canonici*, which Luther publicly burned at Wittenberg on 10 December 1520, but solely in the ministry of God’s Word. Since Christ is the only head of the church, in the church only the Word of God can and may rule—not by coercion but only by love and free obedience, Luther in Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 464, 468. The ministry of Word and sacrament is the only form of church government, the sum of all ecclesiastical power, the whole power of the keys, which, according to the Lutherans too, nevertheless also included lawful calling, obedience to the overseers, judgment of doctrine and life, exercise of discipline, exclusion of the ungodly from the congregation, and so forth: *Conf.* and *Apol. Aug.*, arts. 14, 20, 28; *Smalc. art. de potestate et primatu papae*. Sohm has clearly brought to light this distinction in the power ascribed to the church by Rome and by Luther. He admittedly goes too far when, holding that all law is coercion, human dominion, and earthly power, he considers all church law in conflict with the essence of the church; cf. Rutgers, *Het kerkrecht, inzoover het de kerk met het recht in verband brengt*, Amsterdam, 1894. But he nevertheless demonstrates clearly that the great difference between Rome and Protestantism concerning the power of the church is connected with the political, juridical conception of Christianity or the spiritual, ethical one. And he himself repeatedly acknowledges that the congregation of Christ, although a communion of saints, nevertheless requires order, already possessed a certain order in the earliest period, pp. 51f., and according to Luther and the Lutherans too cannot exist without office, lawful calling, pastoral care, discipline, the ban, and government, pp. 471, 476, 486, 494, 519f. All power in the church is, directly or indirectly, ministry of the Word; every regulation she makes is subordinate and subservient to it. The Reformed understood it in the same way. All power in the church rests originally with Christ, whom God anointed king over Zion, and therefore bears a spiritual character, for his kingdom is not of this world. Insofar as Christ makes use of organs in exercising that power, these are not autonomous, independent, or sovereign, but bound to him—that is, to his Word. Every office in Christ’s congregation is a διακονια, a *ministerium*, without legislative, judicial, and executive power in itself, able only to administer what is contained and comprehended in Christ’s Word. Thus in fact there is no other power in the church than the power of the keys, the ministry of Word and sacrament, which was nevertheless in turn commonly distinguished into *potestas doctrinae, potestas disciplinae*, and *potestas ordinis* or *regiminis*: Calvin, *Inst.* IV, chs. 8–12; Martyr, *Loci Comm.*, 405 sq.; Polanus, *Synt. Theol.* VII 10 sq.; Junius, *theses theol.* 46; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII 29 sq.; etc.

Although there was thus agreement in the basic ideas, an important difference soon appeared between Lutherans and Reformed in their elaboration and application. First, Luther took over from the Roman church the official ministry of the Word to the individual and was accordingly intent upon retaining confession. Although he understood the preaching of the gospel as forgiveness of sins—*also dass ein christlicher Prediger nimmer das Maul aufthun kann, er muss eine Absolution sprechen*, Sohm, loc. cit., 488—this was nevertheless not enough for him. Absolution also had to be applied individually by the pastor in confession, which was indeed not necessary but nevertheless highly useful: Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 528; Caspari, article “Beichte” in Herzog³; *Conf. Aug.* 11, 25, and *Apol. Conf.*; *Catech. minor* I 5; *Appendix* I *ad Catech. maj.*; *Art. Smalc.* III 8; Gerhard, *Loc.* XV 97–117; Quenstedt, *Theol.* III 584, 598; Harnack, *Prakt. Theol.* II 463f., and the literature cited there; Gandert, *Zur Revision des Beichtwesens in der ev. Kirche*, Wittenberg, 1898. But this private confession encountered insurmountable difficulties—the insufficient number of pastors, the confessional fee, the uncertain meaning of absolution, and so forth—and gradually fell into disuse. Although the Reformed considered it useful to confess offenses to one another, they permitted the official ministry of the Word, and thus also the proclamation of the forgiveness of sins—that is, absolution—to take place only in the public assembly of believers; retained from confession as an ecclesiastical institution only the regular confession of sins, or that sometimes customary in preparation for the Supper; and otherwise replaced private confession with personal household visitation: Zwingli in Zeller, *Das theol. System Zwingli’s*, 153; Calvin, *Inst.* III 4; IV 1, 22; Martyr, *L. C.*, 274; Amesius, *Bellarminus enervatus* III 481; Rivetus, *Op.* III 316; M. Vitringa III 127 sq.; Biesterveld, *Het Huisbezoek*, Kampen, 1900. Second, discipline in the Lutheran church followed a different course from that in the Reformed. Luther himself very much desired discipline to be applied in the congregation of Christ. Although he rejected the Roman ban and removed every civil penalty from ecclesiastical discipline, his ideal was nevertheless a congregation that, after repeated admonition, removed the evil from its midst: Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 530f., 560f.; Herzog² 8, 14; 13, 588; Th. Harnack, *Prakt. Theol.* II 497f.; Dieckhoff, *Luthers Lehre v. d. kirchl. Gewalt*, Berlin, 1865; the Lutheran confessional writings in Müller, pp. 64, 152, 165, 288, 323, 340, 342. But the absence of the presbyterial office and the exercise of ecclesiastical discipline by the pastor alone led to such abuses that discipline was soon wholly destroyed and, insofar as it remained, was left to the mixed consistories. In practice this yielded the same result as the doctrine of Zwingli, Erastus, the Remonstrants, the Rationalists, and many more recent theologians, according to whom the congregation, since the civil government had become Christian, had transferred to it her power to exercise discipline. For Calvin, by contrast, ecclesiastical discipline was a vital question. For the church’s right to remove evil from her midst he fought in Geneva for twenty years; he first secured it in the year 1555, Choisy, *La théocratie à Genève*, 166. Discipline might not be the soul of the church, yet it was her sinew. And this conception of the obligatory character, necessity, and utility of ecclesiastical discipline became the property of the Reformed and distinguished them from Roman Catholics and Lutherans on the one hand, but on the other also from Anabaptists and Mennonites, who through their antithesis of nature and grace sometimes applied the ban with excessive strictness and deprived it of its spiritual character. Cf. Calvin, *Inst.* IV 12; Martyr, *L. C.*, 411; Zanchius, *Op.* IV 736; Junius, *Theses Theol.* 47; Bucanus, *Instit. theol.*, 581; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 6; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 48; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII, qu. 32; Maresius, *Syst. Theol.* XVI 79–87; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* IV 841 sq.; *Conf. Gall.* 27; *Belg.* 29; *Cat. Heid.* 83–85; *Helv.* II 18; etc. Third, the relation of Christianity to natural life was defined differently by Luther and Calvin. All the Reformers agreed in liberating natural life from the pressure and power of the church, which at Rome coincides with Christianity, comes to nature as a supernatural addition, but otherwise beneath this grants natural life a large sphere of operation. Over against this, Protestantism set the confession that the whole world, although lying in evil, is nevertheless holy and good in itself, a work of God, the Almighty, the Creator of heaven and earth. It exchanged the quantitative antithesis of natural and supernatural for the qualitative, ethical antithesis of sin and grace. Yet in this there was a great difference among the Reformers. Zwingli never entirely overcame the medieval dualism of flesh and spirit, of *humana* and *divina justitia*. Luther frequently restricted the work of Christ so much to the religious-ethical domain that the natural stood detached alongside it; the gospel changed only the inward, the disposition, the heart, but did not work renewingly and reformingly upon the whole of natural life. Hence the contempt with which Luther frequently spoke about reason, philosophy, and jurisprudence; hence the harsh judgment the *Formula Concordiae* pronounced upon the natural human being as *lapis, truncus aut limus*; hence the Lutheran distinction and separation between the sensory and the spiritual as *duo hemisphaeria, quorum unum inferius, alterum superius*; cf. my address on *de Kathol. van Christ. en Kerk*, 1888, p. 28f., and on *de Algemeene Genade*, 1894, p. 24f. This also explains why the Lutheran church, provided only that she had the pure ministry of Word and sacrament, was fairly indifferent toward every other power Christ had granted to the church. She knew better and confessed that the church had to have her own overseers and deacons, her own government and discipline. But in practice she surrendered all this immediately and almost without a struggle to the civil government. If necessary she could allow a monarchical (papal) and episcopal government, recognize many ceremonies as *adiaphora*, leave discipline to the consistories, and entrust the whole external government of the church to the civil government. The church retained for herself only the preaching office, the ministry of Word and sacrament, but otherwise became a territorial and state church in which the civil government, as substitute for the Roman episcopate, as *praecipuum membrum ecclesiae*, or as the church’s plenipotentiary, had virtually everything to say; cf. above, p. 108; Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 555f.; Lechler, *Die neut. Lehre v. h. Amte*, 1857, pp. 223f., 230f.; Sohm, *Kirchenrecht*, 542f.; and further Melanchthon, *Loci Comm. de magistr. civil.*; Gerhard, *Loc.* XXIV; Quenstedt, *Theol.* IV 420–450; Hollaz, *Ex.*, 1352–1366; Baier, *Comp.*, 639–649; Buddeus, *Inst. Theol.*, 1267–1286. In substance this again agreed entirely with the power Zwingli, Erastus, the Remonstrants, and others ascribed to the civil government in relation to the church. But the Reformed opposed this in principle. Just as God had appointed the civil government sovereign in the state, so he anointed Christ king of his church. The two, state and church, were therefore essentially distinct from each other in origin, nature, and government; to assign the power of the church to the civil government was an assault upon the kingship of Christ. But the Reformed never intended this distinction as a separation. On the contrary, just as the church distributes her spiritual goods for the well-being of the whole of natural life in family and society, in art and science, so in a Christian country the civil government has the solemn calling to protect the true church, to support her expansion and propagation, to ward off and eradicate all idolatry and false religion, and to overthrow the kingdom of antichrist. They could teach no differently, because they believed that the civil government had been instituted by God himself on account of sin and for its restraint; that as such it was bound to God’s law and Word; that not only the second but also the first table of the law had to be upheld in its domain and in its way; that Holy Scripture was not a book with exclusively religious-ethical content, valid only for the church, but a Word of God that goes out to all human beings and spreads light upon every creature and all of life; that under the Old Testament the civil government was specially charged with such a task by God; and that Christian truth was universal and catholic, distinct and clear, and thus knowable also by the civil government. Cf. the Reformed confessional writings in Niemeyer, 9, 32, 54, 55, 82, 98, 114, 122, 326, 355, 387, 534, 610, 765, 810; Calvin, *Inst.* IV 20; on the punishment of heretics, *Op.*, ed. Amsterdam, VIII 510–567; cf. Choisy, *La théocratie à Genève au temps de Calvin*, 1898; Bloesch, *Gesch. d. Schweiz.-ref. Kirchen*, Bern, 1899, I 227 sq.; Beza, *de haereticis a civili magistratu puniendis*, *Tract. Theol.* I 85–169; Zanchius, *Op.* IV 580–587; Martyr, *L. C.*, 473; Bullinger, *Huisboek* II 7; Junius, *Op.* I 544; Bogerman, in the dissertation of van der Tuuk, 1868, p. 32f.; Trigland, *Antapol.*, ch. 29; *Kerckel. Gesch.*, passim, especially p. 440f.; Rutherford, *Examen Arminianismi*, ch. 19; Revius, in the dissertation of Posthumus Meyjes, 1895, pp. 151–171; Voetius, *Pol. Eccl.* I 124 sq. and passim; *Disp. sel.* II 692–809; III 206; *Synopsis pur. theol.* 50; Mastricht, *Theol.* VII 7, 14; Turretinus, *Theol. El.* XVIII, qu. 34; Moor VI 470–518; Vitringa IX 1, 700 sq.

But life proved stronger than doctrine; the absolute standpoint was gradually weakened. Already in the 16th century some Anabaptists and Socinians demanded that the civil government abstain from all interference in matters of religion, and specifically from the punishment of heretics. The Reformed doctrine accordingly also encountered many practical difficulties. In theory state and church were indeed distinguished, but in fact the state was frequently subject to the pronouncements of the church and bound to her confession. By virtue of its close union with the church and the obligation it had assumed, the civil government came to deeds of violence and coercion toward which it usually itself felt an aversion, which gave it a bad name among many noble-minded people, burdened it with the appearance of Roman tyranny, and conflicted with the Protestant demand for freedom of conscience and religion. As long as one confession united all citizens in a country, or at least the great majority, the union of church and state could still be maintained. But when the Roman church gradually revived and many kinds of churches and confessions arose within Protestantism, from which the Christian character could not be withheld, it became impossible even for the strictest to maintain the confessional character of the state and the demand for the punishment of heretics. In England this first became clearly evident in the 17th century. Not only Roman Catholics and Episcopalians contended there with each other for precedence, but Presbyterians, Independents, Quakers, Levellers, and Deists soon appeared one after another. Thus, led by the facts, people gradually proceeded from the confessional to the generally Christian, and from there to the deistic character of the state, and tolerance and moderation became the watchword of the eighteenth century. Roger Williams, the “arch-individualist,” was the first in the 17th century to express the demand for separation between church and state and to require absolute religious freedom for everyone, including heretics and Jews, and he put these principles into practice in his colony at Rhode Island. On both the Christian and the revolutionary side this theory increasingly found agreement. Some states in America applied it from 1776 onward; the French Revolution forced it through in many countries. Nevertheless, nowhere does it exist purely and consistently, and in practice everyone recoils from its consequences.
