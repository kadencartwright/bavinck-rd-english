## 12.

Thus the church stands in the midst of the world with an origin, essence, activity, and goal of her own. She is distinct from that world in every respect, yet never stands alongside it in separation. Various movements within Christendom have indeed placed church and world over against each other in an absolute ethical antithesis and identified creation and re-creation with sin and grace. But these movements, however powerful they may have been from time to time, have never governed the history of Christianity and could always lead only a sectarian life alongside the churches. Apart from these movements, there are only two ways in which the relation between church and world can be determined in principle: the Roman Catholic and the Protestant, the supernatural and the ethical. Rome does not regard the natural as sinful, as Anabaptism does, and does not arrive at avoidance and separation, but it does teach that the natural is of a lower order, easily becomes an occasion for sin, and therefore needs the bridle of the supernatural. Just as the image of God was added to natural humanity as a *donum supernaturale*, so grace is added mechanically from above to nature, the church to the world, and the higher morality to the lower. Whoever wishes to live according to Rome’s ideal must become an ascetic, suppress the natural, and devote himself entirely to religion. Whoever cannot do so is given the necessary latitude for the natural and finds in the supernatural the boundary that determines this latitude. Entirely different was the relation that Protestantism adopted between church and world. It replaced the quantitative, supernaturalistic antithesis with the ethical one. The natural was not of a lower order but in its own kind was just as good and pure as the supernatural, for it had been created by the same God who revealed himself in re-creation as the Father of the Lord Jesus Christ. It had only been corrupted by sin and therefore had to be reconciled and renewed by the grace of Christ. Grace here therefore serves not to avoid, suppress, or kill the natural but precisely to free it from its sinful corruption and make it truly natural again. It is true that Luther stopped halfway in applying these principles, left the natural untouched, and restricted Christianity too much to the ethical-religious domain. But Calvin, the man of action, who came after Luther and could therefore take Luther as his example and warning, continued the work of reformation and sought to reform the whole of life through Christianity. Avoidance is the watchword of the Anabaptists; asceticism that of the Roman Catholics; renewal and sanctification that of the Protestant, specifically the Reformed, Christian. This last conception is without doubt the richest and most beautiful. For there is only one God in both creation and re-creation. The God of creation, of the Old Testament, is not a lower God than the God of re-creation, than the Father of Christ, than the God of the New Covenant. Christ, the mediator of the New Covenant, is also the one through whom God created all things. And the Holy Spirit, who is the author of regeneration and sanctification, is the same one who in the beginning hovered over the waters and adorned the heavens. Creation and re-creation therefore cannot stand over against each other as lower and higher. They are both good and pure, both glorious works of the one triune God. Furthermore, the sin that entered the world has indeed corrupted everything: not only the spiritual, ethical-religious life but also all that is natural—the body, the family, society, the entire world. Yet sin is not a substance, not *materia* but *forma*, and is therefore not identical with what is created but dwells in and adheres to what is created and can always be detached and removed from it by the grace of God. Substantially and materially, creation after the fall is the same as before that time; it remains a work of God and as such is to be honored and praised. To regain that fallen world, God now indeed brings the powers of grace into that creation. But this grace too is not a substance or *materia*, enclosed in Word or sacrament and dispensed by the priest, but is a renewing, recreating power. It is not supernatural *per se*, but bears that character only because of sin and thus bears it, in a certain sense, contingently and temporarily in order to restore creation. This grace is distributed in a twofold form: as common grace for restraint, and as special grace for renewal. Both have their unity in Christ, who is king in the *regnum potentiae* and *gratiae*; both are directed against sin; both bring and keep creation and re-creation in connection with each other. The world too, after the fall, has not been abandoned to itself and is not destitute of all grace, but by common grace it is sustained and spared, guided and preserved for the special grace in Christ. Separation and suppression are therefore unlawful and impossible. Human being and Christian are not two beings. Creation is taken up into re-creation and restored. The human being who is regenerated is substantially no other than he was before regeneration. Incorporated into the church, he nevertheless remains in the world and has only to keep himself from the evil one. Just as Christ, the Son of God, assumed the full human nature from Mary and with it regarded nothing human and nothing natural as alien to himself, so the Christian is nothing other than the reborn and renewed, and therefore the true, human being. The same people who are Christians are and remain in the same calling with which they were called; they remain members of the family, citizens of society, subjects of the civil government, practitioners of science and art, men or women, parents or children, masters or servants, and so forth. The relation that must exist between church and world is therefore in the first place organic, moral, and spiritual in nature. Christ is prophet, priest, and king even now, and through his Word and Spirit he works upon the whole world. Through him everyone who believes in him exercises a renewing, sanctifying influence in family, society, state, occupation, business, art, science, and so forth. The spiritual life is destined to bring the natural and moral life, in its full depth and extent, into conformity with the law of God once again. Along this organic path Christian truth and Christian life are carried into all the spheres of natural life, so that household and family life are restored to honor, woman is again regarded as the equal of man, science and art are Christianized, the level of moral life is raised, society and state are reformed, and laws and institutions, morals and customs, are stamped as Christian. But there is still another ordering of the relation between church and world, which is much more difficult and about which the greatest difference of opinion exists. Christ also governs his church through offices and institutions, and the question is whether the relation of the church to the various domains of natural life is also to be regulated officially and institutionally. Papalism and Caesaropapism stand opposed here. Caesaropapism orders the relation in such a way that the church is subject to the Christian state and must conduct herself according to its laws. There is some truth in this; the relation of the church to the state has been entirely different since the latter became Christian. Before the civil government was Christian, for example, many more sins fell under Christian discipline than afterward. Attendance at pagan festivals, idolatry, worship of the emperor, Sabbath desecration, perjury, blasphemy, marriages within prohibited degrees, dreadful sins of fornication, adultery, and so forth, were recognized and punished as sins by the church but not by the state. Since the civil government was Christianized, much greater agreement has arisen in moral conception and judgment. In many cases the church can therefore await the handling of scandalous offenses by the judiciary and needs no court of her own. Yet too much is inferred from this when all power is taken away from the church and entrusted to the Christian civil government. For the power of the church has essentially remained the same, even though its exercise has been significantly altered. The administration of Word and sacrament, after all, is the inalienable right of the church. Furthermore, there always remain many sins, such as Sabbath desecration, fornication, drunkenness, cursing, and so forth, that the civil government does not punish at all or punishes only moderately when they are very public and scandalous. And finally, the church also has a task of her own with those sinners whom the civil government punishes, for the government is satisfied with punishment, but the church seeks to convince, bring to repentance, and save: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 11, 4. On the other side stands the papal system, which indeed deserves praise insofar as it maintains the independence and freedom of the church, but which otherwise wishes to subject, if not the whole world, then at least the whole of baptized Christendom in all its spheres and relations of life judicially and legally to the pope. Family, society, state, art, science, and so forth must be ecclesiastical, for ecclesiastical is identical with Christian, Roman Catholic, and papal. This claim of Rome is not meant morally and spiritually, so that everyone who does not submit to the pope stands guilty before God; rather, it distinctly bears this character: everyone who refuses obedience to the pope also stands judicially and legally guilty before this *vicarius Christi*, can be punished by him if he deems it useful and has the power to do so, and can be compelled to obedience not only by spiritual and moral means but also by physical and civil penalties. The courage of faith and spiritual power of Luther and Calvin freed us from this papal tyranny. Their mighty deed of reformation consisted in their restoring Christianity in its religious-ethical significance, as the religion of grace, and liberating the natural, not from this Christianity, but from the jurisdiction of the Roman Catholic Church. It followed of itself from this that the bond between church and world, apart from the organic way mentioned above, could be established only contractually. It is true that Calvin held tenaciously to the position that the civil government was subject to God’s Word, had to uphold both tables of the law, had to listen to the church as interpreter of God’s Word, and also had to punish civilly various sins over which the church exercised discipline. He drew the boundary line between church and state clearly and sharply, but he drew it differently than we do; the domain in which both had something to say was much larger than we now determine it to be. The civil government, as Christian, also had in its own domain and measure to watch over the honor of God, the flourishing of his church, and the expansion of his kingdom. But nevertheless, the relation between church and state was contractual and free. The church could do nothing other than preach the Word of God and testify in his name to his commandments. But if the civil government, or anyone at all, refused to listen, then the church, Calvin himself, and every Christian had no further power or right to use coercion. Nothing then remained except *resistentia negativa*, passive resistance: Calvin, *Inst.* IV 20, 29; cf. for others, Moor VI 513. Such resistance too was a deed, for as Doumergue, *Calvin le fondateur des libertés modernes*, Montauban, 1898, p. 14, says so beautifully: *c’est la soumission, mais du corps et non de l’âme. Humilié devant le Dieu, qui le châtie, le calviniste reste le juge inexorable du despote qui l’oppresse. Il y a des soumissions plus mortelles à la tyrannie que des révoltes!* Yet every right to coercion and punishment over against the civil government and every human being was taken away from the church, and Christianity was restored and honored in its purely spiritual power. The civil government remained, like every human being, accountable to God alone for its unbelief, its rejection of God’s Word, its transgression of his commandments, and its persecution and oppression of his church. If, however, the civil government freely and independently made profession of the Christian, the Reformed religion—as was, indeed, always preached as its duty and calling—then it followed from this that in its capacity and domain it had to promote this religion and ward off and eradicate heresy and idolatry. The error here lay not in entrusting the Christian civil government with the promotion of God’s honor and service, but in drawing the boundaries of state and church incorrectly and regarding unbelief, heresy, and so forth as crimes against the state. In the century of the Reformation this could scarcely have been otherwise. But since the task of the civil government has been limited, peoples have become free and mature, the churches have divided and subdivided more and more, and all kinds of movements in thought and life have arisen, the distinction between crime and sin has come to be seen more clearly and all coercion is increasingly acknowledged as precisely contrary to the Christian confession. In ordering the relation between church and state, therefore, the following must be maintained: 1º that the church, although her pluriformity has weakened her witness, cannot relinquish the demand that all creatures, art, science, family, society, state, and so forth submit themselves to the Word of the Lord; 2º that this demand is only a proclamation, a moral witness, and may never be pressed directly or indirectly by coercion or punishment; 3º that a Christian, Reformed civil government has the calling to promote God’s honor, protect his church, and overthrow the kingdom of the antichrist; 4º that it can and may never do this except by means that accord with the nature of the gospel of Christ, and only in the domain entrusted to its guardianship; 5º that, itself accountable to God for its attitude toward his Word, it may not interfere with the rights of the individual human being or with those of family, society, art, and science, and thus is not responsible for what occurs within these domains contrary to God’s Word and law; 6º that it must draw the boundaries between sin and crime according to the demand of the gospel and in conformity with the guidance of God’s providence in the history of the peoples—boundaries that, however, do not coincide with those between the first and second tables of the law, for many sins against the second table fall outside the oversight and punishment of the civil government, and many others against the first table (perjury, Sabbath desecration) are punishable also by the Christian civil government; 7º that fixed boundaries can be indicated in the abstract by no one, since they vary with people and age and can be determined to some degree in their direction only through the testimony of the people’s conscience.
