# § 49. The Power of the Church

## 1.

No more than the state does the church belong to the original institutions of the human race. The oldest form of social life was the family, in which civil and religious life still lay interwoven and stood under the leadership of the father or patriarch, who was prophet, priest, and king in his household. Sin, however, made the institution of church and state necessary for the preservation of the human race. In the command of capital punishment for homicide, Gen. 9:6, God institutes civil government in principle; and soon after the building of the tower, under the direction of God’s providence, it accordingly appears among all the nations into which humanity is divided. As soon as a civil government arises, a certain distinction and separation between civil and religious life also comes about of itself; alongside the rulers priests appear. With this the possibility of conflict is naturally given; among the nations the boundaries are repeatedly drawn differently and the bonds established in various ways. While in the East the power of rulers is generally subordinated to the priesthood, in the West, among Greeks and Romans, religion is a political concern and priests are officials of the state. Nowhere in antiquity did a complete separation occur; a neutral state is altogether unknown there. The state maintains and protects religion, if necessary by exile and capital punishment (Socrates), for religion is the foundation and guarantee of its own existence. Israel too was originally organized patriarchally and divided into households, families, clans, and tribes. Under the kings the genealogical division remained in existence and gave the political order a democratic stamp, so that the heads of the tribes and others had to decide important matters in the assemblies of the people. Already under this patriarchal form of government there was a distinction between civil and religious interests, between Moses and Aaron, between officers, שֹׁטְרִים, and judges, שֹׁפְתִים, on the one hand, and priests and Levites on the other. Only in the supreme court, which was established at Jerusalem and had to judge very difficult cases, did priests also have a seat, Deut. 17:8–13; 19:17, 18. It is therefore incorrect to say that in Israel church and state were one. Both were clearly distinguished from each other in laws, institutions, offices, officebearers, and partly even in members; cf. Hoedemaker, “Kerk en Staat in Israel,” in the journal *Troffel en Zwaard*, 1898, pp. 208–237. The priests had to serve in the temple, approach God with the people’s sacrifices, dispense God’s grace and blessing to the people, and instruct them in the law, Lev. 9:22; 10:11; 21:8; Num. 6:22; 16:5; Ezek. 44:23. But they also had to sacrifice for themselves, Lev. 9:7; 16:6; were bound to the law, Deut. 33:10; Jer. 18:18; and were dependent upon the people for their livelihood, Lev. 23:10; Num. 18:8–32; etc. Nor did they possess any secret doctrine or art, any political or civil power, any hierarchical dominion. Israel never was a priestly state; the people’s freedom over against the priestly estate was safeguarded in every way. The prophets appeared freely, had to proclaim the Word of God, had unsparingly to make known to Israel its sins and announce God’s judgments to people and government, but they had no power other than the power of the Word. Foreigners could be incorporated into Israel through circumcision, Ex. 12:48, and the unclean and lepers remained citizens even when they were temporarily set apart. Despite the schism, the religious unity of the people could in itself very well have continued to exist. But the distinctive feature of Israel’s organization consisted in this, that all these laws, offices, and institutions were given and maintained by God. Israel was a theocracy; God was its lawgiver, judge, and king, Isa. 33:22. Therefore, in no sphere in Israel was there room for an independent sovereignty. The king too might not be a despot, but had to be chosen by God, taken from the midst of his brothers, and bound to God’s law, Deut. 17:14–20; 1 Sam. 10:25. Above all offices, institutions, and persons stood the law of God, which regulated the whole life of Israel and had to be observed by everyone without distinction; Israel had to be a holy people and a priestly kingdom, Ex. 19:3; Deut. 7:6. From this it follows that, without erasing the distinction between civil and religious life, the civil government nevertheless also had to uphold the law of God in its own domain. Idolatry, image worship, sorcery, blasphemy, Sabbath desecration, and so forth—all sins against the first table—were therefore frequently punished with death, Ex. 22:18, 20; Lev. 20:2, 6, 27; 24:11–16; Num. 25:5, 7; Deut. 13:1–5; 17:2–7; 18:9–12, 20; 1 Kings 15:12; 18:40; 2 Kings 9:7, 24, 33; 10:6, 11, 17, 18f.; 11:15; 12:2; 18:4; 2 Chron. 15:13; 17:6; 29:10; 31:1; 34:5, 33. Religion was a national matter, sin was crime, and transgression of the first table of the law was a breaking of the covenant. Yet it must be remembered here that the law gave only a very few general rules and frequently left the execution of the penalty to God himself; that the killing of the Canaanites, of Agag, and of Ahab’s house were isolated cases; that Jehu’s zeal went much further than God’s command; that the kings’ reformation was for the most part limited to destroying the idols and restoring the public worship of YHWH; that unbelief and heresy in Israel were not tracked down by any inquisition and occurred frequently; that compulsion of conscience was altogether unknown; that foreigners, on condition of abstaining from public violation of Israel’s religion, were not only tolerated but also treated with courtesy; that priests and prophets never incited the persecution of the ungodly but only admonished them to conversion, for example also in Ps. 2:10; and that they expected from God himself the political and religious victory of Israel over all its enemies. Therefore, when Israel increasingly lost its political independence, the religious congregation could continue to exist and organize itself in its own way. Although the power of priests and high priest gradually expanded after the exile, they soon acquired dangerous competitors in Pharisees and scribes. In the synagogue religious life became independent, not only over against the state but also over against temple and priesthood; and that whole life was concentrated increasingly around the law, instruction in which was the chief purpose of the synagogue, Matt. 4:23; Mark 1:21; etc.; Acts 15:21; 2 Tim. 3:15. That law, or, taken more broadly, the Old Testament Scripture, was the foundation, center, and source of Israel’s religious life; it possessed no other power than that which lay in that Word.
