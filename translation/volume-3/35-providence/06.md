## 6.

In providence, conceived as preservation or concurrence, government is already implied.
The one who so upholds things that by his will and his being he sustains not only their being but even their powers and operations is absolutely sovereign, a king in the true sense.
Government is therefore not a new element added to preservation and concurrence; like each of these two, it is by itself the whole of providence, but now considered only from the standpoint of the final goal toward which God leads all created things through his providence.
It is a beautiful, rich thought when Holy Scripture repeatedly calls God King and describes his providence as government.
There are many in this age who reject every idea of sovereignty in family, state, and society and want to know of nothing but democracy and anarchy.
Under the influence of these views, there are also those who in theology regard the conception of God as King as Old Testament and obsolete and at most still wish to speak of God as Father.
But this judgment is superficial and false.
First, the name Father for God is not confined to the New Testament but was already in use in the Old Testament and even among the pagans; the New Testament may have conceived its meaning more richly and deeply, but it did not first give this name to God; cf. vol. II, 113.
Conversely, the name King for the divine being occurs repeatedly not only in the Old but also in the New Testament, Matt. 6:10, 13, 33; 1 Tim. 1:17; 6:15; Rev. 19:6; and so forth.
And second, the designation King is no less worthy of God than that of Father.
Every πατρια in heaven and on earth is named from him who is the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, Eph. 3:15.
All the relations of higher and lower that exist among creatures are a likeness of that one original relation in which God stands to the works of his hands.
What a father is to his family, what an educator is to the young, what a commander is to the army, what a prince is to his people—all this and so much more God is to his creatures in an altogether original manner.
Not one but all his attributes come to revelation in the world and therefore ought to be honored by us.
And kingship in particular is also a glorious divine institution.
It gives the people not only a personal unity but, as hereditary kingship, also assumes the character of originality, exaltedness, independence, and immutability.
In all this it is a beautiful, though weak, image of God’s kingship.
All sovereignty on earth is borrowed, derived, temporal, limited, and frequently not a blessing but a curse.
But God is King in the absolute and true sense.
The government of the world is not democratic or aristocratic, republican or constitutional, but monarchical.
To God belongs the one, undivided legislative, judicial, and executive power; his sovereignty is original, eternal, unlimited, and beneficent.
He is the King of kings and Lord of lords, 1 Tim. 6:15; Rev. 19:6.
His kingdom is the whole universe.
Heaven and earth are his, Ex. 19:5; Ps. 8:2; 103:19; 148:13.
He possesses all nations, Ps. 82:8; rules over the Gentiles, Pss. 22:29; 47:9; 96:10; Jer. 10:7; Mal. 1:14; and is the Most High over the whole earth, Pss. 47:3, 8; 83:19; 97:9.
He is King forever, Ps. 29:10; 1 Tim. 1:17; no opposition is of any significance against him, Ps. 93:3, 4.
His kingdom will surely come, Matt. 6:10; 1 Cor. 15:24; Rev. 12:10; his glory will be revealed and his name feared from the rising to the setting of the sun, Isa. 40:5; 59:19; he will be King over the whole earth, Zech. 14:9.
In this government too, God deals with each thing according to its nature.
_Deus regit res, naturae ipsarum convenienter_, Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 301.
And therefore this government of God is also represented in different ways and called by different names in Scripture.
Through his government he keeps the world standing and establishes it so that it will not be moved, Ps. 93:1; he appoints light and darkness, Ps. 104:19, 20; commands and withholds the rain, Gen. 7:4; 8:2; Job 26:8; 38:22 f.; gives frost, snow, and ice, Ps. 147:16; rebukes, that is, chastises, and stills the sea, Nah. 1:4; Pss. 65:8; 107:29; sends curse and destruction, Deut. 28:15 f.; all things do his word, Ps. 148:8.
With equal might and sovereignty he governs in the world of rational creatures: he rules among the Gentiles and possesses all nations, Pss. 22:29; 82:8; regards the nations as less than nothing and vanity, Isa. 40:17; does according to his pleasure with the inhabitants of the earth, Dan. 4:35; and directs every heart and thought, Prov. 21:1.

And this government of God over his rational creatures extends not only to the good, of which he is the giver in both nature and grace, James 1:17, and not only to his favored ones, whom he elects, calls, preserves, cares for, and leads to eternal blessedness, but extends also to evil and to those who love and do evil.
It stands firm throughout Scripture that God hates sin with his whole being, Deut. 32:4; Ps. 5:5-7; Job 34:10; 1 John 1:5; and so forth; and his government bears irrefutable witness to this by the prohibition of sin in law and conscience and by its judgments and acts of judgment.
But nevertheless the whole of Scripture also teaches that sin from beginning to end stands under his divine government; cf. Clemen, _Die christl. Lehre von der Sünde_, Göttingen, 1897, I 123-151.
At its beginning God sometimes acts to prevent it; he keeps someone from sinning, Gen. 20:6; 31:7; frustrates the counsel of the wicked, Ps. 33:10; grants strength to stand firm in temptation, 1 Cor. 10:13; and always restrains sin insofar as he forbids it and curbs the sinner by some anxiety and fear in the conscience.
But this _impeditio_ is by no means the only form in which God governs sin.
Frequently he permits it and does not prevent it.
He gave Israel over to the stubbornness of its heart, Ps. 81:13; allowed the Gentiles to walk in their own ways, Acts 14:16; 17:30; and gave them over to a depraved mind, Rom. 1:24, 28; and thus it can be said that God permitted the fall of Adam, the murder of Abel, the unrighteousness of human beings before the flood, Gen. 6:3, the sale of Joseph, Gen. 37, the condemnation of Jesus, and so forth.
But this _permissio_ is so little negative that sin even in its very first beginning stands under God’s directing power and sovereignty.
He creates and orders the occasions and inducements to sin in order to test the human being and thereby either strengthen and establish or punish and harden him, Gen. 2:7; 2 Chron. 32:31; Job 1; Matt. 4:1; 6:13; 1 Cor. 10:13.
Although sin at first seemed to be nothing but an arbitrary act of human beings, it nevertheless becomes evident later that God had his hand in it and that it occurred according to his counsel, Gen. 46:8; 2 Chron. 11:4; Luke 24:26; Acts 2:23; 3:17, 18; 4:28.
Even in its beginning, sin is attributed to God not _formaliter_ and _subjective_ but nevertheless _materialiter_.
God is the potter and the human being is the clay, Jer. 18:5; Lam. 3:38; Isa. 45:7, 9; 64:7; Amos 3:6; he makes obstinate, hardens, blinds, Ex. 4:21; 7:3; 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:4; Deut. 2:30; Josh. 11:20; Isa. 6:10; 63:17; Matt. 13:13; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10; John 12:40; Acts 28:26; Rom. 9:18; 11:8; he turns the heart so that it hates and is disobedient, 1 Sam. 2:25; 1 Kings 12:25; 2 Chron. 25:20; Ps. 105:24; Ezek. 14:9.
He sends an evil spirit, a lying spirit, Judg. 9:23; 1 Sam. 16:14; 1 Kings 22:23; 2 Chron. 18:22; through Satan he incites David, 2 Sam. 24:1; 1 Chron. 21:1; causes Shimei to curse, 2 Sam. 16:10; gives human beings over to their sins; allows the measure of their unrighteousness to become full, Gen. 15:16; Rom. 1:24; sends a powerful delusion, 2 Thess. 2:11; appoints Christ for a fall and rising, for a savor of death and life, Luke 2:34; John 3:19; 9:39; 2 Cor. 2:16; 1 Pet. 2:8; and so forth; cf. vol. II, 315 f., 369 f.
And not only at its beginning but also in its progress God keeps sin under his almighty government; frequently he restrains it, limits it, stops it in its course, and puts an end to it through judgments and acts of judgment, Gen. 7:11; Ex. 15; and so forth; Matt. 24:22; 2 Pet. 2:9; but even where he allows it to continue, he directs it, Prov. 16:9; 21:1, and at its end, whether he forgives it or punishes it, against its will and intention he makes it serviceable to the execution of his counsel and the glorification of his name, Gen. 45:7, 8; 50:20; Ps. 51:6; Isa. 10:5-7; Job 1:20, 22; Prov. 16:4; Acts 3:13; Rom. 8:28; 11:36.
Like sin, the _malum culpae_, suffering too, the _malum poenae_, stands under God’s dominion.
He is the Creator of light and darkness, of good and evil, Amos 3:6; Isa. 45:7; Job 2:10.
Death is his punishment and entered at his command, Gen. 2:17; and all calamities and adversities, all sorrow and suffering, all afflictions and judgments come to human beings from God’s almighty hand, Gen. 3:14 f.; Deut. 28:15 f.; and so forth.
Already under Israel, however, notice was taken of the disharmony that exists in this life between sin and punishment, holiness and blessedness, Ps. 73; Job; Ecclesiastes.
Faith wrestled with this terrible problem, but it nevertheless raised its head from it triumphantly again, not because it saw the problem solved but because it continued to cling to the kingly power and fatherly love of the Lord.
The prosperity of the wicked is only appearance and in any case only temporary, and even in the most grievous suffering the righteous still partake of God’s love and favor, Ps. 73; Job.
The suffering of the devout frequently has its ground not in their personal sin but in the sin of humanity, and has its goal in the salvation of humanity and the honor of God.
Suffering serves not only for retribution, Rom. 1:18, 27; 2:5, 6; 2 Thess. 1:9, but also for testing and chastening, Deut. 8:5; Job 1:12; Ps. 118:8; Prov. 3:12; Jer. 10:24; 30:11; Heb. 12:6 f.; Rev. 3:19; for strengthening and confirmation, Ps. 119:67, 71; Rom. 5:3-5; Heb. 12:10; James 1:2-4; as testimony to the truth, Ps. 44:23; Acts 5:41; Phil. 1:29; 2 Tim. 4:6; for the glorification of God, John 9:2.
In Christ, justice and grace are reconciled with one another; suffering is the way to glory, the cross points toward the crown, _lignum crucis arbor vitae_.
The end toward which all things are led through God’s providence is the establishment of his kingdom, the revelation of his attributes, the honor of his name, Rom. 11:32-36; 1 Cor. 15:18; Rev. 11:15; 12:13; and so forth.
In this comforting manner Scripture treats God’s providence.
Enough riddles remain, both in individual life and in the history of the world and humanity; from this point onward dogmatics occupies itself with nothing other than the mysteries that God’s providence has placed before us in sin, freedom, responsibility, punishment, suffering, death, grace, reconciliation, prayer, and so forth, and therefore need not enter into all those subjects here.
But over all those riddles and mysteries God causes the light of his Word to shine, not in order to solve them but so that through the patience and comfort of the Scriptures we might have hope, Rom. 15:4.
The doctrine of providence is not a philosophical system but a confession of faith, a confession that, even though the appearance of things often speaks against it, nevertheless no Satan, no human being, and no single creature, but God and he alone, by his almighty and omnipresent power, preserves and governs all things.
Such a confession is able to preserve us both from a superficial optimism that fails to recognize the riddles of life and from a proud pessimism that despairs of the world and its lot.
For God’s providence extends over all things, not only over the good but also over sin and suffering, sorrow and death; for if these were withdrawn from God’s direction, what would remain in this world for his government?
It reveals itself not only and not primarily in extraordinary events and miracles but just as much in the fixed order of nature and in the ordinary events of daily life; for what a poor faith it would be that saw God’s hand and counsel from afar in a few momentous events but did not perceive it in one’s own life and lot!
And it leads all these things not contrary to but in accordance with their nature, not apart from the means but through them; for what power would lie hidden in a faith that praised Stoic indifference or fatalistic resignation as true godliness?
Understood in this way, however, as God’s almighty and omnipresent power, it makes us thankful in prosperity and patient in adversity; it causes us to rest with childlike submission in the Lord’s leading and yet at the same time awakens us from our indolence to the highest activity; and under all circumstances it grants us a firm confidence in our faithful God and Father that he will provide us with every necessity of body and soul and will turn to our good all the evil that he appoints for us in this vale of tears, since he is able to do so as almighty God and also willing to do so as a faithful Father.
