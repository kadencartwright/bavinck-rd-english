## 5.

This already indicates the manner in which God exercises his providence in the world, formerly expressed by the doctrine of _concursus_.
This manner is as rich as the diversity introduced among creatures at creation: _Sicut creationis magna est varietas, ita et gubernationis_; Alsted, _Theol. schol._, 315.
Through creation a world was called into being that deserves to be called both a κοσμος and an αἰων and that in both space and time is a _speculum lucidissimum gloriae divinae_; cf. vol. II 420 f.
Providence now serves to lead the world from its beginning toward its final end; it enters into operation immediately with creation and maintains and brings to development what was given in that creation.
Conversely, creation is designed for providence; creation gives creatures a mode of being that can be brought to unfolding in and through providence.
For the world was not created in a state of pure potency, as a chaos or nebulous mass, but as a cosmos, and the human being was placed in it not as a helpless infant but as man and woman; development could proceed only from such a world made ready, and it was thus presented by creation to providence.
Further, that world was a harmonious whole in which unity was joined with the richest diversity; every creature received its own nature and therein its own being, its own life, and its own law of life.
Just as the moral law was implanted in Adam’s heart as the rule for his life, so all creatures bore in their own nature the _principia_ and laws for their development.
All things were created through the word.
Everything rests upon thought.
The whole creation is a system of God’s ordinances, Gen. 1:26, 28; 8:22; Ps. 104:5, 9; 119:90, 91; Eccles. 1:10; Job 38:10 f.; Jer. 5:24; 31:25 f.; 33:20, 25.
He gave all creatures an order, a law that they do not transgress, Ps. 148:6; cf. vol. I 259, 287 f.
In all its parts creation rests upon God’s counsel, and this counsel comes to expression in the small and the great; it all proceeds from the Lord of hosts, who is wonderful in counsel and great in deed, Isa. 28:23–29.
Thus Scripture teaches us to understand the world, and thus Christian theology has also understood it.
Augustine said that _semina occulta, originales regulae, seminariae rationes_ were implanted in creatures and, hidden in the secret womb of nature, are the _principia_ of all development.
_Quaecunque nascendo ad oculos nostros exeunt, ex occultis seminibus accipiunt progrediendi primordia, et incrementa debitae magnitudinis distinctionesque formarum ab originalibus tamquam regulis sumunt_; _de trin._ III 7; _de Gen. ad lit._ IV 33.
The world is therefore pregnant with the causes of beings: _sicut matres gravidae sunt foetibus, sic ipse mundus gravidus est causis nascentium, quae in illo non creantur, nisi ab illa summa essentia, ubi nec oritur nec moritur aliquid nec incipit esse nec desinit_; _de trin._ III 9.
It is an _arbor rerum_ that brings forth branch and blossom and fruit from itself, _de Gen. ad lit._ VIII 9.
Yet God preserves things and works in them in such a way that they themselves cooperate as _causae secundae_.
This does not mean that one should stop at these secondary causes; we must always ascend to the cause of all being and motion, and that is the will of God alone: _voluntas conditoris conditae rei cujusque natura est_; _de civ._ XXI 8; cf. _de trin._ III 6–9.
To this extent providence is not only a positive but also an immediate act of God.
His will, his power, and his being are immediately present in every creature and every event.
Everything exists and lives together in him, Acts 17:28; Col. 1:17; Heb. 1:3.
Just as he created the world by himself, so he also preserves and governs it by himself.
Although God works through the _causae secundae_, this may not be interpreted deistically, as though they stood between God and the operations with their effects and removed these from him.
_Immediate Deus omnibus providet, quoad ordinis rationem_; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 22 art. 3; qu. 103 art. 6; qu. 104 art. 2; _c. Gent._ III 76 sq.
For this reason, a miracle is neither a breach of the law of nature nor an intervention in the order of nature from without.
From God’s side, it is an act that has God as its cause no more immediately and directly than does every ordinary event, and in God’s counsel and in the world-idea it occupies a place just as ordered and harmonious as does every natural phenomenon.
In the miracle God merely brings a special power into operation that, like every other power, works according to its own nature and law and thus also has its own product as a consequence; cf. vol. I 289.
But in creation God placed his ordinances in things, an _ordo rerum_ by which the things themselves are mutually connected.
God does not depend upon those causes, but things do depend upon one another.
That connection is manifold; although it can in general be called causal, causality in this sense is by no means to be identified with mechanism, as materialism would have it.
The mechanical connection is only one manner in which a portion of the things in the world are related to one another.
Just as creatures received their own nature in creation and differ among themselves, so there is also a distinction in the laws according to which they operate and in the relations in which they stand to one another.
These differ in the physical and psychical realms, in the intellectual and ethical worlds, in family and society, in science and art, in the kingdoms of the earth and in the kingdom of heaven.
It is God’s providence that, in continuity with creation, maintains all these distinct natures, powers, and ordinances and brings them to full unfolding.
In providence God does not nullify but respects and develops what he called into being in creation.
Thus he preserves and governs all creatures in accordance with their nature, the angels differently from human beings and the latter in turn differently from animals and plants.
But insofar as God in his providence maintains the mutual relations of things and makes creatures mutually serviceable to one another’s existence and life, providence may be called mediate.
_Immediate Deus omnibus providet, quoad ordinis rationem, quoad executionem ordinis vero per aliqua media providet_; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 22 art. 3.
Thus he created the angels all at once but makes human beings proceed from one blood; thus he preserves some creatures individually, others as species and genus; thus he repeatedly employs all kinds of creatures as means in his hand to execute his counsel and attain his end.

Christian theology did not deny this; on the contrary, following Scripture it always emphatically maintained the order of nature and the causal connection of phenomena.
It is untrue that Christianity, with its supernaturalism, is hostile to an order of nature and makes science impossible, as Draper’s _History of the Conflict between Religion and Science_, 2nd ed., Haarlem, 1887, attempts to demonstrate with evident delight.
Much more accurate is the judgment of Du Bois Reymond when he said: _die neuere Naturwissenschaft, wie paradox dies klinge, verdankt ihren Ursprung dem Christenthum_; _Kulturgesch. und Naturw._, Leipzig, 1878, S. 28; cf. also Lange, _Gesch. des Mater._, 1882, S. 129 f., and others in Martensen Larsen, _Die Naturwiss. in ihrem Schuldverhältnis zum Christenthum_, Berlin, 1897.
In any event, Christianity made science, specifically natural science, possible and prepared the ground for it.
For the more the phenomena of nature are deified, as in polytheism, and regarded as visible images and bearers of the Deity, the more impossible scientific investigation becomes, since it naturally assumes the character of sacrilege and disturbs the mystery of the Deity.
But Christianity distinguished God and the world and, through its confession of God as the Creator of all things, released God from the nexus of nature and placed him high above it; the investigation of nature is no longer an assault upon the Deity.
Further, it thereby at the same time set the human being free from nature and placed him in an independent position over against it, as the beautiful contemplation of nature among the psalmists and prophets and in Jesus and the apostles proves so clearly; for the believer, nature is no longer an object of worship and fear; while he bows before God in deep humility and is absolutely dependent upon him, he has precisely the calling to rule the earth and subject all things to himself, Gen. 1:26.
Dependence upon God is something altogether different from _convenienter naturae vivere_ and accommodating oneself to circumstances.
Many reason in such a way that they either attribute all things and events to God’s will and regard resistance as unlawful, or limit God’s providence and place many things in human hands; cf., for example, Beyschlag, _Zur Verständigung über den christl. Vorsehungsglauben_, 1888, S. 24 f.
But Scripture warns us against both this antinomianism and this Pelagianism and cuts off at the root all false, fatalistic resignation and all proud self-confidence.
Bowing before the power of nature is something entirely different from submitting to God as a child; and ruling the earth is a service of God.
The captain who during a storm went into his cabin to pray and read the Bible submitted himself to the power of the elements but not to God; Harris, _God the Creator and Lord of All_, I 545.
There is far more true piety in Cromwell’s saying: _trust God and keep your powder dry_.
Next, it is the confession of God as the Creator of heaven and earth that immediately brings with it the thought of the one absolute truth, never divided against itself; of the harmony and beauty of God’s counsel; and thus also of the unity of the world-plan and of the order of all nature.
_Wenn in freier und grossartiger Weise dem einen Gott auch ein einheitliches Wirken aus dem Ganzen und Vollen zugeschrieben wird, so wird der Zusammenhang der Dinge nach Ursache und Wirkung nicht nur denkbar sondern er ist sogar eine nothwendige Consequenz der Annahme_; Lange, _Gesch. d. Mater._, 130.
Scripture itself leads the way in acknowledging such an order of nature and all kinds of ordinances and laws for created things.
And the miracle so little infringes upon it that it instead presupposes and confirms that fixed order of nature.
The Christian church and theology have gladly acknowledged such an _ordo rerum_ at all times; Augustine repeatedly appealed to the word in Wisdom 11:20, _omnia mensura numeroque et pondere ordinasti_.
At least in the earliest period they vigorously resisted the dreadful superstition that rose to an extraordinary height in the third and fourth centuries, and in particular also opposed astrology; Augustine, _de civ._ V 1–8; Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ III 84 sq.; Calvin, _Contre l’astrologie_, _C. R._ 35, pp. 509–544; Turretinus, _Theol. El._ VI qu. 2; Moor II 435; M. Vitringa II 180, etc.
The conflict that repeatedly broke out was not waged between Christianity and natural science; the parties were grouped quite differently; it was usually a conflict between the old and the new worldview, in which believing Christians stood on both sides; cf. vol. II 465.

This fundamentally correct view of nature espoused by theology appears nowhere more clearly than in its doctrine of _concursus_ and the _causae secundae_.
In pantheism and deism this doctrine cannot come into its own.
In pantheism there are no _causae_, and in deism there are no causae _secundae_.
In pantheism the _causae secundae_, that is, the proximate causes of things within the sphere of creation, are identified with the _causa prima_, that is, God.
There is no distinction of substance and operation between them; God is _materialiter_ and _formaliter_ the subject of everything that occurs, thus also of sin; at most, the so-called secondary causes are occasions and passive instruments for the operations of God.
Previously occurring only sporadically, this doctrine came to dominance in the modern philosophy of Descartes and thus led to the idealism of Berkeley and Malebranche and to the pantheism of Spinoza, Hegel, and Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 46; Strauss, _Gl._ II 384, etc.
Thus Malebranche says: _qu’il n’y a qu’une vraie cause parce qu’il n’y a qu’un vrai Dieu, que la nature ou la force de chaque chose n’est que la volonté de Dieu, que toutes les causes naturelles ne sont point de véritables causes, mais seulement des causes occasionnelles._
God alone can be a true cause, because he alone can create and cannot communicate this power to any creature; if creatures could truly be causes of motions and phenomena, they themselves would be gods; but _toutes ces petites divinités des payens et toutes ces causes particulières des philosophes ne sont que des chimères, que le malin esprit tâche d’établir pour ruiner le culte du vrai Dieu_; _De la recherche de la vérité_, l. 6, p. 2, ch. 3; cf. also _Eclaircissement_ 15.
There are therefore only _phaenomena_, representations, and the only reality, power, and substance concealed behind them is that of God himself; cf. also Kleutgen, _Philos. der Vorzeit_ II² 336–347; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ I 592.
Conversely, in deism the _causae secundae_ are separated from the _causa prima_ and made independent; the _causa prima_ is restricted entirely to creation, to the giving of the _posse_, and is entirely excluded from the _velle_ and _facere_, as in original Pelagianism; or both causes are conceived as _causae sociae_ that operate beside and with one another, as two horses pull a wagon, even if one is perhaps stronger than the other, as in semi-Pelagianism and synergism; here the creature becomes the creator of its own acts.
Scripture, however, says both that God works all things, so that the creature is only an instrument in his hand—for example, Isa. 44:24; Ps. 29:3; 65:11; 147:16; Matt. 5:45; Acts 17:25, etc.—and that providence is distinct from creation and presupposes the existence and self-activity of creatures—for example, Gen. 1:11, 20, 22, 24, 28, etc.
In agreement with this, Christian theology teaches that the _causae secundae_ are absolutely subordinated to God as _prima causa_ and yet remain genuine, true _causae_ in that subordination.
A few individuals admittedly deviated to one side here, such as the nominalist Biel in the Middle Ages and Zwingli at the time of the Reformation, who thought that the second causes were wrongly so called and preferred to call them instruments, _de provid._, _Op._ IV 95 sq.; and the American theologian Emmons in later times; cf. Strong, _Syst. Theol._, New York, 1890, p. 205; Hodge I 594; H. B. Smith, _Syst. of christ. Theol._, 1890, p. 103.
Nevertheless, it was the constant teaching of the Christian church that the second causes are entirely dependent upon the first cause but at the same time are also true, essential causes.
God acts within every _causa secunda_ with his almighty power and is present to it with his being at its beginning, progress, and end.
It is he who posits it and brings it into action (_praecursus_) and then also accompanies and directs its operation all the way to its effect (_concursus_); he works the willing and the working according to his good pleasure.
But this operation of the _causa prima_ within the _causae secundae_ is so divinely great that precisely thereby he brings those _causae secundae_ to activity of their own.
_Providentia Dei causas secundas non tollit sed ponit_; Wollebius in Heppe, _Dogm. d. ev. ref. K._, 191.
The _concursus_ is precisely the cause of the self-activity of the secondary causes; and these, borne from beginning to end by God’s might, operate with a _propria et insita virtus_.
So little does God’s activity nullify the activity of the creature that the latter becomes more powerful in proportion as the former reveals itself more richly and fully.
The _causa prima_ and _causa secunda_ thus remain two distinct causes; the first does not destroy the second but instead grants it reality, and the second exists only through the first.
Nor are the _causae secundae_ mere _instrumenta_, mere _organa_, sticks and blocks, but genuine, essential causes with their own nature, power, spontaneity, operation, and law.
_Sathan et les mechans ne sont pas tellement instrumens de Dieu, que cependant ils n’operent aussi bien de leur coste. Car il ne faut pas imaginer, que Dieu besongne par un homme inique comme par une pierre ou par un tronc de bois, mais il en use comme d’une creature raisonnable, selon la qualité de sa nature, qu’il luy a donnée. Quand donc nous disons, que Dieu opere par les mechans, cela n’empeche pas que les meschans n’operent aussi en leur endroict_; Calvin, _C. R._ 35, 188.
With respect to God, the _causae secundae_ can be compared to instruments, Isa. 10:15; 13:5; Jer. 50:25; Acts 9:15; Rom. 9:20–23; with respect to their effects and products, they are _causae_ in the proper sense.
And precisely because the first and second causes do not stand and operate dualistically alongside each other, but the first operates through the second, the operation that proceeds from both is one, and the product is also one.
There is no division of labor between God and his creature, but the same operation is entirely the operation of the _causa prima_ and likewise entirely the operation of the _causa proxima_; and in the same sense the product is entirely the product of the first and entirely the product of the second cause.
But because the _causa prima_ and the _causa secunda_ are not identical but differ in essence, the operation and product are indeed _realiter_ entirely the operation and product of both causes, but _formaliter_ they are nevertheless only the operation and product of the second cause.
The wood burns, and it is God alone who causes it to burn, but formally the burning may not be attributed to God and must be attributed solely to the wood as its subject.
The human being speaks, acts, and believes, and it is God alone who causes him to speak, act, and believe; yet it is not God but the human being who is the formal subject of all these acts.
And so it is also God alone who grants the sinner all the life and power he needs to commit a sin; but sin has the human being, not God, as its subject and author.
In this way Holy Scripture draws the lines within which the reconciliation of God’s sovereignty and human freedom must be sought.
