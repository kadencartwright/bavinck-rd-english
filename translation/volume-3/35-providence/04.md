## 4.

God’s providence, thus distinguished from God’s knowledge and decree and maintained over against pantheism and deism, is, according to the beautiful declaration of the Heidelberg Catechism, that almighty and omnipresent power of God by which he still upholds and governs heaven and earth, together with all creatures, as if by his hand.
Even understood in this way, the doctrine of providence is still very broad in scope.
It properly encompasses the entire execution of all the decrees that relate to what was once called into existence through creation.
If the act of creation is excepted, it is as rich as the _scientia libera_, as the decrees of God, as everything that exists and happens in time.
It extends over everything treated in dogmatics after creation and includes both the works of nature and those of grace; all God’s outward works that follow creation are works of his providence.
The locus _De providentia_, however, does not enter into those works themselves but describes in general the nature of the relation in which God stands to the created world, a relation that always remains the same despite the many different works he brings about in the world in his providence.
It is therefore also not desirable to discuss in this locus all kinds of subjects such as miracle, prayer, the end of life, freedom of the will, sin, theodicy, and so forth, for some of these subjects have already been treated earlier in the doctrine of God’s attributes and decrees, while others will later be discussed at length in their own place.
The task of theodicy rests not upon the locus _De providentia_ alone but upon the whole of dogmatics.
The doctrine of providence therefore does not incorporate the material for the following loci but confines itself to describing the selfsame relation in which God stands to the creatures throughout all his different works.
That relation is expressed by the words preservation, concurrence, and government, which gradually came to be understood as parts of providence.
Whatever God may do in nature and grace, it is always he who preserves all things, exerts his power within them, and governs them.
Preservation, concurrence, and government are therefore not parts or pieces into which the work of providence is divided and which, separated substantively and temporally, follow one another.
Nor do they differ from one another in such a way that preservation would relate only to the being of creatures, concurrence only to their activities, and government exclusively to their direction toward the final goal.
Rather, they are always connected with one another and at every moment interpenetrate one another: preservation is also government from the very beginning, government is concurrence, and concurrence is preservation.
Preservation tells us that nothing exists—not a substance, power, activity, property, or idea—that does not exist from, through, and unto God.
Concurrence makes that same preservation known to us as one that does not annul the being of creatures but precisely posits and maintains it.
And government shows us both as guiding all things in such a way that the final goal established by God is attained.
And always, from beginning to end, providence is one simple, almighty, and omnipresent power.

Understood as such a power and act of God, providence stands in the closest connection with God’s activity in creation and yet is also essentially distinct from it.
Pantheism and deism seek to solve the problem that arises here by denying either creation or providence.
Theism maintains both and seeks to bring their unity and distinction to light for both the theory and the practice of life.
Always to be a theist in the full, true sense—that is, to see God’s counsel, hand, and work in everything and yet at the same time, indeed precisely for that reason, to develop every power and gift to the highest activity—that is the glory of the Christian faith, the secret of the Christian life.
Scripture leads the way for us in this.
On the one hand, it designates providence as creating, Ps. 104:30; making alive, Neh. 9:6; speaking, Pss. 33:9; 105:31, 34; 107:25; Job 37:6; sending forth his Word and Spirit, Pss. 104:30; 107:26; commanding, Ps. 147:15; Lam. 3:37; working, John 5:17; upholding, Heb. 1:3; willing, Rev. 4:11, so that without exception everything is from God and exists through and unto him, Acts 17:28; Rom. 11:36; Col. 1:17.
God is never idle.
He never looks on passively.
He always works with divine power in nature and grace.
Providence is therefore a positive act, not a _letting_ but a _causing_ to exist and operate from moment to moment.
If it consisted only in a _non-destruere_, it would not be God who upheld things, but they would exist in and through themselves, even if by means of a power granted at creation.
And this is absurd to think; a creature is by its very nature an absolutely dependent being; what does not exist _of_ itself cannot exist for a single moment _through_ itself either.
If God does nothing, then there is nothing and nothing happens.
_Virtus Dei, ab eis quae creata sunt regendis si aliquando cessaret, simul et illorum cessaret species omnisque natura concideret_, Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ IV 12; _Conf._ IV 17; cf. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 104, art. 1-4; _C. Gent._ III 65 sq.; Calvin, _Inst._ I 16, 4; Leydecker, _Fax verit._ VIII 2; Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 304; and so forth.
And just as providence is a power and an act, so it is also an almighty and omnipresent power.
God is present immanently in all creatures with his being.
His providence extends to all creatures; everything exists in him.
Scripture declares most emphatically that nothing, however insignificant, falls outside God’s providence.
Not only all things in general, Eph. 1:11; Col. 1:17; Heb. 1:3, but even the hairs of the head, Matt. 10:30; the sparrows, Matt. 10:29; the birds of heaven, Matt. 6:26; the lilies of the field, Matt. 6:28; the young ravens, Ps. 147:9, are objects of his care.
What, after all, is small or great to him who alone is great?
Within the coherence of the world, the small has its place just as much as the great, is equally indispensable and necessary, and is frequently of richer significance and more momentous consequence.
Providence may therefore indeed be distinguished as _generalis_, Pss. 104; 148:1-3; _specialis_, Ps. 139:15 f.; Job 10:9-12; Matt. 12:12; Luke 12:7; and _specialissima_, 1 Tim. 4:10.
Yet as the power of God it extends to all creatures and to each creature.
Habakkuk does indeed complain in chapter 1:14 that through his chastisement God makes human beings like the fish of the sea that are caught in the net and כְּרֶמֶשׂ לֹא־מֹשֵׁל, like creeping things that have no ruler, namely, to protect them against their enemies, but by this he does not say in the least that God’s providence does not extend over these creatures.
With more apparent right, appeal is made to 1 Cor. 9:9 in support of restricting God’s providence; yet Paul, who everywhere else conceives God’s sovereignty so absolutely, for example, Acts 17:28; Rom. 11:36; Col. 1:17, by no means denies here that God also cares for oxen but only indicates that the reason this word was included in God’s law lies not in the oxen but in human beings.
God also says this word concerning the oxen δι’ ἡμας, v. 10; cf. Rom. 4:23, 24; 15:4; 2 Tim. 3:16, so that we might learn from it that the laborer in the gospel is worthy of his wages.
Providence, then, is as great, almighty, and omnipresent an act of God as creation; it is a _creatio continua_ or _continuata_; they are both one act and differ only _ratione_, Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ IV 15; _Conf._ IV 12; _De civ._ XII 17; Thomas I, qu. 104, art. 2; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I, p. 351; Ursinus, _Explic. Cat._, qu. 27; M. Vitringa II 183; Heppe, _Dogm. d. ev. ref. K._ 190.

In speaking this way, earlier theologians by no means intended to abolish the distinction that exists between creation and providence, as, for example, Hodge I 577 fears.
For on the other hand Scripture presents providence as a resting from the work of creation, Gen. 2:2; Ex. 20:11; 31:17, and further as seeing, Ps. 14:2; as beholding, Ps. 33:13; as attending, Ps. 33:15; as observing, Ps. 130:3; and so forth, all of which presupposes the existence, self-activity, and freedom of the creature.
These data of Scripture may not be neglected either.
Creation and providence are not the same.
If providence were a creation renewed at every moment, creatures too would be brought forth from nothing at every moment.
The coherence, connection, the _ordo causarum_, would then be entirely lost, and development and history would be impossible.
All creatures would then exist not really but only in appearance and would lack all independence, freedom, and responsibility; God himself would be the cause of sin.
Although many called providence a _creatio continua_, they by no means intended thereby to erase the distinction between the two; rather, they all understood providence at the same time as causing things to persist in existence, as a _conservatio_ that presupposes creation.
Thus Augustine says, for example, that God rested on the seventh day and created no new _genera_, and he then describes the work of providence in distinction from that of creation as follows: _movet itaque occulta potentia universam creaturam suam...... explicat secula, quae illi cum primum condita sunt tanquam implicita indiderat, quae tamen in suos cursus non explicarentur, si ea ille qui condidit provido motu administrare cessaret_, _De Gen. ad lit._ V 20.
Providence may therefore sometimes be called a _creatio_, but it is always distinguished from the first and proper creation in that it is a _creatio continua_.
The two thus agree in this: it is the same divine, almighty, and omnipresent power that is active in creation and in preservation; the latter is no lesser act than the former; power, divine omnipotence, is required for both.
Further, creation and preservation are naturally not distinguished in God himself either, for in him, the Eternal One, there is neither change nor shadow of turning; he did not pass from not creating to creating, nor from creating to preserving; he is immutably the same; cf. vol. II, 411.
Creation and preservation are therefore not distinguished objectively and substantively, as acts of God, in God’s being, but only _ratione_.
This does not mean, however, that our thought simply makes an arbitrary distinction between the two; no, that distinction is most certainly grounded in God’s revelation and derived from it by our thought.
There is a difference between creation and preservation, but that difference lies not in God’s being _an sich_ but in the relation in which God places himself toward the creatures.
What happens to things through creation is one thing, and what happens to them through preservation is another.
The relation in which the creatures are placed toward God through each of these acts is different.
This difference cannot be indicated by saying simply that creation is from nothing and preservation from what exists; rather, creation calls forth the things that do not exist, that have no other being than that of ideas and decrees in the being of God; through preservation, with the same power, God calls those things that have received an existence distinct from his being and yet are solely and exclusively from, through, and unto God.
Creation gives being; preservation gives persistence in being.
The difficulty for thought in maintaining both creation and preservation lies repeatedly in this: that through creation creatures have received a being of their own, distinct from God’s being, and yet that being cannot or may not for a single moment be regarded as a being independent of God and resting in itself.
Here we stand before a mystery that far surpasses our understanding, and we are always inclined to do injustice to either the one or the other.
Pantheism and deism rest upon this inclination.
Both proceed from the same error and set God and the world over against each other as two magnitudes.
The former sacrifices the world to God, creation to preservation, and thinks that God’s being is a divinely infinite being only when the being of the world is denied, dissolved into appearance, and swallowed up in the divine being.
The latter sacrifices God to the world, preservation to creation, and judges that the creature receives its due all the more as it becomes less dependent upon God and moves further away from him.
The Christian, however, confesses that the world and every creature in it have received a being of their own, but nevertheless increase in reality, in freedom, and in true being to the same degree that they are more dependent upon God and are from moment to moment from, through, and unto him.
A creature stands higher in proportion as God indwells it more and permeates it with his being.
In this respect preservation even surpasses creation; for creation gave only the beginning of being, but preservation is God’s ongoing and ever-increasing communication to his creatures.
Providence is “the progressive expression in the universe of his divine perfection, the progressive realization in it of the archetypal ideal of perfect wisdom and love,” Sam. Harris, _God the Creator and Lord of all_, Edinb.: Clark, 1897, I 532.
