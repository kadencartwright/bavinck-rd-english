## 3.

The Christian doctrine of providence as an almighty act of God by which he sustains and governs all things must be distinguished not only from pagan fate and chance but for that very reason also from the pantheism and deism that repeatedly revive during the Christian centuries.
For “there are but three alternatives for the sum of existence, chance, fate or Deity. With chance there would be variety without uniformity, with fate uniformity without variety, but variety in uniformity is the demonstration of primal design and the seal of the creative mind. In the world as it exists, there is infinite variety and amazing uniformity,” James Douglas in U. B. Smith, _System of Christian Theol._, New-York: Armstrong, 1890, p. 107.
Pantheism knows no distinction between the being of God and the being of the world and causes, idealistically, the world to be absorbed into God or, materialistically, God into the world.
On this standpoint there is no place for creation and therefore, in the proper sense, none for preservation and government either.
Providence coincides with the course of nature; the laws of nature are identical with the decrees of God; and God’s government is nothing but the _fixus et immutabilis naturae ordo sive rerum naturalium concatenatio_, Spinoza, _Tract. theol. pol._, c. 3; cf. Strauss, _Gl._ II 384; Schleiermacher, _Gl._ § 46 and further vol. II, pp. 388–396.
With this, the miracle, the self-activity of the _causae secundae_, personality, freedom, prayer, sin, and the whole of religion necessarily fall away.
No matter how beautiful and seductive a form pantheism may assume, in fact it leads into the arms of a pagan fate.
There is then no other being than the being of nature; there is no higher power than that which works in the world according to fixed law; there is no other, better life than that for which the data are present in this visible creation; for a time the human being may flatter himself with the idealistic hope that the world will perfect itself through immanent development, but soon this optimism turns into pessimism and this idealism into materialism.
Over against this pantheism Christian theology had to uphold the distinction between creation and preservation, the self-activity of the _causae secundae_, the freedom of personality, the character of sin, and the truth of religion.
And it did so by rejecting the _fatum_ and clearly setting forth the confession of God’s providence in distinction from it.
The characteristic feature of the doctrine of _fatum_ does not consist in the fact that all that exists and occurs in time is grounded and determined in God’s eternal counsel, but in this: that all being and occurrence is determined by a power that coincides with the world and that, without consciousness and without will, determines everything with blind necessity.
According to Cicero, the _fatum_ of the Stoa was an _ordo seriesque causarum, cum causa ex se causam gignit_, _De divin._ 1; cf. Seneca, _De benef._ IV 7; _Nat. qu._ II 36.
It was then commonly further distinguished into a _fatum mathematicum_ or _astrale_, when events on earth were thought to be determined by the stars, and a _fatum physicum_, when they were thought to be determined by the nexus of nature.
In the latter form the doctrine of _fatum_ now appears in pantheism and materialism; but it is remarkable that the _fatum astrale_ too has recently been revived and finds ardent defenders, cf. _Wetensch. Bladen_, 1896, IV 453.
Christian theology by no means disputed that everything was eternally known and determined by God; to that extent it even acknowledged a _fatum_, and some thought that the word too could be used in a good sense.
If we consider, says Augustine, that _fatum_ comes from _fari_, and if by it we then designate the eternal, immutable word by which God sustains all things, the name can be approved, _De civ._ V 9.
Boethius spoke of _fatum_ as an _inhaereus rebus mobilibus dispositio, per quam providentia suis quaeque nectit ordinibus_, _De cons. philos._ IV, prose 6.
And even Maresius thought that a Christian sense could be attached to the word, _Syst. Theol._, p. 149.
Usually, however, people were more cautious; belief in fate proceeded from the thought that everything occurs as a consequence of a blind, irresistible power without consciousness and will, and those events were called _fatalia_ that _praeter Dei et hominum voluntatem cujusdam ordinis necessitate contingunt_, Augustine, _De civ._ V 3.
In this sense _fatum_ was opposed in the strongest terms by all Christian theologians, no less by Augustine and his followers than by those who defended freedom of the will.
_Omnia vero fato fieri non dicimus, imo nulla fieri fato dicimus_, Augustine, _De civ._ V 9.
The only _ordinis necessitas_ on the Christian standpoint is the wise, almighty, loving will of God.
This did not entail denying, as will become clear later, that in the world of creatures there is a nexus of causes and effects and that there are fixed ordinances; but the order of nature does not stand behind and above, nor outside and over against, God’s will; rather, it is grounded in that will of an almighty and loving God and Father, determined by that will, and subservient to that will; nor does it stand as a blind, coercive power outside and over against our will, for _ipsae nostrae voluntates in causarum ordine sunt, qui certus est Deo ejusque praescientia continetur_, Augustine, ibid.; and further the commentaries on _Sent._ I, dist. 35; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 116; _C. Gent._ III 93; Petavius, _De Deo_ VIII 4; Gerhard, _Loc._ VI 13; Calvin, _Inst._ I 16, 8; Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 313 sq.; Alting, _Theol. elenct. nova_, p. 290; Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ VII 2; Turretin, _Theol. El._ VI 2; M. Vitringa II 170, 177–181; Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._ 472; etc.

On the other side stands deism, which separates God and the world; allows creatures, once they have been created, either wholly or partly—and in the latter case to a greater or lesser extent—to exist and work through their own power received with creation; and thus essentially revives the pagan doctrine of chance.
In the spirit of _magna Dei curant, parva negligunt_, Jerome once stated that God’s providential care did not extend to all small insects, cf. vol. II, p. 160.
Pelagianism, like Cicero, _Nat. D._ III 36, ascribed virtue to the human being’s own will and power, and Semi-Pelagianism divided the labor and ascribed something to both God and the human being.
When this system later penetrated Roman Catholic theology, no small disagreement arose concerning God’s cooperation in providence; the Thomists conceived it as a _praedeterminatio physica_, an _applicatio ad operandum_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2, qu. 9, art. 6, ad 3; qu. 79; 109; _C. Gent._ III 67–70, 162; the Molinists, by contrast, understood by it a _concursus simultaneus, mere cooperans, quo Deus cum alio concurrente in eundem actum et effectum influit_; cf. Daalman, _Summa S. Thomae_ II 286–314; _Theol. Wirceb._ I, c. 2; Dens, _Theol._ I 66 sq.; Liberatore, _Instit. philos._ III, c. 4, a. 1, 2; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 22 f.; Jansen, _Prael._ II 334.
Socinianism set the infinite and the finite over against one another so abstractly and dualistically that God could not even create the world from nothing but only from an eternal and finite matter; and accordingly it also withdrew a large part of the world from God’s providence and left it to the human being’s own insight and judgment.
The will is also so free by nature that God cannot even calculate with certainty beforehand what a human being will do in a given case; only after the decision has been made does God conform his action to it; the free causes therefore stand entirely independently alongside and outside God.
God’s relation to the world is like that of a craftsman to a machine; after he has made it and set it in motion, he leaves it to itself and intervenes only when something needs to be repaired, Volkelius, _De vera relig._ II, c. 7; Crell, _De Deo et ejus attrib._, cc. 2–6; cf. Fock, _Der Socin._ 496 f.
The Remonstrants likewise judged that powers had been bestowed upon creatures in creation on which they could now subsist.
Preservation was therefore a negative act of God by which he _essentias, vires ac facultates rerum creatarum non vult destruere sed eas vigori suo relinquere, quoad vigere ac durare possunt ex vi per creationem ipsis indita_, or at least this conception was not called incorrect.
In connection with this, the _concursus_ as a _naturalis quaedam influentia in res omnes ex naturae divinae perfectione emanens_ was rejected; the predestination of the number of human beings, of marriages, of the end of life, of the elect and the lost was opposed; free will was defended; and every _providentia efficax circa peccatum_ was replaced by a negative _permissio_ or _non-impeditio_, _Conf. Rem._, c. 6; _Apol. Conf._, ibid.; Episcopius, _Inst. Theol._ IV, sect. 4; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II 25 sq.
Arminianism was indeed condemned at Dort and placed outside the Reformed domain, but as a tendency of thought it found acceptance everywhere and penetrated all Christian countries and churches.
The period that began in the middle of the 17th century was characterized by a powerful striving to emancipate nature, the world, the human being, science, and so forth from God, Christianity, the church, and theology and to make them independent of these; latitudinarianism, deism, rationalism, pietism, the _Aufklärung_, and so forth agree in this.
This world is the best possible; the human being is sufficient unto himself with his understanding and will; natural law, natural power, natural right, natural religion, and natural morality together constitute a fund of powers given to the world by God at creation and now fully sufficient for existence and development; revelation, prophecy, miracle, and grace are entirely superfluous.
Deism did not deny the existence of God, nor did it deny creation and providence; on the contrary, it gladly spoke of the Supreme Being and held forth at length about providence.
But the power had gone out of this faith.
In principle deism denied that God worked in creation otherwise than according to and through natural laws and powers; it was anti-supernaturalistic from the outset.
The _conservatio_ was enough; a _cooperatio_ or an _influxus Dei_ at every action of the creature was unnecessary, Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 61; Wegscheider, _Inst. theol._ § 106.
In its 18th-century form this deism does indeed belong to the past.
But in fact it still prevails today in wide circles, both in theory and in practice.
Especially since knowledge of nature has expanded in this century and the fixity of its ordinances has been recognized, many are inclined to withdraw pitiless, immutable nature from God’s government, to let it rest independently in itself, and to restrict God’s providence to the religious-ethical domain.
But naturally providence cannot then be conceived absolutely here either; it finds its boundary in human freedom, cf. Kreibig, _Die Räthsel der göttl. Vorsehung_, Berlin, 1886; Schmidt, _Die göttl. Vorsehung und das Selbstleben der Welt_, 1887; Beyschlag, _Zur Verständigung über den christl. Vorsehungsglauben_, Halle, 1888.
No wonder that with such a conception the old doctrine of the _concursus_ is no longer understood and is set aside as superfluous or incorrect, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 54; Müller, _Sünde_ I⁵ 318; Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 255; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 397 f.; van Oosterzee, § 59, 5, 7; cf. also Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ II³ 266.
Indeed, the obvious inference is then to place, as the ethical modernists in the Netherlands do, the power of nature and the moral power, as it were, alongside and over against one another like two gods in a Manichaean manner, at the risk that the territory of the latter, like that of the Native Americans in America, will shrink more and more and finally be taken over entirely by blind, irrational power, cf. vol. I, p. 451.
For this is certainly the principal objection to deism: by separating God and the world, the infinite and the finite, and placing them dualistically alongside one another, it makes them into two competing powers that are involved in continual conflict and contest dominion with each other.
What is given to God is taken from the world.
The more God’s providence extends, the more the creature loses its independence and freedom; and conversely, the creature can maintain its self-activity only by pushing God back and depriving him of dominion.
Peace between the two is therefore possible only with complete separation.
Deism is irreligious at bottom.
The blessedness of the human being lies not in communion with but in separation from God; the deist feels at ease only when he is free from God, that is, when he is a practical atheist; and because he realizes that he can never be this, he is a fearful creature, always concerned that a piece of his territory will be taken away from him.
Hence there are all kinds of degrees in deism; the boundaries between God’s activity and that of the world are drawn differently each time.
There are whole, half, and three-quarter Pelagians, and so forth, according as the world and the human being are withdrawn wholly or to a greater or lesser extent from God’s governance.
In principle deism is always the same: it renders God inactive, but one person travels farther down that road than another.
A deist is a person who in his brief existence has not found the time to become an atheist, Quack, _Port Royal_ 180.
That territory which deism withdraws from God’s government then comes under the power either of fate or of chance.
In this respect too deism repeatedly comes into conflict with itself.
Especially today, when everyone is so deeply convinced of the fixity of the order of nature, there is no place in it for chance and deism falls back into the hands of the old fate; chance remains preserved chiefly for the religious-ethical domain alone.
But the doctrine of chance is no better than that of fate.
_Fatum_ could, if necessary, still have a good meaning in the Christian view of life and the world; but _casus_ and _fortuna_ are thoroughly unchristian.
Something is accidental only in the eyes of human beings, because at that moment they do not know the cause.
But objectively there is and can be nothing accidental.
Everything must have a cause and has it in the final instance in the almighty and all-wise will of God; cf. Augustine, _Qu._ 83, qu. 24; _C. Acad._ I 1; _De ord._ I 2; _De civ._ V 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 22, art. 2; qu. 103, art. 5; _C. Gent._ III 72; Gerhard, _Loc._ VI 3; Calvin, _Inst._ I 16, 2, 9; Chamier, _Panstr. Cathol._ II 2, 4 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ III, qu. 12; Mastricht, _Theol._ III 10, 30; J. Müller, _Sünde_ II 34 f.; Weisse, _Philos. D._ I 518; Kirchner, _Ueber den Zufall_, Halle: Pfeffer, 1888; G. Rümelin, “Ueber den Zufall,” _Deutsche Rundschau_, März 1890, S. 353–364; and further on deism: Lechler, _Gesch. des engl. Deismus_, 1841, and art. in Herzog²; Pünjer, _Gesch. d. chr. Relig. Philos._ I 209 f.; Hanne, _Die Idee der absol. Persönl._ II² 76 f.; Pesch, _Die grossen Welträthsel_ II² 534 f.; Doedes, _Inl. tot de leer v. God_, 80 f.
