# Chapter VI. The World in Its Fallen State

# § 35. Providence

## 1.

When on the seventh day God had finished the work that he had made, he rested on the seventh day from all the work that he had made, Gen. 2:2; Ex. 20:11; 31:17.
In this way Scripture indicates the transition from the work of creation to that of preservation.
That this rest of God has its cause neither in weariness nor consists in idle observation is repeatedly stated clearly and plainly by Holy Scripture, Isa. 40:28; John 5:17.
Creating is no labor for God, and preserving is no rest.
God’s rest indicates only that he put an end to the production of _nova genera_, Eccles. 1:9, 10; that the work of creation in the proper and narrower sense, as _productio rerum e nihilo_, was completed; and that he delighted in this completed work with divine pleasure, Gen. 1:31; Ex. 31:17; Ps. 104:31; cf. Augustine, _de civ._ XI 8; XII 17; _de Gen. ad lit._ IV 8 sq.; Lombardus, _Sent._ II dist. 15; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 73; Calvin on Gen. 2:2; Zanchius, _Op._ III 537, etc.
Creation now passes over into preservation.
The two are so essentially distinguished in Scripture that they can be set over against one another as labor and rest.
And yet they are also so intimately related and connected that preservation itself can be called creation, Ps. 104:30; 148:5; Isa. 45:7; Amos 4:13.
For preservation is itself also a divine work, no less great and glorious than creation.
God is no _Deus otiosus_; he is always working, John 5:17, and the world has no subsistence in itself.
From the moment of its origin it exists only in and through and unto God, Neh. 9:6; Ps. 104:30; Acts 17:28; Rom. 11:36; Col. 1:15; Heb. 1:3; Rev. 4:11.
Although distinct from his being, it is never independent in its existence; independence would be nonbeing.
The whole world, with all that is in it and occurs in it, stands under God’s governance; summer and winter, day and night, fruitful and unfruitful years, light and darkness—all are his work and are formed by him, Gen. 8:22; 9:14; Lev. 26:3 f.; Deut. 11:12 f.; Job 38; Pss. 8; 29; 65; 104; 107; 147; Jer. 3:3; 5:24; Matt. 5:45, etc.
Scripture knows no independent creature; it would be a contradiction in itself.
God cares for all creatures, for animals, Gen. 1:30; 6:19; 7:2; 9:10; Job 38:41; Ps. 36:7; 104:27; 147:9; Joel 1:20; Matt. 6:26, etc., and especially also for human beings.
He sees them all, Job 34:21; Ps. 33:13, 14; Prov. 15:3; forms the hearts of them all and attends to all their works, Ps. 33:15; Prov. 5:21; they are all the work of his hands, Job 34:19, the poor and the rich, Prov. 22:2.
He determines everyone’s dwelling place, Deut. 32:8; Acts 17:26; inclines every heart, Prov. 21:1; directs everyone’s steps, Prov. 5:21; 16:9; 19:21; Jer. 10:23, etc.; and does according to his good pleasure with the host of heaven and the inhabitants of the earth, Dan. 4:35.
They are in his hands like clay in the potter’s hand, like a saw in the hand of the one who draws it, Isa. 29:16; 45:9; Jer. 18:5; Rom. 9:20, 21.
In a very special way his providential governance extends over his people.
The whole history of the patriarchs, of Israel, of the church, and of every believer furnishes proof of this.
What human beings have intended against them for evil, God intends for their good, Gen. 50:20; every weapon fashioned against them will fail, Isa. 54:17; even the hairs of their head are all numbered, Matt. 10:30; all things work together for their good, Rom. 8:28.
Thus all creation stands in God’s power and under his governance; both chance and fate are unknown to Scripture, Ex. 21:13; Prov. 16:33.
It is God who works all things according to the counsel of his will, Eph. 1:11, and makes all things serviceable to the revelation of his virtues, to the honor of his name, Prov. 16:4; Rom. 11:36.
Scripture sums all this up beautifully by repeatedly speaking of God as a King who governs all things, Ps. 10:16; 24:7, 8; 29:10; 44:5; 47:7; 74:12; 115:3; Isa. 33:22, etc.
God is a King, the King of kings and the Lord of lords; a King who in Christ is a father to his subjects, and a Father who is at the same time king over his children.
All the care for, love toward, and protection of one by another that is found among creatures—in the animal, human, and angelic worlds, in family and society and state—is a faint shadow of God’s providential ordering over all the works of his hands.
His absolute power and his perfect love are the proper object of faith in providence in Holy Scripture.

To this testimony of Scripture is added that of all peoples.
The doctrine of God’s providence is an _articulus mixtus_, known in part to all human beings from God’s revelation in nature.
It is an article of faith in every religion, even the most corrupted; whoever denies it undermines religion; without it there is no longer any place for prayer and sacrifice, for faith and hope, for trust and love.
Why serve God, Cicero asks, _Nat. D._ I 2, if he does not concern himself with us at all?
All religions therefore agree with the word of Sophocles, _Elect._ 173: ἐστι μεγας ἐν οὐρανῳ Ζευς, ὁς ἐφορα παντα και κρατυνει.
Philosophy too has frequently acknowledged and defended this providence of God, for example, Socrates in Xenophon, _Mem._ I 4; IV 3; Plato in _Timaeus_, _Leges_ X 901, _Rep._ X 613 A; Aristotle in _Eth. Nic._ X 9; the Stoa in Cicero, _Nat. D._ II; Seneca, _de providentia_ and _de beneficiis_; Cicero, _Nat. D._ I 2; III 26; Plutarchus, _de fato_; Plotinus, περι εἱμαρμενης and περι προνοιας; cf. also Philo’s work περι προνοιας; Wendland, _Philo’s Schrift über die Vorsehung_, Berlin, 1892.
Yet the doctrine of providence in pagan religion and philosophy was not therefore the same as it is in Christianity.
Among the pagans, faith in providence was more theory than practice, more philosophical reflection than religious dogma; it did not suffice in distress and death; it continually swung back and forth between chance and fate.
Since in Plato, for example, God was not the creator but only the shaper of the world, his power found its limit in finite matter; Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II⁴ 928.
Although Aristotle repeatedly expresses his faith in God’s providence, for him it nevertheless coincides entirely with the operation of natural causes; the Deity as νοησις νοησεως stands outside the world in solitary self-contemplation, without will, without action, and the creature can expect no help or love from it; ib. III 368 f., 790 f.
Among the Stoics, προνοια was identical with εἱμαρμενη and φυσις, ib. IV 143; and according to Epicurus, providence was in conflict with the blessedness of the gods, ib. 428.
Some, such as Plutarch and Plotinus, did indeed strive to escape both chance and fate; but in fact fate always rose again behind and above the Deity, while chance forced its way back from below into the lower creatures and lesser events; _magna Dii curant, parva negligunt_, Cicero, _Nat. D._ II 167.
Cf. Pfanner, _Syst. theol. gent._ c. 8; Creutzer, _Philosophorum veterum loci de provid. divina ac de fato_, 1806; Schneider, _Christl. Klänge aus den gr. u. röm. Klassikern_, 1865, S. 231 f.
