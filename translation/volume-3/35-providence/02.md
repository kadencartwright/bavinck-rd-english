## 2.

The Christian faith in God’s providence, however, is not of this kind.
On the contrary, it is a source of comfort and hope, of trust and courage, of humility and resignation, Pss. 23; 33:10 f.; 44:5 f.; 127:1, 2; 146:2 f.; and so forth.
In Scripture, faith in providence rests by no means only upon God’s revelation in nature but much more upon his covenant and promises; its foundation is not only God’s righteousness but above all also his compassion and grace; it presupposes knowledge of sin far deeper than among the pagans, but also the experience of God’s forgiving love; it is not a cosmological speculation but a glorious confession of faith.
And therefore through all sorrow and suffering it again looks joyfully toward the future; although the riddles are not solved, faith in God’s fatherly hand always rises again from the depths and even causes one to boast in tribulations.
In this connection it is remarkable that Scripture does not know the abstract word _providence_.
Some have sought to give this word a scriptural character by appealing to Gen. 22:8; 1 Sam. 16:1; Ezek. 20:6; Heb. 11:40; a few times the word also occurs of human foresight, Acts 13:14; Rom. 12:17; 13:14; 1 Tim. 5:8.
But none of this alters the fact that when Scripture deals with God’s providence, it uses entirely different words.
It does not summarize the activity of God expressed by this word in an abstract concept and does not offer a theological treatise about it.
Rather, it portrays that activity itself in the richest and most vivid manner and lets us see it in history; Scripture as a whole is the book of God’s providence.
And so, in depicting that providence, it speaks of creating, Ps. 104:30; 148:5; making alive, Job 33:4; Neh. 9:6; renewing, Ps. 104:30; seeing, beholding, attending, Job 28:24; Ps. 33:13, 15; saving, protecting, preserving, Num. 6:24; Ps. 36:7; 121:7; leading, teaching, governing, Pss. 25:5, 9; 93:1; and so forth; working, John 5:17; upholding, Heb. 1:3; caring, 1 Pet. 5:7.
The word _providence_ is borrowed from philosophy.
According to Laertius, Plato was the first to use the word προνοια in this sense; cf. Zeller, _Philos. der Gr._ II⁴ 929.
The apocryphal books already use it, Wis. 14:3; 17:2; 3 Macc. 4:21; 5:30; 4 Macc. 9:24; 13:18; 17:22, alongside διατηρειν, Wis. 11:25; διακυβερναν, 3 Macc. 6:2; διοικειν, Wis. 8:1; and so forth.
And the church fathers adopted it and gave it citizenship in Christian theology; cf. Suicerus, s.v.
In the process, however, the word underwent a not insignificant modification.
Originally, providence means the foreseeing, _providentia_, or the foreknowing, προνοια, of what will happen in the future.
_Providentia est, per quam futurum aliquid videtur_, Cicero, _Inv._ II 53.
Understood in this way, the word was by no means suited to encompass everything that the Christian faith confesses in the doctrine of God’s providence.
As foreknowledge of the future, God’s providence would belong only to the _scientia Dei_ and would already have been treated exhaustively in the locus on God’s attributes.
The Christian faith, however, understands by God’s providence not a _nuda praescientia_ but confesses that all things have been not only foreknown by God but also predetermined and ordained.
Providence was therefore soon reckoned as belonging not only to God’s understanding but also to his will, and was described by John of Damascus as βουλησις θεου, δι’ ἡν παντα τα ὀντα την προσφορον διεξαγωγην λαμβανει, _De fide orthod._ II 29.
Understood in this sense, God’s providence would belong in the doctrine of God’s decrees and would already have been treated there in its entirety.
But once again, the Christian faith confesses more than is indicated by the word in this sense.
For God’s decrees are executed; and the creatures that consequently receive existence do not subsist for a single moment of themselves but from moment to moment are upheld solely by God’s almighty hand.
The origination and continued existence of all creatures have their source not in foreknowledge, nor even in a decree, but specifically in an almighty act of God.
And thus, according to the teaching of Scripture and the confession of the church, providence is that act of God by which from moment to moment he sustains and governs all things, not only _Fürsehung_ but also _Vorsehung_.
These different meanings in which the word _providence_ was understood, however, caused the place and content of this doctrine in Christian dogmatics to vary repeatedly and to be subjected to all kinds of changes.
At one time it is reckoned among the attributes, at another among the decrees (_opera Dei ad intra_), and at another among the _opera ad extra_; cf. vol. II, 374 f.
John of Damascus defines it as ἡ ἐκ θεου εἰς τα ὀντα γενομενη ἐπιμελεια, _De fide orth._ II 29, and although he treats it after creation, he nevertheless does so in close connection with the _praescientia_ and _praedestinatio_, II 30.
Lombard discusses it in the chapter on predestination, but before creation, _Sent._ I, dist. 35.
Thomas gives a very clear exposition, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 22, art. 1; he first describes it in general as _ratio ordinis rerum in finem_ and regards it as the _principalis pars prudentiae_, whose task it precisely is _ordinare alia in finem_; but then he says more specifically that _ad providentiae curam duo pertinent, scilicet ratio ordinis, quae dicitur providentia et dispositio, et executio ordinis, quae dicitur gubernatio_; cf. also Bonaventure, _Sent._ I, dist. 35, and Hugh of St. Victor, _S. Sent._, tr. 1, c. 12.
Following these and other examples, the doctrine of providence in Roman Catholic theology was treated either together with predestination under the will of God, Petavius, _De Deo_ VIII, cc. 1-5; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ I, c. 13; _Theol. Wirceb._, Paris, 1880, III 175; Perrone, _Prael. Theol._ II, 1838, p. 233; C. Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ II 158; or only as _conservatio_ or _gubernatio_, separately after creation, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 103-105; _C. Gent._ III 65; and commentators on _Sent._ II, dist. 37; _Theol. Wirceb._ III 497; or entirely in the broadest sense after the locus _De creatione_, Schwetz, _Theol. dogm._ I 405; Jansen, _Prael. theol._ II 329; Simar, _Dogm._³ 252; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 12.
And likewise in the theology of the Reformation, providence was understood at one time as a _consilium_ according to which God governs all things, _Conf. Helv. post._, art. 6; Ursinus, _Explic._, qu. 27; Zanchius, _Op._ II 425; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ IV § 19; Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 174; and at another as a work of God outwardly, Calvin, _Inst._ I 16, 3, 4; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ VI 1; Junius, _Theses Theol._ XVII 1, 2; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XI 3; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ VII 3; and so forth.
The difference naturally concerns the name more than the thing, as Alsted, _Theol. schol._ 175, and Baier, _Comp. theol._ I 5, 2, rightly observe.
If God truly sustains and governs the world, then he must know it beforehand (_providentia_), must also will and be able to care for it (_prudentia_), and in time must actually sustain and govern everything in such a way that the end he has set is attained.
Taken in this broad sense, providence comprises 1º an _actus internus_, which was then further distinguished into προνωσις, προθεσις, and διοικησις; cf. Gerhard, _Loc._ VI, c. 2; and 2º an _actus externus_, which was described as _executio ordinis_, as _conservatio, concursus, gubernatio_.
The _actus internus_ of this _providentia_, however, has already been treated exhaustively in the doctrine of God’s attributes and decrees; here, after the doctrine of creation, providence can therefore come under discussion only as an _actus externus_, as an act of God outwardly.
Although providence in this sense can neither be detached from nor conceived apart from the _actus internus_, the προγωσις, προθεσις, and διοικησις, it is nevertheless distinct from it, as the _executio ordinis_ is distinct from the _ordo_ itself.
The word _providence_ has thereby undergone a complete change.
And the question can arise whether the word is still suitable to designate the thing.
When providence was formerly still treated in the doctrine of God’s attributes or decrees, it retained its original meaning; but since it has increasingly been understood as _conservatio_ and _gubernatio_ and comes under discussion after creation, that original meaning has been almost entirely lost.
Providence in this latter, narrower sense is no longer a proper _providentia_, no _ratio ordinis rerum in finem_, for this precedes it and is presupposed by it; providence itself is _executio ordinis_.
This last was therefore described more specifically in dogmatics by _conservatio_ or by _gubernatio_ or by both together, Lactantius, _De ira Dei_, c. 10; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qq. 103, 104; Bonaventure, _Brevil._, ed. Frib., 1881, p. 93; Belgic Confession, art. 13; Heidelberg Catechism, X; Zanchius, _Op._ II 425; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XI 3; and so forth.
Between these two, the _concursus_ or _cooperatio_ was later inserted to ward off pantheism and deism; materially, it had indeed always been treated in the doctrine of providence, for example, in Augustine, _De trin._ III 4; _De civ._ V 8-11; Theodoret, _De provid. or._ X; Boethius, _De cons._ IV and V; John of Damascus, _De fide_ II 29; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qq. 48, 49; qu. 104, art. 2; qu. 105, art. 5; I 2, qu. 19, art. 4; _Cat. Rom._ I, c. 2, qu. 20; Zwingli, _De provid._, c. 3; _Op._ IV 86; Calvin, _Inst._ I 16, 2; _Contre la secte des libertins_, C. R. 35, p. 186; _De aet. Dei praed._, C. R. 36, pp. 347-366; Zanchius, _Op._ II 449; Martyr, _Loci C._, pp. 56, 59; Wollebius, _Theol._, c. 30; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XI 13; Gerhard, _Loc._ VI, c. 9; and so forth; but later it also received formally a place of its own between _conservatio_ and _gubernatio_, Mastricht, _Theol._ III 10, 10, 29; Turretin, _Theol. El._ VI, qu. 4; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VI 270; IX 210; Brakel, _Red. Godsd._ XI 6; Marck, _Godg._ X 9; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 531; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 421; Buddeus, _Inst._ 409.
From this it is evident that the word _providence_ was not regarded as sufficient to designate the _executio ordinis_ and was more precisely defined by preservation and government.
These are also undoubtedly more correct in conception, more vivid in representation, and more in agreement with the usage of Holy Scripture.
Especially when the word _providence_ is taken abstractly and substituted for God himself, as Plutarch already began to do, cf. Cremer, s.v. προνοια, and as the rationalism of the previous century imitated him in doing, the word is open to objection.
Nevertheless, the word, which has acquired citizenship in the language of theology and religion, may be retained, provided only that the thing designated by it is understood in a scriptural sense.
