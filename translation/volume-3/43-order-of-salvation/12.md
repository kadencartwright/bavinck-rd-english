## 12.

Only on the foundation of the Trinitarian confession is there room for an order of salvation in the scriptural, Christian, Reformed sense. From that confession it follows, in the first place, that the application of salvation is distinct from its acquisition. For although the Holy Spirit is one in essence with the Father and the Son, he is nevertheless as person another than they. He has his own mode of existence, his own order of working. Although all God’s works toward the outside are undivided and inseparable, an economy may nevertheless be observed in creation and re-creation which gives us the right to speak of the Father and our creation, the Son and our redemption, the Spirit and our sanctification. Why could Christ testify that the Holy Spirit was not yet because he was not yet glorified, and why did the Holy Spirit have to be poured out on the day of Pentecost, if sanctification were not a work distinct from creation and redemption as the Spirit is distinct from the Father and the Son? Accordingly, the work assigned to the mediator was not ended with his suffering and death. Christ is not a historical person in the same sense as others, such that after living and laboring on earth for a time he now continues to work only through his spirit and exerts influence through his word and example. Although on earth he accomplished all the work that the Father had assigned him to do, in heaven he continues his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity. For precisely this purpose he was raised and exalted to God’s right hand. He is the living Lord from heaven. That activity is different from the one he performed on earth, although it stands in the closest connection with it. Through his earthly sacrifice he accomplished everything that had to be done in the sphere of right; he satisfied God’s demand, fulfilled the law, and acquired all the benefits of grace. That work is finished and is susceptible of neither increase nor decrease; nothing can be added to it and nothing taken away; it is perfect. The Father rests in it and sealed it by raising his Son. All the benefits that God grants in the covenant of grace he grants *per et propter Christum*. But there is a distinction between title and possession. Just as a child, even before his birth, already has a right to all his father’s goods but only at a much later age enters into possession of them, so also all who will later believe have, long before their faith, a proprietary right in Christ to all the benefits he acquired, but they enter into possession of them only through faith. The acquisition of salvation thus demands its application; it includes and produces it. As the exaltation of Christ is related to his humiliation and his activity in heaven to that on earth, so the application of salvation is related to its acquisition. And that application is twofold. For redemption through Christ is a redemption from sin and all its consequences. He not only took over our guilt and punishment but also fulfilled the law in our place. Accordingly, the application of Christ’s benefits must consist in justification, that is, in the assurance of the forgiveness of sins and the right to eternal life, but also in sanctification, that is, renewal after the image of Christ. Not only the guilt but also the stain and power of sin must be removed. It must be a perfect redemption, a total re-creation. In order to work out and accomplish this on the ground of his completed sacrifice, Christ was exalted to the Father’s right hand. For this purpose he sent forth the Holy Spirit, who not only bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God but also regenerates and re-creates us after the image of God. This work of application is therefore a divine work just as much as the creation of the Father and the redemption of the Son; and the Holy Spirit who accomplishes it is therefore, with the Father and the Son, one only God, to be lauded and praised forever.

In the second place, the confession of the Trinity implies that the work of sanctification, assigned in the economic sense to the Holy Spirit, though distinct, is nevertheless not for one moment separated from the work of redemption and creation carried out by the Son and the Father. This is already evident from the fact that in the divine being the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son and participates with them in the same essence. And as he exists, so he works, in creation and also in re-creation. From this it follows first of all that the work of the Spirit is connected and agrees with the work of the Father. There is no opposition and no contradiction between the two. It is not the case that the Father wills the salvation of all and the Holy Spirit applies it only to a few, or the reverse; rather, both work together because they are one in essence. It also follows from this that nature and grace, however distinct, do not exclude one another. The Roman Catholic doctrinal system is governed entirely by the antithesis between nature and supernatural grace, and various Protestant tendencies and sects have lapsed into that error. Pietism and Methodism fail to recognize the right and value of nature both before and after conversion. But in principle the Reformation knew no other antithesis than that between sin and grace. Nature too was a creation of God and stood under his providence; in itself it is of no less value than grace. Therefore the Reformation could ascribe to nature—that is, to God’s guidance in natural life both among peoples and among individual persons—a pedagogical significance. It is God himself who already prepares the gracious operation of the Holy Spirit in the generations, and in his activity the Holy Spirit joins himself to God’s leadings in natural life and seeks through his grace to restore the whole of natural life, free it from the power of sin, and dedicate it to God. But from the unity of essence of Father, Son, and Spirit it also follows that the Holy Spirit is connected with the work of the Son. The Son and the Spirit too do not work against each other, such that, for example, the Spirit would apply salvation to a few although the Son had acquired it for all, or the reverse. One in essence, they cooperate with one another in their distinct activity. Through his humiliation the Son himself, after all, became a life-giving Spirit. He lives wholly through the Spirit. The death he died, he died to sin once; the life he now lives, he lives to God. He has become a full participant in immortality, in eternal, spiritual life. There is nothing natural, psychical, left in him that can suffer and die. Having already been equipped by the Spirit for his work on earth and anointed with him without measure, he fully acquired that Spirit, received all the gifts of that Spirit, and now lives, reigns, and rules through that Spirit. The Spirit of the Father and the Son has become his Spirit, the Spirit of Christ. He was not yet such before Christ was glorified. But now he is the Spirit of Christ, his property, his possession. And therefore on the day of Pentecost Christ sends that Spirit in order through him to apply all his benefits to his church. The Holy Spirit does not acquire those benefits; he adds not a single one to them, for Christ has accomplished everything. In no respect is he the meritorious cause of our salvation; this is Christ alone, in whom the fullness of deity dwells bodily and whose work therefore does not need to be supplemented or improved. On the contrary, the Holy Spirit takes everything from Christ. Just as the Son came to glorify the Father, so the Holy Spirit in turn descended to glorify the Son. He bears witness to that Son, from his fullness communicates grace upon grace, leads to that Son, and through the Son to the Father. And he applies all Christ’s benefits, to each in his measure, at his appointed time, according to his order. He does not end his activity before he has made the fullness of Christ dwell in his church and she has become a mature man and attained to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ. Therefore the activity of the Holy Spirit is nothing other than applicatory; the order of salvation is an *applicatio salutis*. The question in this connection is by no means: What must the human being do to become partaker of salvation? It is only: What does God do in his grace to make the church partaker of the perfect salvation acquired by Christ? The *applicatio salutis* too is a work of God that must be considered theologically, not anthropologically.

Against this conception of the order of salvation, however, the objection is always raised from the Pelagian side that in this way the right of the human being is disregarded, his self-activity suppressed, and a godless life promoted. Insofar as this objection would seek in principle to overthrow Scripture’s testimony that no flesh will be justified by works of the law, it is inadmissible from a Christian standpoint; anyone who sought to accommodate it would at that very moment and to that same degree abandon the ground of Scripture. Insofar as it is a real objection and merits consideration, it is untrue and rests upon misunderstanding. For the conception of the *applicatio salutis* as a work of God does not exclude but includes the full acknowledgment of all those moral factors which under the direction of God’s providence act upon the understanding and heart of the unconverted person. They may be insufficient for salvation, as Scripture and experience plainly declare, but from the purely Reformation standpoint there is no question of failing to recognize their value, even for the work of grace. For it is God himself who thus leads his human children, bears witness to himself among them, and does them good from heaven, that they might seek and find him. Further, there is no reason why the Holy Spirit, calling through the Word to faith and conversion, would nullify that moral operation of the Word upon heart and conscience which Pelagianism ascribes to it. The Reformation doctrine contains not less, but only more, than is acknowledged by Pelagius and his followers. They think they can be content with that moral operation; Augustine and his allies, however, considered it insufficient but nevertheless fully incorporated it into the gracious operation of the Holy Spirit. Further, the application of salvation is and always remains a work of the Spirit, a work of the Holy Spirit, of the Spirit of Christ, and is therefore never coercive and violent but always spiritual, gracious, and gentle. It treats the human being not as a stick or block but as a rational being, illumining, convincing, drawing, bending; making his darkness yield to light and replacing his spiritual impotence with spiritual power. Grace and sin form an antithesis; the latter is conquered only by the power of the former. But as soon as and to the same degree that the power of sin is broken, the opposition between God and the human being falls away. It is God’s Spirit who bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God. I live, yet no longer I, but Christ lives in me; and the life I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God. It is God who works in us both to will and to work according to his good pleasure and who himself causes us to work out our salvation with fear and trembling. So far is this theological conception from cultivating a godless life that it alone guarantees the reality of a new, Christian life, assures believers of the security of their salvation, infallibly promises the victory of God’s kingdom, and causes the work of the Father and the Son to attain completion in the work of God the Holy Spirit. Pelagianism makes everything wavering and uncertain, even the victory of the good and the triumph of the kingdom of God, because it makes everything depend upon the incalculable arbitrariness of the human being. In standing up for human rights, it tramples God’s rights underfoot and leaves the human being only the right of caprice. But in standing up for God’s rights, the Reformation thereby also brought human rights once again to acknowledgment, for here too the word of Scripture applies: Those who honor me I will honor, but those who despise me will be lightly esteemed. The theological conception of the order of salvation takes up into itself all the good that lies hidden in the anthropological conception, but the reverse does not occur. God comprehends the human being, but whoever begins with the human being excludes God.
