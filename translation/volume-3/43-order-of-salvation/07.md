## 7.

Despite all the agreement, the order of salvation in Reformed theology nevertheless bore an entirely different character from the outset. Calvin does indeed treat justification, *Inst.* III 12–18, and election, ib. 21–24, after faith, regeneration, conversion, and the Christian life, but by this he by no means intends to indicate that they then first come into being objectively. The fundamental thought from which Calvin proceeds is entirely different: election is an eternal decree, even though the human being first becomes conscious of it through faith, and the forgiveness of sins rests in Christ alone, even though it is first granted to us in faith. For the thought repeatedly returns in Calvin that there is no participation in the benefits of Christ except through communion with his person, *Inst.* III 1, 1; 3, 2, 24; 3, 9; 11, 10; etc. In this is contained in principle the entire difference that exists between Reformed and Lutheran theology in the order of salvation; cf. Heppe, *Dogm. d. d. Prot.* II 311–316; Schneckenburger, *Vergl. Darst.* I 195, II 22; Philippi, *Kirchl. Gl.* V 115. For if it is true that the very first benefit of grace already presupposes communion with the person of Christ, then the imputation and gift of Christ to the church precedes everything. And this is also the Reformed doctrine. Already in eternity, in election, and more particularly in the *pactum salutis*, a bond was established between the mediator and those who were given to him by the Father. Even then, in the decree of God, a *unio mystica* was established between the two and an exchange of places was effected. By virtue of that covenant Christ became human and acquired salvation for his people. He could do this precisely because he already stood in communion with them and was their surety and mediator, their head and substitute; and the whole church, comprehended in him as its head, was objectively crucified and died, arose and was glorified with him. All the benefits of grace thus lie ready for the church in the person of Christ. Everything has been accomplished; God is reconciled; nothing from the human being is added to it. Reconciliation, forgiveness, justification, *unio mystica*, sanctification, glorification, and so forth do not come into being after and through faith, but in an objective, active sense they are present in Christ. They are fruits of his suffering and death alone, and on our part they are only received through faith. God grants and imputes them to the church in the decree, in the resurrection of Christ, and in the calling through the gospel. At God’s time they also enter subjectively into the possession of believers. For although the human being has nothing to add to the work of Christ, Christ himself has by no means completed the work assigned to him with the acquisition of salvation. He undertook actually and fully to save his people. He does not lay down his office as mediator before he has presented his church to the Father without spot or wrinkle. The application of salvation is just as essential a constituent of redemption as its acquisition. *Dempta applicatione, redemptio non est redemptio.* Christ therefore continues his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity in heaven. The application of salvation is his work; he is the acting subject; by a *gratia irresistibilis* and *inamissibilis* he communicates himself and his benefits to his own. Soteriology too must be considered theologically, as a work of Father, Son, and Spirit. And just as the acquisition of salvation through Christ took place in the way of the covenant, so also does its application. First, the gathering of the elect must not be conceived individualistically and atomistically, for they were all given to Christ, are comprehended in the covenant, are at their appointed time born from Christ as a body with its members from the head, and become partakers of all his benefits. The church is an organism, not an aggregate; the whole precedes the parts. Some Reformed theologians—Wollebius, *Theol.*, chs. 21–27; Keckermann, *Syst. Theol.*, 1603, p. 370; Brakel, *Red. Godsd.* I, chs. 24–29; and others—therefore even treated the doctrine of the church before that of salvation. This is not necessary, because Reformed theology possesses in the covenant of grace what these theologians intended by this ordering of theirs; it also confuses the church as the body of Christ with the church as institution, as Ritschl also does when he claims that Calvin places the church above the order of salvation, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* I² 216. Yet it does express a true thought: the covenant of grace does not come into being through the order of salvation but precedes it. Second, regeneration, faith, and conversion are not preparations that take place outside Christ and the covenant of grace and first lead the human being toward Christ. Rather, they are benefits that already flow from the covenant of grace, from the *unio mystica*, from the gift of the person of Christ. The Holy Spirit, who is the author of these benefits, was acquired by Christ for his own; the imputation of Christ thus precedes the gift of the Spirit. Regeneration, conversion, and faith do not first lead to Christ but originate from him. Third, faith and justification do not occupy in the Reformed order of salvation that central place which is assigned to them by the Lutherans. For faith in no way brings the benefit of forgiveness into being but only receives it from Christ. The emphasis falls not upon the subject and his experiences but upon the objective work of Christ. Faith itself is only one link in the long chain of salvation. It is rooted in election, is a benefit of the covenant of grace, is a gift of Christ, and is a fruit of regeneration. As a *habitus* it is even present in the children of the covenant and in principle remains present also in believers amid their falling and stumbling. Even in those who are gathered at a later age it is not worked magically but is rather prepared by all kinds of providential leadings in life; special grace joins itself to common grace in nature. Fourth, this faith brings with it the assurance of salvation, for salvation lies immovably secure in the counsel of God, in the person of Christ, and in the covenant of grace. And because sanctification is just as much a benefit of Christ as justification, and because the good works in which believers must walk have been prepared by God in Christ, faith cannot stop at the forgiveness of sins but also reaches out toward the perfection that is in Christ, seeks to confirm and strengthen itself from good works as its fruits, and girds itself with the courage and strength not only to live with Christ in hidden communion but also under him as king to fight against sin, the world, and the flesh in every domain and to make everything subservient to the honor of God’s name.
