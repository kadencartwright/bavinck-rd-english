## 13.

All the benefits of the covenant that Christ acquired and the Holy Spirit applies can be summarized under the name grace. Grace, however, can be spoken of in different senses. First, it can designate God’s unmerited favor toward his creatures, particularly toward sinners; as such it is discussed in the doctrine of God’s attributes, volume II 182. Next, it can be the name for all kinds of bodily and spiritual benefits that God graciously grants to his creatures and that are all gifts of grace, indeed are themselves grace, Rom. 5:20; Eph. 1:7; 2:5, 8; Phil. 1:2; Col. 1:2; Titus 3:7, and so forth. Further, grace can signify the gracefulness someone displays through the gifts with which he is adorned in soul or body. And finally, _χαρις_ and _gratia_ also have the meaning of thanks shown by one person to another for a favor received (_gratias agere_). Here we are concerned only with grace in the second sense. But even so the concept is still much too broad. For this locus does not discuss the objective benefits of grace that God has given to human beings in his law, in the gospel, in Christ, the church, the sacrament, and so forth, and that are treated separately elsewhere in dogmatics. Only those gifts of grace belong here that are subjectively and inwardly communicated to the human being by the Holy Spirit and stand in connection with his salvation. These gifts of grace must in turn be distinguished into gifts of God’s common grace, which are granted in greater or lesser measure to all people and to all kinds of people; gifts of God’s special grace, which are the portion of believers alone; and extraordinary gifts (_charismata_), which the Holy Spirit distributes to each in the church as he wills, 1 Cor. 12:4–11. It is clear that especially the second group of gifts of grace has a claim to treatment in this part of dogmatics. Concerning this grace there is an important difference between Rome and the Reformation, particularly in its Reformed development. First, Rome does decidedly teach the necessity of _gratia actualis_ (_praeveniens, antecedens, excitans_, also called _operans_) and thereby rejects Pelagianism and Semi-Pelagianism. Nor does it understand by this grace merely the external calling through the gospel with its accompanying moral influence upon understanding and will. It has in view an _illustratio intellectus_ and _inspiratio voluntatis_ that add to the human being not only _vires morales_ but also _vires physicas_. Yet at both the beginning and the continuation it understands this grace as resistible and losable. The decision whether the human being will accept that grace, prepare himself with it for his justification, receive the grace of justification (_gratia infusa_), and retain it to the end depends upon himself. Second, the nature of grace is described differently in Rome than in the Reformation. Roman Catholic theologians expressly say that _gratia actualis_ and _infusa_ are not a substance but a quality; Heinrich, _Dogm. Theol._ VIII 556 f.; Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ V 174. But already of _gratia excitans_ it is said that it _non moraliter sed physice elevat facultates nostras, ut supernaturaliter agere possint_, Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ V 19, and thus is entitatively supernatural, _totum ordinem naturalem transcendens_, ibid., 21. And it is said still more strongly of _gratia infusa_ that it is a gift of God through which the human being _in ordinem supernaturalem elevatur et naturae divinae aliquo modo particeps fit_, ibid., 172, 188. Third, this is connected with the fact that the purpose of grace in Rome is different from that in the Reformation. There it has a twofold task, _ut elevet et sanet_. The former completely overshadows the latter: _munus elevandi est principale et gratiae convenit in omni ordine supernaturali. Munus autem sanandi est secundarium et gratiae accessit in ordine naturae lapsae_. In the first sense grace is absolutely necessary, in the second only contingently necessary, ibid., 32, 33; and so all the authors cited above, p. 441 f. In Rome grace is in the first place a quality added to the human being above the order of nature, through which he is in principle admitted into a supernatural order, becomes a partaker of the divine nature, holiness, sonship, communion with God, the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, and the vision of God, and can perform such supernatural works as merit eternal life _ex condigno_. The forgiveness of sins is subordinate here; faith has only a preparatory value. The principal matter is the elevation of the human being above his nature, deification, _ἡ προς θεον αφομοιωσις τε και ἑνωσις_; Dionysius in Heinrich, _Dogm. Theol._ VIII 595; cf. also volume II 152, 518.

The Reformation rejected this Neoplatonic mysticism, returned to the simplicity of Holy Scripture, and therefore obtained an entirely different conception of grace. Grace does not serve to receive the human being into a supernatural order but to deliver him from sin. Grace is opposed not to nature but only to sin. In the proper sense it was not necessary for Adam before the fall but first became necessary through sin; it is therefore not absolutely but only contingently necessary. The physical opposition between natural and supernatural gives way to the ethical opposition between sin and grace. When grace completely removes sin with its guilt, pollution, and punishment, it has accomplished its work. The human being is then once more the image of God as a matter of course, for the image of God is no _donum superadditum_ but belongs to the essence of the human being. Alongside the grace that redeems from sin, therefore, there need be no other grace that then further elevates the human being above his nature. According to the Reformed, grace has indeed restored to us more than we lost through Adam. For Christ acquired not only the _posse non peccare_ and _posse non mori_, as the Lutherans represent it, but he immediately grants believers the _non posse peccare_ and _non posse mori_. He does not bring us back to that point on the way where Adam stood but has traversed the entire way for us to the end. He accomplished not only passive but also active obedience. He acquired the indefectible blessedness and eternal life that for Adam still lay in the future. Precisely because Adam’s destiny lay in eternal blessedness, Christ could acquire it for us in Adam’s place. Nevertheless, grace gives nothing more than what Adam, had he remained standing, would have obtained in the way of obedience. The covenant of grace differs from the covenant of works in the way but not in the final goal. It is the same good that was promised in the covenant of works and is granted in the covenant of grace. Grace restores nature and raises it to its highest height but adds no new, heterogeneous component to it. From this it follows that in Reformation theology grace can in no respect bear the character of a substance. The Reformation did confess concerning grace that it is not only external but also inward, that it grants not only moral but also hyperphysical powers, that it is a quality, a _habitus_. But even though it sometimes expressed itself in the same words as Rome, it attached a different sense to them. In Rome grace is a physical power because it must elevate nature to the supernatural order. If it served only to deliver the human being from sin, in view of the Roman Catholic doctrine of sin a moral power of grace would probably be sufficient. But the Reformation thought differently about sin. Sin was guilt and at the same time a total corruption of human nature. The human being was by nature dead in sins and transgressions. His inability can in a certain sense be called natural; see above, pp. 150, 151. And therefore grace too must be such that it enlightens the understanding and bends the will, thus works not only morally but also hyperphysically and restores the powers. But in Rome this physical operation of grace is an opposition to the ethical, in any event one that far surpasses the ethical. In the Reformation it is and remains ethical. For natural inability is in its nature a spiritual, moral inability, an inability for good caused solely and exclusively by sin. It is called natural only because it belongs to the human being “by nature,” that is, by virtue of his fallen, sinful nature, was not introduced into him by habit, education, and so forth, and therefore cannot be removed by such moral powers either. Grace works hyperphysically only because it removes that fallen-natural inability and restores the original-natural capacity for good. In Rome grace in the strict sense gives a physical power that the _homo naturalis_ without the _donum superadditum_ does not possess, even if he is completely sinless, and that must be separately granted to him. It is physically impossible for that human being to perform supernaturally good works, just as it is physically impossible for him to touch the stars with his hand. But the inability in the Protestant confession is not of this kind, and therefore grace here is not a physical quality in the Roman Catholic sense, even though it restores the original power for good lost through sin. And since sin is not a substance and has taken nothing substantial away from the human being, grace can never be conceived as a substance either. It is a restoration of the _forma_ originally impressed upon the human being and creatures in general at creation. Re-creation is no second, new creation. It adds no new creatures to what exists and introduces no new substance into it but is essentially reformation. Yet in this, the operation of grace extends intensively as far as the power of sin extends. Sin affected everything, corrupted the whole organism of creation and the very nature of creatures; and therefore grace is a power of God that also inwardly delivers humanity from sin, in the core of its being, and will one day place it without spot or wrinkle before God’s face. Therefore a merely morally operating grace is not enough. Rome appears to honor grace when it calls it absolutely necessary and has it grant physical powers that far surpass nature. But in the end it makes all that grace so powerless again that in its operation it depends upon the human will. It accomplishes nothing when the will resists it. And when the will assents to it, it serves only to grant the human being the powers needed to merit every subsequent grace and eternal life. It is an aid to the human being for his deification. But in the Reformation grace is the beginning, middle, and end of the whole work of salvation. Nothing of human merit enters into it. Just as creation and redemption are works of God, so too is sanctification. It is from him and through him, and therefore it also leads to him. To him be the glory forever.
