## 8.

Alongside these conceptions of the order of salvation, however, many others arose. Mysticism, which always and everywhere—in India and Arabia, among Jews and Greeks, among Roman Catholics and Protestants—displays the same character, makes everything objective and external recede before the inward process of salvation. In Christianity it therefore takes little account of Holy Scripture, the Christ for us, satisfaction, imputed righteousness, justification, church, sacraments, and so forth, but places all the weight upon inward grace, illumination, regeneration, Christ in us, infused righteousness, renewal by the Holy Spirit, and so forth. By three stages it seeks to reach perfection: καθαρσις (*via purgativa*, asceticism), φωτισμος (*via illuminativa, meditatio*), and ἐποπτεια (*via contemplativa*, ecstasy). That is, when through asceticism the soul has withdrawn from the whole world and through meditation has concentrated itself entirely upon one point, then it becomes partaker of the vision of God, deification, transformation into Christ, and so forth; then it loses itself and sinks away into God. Cf. Zeller, *Philos. d. Gr.* V 599 f.; Voetius, *Exerc. pietatis*, 56 sq.; Hollaz, *Ex. theol.*, 208, 796, 821; Erbkam, *Gesch. d. prot. Sekten*, 52 f.; and further literature in Herzog² 15, 487–504. Directly opposite it stands rationalism, which was prepared by Socinianism and Remonstrantism and dominated minds in the 18th century. It sees in Christ no more than a prophet and teacher who proclaimed the truth of God and sealed it with his life and death. By following him, the human being, weakened indeed but not rendered powerless by sin, becomes partaker of salvation. The call that comes to him in the gospel therefore exerts only a moral influence upon his understanding and will. If from his own free choice the human being obeys that call, assents to the truth, trusts in God’s grace, and fulfills Christ’s commandments—for the essence of faith consists in *assensus, fiducia*, and *obedientia*—then on account of this faith, which includes all obedience in principle and is already reckoned by God out of grace, for Christ’s sake, as perfect obedience, he is justified and, if he perseveres, becomes partaker of eternal salvation. Cf. Fock, *der Socin.*, 651–689; *Conf. Rem.* and *Apol. Conf.* VII; Limborch, *Theol. Chr.* IV 11 sq., V 8 sq., VI 4 sq.; Wegscheider, *Inst. theol.*, § 146 sq.; Bretschneider, *Dogm.*, § 177 f.; Knapp, *Glaub.* II 323 f., 382 f.; Reinhard, *Dogm.*, § 130.

In the same way as mysticism and rationalism, antinomianism and neonomianism stand opposed to one another in the order of salvation. Antinomianism is in general that tendency which, in the first place, reduces the application of salvation entirely to, and identifies it with, the acquisition of salvation. Christ, namely, has accomplished everything; he has taken over from us not only our guilt but even the stain of sin; he has acquired for us not only righteousness but also regeneration and sanctification. Nothing therefore remains for the human being to do. Contrition, conversion, repentance, prayer for forgiveness, the doing of good works—all of it is unnecessary, bears a legalistic character, and detracts from the perfection of Christ’s sacrifice. A person needs only to believe, that is, to come to the insight that he is justified, regenerated, and sanctified, that he is perfect in Christ. The sins he then still commits are no longer sins; they are works of the old man, which no longer concern the believer as such, for he is perfect in Christ, is freed from the law, and boasts in grace. Ordinarily, however, antinomianism does not stop here but takes one more step backward. It first reduces the application of salvation to its acquisition and then the latter in turn to the decree of God. Even Christ did not acquire salvation, for it lay ready eternally in God’s decree, but he only revealed God’s love. Believing is therefore nothing other than putting off the delusion that God is angry with us; sin consists only in that delusion. Such sentiments were proclaimed in ancient times by the Gnostics and in the Middle Ages by many libertinist sects, and they gained entrance into the Reformed church among the Hattemists and Hebrews. Cf. Hulsius, *De hedendaagsche Antinomianerye*, 2nd ed., 1738; Fruytier, *Klaer en kort vertoog van de valsheit en gedeformeertheit van het gevoelen der sogen. Hebreën*, 1697; M. Leydecker, *Hist. en Godg. Oefeningen over de oorsprong, voortgang en gevoelens v. d. oude en nieuwe Antin.*, 1700; Ypey, *Gesch. d. chr. Kerk in de 18e E.* VII 290f.; J. van Leeuwen in *Ned. Archief v. Kerk. Gesch.* VIII, 1848, pp. 57–169; Van Manen in *Gids*, Sept.–Oct. 1885. It is understandable that this antinomianism gained so much entrance precisely in the Reformed church; the Reformed order of salvation displays an unmistakable affinity with it. From the Reformation onward many had already, because *remissio peccatorum fide non efficitur sed accipitur*, placed justification or the forgiveness of sins before faith and described the essence of faith as *cognitio, certitudo*: Luther in Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 223; Melanchthon in Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* I² 197; Calvin, *Inst.* III 2, 7, 14; Hyperius, *Meth. Theol.*, 1574, p. 205; Ursinus on Q. 21 of the *Heid. Cat.*; Olevianus, *Verklaring der Apost. geloofsbel.*, 2nd ed., Doesburg, 1868, pp. 131, 261, 418, 462; and further Pareus, Tossanus, Piscator, Twissus, and others. Especially in England, over against the advancing neonomism, this sentiment was clearly developed by the so-called antinomians, though they are better designated antineonomians: John Eaton, *The honeycombe of free justification by Christ alone*, London, 1642; William Eyre, *Vindiciae justificationis gratuitae*, 1654; Tobias Crisp, *Christ alone exalted*, 1643; John Saltmarsh, *Free grace*, 1645; Samuel Crisp, *Christ made sin*, 1691; Thomas Tully, *Justificatio paulina sine operibus*, 1674; Isaac Chauncy, *Neonomianism unmasked*, 1692; idem, *Alexipharmacon, a fresh antidote against neonomian bane*, 1700. Cf. Witsius, *Misc. Sacra* II 753 sq.; Hoornbeek, *Summa Contr.*, 1653, p. 701; Pfaff, *Hist. theol. litt.*, 257 sq.; Walch, *Bibl. theol. sel.* II 1069; idem, *Einl. in die Religionsstr. auss. d. luth. K.* III 999. In the Netherlands this conception of justification as preceding faith—whether in the decree of God, in the resurrection of Christ, in the promise of the gospel, or in the inward calling—and accordingly also the conception of faith as *cognitio* and *certitudo* was defended by Trigland, *Antapol.*, 314; Maccovius, *Loci*, chs. 69–72; *Coll. theol.*, p. 128; *Loc. Comm. Disp.*, 1641, p. 309; Voetius, *Disp.* II 453 sq., V 277 sq.; Hoornbeek, *Summa Contr.*, 705; and especially in the 18th century by Holtius, *Rechtv. door het geloof*, 1750; Comrie, *Heid. Cat.*, Q. 21; idem, *Brief over de rechtv. des zondaars door de onmidd. toerekening der gerecht. van Christus*, new ed., Utrecht, 1889; *Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol.*, preface to the 7th Dialogue; *Walch. art.* 3; Brahe, *Aanm. over de vijf Walch. art.*, 1758, p. 60f.; *Godgeleerde stellingen over de leer der rechtv. des zondaars voor God*, composed by J. J. Brahe, new edition by A. Capadose, Amsterdam, 1833; cf. also Hartmann, *Huisbijbel* on Rom. 4:5; Van Thuynen, *Korte Uitlegging van het Geref. geloof*, 1722; Vrolikhert, *Twee Godg. verh. over werkverbond en toerekening van Christus’ dad. gehoorz. en over den aard en het wezen des geloofs*, 1732; B. S. Cremer, *Summa theol. supran.*, 1730; idem, *Evang. Geloofsketen der waarheid*, 1740. But by no means could everyone accept this presentation. Opposed to it stood another description of the order of salvation that may be designated neonomian and can in turn be distinguished into a more rationalistic and a more pietistic form. The former tendency was already rooted in principle in Piscator’s doctrine that Christ’s active obedience does not belong to the satisfaction, and it soon gained great significance and influence through the school of Saumur. There Camero taught that the will always follows the understanding and that therefore illumination of the understanding is sufficient for conversion. Amyraldus made objective grace universal. Pajon considered inward grace unnecessary and, in the manner of Bellarmine, made the *efficacia* of the call depend upon its congruity with the circumstances in which it comes to someone. The result was that natural inability, the covenant of works, the immediate imputation of Adam’s sin and of Christ’s righteousness, and so forth were denied, faith was joined with works, and was thus conceived as the means or ground of justification. This occurred, among others, in England through George Bull, *Harmonia apostolica*, 1670, who was indeed opposed by many but defended by Morus, Glanville, Whitby, Tillotson, Cave, and others, M. Vitringa III 296; in Scotland through the Anti-Marrowmen Simson, Hadow, and others, above, p. 395; in America through the followers of Jonathan Edwards, above, p. 127; and in the Netherlands through Vlak, who in his *Eeuwig Evangelie*, 1684, taught a justification from the works of faith; through Van den Os, 1740, who described faith as trust in Christ and obedience to his commandments; through J. van den Honert, *Verh. van de rechtv. des sondaers uyt en door het geloof*, and J. J. Schultens, *Uitv. Waarschuwing op verscheiden stukken der Kategismusverklaaringe van Al. Comrie*, 1755, and (Petrus Boddaert), *Wolk van getuigen voor de leer de rechtv. door en uit het geloove, verzameld bij gelegenheid van het in ’t licht geven der Aanm. over de vijf Walch. art. door J. J. Brahe*, all of whom denied immediate imputation and placed faith before justification; through Kleman, who in his *Orde des heils*, 1774, assumed such a connection, established by God’s wisdom and goodness, between the human being’s moral actions and the supernatural communication of faith that all who make proper use of their natural powers can certainly count upon obtaining supernatural grace, and so forth.
