## 4.

Proceeding from the absolute moral corruption of the human race through Adam’s sin and from its complete inability to do any spiritual good, Augustine also arrived at an entirely different doctrine of grace. He often also designated the objective benefits—the gospel, baptism, the forgiveness of sins, and so forth—by the name grace. But this grace is not sufficient; another is still needed, a *gratia interna, spiritalis*, which illumines the understanding and bends the will. At first Augustine had still taught otherwise, namely, that God called us but that believing was our part, as in his work *de diversis quaestionibus* from the year 386 and *de libero arbitrio*, 388–395. But later, about 396, he came to another insight, especially through 1 Cor. 4:7, *de div. qu. adv. Simpl.* ± 397. And now he says that grace consists not only in an external preaching of law and gospel, which teaches and admonishes us and in that sense offers us help, but that it is above all an *occulta inspiratio Dei, inspiratio fidei et timoris Dei*, an *adjutorium bene agendi adjunctum naturae atque doctrinae per inspirationem flagrantissimae et luminosissimae caritatis*, *de gratia Christi* 35; a *subministratio virtutis*, ib. 2; an *inspiratio caritatis per Spiritum Sanctum*, ib. 39. Itself a fruit of election, *ipsius praedestinationis effectus*, *de praed. sanct.* 10, it is distributed not according to merit but according to God’s mercy, *de dono pers.* 12; *c. duas ep. Pelag.* I 24. Therefore it is of itself *gratuita*; it would not be *gratia* if it were not given *gratis*, *de pecc. orig.* 24. The Holy Spirit blows where he wills, *non merita sequens sed merita faciens*, ib. Grace precedes all merits; it is a *gratia praeveniens, praeparans, antecedens*, and *operans*; *nolentem praevenit, ut velit*, *Enchir.* 32. It inwardly illumines the understanding and removes its *coecitas*, *de pecc. mer.* I 9; it works faith, which is a gift of God, and creates in the human being the good will, the love of the good, and the power to do the good, and removes his *infirmitas*: *fateamur, interna atque occulta, mirabili ac ineffabili potestate operari Deum in cordibus hominum, non solum veras revelationes sed etiam bonas voluntates*, *de gratia Chr.* 24. This grace, moreover, is *irresistibilis*; it works *indeclinabiliter* and *insuperabiliter* upon the human will, *de corr. et gr.* 12; it is rejected by no heart, however hard, *a nullo corde duro respuitur*. By grace God takes away the heart of stone and gives a heart of flesh in its place, *de praed. sanct.* 8. The elect who receive it are not only able through it to come to Christ, but actually do come to him. Yet this is not to be understood as if by grace God suppressed or destroyed the human free will; on the contrary, grace makes the will free precisely from the slavery of sin. *Liberum ergo arbitrium evacuamus per gratiam? Absit, sed magis liberum arbitrium statuimus. Sicut enim lex per fidem, sic liberum arbitrium per gratiam non evacuatur sed statuitur quia gratia sanat voluntatem*, *de spir. et litt.* 30, cf. 33, 34; *de gratia et lib. arb.; de civ.* XIV 11; *c. duas ep. Pel.* I 2. Therefore Augustine could also say, *consentire vocationi Dei vel ab ea dissentire, propriae voluntatis est*, *de spir. et litt.* 34, for both the one who believes and the one who does not believe do so voluntarily, *nemo credit nisi volens*. Yet he was so far from thereby placing the final decision once again in human hands that he immediately continues: this statement does not weaken but confirms the apostle’s word, What do you have that you did not receive? *Accipere quippe et habere anima non potest dona, de quibus hoc audit, nisi consentiendo; ac per hoc, quid habeat et quid accipiat Dei est; accipere autem et habere utique accipientis et habentis est. Iamsi ad illam profunditatem scrutandam quisquam nos coarctet, cur illi suadetur ut persuadeatur, illi autem non ita, duo sola occurrunt interim quae respondere mihi liceat; o altitudo divitiarum, et: numquid iniquitas apud Deum? de spir. et litt.* 34. And just as the beginning, so also the progress of faith and love is due solely to God’s grace; the *gratia operans* passes over into the *gratia cooperans, consequens, subsequens*; it works not only the willing but also the doing and the completing. Without Christ we can do nothing; and therefore, *ut incipiamus, dictum est: misericordia ejus praeveniet me, ut perficiamus, dictum est: misericordia ejus subsequetur me*, *c. duas ep. Pel.* II 9. It is God *qui praeparat voluntatem et cooperando perficit quod operando incipit.... Ut ergo velimus, sine nobis operatur; cum autem volumus, et sic volumus ut faciamus, nobiscum cooperatur; tamen sine illo vel operante ut velimus vel cooperante cum volumus, ad bona pietatis opera nihil valemus*, *de gr. et lib. arb.* 17. *Volentem subsequitur ne frustra velit*, *Enchir.* 32. And that grace is necessary not for some but for all good actions: *voluntas hominis Dei gratia ad omne bonum actionis, sermonis, cogitationis adjuvatur*, *c. duas ep. Pel.* II 5. Objectively and subjectively, the work of salvation from beginning to end is a work of God’s grace and of his grace alone. Cf. Wiggers, I 244 f.; Luthardt, *Die Lehre vom freien Willen*, 1863, p. 30 f.

Pelagianism was condemned at the Synod of Carthage in 418, whose canons were approved by Pope Zosimus and later by Coelestinus I; further, at the Council of Ephesus in 431 and at the Synod of Orange in 529. At this last synod semi-Pelagianism was also rejected, and its canons were ratified by Boniface II, Denzinger, *Enchir.* no. 64 sq.; cf. vol. II, 322f. Through this it became the church’s doctrine that the whole human being is corrupted through Adam’s sin, and that both the *initium* and *augmentum fidei* are due not to ourselves, to our natural powers, but to the grace of God, which not only teaches us what we are to do and leave undone, but also grants us *ut quod faciendum cognoverimus, etiam facere diligamus atque valeamus*, Denzinger, no. 68; to the *infusio, operatio, inspiratio, illuminatio* of the Holy Spirit in us, who precedes and prepares our will, restores it (*corrigens*) from unbelief to faith, and makes us willing and working, ib. 147 sq. The necessity of the *gratia interna, praeveniens* has since been taught by all. The Synod of Quierzy in 853, which condemned Gottschalk, likewise confessed: *habemus liberum arbitrium ad bonum, praeventum et adjutum gratia,.... gratia liberatum et de corrupto sanatum*, ib. 280, just as Rabanus said that God *Spiritu Sancto intus regit et zelo spiritali foris confortat*, in Weiszäcker, *Jahrb. f. d. Th.*, 1859, p. 545. Scholasticism walked in this same track. Lombard says that through sin the human being lost freedom of the will and that he *non valet erigi ad bonum efficaciter volendum vel opere implendum, nisi per gratiam liberetur et adjuvetur; liberatur quidem ut velit et adjuvatur ut perficiat*, *Sent.* II, dist. 25; cf. commentary on Eph. 2:4–10; Rom. 5:1, 2; 6:23, etc. According to Thomas, without grace a human being can indeed do all sorts of naturally good things, *S. Theol.* II 1, qu. 109, art. 1 and 2, but he needs grace *ut sanetur et ut bonum supernaturalis virtutis operetur*, art. 2; in order to love God, art. 3; to keep the law, art. 4; to acquire eternal life, art. 5; to prepare himself for the grace of justification, art. 6; to rise from sins, art. 7; to be able not to sin, art. 8; and he repeatedly needs new grace in order to know and do the good, art. 9, and to persevere in it, art. 10. Trent likewise established that, in order to prepare themselves for the grace of justification, adults need the *gratia praeveniens*, so that they, *qui per peccata a Deo aversi erant, per ejus excitantem atque adjuvantem gratiam ad convertendum se ad suam ipsorum justificationem.... disponentur*, VI 5. Rome thus decidedly teaches the necessity of the *gratia praeveniens* (*actualis, excitans*) and thereby rejects Pelagianism and semi-Pelagianism, which ascribed the *initium* and *augmentum fidei* to the powers of the natural human being. Now it may indeed be asked whether by this *gratia praeveniens* Rome understands anything more than the external call of the gospel, which acts morally upon understanding and will and which was also acknowledged by Pelagius and his associates. For sometimes the description of that grace is very weak; Trent identifies it with the call, *qua nullis eorum existentibus meritis vocantur*, ib. Yet Rome nevertheless appears to understand by that *gratia praeveniens* an operation of the Holy Spirit upon understanding and will. The Synod of Orange spoke of a *Sancti Spiritus infusio et operatio in nobis*; Trent called it *excitans* and described it in the words *tangente Deo cor hominis per Spiritus Sancti illuminationem*, ib., cf. can. 3. Thomas says that the grace by which an adult prepares himself for justification does not consist in *aliqua habitualis gratia* but in an *operatio Dei ad se animam convertentis*, I, qu. 62, art. 2 ad 3; an *auxilium Dei interius moventis sive inspirantis bonum propositum*, II 1, qu. 109, art. 6; qu. 112, art. 2; III, qu. 89, art. 1 ad 2. Bonaventure also calls it *gratia gratis data* and says that the human being needs it for preparation for justification and that it *excitat* his free will, *Brevil.* V 3. Bellarmine describes it as a *gratia auxilii specialis*, as a *motio* or *actio, qua Deus hominem movet ad operandum*, *de gratia et lib. arb.* I, c. 2, and sets it as *speciale auxilium, gratia extrinsecus excitans et adjuvans* over against the *gratia intus inhabitans, de gratia infusa, Spiritus S. in nobis inhabitans*, *de justif.* I, c. 13. There was also great disagreement among theologians over the nature of that preparatory grace. The Thomists held it to be a *qualitas physica supernaturaliter infusa, entitas quaedam physica*; Molina, Lessius, and Ripalda conceived it as an *illustratio mentis et inspiratio voluntatis*; Suarez, Tanner, and others thought that it was not something created but that the Holy Spirit himself moved the will immediately. Yet it is nevertheless generally conceived as a *gratuitum auxilium, donum Dei internum et supernaturale*, as an *illustratio mentis* and *motio immediata voluntatis*, which adds to the human being not only *vires morales* but also *vires physicas* and enables him to prepare himself for justification. *Theol. Wirceb.* VII 262, 268; Perrone V, p. 6; Kleutgen, *Theol. d. Vorz.* II 152 f.; Jansen III, p. 8 sq.; Pesch, *Prael. dogm.* V 20, 46; Heinrich, *Dogm. Theol.* VIII 19 f.; Simar, 492; Scheeben III 683 f.
