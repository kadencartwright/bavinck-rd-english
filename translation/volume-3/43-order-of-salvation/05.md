## 5.

But with that Rome’s rejection of Semi-Pelagianism also comes to an end; by a detour it is nevertheless brought back in. First, Rome teaches that freedom of the will was weakened by sin but not lost, _Syn. Araus._, can. 8, 13, 25; _Trid._ VI, ch. 1 and can. 5. Without grace the human being can do many good works in the natural and civil spheres that are by no means sins. He can know and love God as Creator and lead an honorable life. And although it is difficult to keep the whole law over the long term and to resist all temptations, this is nevertheless not impossible in itself. The _homo naturalis_ is in himself a complete human being; volume II 525; cf. Pesch V 66, 100. Second, Rome departs from Augustine in that it understands _gratia praeveniens_ as a grace that indeed grants the ability to believe but not belief itself. On the contrary, every adult who lives under the gospel is granted _gratia praeveniens_ (_actualis_), but it lies within the human being’s power to accept or reject it. _Hoc etiam secundum fidem catholicam credimus_, said the Synod of Orange, ch. 25, _quod accepta per baptismum gratia omnes baptizati, Christo auxiliante et cooperante, quae ad salutem pertinent, possint et debeant, si fideliter laborare voluerint, adimplere_. And Trent declared that the human being can assent to the _gratia praeveniens_ and cooperate with it but can also reject it, VI, ch. 5 and can. 4. Among the theologians, however, there was great disagreement about this. The Augustinians, among whom Berti is the foremost, teach that _gratia praeveniens_ (_actualis, sufficiens_) does indeed give the _posse_ but not the _velle_. For the human being not only to be able but also to will to believe and actually to believe, and thus for the _gratia sufficiens_ actually to become _efficax_, a _delectatio victrix_ is necessary that overcomes the opposing _carnalis delectatio_ and causes the _posse_ to pass into the _velle_. The will must therefore be transformed by a _delectatio victrix_ stronger than the _concupiscentia_. The Thomists—Bannez, Gonet, Lemos, Billuart, and others—similarly say that the _gratia sufficiens_ indeed gives the _posse_ but not the _velle_ and, in order to produce the latter, must be supplemented by an _actio Dei physica_, _promotio_, or _praedeterminatio physica_. Augustinians and Thomists therefore agree that the _efficacia gratiae_ depends not upon the human will but upon grace itself and that the act of faith infallibly follows the _delectatio victrix_ or _praedeterminatio victrix_. They differ, however, in that the latter assume an essential objective distinction between the _gratia sufficiens_ and the _gratia efficax_, while the former judge that these two do not differ essentially but that there are degrees in grace, by which a grace that is only _sufficiens_ in one person can be _efficax_ in another because of lesser hardening. But one cannot say that this conception does full justice to Trent’s _assentire vel abjicere_. The Molinists therefore made the _efficacia_ of grace depend upon the human will, and the Congruists, such as Bellarmine, _De gratia et lib. arb._ I 12; IV 14–16, made it determined by the _praevisa gratiae congruentia_ or _incongruentia_ with the condition and circumstances of those to whom grace came at a particular moment; cf. volume I 92 f.; and further _Theol. Wirceb._ VII 375; Perrone V 138; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._ II⁸ 338; Jansen III 79; Dens, _Theol._ II 219; Schäzler, _Neue Unters. über das Dogma v. d. Gnade_, Mainz, 1867, p. 87 f.; Simar, _Dogm._ 502; Pesch, _Prael._ V 136–164; Scheeben, _Dogm._ IV 1, 1898, p. 202 f.

The Roman Catholic doctrine therefore comes down to this: children born in the church receive regeneration (_justificatio, gratia infusa_) in baptism, but those who hear the gospel at a later age receive the _gratia sufficiens_, which consists in an illumination of the understanding and a strengthening of the will by the Holy Spirit. The human being can reject this grace but can also assent to it. If he assents to it, the _gratia excitans_ passes into the _gratia adjuvans_ or _cooperans_. The human being cooperates with it in order to prepare himself for the _justificatio_ (_gratia infusa, habitualis_). This preparation comprises these seven moments: that the human being, helped by God’s grace, begins to believe God’s Word, begins to see that he is a sinner, obtains hope in God’s mercy, begins to love him, begins to hate sin, resolves to be baptized, and resolves to lead a new life; _Trid._ VI, ch. 6. Faith occupies no central place here but is coordinated with the six other _praeparationes ad gratiam justificantem_. It is therefore nothing other than assent to the truth of Christianity, that is, to the doctrine of the church (_fides informis_), and first receives its justifying power through love (_fides caritate formata_), which is communicated in the _gratia infusa_. Taken by itself and alone, it cannot justify but is called justifying faith only because it is the _humanae salutis initium, fundamentum et radix omnis justificationis_, that is, the first of the preparations named above. When the human being has thus prepared himself and done _quod in se est_—whether this consists in the good use of his powers supported by grace, Thomas, _S. Th._ II 1, qu. 109, art. 6 ad 2; qu. 112, art. 3, or even of his natural powers, Thomas, II 1, qu. 98, art. 6; cf. _Theol. Wirceb._ VII 288; Dens, _Theol._ II 209; Pesch, _Prael._ V 114–120—God cannot refuse him the _gratia infusa_. To be sure, through that preparation the human being has not merited this grace, for it far surpasses his preparation in value. Nevertheless, it is equitable that God should reward the person who thus does his best with the _gratia infusa_ according to a _meritum de congruo_. It is granted to him in baptism and consists in the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, the infusion of supernatural virtues, and participation in the divine nature, and is followed by the forgiveness of sins, which together with it constitutes the two parts of justification; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 113. The forgiveness of sins is therefore in Rome the negative reverse side of the positive renewal of the human being. Sin is forgiven because and insofar as it has been eradicated. Now when the human being has become a partaker of this _gratia infusa_ in baptism, he can indeed lose it again through mortal sins, and he has to make satisfaction for other sins not only with _contritio cordis_ but also with _confessio oris_ and _satisfactio operis_. Yet in the _gratia infusa_ he has received the supernatural power to do good works and thereby to merit all subsequent grace, indeed even eternal life, according to a _meritum de condigno_. For the good works he does flow from a supernatural principle and are therefore worthy of a supernatural reward. From this it becomes clear what this Roman Catholic doctrine of grace is ultimately about. Grace serves only to make it possible once again for the human being to merit heavenly blessedness. At bottom this was the case even in Augustine. Grace, however unmeritedly bestowed, consisted for him not first of all in the forgiveness of sins but in regeneration, in the infusion of love, which enables one to do good works and thus to acquire eternal life. _Accepit (homo) justitiam, propter quam beatitudinem accipere mereretur_, _De trin._ XIV 15. Merits do not precede grace and faith, but they do nevertheless follow them, _Ad Simplic._ I, qu. 2. _Credendo meritum comparatur_, _83 qu._, qu. 68. _Gratia praevenit meritum, non gratia ex merito sed meritum ex gratia; omnia merita praecedit gratia, ut dona Dei consequantur merita mea_, Augustine in Lombard on Eph. 2:4–10. _Ipsa gratia meretur augeri, ut aucta mereatur et perfici, voluntate comitante, non ducente, pedissequa non praevia_, Ambrose in Lombard on Rom. 5:1, 2. Later, when the doctrine of the image of God as a _donum superadditum_ arose, this became still worse. The concept of grace then underwent an important modification. Grace became something needed not only for the fallen human being, but Adam too had to be elevated by it from an ordinary, natural human being to the image of God. After the fall grace therefore receives a twofold task: first, to deliver the human being from sin (_gratia sanationis, medicinalis_), and second, to elevate him to the supernatural order (_gratia elevans_); Thomas, _S. Th._ II 1, qu. 109, art. 2. For the first, grace is only accidental, necessary only in a moral sense; for the second, it is absolutely and physically necessary. The latter therefore pushes the former increasingly into the background. The ethical opposition between sin and grace gives way to the physical opposition between natural and supernatural. In Rome, grace is a supernatural, created, physical power infused into a natural human being in a magical manner through the mediation of priest and sacrament, which elevates him to the supernatural order and enables him through good works to merit all subsequent grace and, along this way, ultimately heavenly blessedness as well _ex condigno_.
