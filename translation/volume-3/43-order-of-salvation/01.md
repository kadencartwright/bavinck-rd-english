# Chapter VIII. On the Benefits of the Covenant

# § 43. The Order of Salvation

## 1.

Our heart is created for God, and it does not rest until it finds rest in him. Insofar as every human being consciously or unconsciously strives for an immutable good and an enduring happiness, it can be said that every human being also seeks God, who alone is the highest good and eternal blessedness, Acts 17:27. Yet they do not seek him in the right way and not where he is to be found. The pagan religions know of no covenant of grace, do not know the person of Christ, and all regard the way of works as the _via salutis_. Negatively, the principle of paganism is denial of the true God and of the gift of his grace, and positively, the delusion that one can acquire salvation for oneself through one’s own power and wisdom. Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower whose top is in heaven, and let us make a name for ourselves, Gen. 11:4. Whether the works by which paganism seeks the way to salvation bear a more ceremonial or a more ethical character, whether they are more positive or more negative in nature, the human being is nevertheless always his own savior. All religions outside Christianity are autosoteric. In the lowest religions the awareness of sin has been almost entirely lost, and peace, reconciliation, and happiness are obtained through magical acts and formal ceremonies. Higher religions add all kinds of moral duties to these and make salvation depend upon their fulfillment. Islam too knows no proper redemption but binds entrance into paradise to orthodox faith and to the observance of prescribed prayers and good works. In Buddhism redemption consists in the mortification, to be effected by various means, of the desire for being; Falke, _Buddha, Moh., Christus_ II, 1897, p. 103 f. And philosophy has advanced no further. The only way to salvation is the path of virtue, moral self-perfection. Even Kant and Schopenhauer, who in view of innate sinfulness considered regeneration necessary, nevertheless ended once again by appealing to the will, wisdom, and power of the human being.

The view of Scripture is entirely different. Already in the Old Testament it is God who immediately after the fall graciously places enmity between the human being and the serpent and brings the human being over to his side, Gen. 3:15; who chooses Abraham and the people of Israel born from him as his possession, Gen. 12:1; Ex. 15:13, 16; 19:4; 20:2; Deut. 7:6 f.; who establishes the covenant with them and gives them his laws, Gen. 15:1; 17:2; Ex. 2:24, 25; Deut. 4:5–13; who gives the blood on the altar for atonement, Lev. 17:11; and who spares no effort for his vineyard, Isa. 5; Jer. 2:21. But by virtue of that election and upon the foundation of that covenant, the people are now also obligated, under penalty of the curse of the law, Deut. 27:6, to walk in sincerity before God and to keep his commandments, Gen. 17:1; Ex. 20; Deut. 10:15, 16, and so forth. The covenant relation did not depend upon that keeping of the law as a preceding condition. It was no covenant of works but rested solely upon God’s electing love. Yet it had to receive its proof and seal in a walk according to the Lord’s law. For from Israel’s side it could not be accepted with a whole heart and thus become true reality in Israel except through such faith as at the same time had love and desire to walk in the way of the covenant. If the covenant is not an idea but reality, it includes the obligation and inclination to live according to the demand of the covenant. But for that reason it also goes without saying that the people could adopt very different attitudes toward the covenant and its law. There were antinomian ungodly people, forerunners of the Sadducees, who cared neither for God nor for his commandment and mocked the devout, Ps. 14:2; 36:2; 42:4, 11; 94:2; Mal. 2:17; 3:14. There were those of Pharisaical disposition who emphasized outward observance of the law and connected righteousness and salvation with it, Amos 6:1; Jer. 7:4. But between these two stood the few faithful, the sincerely devout, who were by no means indifferent to the Lord’s law but, on the contrary, meditated upon it all day and loved it with their whole soul, yet did not make their righteousness and salvation depend upon keeping it. For although they frequently appeal very strongly to their righteousness and call upon God to do them justice, Ps. 7:9; 17:1 f.; 18:21; 26:1 f.; 35:34; 41:13; 44:18, 21; 71:2; 119:121; 2 Kings 20:3; Job 16:17; Neh. 5:19; 13:14, and so forth, those same persons at the same time humbly confess their sins, call upon God for forgiveness, and plead his grace, Ps. 31:10, 11; 32:1 f.; 38:2 f.; 40:13; 41:5; 130:3, 5; Isa. 6:5; 53:4; 64:6; Jer. 3:25; Mic. 7:9; Neh. 1:6; 9:33; Dan. 9:5, 7, 18, and so forth. The righteousness of these devout people is not a personal quality but a property of the cause they advocate. They have right on their side because they rely upon God; volume II 197, 198. This trust in God is essentially what makes the righteous righteous in the Old Testament. They believe God, _האמין_, Gen. 15:6; Ex. 14:31; 2 Chron. 20:20; Isa. 28:16; Hab. 2:4; trust in him, _בטח_, Ps. 4:6; 9:11; take refuge in him, _חסה_, Ps. 7:2; 18:3; fear him, _ירא_, Ps. 22:24; 25:12; hope in him, _יחל_, _הוחיל_, Ps. 31:25; 33:18; expect it from him, _קִוָּה_, Ps. 25:21; wait for him, _חִכָּה_, Ps. 33:20; lean upon him, _סמוך_, Ps. 112:8; _נכון_, Ps. 57:8; and cling to him, _דבק_, _חשׁק_, Ps. 91:14; 2 Kings 18:6, and so forth. This faith is counted as righteousness, Gen. 15:6, just as elsewhere the keeping of God’s commandments is called righteousness, Deut. 6:25; 24:13. That this subjective righteousness, which consists essentially in trust in God, is also a fruit of God’s grace and a work of his Spirit does not, by the nature of the case, yet come so clearly to light in the Old Testament. Nevertheless, the data for this are not lacking either. There is never any mention of a righteousness of Israel’s own. It was chosen in spite of its stubbornness, Deut. 9:4–6. God is the source of all life and light, of all wisdom, power, and salvation, Deut. 8:17, 18; Ps. 36:10; 68:20, 21, 36; 73:25, 26; Jer. 2:13, 31. “Not to us, but give glory to your name,” is the prayer of Israel’s devout, Ps. 115:1. Humility is the disposition of their soul, Gen. 32:10; Ps. 116:12. A broken and contrite heart is pleasing to God, Ps. 51:19; Isa. 57:15. Every gift is always ascribed not to the human being but to God, and thanks are rendered to him for everything. Everything is called upon to praise him. Everything is desired from him in prayer, not only deliverance from dangers but also knowledge of God’s law, enlightenment of the eyes, and so forth. For it is God who has mercy on whom he wills, Ex. 33:19, and writes in his book who shall live, Ex. 32:33. Without any condition he promises that he will be their God and they will be his people, Ex. 19:6; Lev. 26:12, and that after unfaithfulness and apostasy on Israel’s part he will continually have mercy on them and give conversion and life, Ex. 32:30–35; Num. 14; 16:45–50; Lev. 26:40–44; Deut. 4:31; 8:5; 30:1–7; 32:36–43; Neh. 9:31. He forgives sins for his name’s sake, Ex. 34:7, and so forth, and sends his Holy Spirit, who is the author of all spiritual life, Num. 11:25, 29; Neh. 9:20; Ps. 51:13; 143:10; Isa. 63:10; cf. volume II 230. And when history then teaches that Israel repeatedly profanes, abandons, and destroys the covenant, Deut. 31:20; 1 Kings 11:11; 19:10, 14; Jer. 22:9; 32:32, and so forth, prophecy proclaims that God on his part will never break the covenant and will never abandon his people. He cannot do so for his name’s sake and for the sake of his renown among the Gentiles, Num. 14:16; Deut. 32:26, 27; 1 Sam. 12:22; Joel 2:17–19; Isa. 43:21, 25; 48:8–11; Jer. 14:7, 20, 21; Ezek. 20:43, 44; 36:32. It is an eternal covenant that cannot waver, since it is anchored in God’s lovingkindness, 2 Kings 13:23; 1 Chron. 16:17; Ps. 89:1–5; 105:20; 106:45; 111:5; Isa. 54:10. He guarantees, as it were, both parties—not only himself but also his people—and he will therefore establish a new covenant, not allow his Word and Spirit to depart from them, forgive their sins for his name’s sake, pour out his Spirit upon all, grant them a heart of flesh, write the law within them, and cause them to walk in his statutes, Deut. 30:6; Isa. 44:3; 59:21; Jer. 24:7; 31:31 f.; Ezek. 11:19; 16:60; 18:31; 36:26; 39:29; Joel 2:28; Mic. 7:19, and so forth; cf. volume II 181, 182.
