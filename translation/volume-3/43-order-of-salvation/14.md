## 14.

Under the general name of grace many particular benefits are comprehended. Scripture is inexhaustible in enumerating the blessings Christ acquired. Theology has at all times been at a loss to treat them completely and in an orderly sequence. When the Reformation once again placed the work of the Holy Spirit in the foreground, it initially discussed this in the three *loci de poenitentia, de fide et de bonis operibus*, but it was soon compelled to provide a more extensive treatment. Not only was the number of *loci* expanded, but gradually, especially in Reformed theology, all sorts of important modifications were introduced into their content and mutual relation. Thus regeneration was at first conceived in a very broad sense, as the renewal of the human being, and therefore treated after faith: Calvin, *Inst.* III, chs. 2, 3; Beza, *Tract. theol.* I 671; Junius, *Theses theol.* 34, 1; *Ned. Gel.*, art. 22. It was even taken for the whole of re-creation, so that it also included justification or forgiveness, *Form. Conc.* in Müller, 611. But soon, also in view of the children of the covenant, whose baptism had to be defended against Anabaptism, it was recognized that *gratia regenerationis prior in nobis est quam fides, quae illius est effectus*, Polanus, *Synt.*, p. 467. Thus theologians came to restrict regeneration to the infusion of the first principle of life and therefore to place it before faith. There was and remained great disagreement about the time of that regeneration. While Roman Catholics and Lutherans had it take place in baptism for all children, the Reformed said that the grace of regeneration was granted to the elect children of the covenant either before or during or after baptism—or, without further determination, before, during, or after baptism—Voetius, *Disp.* II 408 sq.; Witsius, *Misc. S.* II 614, 627; M. Vitringa III 80; and that it was granted to those gathered at a later age during and after, or even before, the calling, Voetius, *Disp.* II 461. From this it followed in turn that in dogmatics regeneration came to stand not only before faith but also before calling. This order was defended on the ground that *verbum Dei nemo salutariter audire potest nisi qui sit regenitus*: Maccovius, *Loci C.*, pp. 710–714; *Coll. theol.*, p. 398; *Theses theol. per loc. comm.*, 1641, p. 320; Voetius, *Disp.* II 445; Kuyper, *Het werk v. d. H. Geest* II 128f. A further consequence of this ordering was that *poenitentia*, which among the Reformers preceded faith and was worked by the law, fell away almost entirely. Luther and Calvin had, after all, already distinguished between the sorrow that proceeded from the law and can also occur in the unconverted, and that other sorrow which already presupposed faith and was a true sorrow over sin. Following Calvin’s example, *Inst.* III 3, 1, 2, the Reformed therefore incorporated *poenitentia* into the Christian life, and soon began to use another word for it, namely *resipiscentia*, because *poenitentia*, like penance, had acquired a Roman Catholic flavor and involuntarily suggested punishment: Beza, *Tract. theol.* I 327 sq.; Musculus, *Loci Comm.*, 1567, p. 550. This conversion thus acquired an entirely different sense. If regeneration as a rule preceded everything and took place in the youth of the children of the covenant, conversion did not always have to be accompanied, as Pietism and Methodism later demanded, *cum notabili concussione et violenta tractione*, but could occur *assiduo, successive et suaviter*. Then it was also unnecessary that someone have clear knowledge of, and be able to give an account of, the manner and time of his conversion, Voetius, *Disp.* II 415, 460, as, for example, Wesley knew that his took place on May 24, 1738, at 8-3/4 o’clock in the evening. It was then no longer concentrated in one point but extended throughout the whole Christian life. Nor could it any longer, like *poenitentia* previously, have *contritio* and *fides* as its parts, but it consisted in a continual dying of the old and rising of the new person: Ursinus in his exposition of Lord’s Day 23; Beza, ib.; Polanus, *Synt.*, p. 468; etc. And finally—to mention no more and not to speak further here of the distinction between external and internal calling, or between faith as *habitus* and *actus*—this further modification was introduced into the order of salvation, that justification was placed even before regeneration and calling. This occurred especially in the struggle against neonomism. Faith and conversion could not and might not be legal conditions to be fulfilled by the human being in order to become partaker of forgiveness and thereby bring it into being. They were, after all, gifts of the Holy Spirit, benefits of the covenant of grace, fruits of the work of Christ. But then no communion in these benefits was possible except after communion with the person of Christ. The imputation of his person with all his benefits thus preceded the granting of those benefits: justification received the first place in the order of salvation. Nevertheless, this scheme was only rarely followed through to the end and fully applied in Reformed dogmatics. Only a few, such as Maccovius, have the order *justificatio activa, regeneratio, fides, justificatio passiva, bona opera*. The conditions that gradually arose in the church did not permit the application of the scheme. In a pure state of the church it is possible to believe that the children of the covenant are regenerated in their youth and come *successive et suaviter* to faith and conversion. But when the world penetrates the church and many grow up and live for years without showing any fruit worthy of faith and conversion, the earnest-minded see themselves called to warn against confidence in a regeneration in youth and an historical faith in the forgiveness of sins, and to insist upon genuine conversion and saving faith, so as truly to become partakers of the forgiveness of sins. Over against a dead orthodoxy, Pietism and Methodism with their revivals always again have a right to exist.

The order of salvation, however, has been changed by both these tendencies into a history of the sinner’s conversion that served as a model for everyone without distinction. In this, weighty truths were disregarded and the order of salvation itself falsified. For it does not have to explain how the human being must come to conversion and assurance; in that way a law is prescribed to the Holy Spirit, one method is chosen for all, and the anthropological standpoint is substituted for the theological. Rather, the order of salvation must indicate which benefits Christ acquired and applies through the Holy Spirit, and in what order they lie in the Word and in the thought of God. Considered thus, 1º there is no doubt that all the benefits of Christ are benefits of the covenant of grace. That covenant has its foundation in eternity; it rests in God’s counsel and good pleasure. Christ is the mediator of that covenant and could therefore in time make satisfaction vicariously for his own. There has been an imputation of Christ to his church and of the church to Christ; an exchange has taken place between the two; a *unio mystica* has been established which does not first come into being in time but is rooted in eternity. If one wishes to call all this justification, there is no objection other than that one uses the word in an unusual sense, departs from the usage of Scripture, treats the same matter twice under different names (once as *pactum salutis*, then as justification), and nevertheless cannot remain faithful to one’s starting point. For even those in the Reformed church who teach an eternal justification have never said that this exchange of places between Christ and his church in the *pactum salutis* was already the whole, full justification. Rather, they have regarded it as the first moment and expressly declared that it had to be continued and completed in the resurrection of Christ, in the gospel, in the calling, in the witness of the Holy Spirit through faith and from works, and in the last judgment. None of them, therefore, treated or completed the treatment of justification in the *locus* on the counsel of God or the covenant of redemption; all discussed it in the order of salvation, and even there not only before but then once again after faith as *justificatio passiva*. 2º From a Christian standpoint there may just as little be any doubt that all the benefits of grace were acquired by Christ, are present in him, and lie ready for his church. Nothing needs to be added on the human side; everything has been accomplished. And because all these benefits are benefits of the covenant, acquired in the way of the covenant and distributed in that same way, there is no communion in those benefits except through communion with his person. The covenant, the *unio mystica*, the imputation of Christ precedes. How otherwise could we receive the Holy Spirit, the grace of regeneration, and the gift of faith, which were, after all, all acquired by Christ and are his property? It is therefore not the case that outside Christ we are first regenerated by the Holy Spirit and receive faith, in order then with it to go to Christ, receive his righteousness, and thus be justified by God. Rather, these benefits too flow to us from the Father’s good pleasure, from the fullness of Christ, and from the covenant of grace. If one now wishes to call this imputation of the person of Christ, which precedes the granting of his benefits and thus also regeneration and faith, justification, then in the main the same objections mentioned above must be raised against it. But one then forgets, in addition, that this imputation of Christ is not identical with justification or forgiveness; that all benefits—regeneration, faith, sanctification, and so forth just as much as the forgiveness of sins—lie objectively ready in Christ, and as such therefore provide no order; and that the *justificatio passiva* through and from faith is an act of God, a witness of the Holy Spirit in and with our spirit, which cannot be temporally separated from the *justificatio activa*. 3º The way in which these benefits, to be described more closely below, come into the possession of believers is that of calling through Word and through Spirit. By these God brought creation into being; by these he also brings re-creation into being. Many have placed this calling after regeneration. And without doubt, in all children of the covenant who are regenerated in their youth, this benefit does precede the calling. Yet it is just as dangerous to make these cases the general rule as to make the reverse the rule. For first, among the Reformed there has always been disagreement and freedom of opinion about the time of regeneration, whether before or during or after baptism. Scripture does not speak clearly enough to make a decision in this matter. Second, in many cases it is very difficult to assume that people who were regenerated in their youth live for years in all kinds of sins and only afterward come to conversion; cf. also Voetius, *Disp.* II 410. Scripture supplies not a single proof for this. Third, it is not permissible in the mission field already to presuppose regeneration in the calling through the gospel, since Word and Spirit are thereby separated in a dubious manner. Fourth, the order of existence among the persons in the divine being and the order of their working in creation and re-creation indicate that the Word preceded the Spirit, Christmas and Easter Pentecost; the Holy Spirit is the witness of Christ. And finally, calling must be conceived much more broadly than is possible when it is placed after regeneration. There is a *vocatio universalis*, a *vocatio generalis*, a *vocatio specialis*. Calling in general by no means intends only to bring the regenerate to faith and conversion but has significance for all human beings. It is by no means only external but also internal and is understood by children already at an early age. That deaf people cannot hear in this connection is no objection at all, because God himself is the one who calls, and just as in creation he called forth everything from nothing by his word, so in re-creation he calls the things that do not exist as though they did. 4º The benefits themselves, which through the calling become the portion of believers, fall into two groups. Sin is guilt and stain, a breaking of the covenant of works and loss of the image of God. Christ made satisfaction through his passive and active obedience. And thus the benefits of Christ consist in this, that he restores us in our relation to God and all creatures (justification, adoption as children, Christian freedom), and further renews us after God’s image (regeneration in the broader sense, sanctification, perseverance). The first series of benefits is granted to us through the illumination of the Holy Spirit, is received by us in faith as *cognitio, assensus, certitudo*, and transforms our consciousness. The second group of benefits becomes ours through the regenerating activity of the Holy Spirit, in faith as *fiducia cordis*, and changes our being. In the first, our eye is directed toward the past, toward the historical Christ, toward the cross and the gospel that bears witness to it; in the second, our gaze is raised upward toward the living Lord in heaven, the king of the church, the bridegroom of the congregation. These benefits are distinct but not separated. It is Christ himself, the glorified Savior, who through his Word and Spirit directs our faith to his sacrifice and takes our persons up into his communion. It is one and the same faith through which he assures us in our consciousness and renews us in our being, justifies us and dwells in our hearts. 5º Accordingly, the order of salvation must be treated in three *loci*: calling and regeneration (in the narrower sense), faith and justification, sanctification and perseverance; cf. Voetius, *Disp.* II 432 sq. In the first *locus* Christ appears principally as prophet, who through his word instructs us unto salvation. The Holy Spirit is in this connection the witness of Christ, who exercises his *officium elencticum* and through *gratia praeparans, praeveniens*, and *operans* grants us the principle of the new life. In the second Christ is principally the priest, who through faith grants us his righteousness and frees us from the guilt of sins. The Holy Spirit there exercises his *munus paracleticum* and through *gratia illuminans* makes us certain of our salvation. In the third Christ appears principally as our king, who through faith governs and protects us. The Holy Spirit there fulfills his *munus sanctificans* and through *gratia cooperans, conservans, perficiens* re-creates us after the image of Christ. In Rom. 8:30 Paul likewise names three benefits in which the προγνωσις is realized, namely, calling, justification, and glorification. All these benefits occur in time. The ἐδοξασεν too refers not to glorification after death or on the day of judgment but, as appears from the aorist, to the glorification which believers in Paul’s day already received on earth through the indwelling of the Spirit, cf. 2 Cor. 3:18, and which is completed in the glorification at the resurrection on the last day, 1 Cor. 15:53; Phil. 3:21. They are all granted simultaneously to the elect in faith, cf. also 1 Cor. 6:11, but a logical order nevertheless exists among them; and this is presented in the *ordo salutis*. Cf. Gennrich, “Studien zur Paulin. Heilsordnung,” *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1898, pp. 377–431.
