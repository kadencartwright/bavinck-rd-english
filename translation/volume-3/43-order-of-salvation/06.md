## 6.

The Roman penitential system brought Luther to his Reformation activity. An entirely new light dawned upon him when he learned the scriptural meaning of μετανοια. In the first seven of his 95 theses, and further in his *sermo de poenitentia* and *Sermon v. Sakr. der Busse*, he expounded this meaning of repentance and taught that the principal part of repentance consists neither in private confession, of which Scripture knows nothing, nor in satisfaction, for God forgives sins freely, but in genuine sorrow over sins, in an earnest resolve to bear Christ’s cross, in a new life, and in the word of absolution, that is, of grace in Christ. The penitent person comes to the forgiveness of sins not through satisfaction and priestly absolution but through trust in the word of God, through faith in God’s grace. It is not the sacrament but faith that justifies. Thus Luther came to place sin and grace at the center of the Christian doctrine of salvation. The forgiveness of sins does not depend upon a penance that always remains imperfect, but rests in God’s promise and becomes ours through faith alone; cf. also Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* I 44, 190 f., 202 f., 213 f., 223 f. But there is great disagreement about the relation in which Luther placed repentance and faith to one another. According to Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* I² 198 f., Luther did at first indeed think that true repentance was a fruit of faith in the gospel and of love for God; but later, especially after Melanchthon’s *Unterricht der Visitatoren* of 1528, in order not to cultivate false security he is said to have placed the contrition worked by the law before faith—precisely the reverse of Calvin, who in the first edition of the *Institutes* is said to have placed contrition before faith, but later, in the *Catech. Genev.* of 1538 and in the second and subsequent editions of the *Institutes*, faith before true contrition; cf. also Harnack, *D. G.* III² 749 f.; Herrmann in Gottschick’s *Zeits. f. Th. u. K.*, 1891, pp. 28–81. The historical investigation of Lipsius, “Luthers Lehre v. d. Busse,” *Jahrb. f. prot. Theol.*, 1892, pp. 161–340, has shown, however, that there is no question of such a reversal in Luther’s doctrine of repentance. For him *poenitentia* always consists of two parts: *contritio*, knowledge of and sorrow over sins, worked by the law; and *fides*, faith in the grace of God in the gospel of Christ. Through the preaching of the law God first breaks the sinner’s hard heart and then through faith leads him to the comfort of the gospel. But when the sinner thus learns to know God’s grace, only then does he receive true love for the good, and from it is born that genuine repentance which continues throughout the whole of life and consists in a dying of the old and a rising of the new person. At one time, over against Roman works-righteousness, Luther indeed lays more emphasis upon the fact that true repentance proceeds from faith and encompasses the whole of life; and at another, over against antinomianism, more upon the fact that genuine faith is preceded by a breaking of the heart. But in substance Luther’s doctrine always remained the same: *contritio* (*poenitentia* in the narrower sense), *fides*, and *bona opera* are the three parts of the *via salutis*; cf. Köstlin, ib. I 36, 72, 159, 368; II 75, 493, 496 f.; and in Herzog²³, art. “Busse”; Sieffert, *Die neuesten theol. Forschungen über Busse u. Glauben*, Berlin, 1896. This presentation is also that of the Lutheran confessional writings, ed. Müller, 41, 167, 312, 534, 634, and of the first dogmaticians—Brenz, Strigel, Chytraeus, and others—down to Gerhard; the order of salvation was treated under these three *loci*. Now in the first period Luther had always proceeded in this order of salvation from absolute predestination; but later, without retracting it, he placed the center of gravity in God’s revelation in Christ, in the universal offer of salvation, in word and sacrament. From 1527 onward Melanchthon came to synergism and taught that in conversion the will also cooperates, *assentiens nec repugnans verbo Dei*, since it has still retained the *facultas applicandi se ad gratiam*, *Loci*, c. *de lib. arb.* The Formula of Concord did expressly reject the doctrine that the will can accommodate itself to grace from its *own, natural* powers, ed. Müller, 607; cf. 608, 713. Yet it nevertheless taught side by side the predestination and impotence of the human being, ib. 594, 705, and the universality and resistibility of grace, 555, 602, and found the reconciliation in this, that the human being has still retained a *capacitatem non activam sed passivam*, can still go to church, and so forth, and especially in this, that he still *patitur* (and *pati potest*), *ut Deus in se operetur*, ib. 609, 610. Later this was ordinarily worked out in Lutheran theology in such a way that God grants to all who live under the gospel, in baptism or through the preaching of the Word, a *gratia sufficiens* (*motus bonos inevitabiles, irresistibiles*), by which the human will is so liberated and renewed that it can either merely not resist, allow God’s grace to work regeneration and conversion within it, and remain entirely passive under it, or can also cooperate positively with it: Gerhard, *Loc.* XI, nos. 56, 57, 81; Quenstedt, *Theol.* III 505, 508, 510, 513; Hollaz, *Ex.* 794, 861 sq., 872 sq.; Buddeus, *Inst. theol.* 921 sq.; Schmid, *Dogm. d. ev. l. K.*, 297–369; Luthardt, *Komp.* §§ 57–66. Under the influence of this concealed or open synergism, when the *ordo salutis* was later expanded and, as in Hollaz with reference to Acts 26:17, 18, treated in the *loci* on *gratia vocans, illuminans, convertens, regenerans, justificans, renovans*, and *glorificans*, it received among the Lutherans the following form. Christian children, because they cannot yet resist, are regenerated in baptism and receive the gift of faith. Others are at a later age first called with a *vocatio sufficiens*, which is the same for all and provides all with that illumination in the understanding and that power in the will which enables them not to resist the operation of God’s grace. In case they do not resist, through the preaching of the law they are brought to *contritio* (*poenitentia*, conversion in the narrower sense), and then regenerated and endowed with faith, which is a fruit of regeneration. Through faith they are then justified, receive the forgiveness of sins, and subsequently, in succession, the *adoptio, unio mystica, renovatio*, and *glorificatio*. But the Christian life does not proceed so regularly in reality. Just as grace in its beginning depends upon the will strengthened by supernatural power, so it continues to do in its progress and to the end. Grace is always resistible and therefore also liable to be lost until the hour of death and also capable of being obtained again—not once, but even repeatedly. The center of gravity in this order of salvation therefore lies in the human being. However strongly it may still be declared that God alone regenerates and converts, whether God will do this nevertheless depends upon the person’s resistance or nonresistance. The human being holds the decision in his hands; by resisting he can nullify the whole work of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, and he keeps that decision in his hands until his death. More particularly still, the center of gravity in this order of salvation lies in faith and justification. Calling, contrition, and regeneration, after all, bear only a preparatory character; they are really not yet benefits of the covenant of grace, they take place, as it were, still outside Christ and serve to lead the sinner toward Christ. Only when the human being believes and through that faith receives the righteousness of Christ does God see him in Christ, forgive him his sins, free him from the law, adopt him as his child, incorporate him into fellowship with Christ, and so forth. Everything depends upon faith, and specifically upon the act of faith. If a person exercises it, then he has everything, and everything at once—peace, comfort, life, salvation; but if he fails to exercise it, then everything becomes wavering, uncertain, and liable to loss. Thus everything is directed toward preserving that faith. But just as the Lutheran believer does not let the work of grace arise from eternal election, from the covenant, so he also does not connect it with nature and the world and humanity. He is saved in his faith but does not let it work upon family and school, society and state. It is enough for him to live in fellowship with Christ, but he feels no impulse to fight under Christ as king. Cf. especially Schneckenburger, *Vergl. Darst. d. luth. u. ref. Lehrbegriffs*, 2 vols., 1855, passim.
