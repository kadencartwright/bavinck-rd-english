## 9.

But neonomianism also appeared in a pietistic form, in which it made faith and experience—not faith and obedience—the condition of justification. From the beginning there was in the Reformed church and theology a practical tendency that was averse to all scholasticism and placed all emphasis upon life. It was especially supported and promoted by the philosopher Petrus Ramus, who was a vigorous opponent of Aristotle, desired greater simplicity in philosophy, and described theology as _doctrina bene vivendi_, whose purpose is not _notitia rerum sed usus et exercitatio_; cf. Tideman, in _Stud. en Bijdr. op het gebied der hist. Theol._, edited by Moll and De Hoop Scheffer, III 1876, pp. 389–429. This conception gained acceptance among many Reformed theologians: in Strasbourg with Sturm, in Heidelberg with Tremellius, in Herborn with Piscator, in the Netherlands with Snellius, Scaliger, and Jac. Alting, and in Cambridge with Perkins, whose pupil Amesius, later professor at Franeker alongside Maccovius, also described theology as _doctrina Deo vivendi_, _studium pietatis_, seated in the will; cf. Dr. H. Visscher, _Guil. Amesius_, Haarlem 1894. Thus there arose a practical, pietistic tendency, represented in England, for example, by R. Baxter, _A call to the unconverted_, 1669; Daniel Williams, _Gospel truth stated and vindicated_, 1692; B. Woodbridge, _The method of grace in the justification of sinners_, 1656; and many practical writers, and advocated in the Netherlands by many theologians and preachers: Witsius, Vitringa, Lampe, Mel, d’Outrein, Brakel, Hellenbroek, Smytegelt, Francken, Groenewegen, Borstius, Van der Groe, Eswijler, Schortinghuis, and so forth; cf. Heppe, _Gesch. d. Piet. u. d. Mystik in der ref. K._, Leiden 1879; Göbel, _Gesch. d. chr. Lebens in der rh.-westph. ev. K._, 3 Bde, 1849–1860; Ritschl, _Gesch. des Pietismus, I in der ref. K._, 1880. As conditions in the church grew more lamentable and a dead orthodoxy gained the upper hand, these writers placed all emphasis upon the necessity of true conversion. Birth from believing parents, membership in the church, baptism, the Lord’s Supper, and orthodox faith were not enough. One had to partake of true, saving faith, which bore an entirely different character from temporary faith, faith in miracles, and historical faith. True faith does not arise unless terror before the law, fear of judgment, and anxiety over sin have preceded it. Nor is the essence of faith assent or conviction; it consists more in trust than in knowledge and is seated more in the heart and the will than in the understanding. And it is not immediately _certitudo_, assurance. No, as Gomarus, _Op._ I 654 sq., had already said, a distinction must be made between refuge-taking and assured trust; the former alone constitutes the essence of faith, while the latter belongs to its well-being and can be added to it much later. That faith as refuge-taking trust, consisting in hungering and thirsting for Christ and his righteousness, is a condition that precedes justification; it entrusts itself to Christ in order to be justified. When it has received the righteousness of Christ, it then comes before God the Father with that righteousness, points him to his promises, and is justified by him. Thus matters do not proceed as simply and easily as many suppose: the gospel is not for all people; the offer of salvation is not universal; the law is for all, but the gospel is only for certain “qualified” sinners, for sinners who had already begun to receive grace. No one may believe except the one who has first received boldness to do so from the Holy Spirit; one must guard against an imaginary and a stolen faith! Continual self-examination therefore remains necessary; one can so easily deceive oneself; the distinction between the regenerate at their worst and the unregenerate at their best is so fine; there is so much similarity between false and true grace. The believer must therefore repeatedly examine himself and test himself by the marks of spiritual life; the way of salvation is a narrow, constricted way along which even the righteous are scarcely saved. It is also a long way. There is a great distance from refuge-taking to assured trust; between them lie many classes and groups of people: the awakened, the convicted, the concerned, those who desire salvation, those of little faith and weak faith, and so forth. Sealing and assurance usually follow only after a long period of doubt and struggle, and then frequently come about in an extraordinary manner, through a voice, a vision, a consoling word of Scripture that suddenly comes to mind, and so forth. Related to this Pietism in the Reformed church is Lutheran Pietism, which was prepared by Musaeus, Arnd, and others, and also by Reformed writers such as Baxter, Dyke, Bayly, and others, and then under Spener, 1635–1705, became a powerful movement and spread widely. Through preaching, discipline, _collegia pietatis_, and many writings, such as _Pia desideria oder herzliches Verlangen nach gottgefälliger Besserung der wahren christlichen Kirche_, 1675, Spener took a stand against dead orthodoxy. He desired a return to the grace of regeneration received in baptism but later lost. Historical faith is insufficient; a living, active faith is necessary for salvation. And one first obtains this faith when one learns to know one’s sins through the preaching of the law and has undergone a long, anxious struggle with the devil, the world, and the flesh, even to the point of despair (_Busskampf_); then from that struggle true faith comes to a _Durchbruch_. This faith therefore consists not only in assent but in trust; it is an experience, a lived experience of the heart, a life of the soul. And only as such is it a means to justification, so as thereafter to manifest itself in a holy life distinct from the world, abstaining even from the _adiaphora_. Cf. Walch, _Hist. u. theol. Einl. in die Religionsstreit. der ev. luth. K._ II 239–436; Ritschl, _Gesch. d. Pietismus_ II 1884; Riggenbach, Herzog² 11, 672, and the literature cited there. Zinzendorf, 1700–1760, was reared in this Pietism and remained in agreement with it in his aversion to dead orthodoxy, but Pietism was nevertheless too legalistic for him. Terror before the law and anxiety over sin, although not wrong and sometimes possessing a preparatory power, are nevertheless not the true thing. True repentance—although the word “penance” is less accurate, since it suggests punishment—arises from the gospel, from the preaching of the suffering Christ. It consists not so much in anxiety and struggle, in lamenting and weeping, but in trust in God’s grace. It is a matter of the heart, of feeling. Therefore the heart must be made sensitive, and that happens best through a vivid portrayal of Christ in his suffering, of his blood and wounds. By this, as by immediate vision, by a deep, vivid impression, by a _Wundenblick_, faith is born in the heart without one’s having undergone a _Busskampf_ or knowing precisely the hour of one’s regeneration. That faith brings about a union, a betrothal, a marriage between Christ and the soul; causes the heart to swim in grace, that is, in Jesus’ blood, as in its element; and causes the believer always to live in the _liebe Nähe_ of the Savior. It justifies and regenerates at the same time; faith and love coincide; more valuable than objective justification is the dynamic communication of the Spirit, the birth from Jesus’ side. Born of him, believers live in his presence without pietistic anxiety, do everything in his name, place everything, including domestic and social life, under his rule, and lead a cheerful Christian life; cf. Plitt, _Zinzendorfs Theol._ I 301–453, II 242–420, III 45–215; Spangenberg, _Idea fidei fratrum_, 1778; Becker in Herzog² 17, 513; Ritschl, _Gesch. d. Pietismus_ III 1886. What Pietism was for the Lutherans, the Methodism of Wesley, 1703–1791, and Whitefield, 1714–1771, later became for the Reformed church. Originally it sought nothing other than to awaken the sleeping church and animate orthodox Christendom with new life. To this end, people first had to be brought suddenly, through gripping preaching of righteousness, sin, judgment, and damnation, to a deep awareness of their lost condition; then, in the same moment, without delay, to be led through faith to Christ and assured of their salvation; and thereafter to be exhorted to a new, sinless life, active in the service of the kingdom of God, devoted to mission and philanthropy, and abstaining from all kinds of indifferent things. In distinction from Pietism, Methodism clearly betrays its English origin and Reformed provenance. It is indeed equally a reaction against dead orthodoxy, but it will know nothing of preparation or of a gradual progress of conversion; it has no protracted _Busskampf_, no finally occurring _Durchbruch_, no later _Versiegelung_; it draws everything together at one point, places conversion in the full light of consciousness, and keeps a record of the souls saved. And when it has converted people, it does not gather them in quiet, secluded circles, societies, and conventicles in order to cultivate piety there; rather, it organizes them into an army that proceeds on the offensive, enters the wide world full of fire, and takes it by storm for Christ; cf. Schaff, _Creeds_ I 882, III 807; Schoell in Herzog² 9, 681; Schneckenburger, _Vorl. über die Lehrbegriffe der kleineren prot. Kirchenparteien_, 1863 S. 103–151; Lecky, _Entstehungsgesch. u. Charakt. des Methodismus, aus d. Engl._, Leipzig 1880; Kolde, _Der Methodismus und seine Bekämpfung_, Erl. 1886; idem, _Die Heilsarmee_, Erl. 1885.
