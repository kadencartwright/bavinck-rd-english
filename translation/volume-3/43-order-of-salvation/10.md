## 10.

In more recent theology too, descriptions of the order of salvation diverge widely. Through his doctrine of the _radikal Böse_, Kant came to the acknowledgment that a _neue Schöpfung_ had to take place in the human being; _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 54. Nevertheless, he sees conversion as a work of the human being. Like Pelagius with his _si debet, potest_, he infers from the _du sollst_ to the _du kannst_, and exhorts everyone to do what lies within his power to become a better human being and then to hope for _höhere Mitwirkung_; ibid., 58, 59. Even though that conversion remains imperfect on earth, God counts the disposition as the deed and justifies us by grace. Even after his conversion the human being also continues to make satisfaction for the sins he previously committed, voluntarily takes their pain and punishment upon himself, and in this way too makes himself worthy of forgiveness; ibid., 82, 86 f. Schopenhauer taught the corruption of human nature even more strongly than Kant. He too therefore said that, in order to improve the human being radically, enlightenment of the head and development of the understanding were wholly insufficient, and that a regeneration of the core of his being had to take place, because _operari sequitur esse_; _Die Welt_ I⁶ 477 f.; II⁶ 692 f. But he himself again turns aside from this line of his thought and seeks redemption from suffering partly in art and philosophy but especially in this: that in the light of knowledge the human being lets the whole wretched life operate as a quietive upon the will, negates the will, and thus enters nirvana, where the will is completely extinguished; ibid., I 448 f. In this Schopenhauer agreed with Schelling, whose negative philosophy ended with the claim that blessedness is attainable not along the path of virtue and the keeping of the law but only in the way of the _“beschauliche”_ life, _Werke_ I 8, p. 235 f.; II 1, p. 554 f., but who nevertheless declared in his positive philosophy that the human being becomes a partaker of justification and blessedness only through religion, through communion with the risen Christ, _denn Person sucht Person_; ibid., 566 f.; II 4, p. 217 f. And so too von Hartmann seeks blessedness not in art, philosophy, or religion but in the decision humanity will one day make to annihilate willing, in _Universalwillensverneinung_; _Philos. d. Unb._ II⁹ 400 f. In Hegel, reconciliation is a process within the divine being itself. The human being is essentially one with God but does not know it. Being and consciousness are divided; the human being is a stranger to himself. But Christ was the first to become conscious of his divine being, of his being one with God. And whoever, like Christ and in communion with him, perceives and acknowledges that unity is reconciled. What matters here is the disposition. The deed still lags far behind that disposition, but this imperfection is a vanishing moment and therefore does not count at all in reconciliation; _Philos. d. Rel._, _Werke_ XII 266 f. In agreement with this philosophy of Hegel, modernist theology teaches that God and the human being, grace and will, form no opposition in the work of conversion, but that conversion is simultaneously and wholly an act of both. For grace in fact coincides with God’s providence, works only ethically and pedagogically, and itself cultivates and strengthens within the religious community the capacity for salvation (_facultas se applicandi ad gratiam_). In the long run it will therefore bring all human beings to salvation and overcome all resistance. Neither does the human being need regeneration in the proper sense. Conversion can be called regeneration only when it is viewed from God’s side. This conversion itself consists in repentance, that is, sorrow over past sins, a disposition willingly to bear their punishment and henceforth to improve one’s life, and in faith, that is, trust in the grace of God in Christ. When the human being converts in this way, he is also immediately justified therein. For justification is no transcendent act of God but nothing other than the removal of the consciousness of guilt, a change in the consciousness of the relation to God, the elimination of the division between the natural ego and its destiny. For in conversion the human being is renewed in principle and as such bears within himself the guarantee of perfection, just as the plant lies hidden in the seed and the man in the child. Cf. Strauss, _Chr. Gl._ II 362 f., 463, 492–497; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ § 847–911; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ § 138–164; Lang, _Versuch einer chr. Dogm._ § 19–25; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 170; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 76 f., 109 f.; _Initia_ ch. 5.

The conception Schleiermacher gives of the order of salvation differs from this more in appearance than in truth. After Christ, he says, entered into our communion of sin and misery, he has the power and calling to receive us into his communion of holiness and blessedness. This occurs through regeneration and sanctification. Regeneration consists in conversion and justification, which are both one and the same thing, viewed in the one case from our side and in the other from God’s side. In conversion, which in turn has two parts—repentance and faith—the human being is neither cooperative nor entirely passive but receptive (in the sense of Melanchthon’s _facultas se applicandi ad gratiam_), so that the whole of humanity too will one day become ripe for grace and partake of salvation. Justification is that act of God by which the human being is placed in communion with Christ. Negatively, it consists in forgiveness, that is, condemnation of the old life, and positively, in adoption, that is, the awakening of a new life. As the reverse side of conversion, it causes forgiveness actually to take place because and insofar as the human being partakes of a new life in the communion of Christ. The _Vermittelungstheologen_ did indeed assign a broader place in dogmatics to the person of Christ and the activity of the Holy Spirit, but in the order of salvation they nevertheless did not entirely rise above Schleiermacher’s ideas. First, nearly all of them ascribe to the human being the power to accept or reject grace, whether that power derives from God’s creation or providence or from the _gratia praeparans_ granted in baptism or calling; cf. volume II 343. And second, they do correct Schleiermacher insofar as they regard justification as an objective act of God, but they nevertheless all have it take place on the ground not of the imputed but of the infused righteousness of Christ, so that they teach not only a judicial but also a communicating, sanctifying act of God and a _προληψις_ of the future; cf., for example, Neander, _Gesch. der Pflanzung u. Leitung der chr. K._⁵ 551 f.; Nitzsch, _Syst. d. chr. Lehre_ § 146, 147; Martensen, _Chr. Dogm._ § 230, 231; Lange, _Chr. Dogm._ II § 95; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 443; Schöberlein, _Prinzip u. Syst. d. Dogm._ 652, and so forth; also Beck, _Chr. Lehrwiss._ I 522 f., 533 f.; _Chr. Glaub._ II 595 f.; cf. _Die Rechtfertigungslehre der Prof. d. Theol. Joh. Tob. Beck, O. F. Myrberg und A. W. Ingman, geprüft u. beleuchtet von mehreren evang. Theologen u. von E. T. Gestrin_, Berlin, 1892; and even Hengstenberg in the _Ev. Kirchenz._ of 1866 and 1867, who appealed for his opinion to the epistle of James and also to Luke 7:36 f.; cf. _Bew. d. Gl._ 1868, p. 381 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 644 f.

Over against this justification built upon the subject, Ritschl returned to the person of Christ and sought the ground of forgiveness in his work. He did not do so in the sense that forgiveness was acquired by Christ and consisted in a change of God’s disposition, for God is eternal love and there is no punitive righteousness in him. But through his perfect faithfulness to his vocation, his uninterrupted communion with God, and his complete surrender to God’s will, Christ nevertheless proclaimed and proved that God is love, that he is not wrathful and does not punish but forgives. This was necessary not because God is far from humanity but because humanity, through its sin—which is really ignorance, not objective guilt but a subjective consciousness of guilt—is far from God. Therefore, even to death, Christ proclaimed God’s love and, making God’s purpose for humanity his own, founded a kingdom of God, a church, into which he transplanted the consciousness that God is love and forgives sin and that, without needing to let its sin hinder it, the church can live in communion with God. Justification is therefore in Ritschl a synthetic judgment, pronounced not on the ground of good works but preceding those good works. For the fear of God as judge must first make way in the human being for the consciousness of communion with him before the human being can do good works and fulfill his moral vocation. Further, this justification is neither a pronouncement concerning nor an experience of the individual believer, but is a benefit of the church, which knows that despite its sin it stands in communion with God. It coincides with the founding of the church itself; _Rechtf. u. Vers._² III 26–131; Kaftan, _Dogm._ 493 f.; Schultz, _Grundriss der ev. Dogm._ § 52. Individual persons become partakers of this benefit of justification only by joining the church and appropriating it to themselves in faith. That faith is free, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 536. Election too has as its object not individual human beings but the church, ibid., III 114. Nor is that faith wrought magically; rather, education is the ordinary form in which someone comes to faith, III 555. It consists essentially in trust, is independent of historical investigation and its results, and rests upon a deep impression made upon the unprejudiced heart by the moral greatness of Jesus; cf. volume I 453. Through that faith the human being acquires a different judgment concerning God, himself, and the world. He learns to know God as love, knows that his sin is no longer an obstacle to communion with God, no longer regards calamities and disasters as punishments but rules spiritually over all things. In a word, his consciousness of guilt is removed, and in this his justification consists; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 38 f. The consequence of this justification is reconciliation, the laying aside of hostility toward God on the ground of that justification; ibid., III 74–76. And regeneration is essentially identical with that justification and reconciliation, for regeneration consists not in a hyperphysical change but in a change of mood and disposition; ibid., III 557, 562.
