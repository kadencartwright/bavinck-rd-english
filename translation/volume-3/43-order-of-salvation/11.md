## 11.

By the order of salvation, *ordo* or *via salutis*, is understood the manner in which, or the way along which, the sinner comes into possession of the benefits of grace acquired by Christ. Only late did it receive an independent place and a somewhat orderly treatment in dogmatics. Among the scholastics the material for this *locus* is still scattered; the principal matters relevant here are found in the commentaries on *Sent.* II, dist. 26–29; III, dist. 25–27; and *Summa* II 1, qu. 109–114. The Council of Trent treats all subjects pertaining to grace under the title *de justificatione*, sess. VI. Roman Catholic theologians ordinarily summarize the material in a *locus de gratia* and successively discuss in it *gratia actualis* (*natura, necessitas, gratuitas; sufficientia, efficacia*, etc.), *gratia habitualis* (*justificatio*), and *fructus gratiae* (*meritum*). The Reformation caused this *locus* to be treated much more broadly than before. It initially discussed only *de poenitentia, de fide*, and *de bonis operibus*, but gradually expanded the number of subjects and treated successively *de vocatione, de illuminatione, de regeneratione, de conversione, de fide, de justificatione*, and so forth. Naturally, the need was soon felt to bring this extensive and rich material together again under one heading. Calvin already led the way in this and gave the third book of his *Institutes* the title *de modo percipiendae Christi gratiae, et qui inde fructus nobis proveniant et qui effectus consequantur*. Others speak of *gratia Sp. S. applicatrix* (Quenstedt), σωτηριοποιια *seu salutis consequendae modus* (Calovius), *redemptionis applicatio* (Mastricht), *ordo salutis seu salutis impetrandae modus* (Reinhard), and so forth. Now this order of salvation has to wrestle with a peculiar difficulty. On the one hand, everything has been accomplished by Christ: sin atoned for, the law fulfilled, death conquered, Satan subjected, forgiveness acquired, and life in incorruption brought to light. One would expect those for whom Christ died to be immediately and completely freed from sin, suffering, and death and made partakers of holiness and salvation. This, however, is not the case. On the contrary, in time they are admonished to faith and conversion, must be regenerated, justified, sanctified, and glorified, remain subject in this life to sin, suffering, and death, and enter the kingdom of heaven only through many tribulations. How can the one be reconciled with the other? On the one hand, everything has been accomplished, so that nothing remains for the human being to do; and on the other hand, the most important thing still seems to have to happen to the human being if he is to become partaker of the salvation acquired. The Christian religion seems to take two irreconcilable positions, the heterosoteric and the autosoteric, when it ascribes the acquisition of salvation entirely to Christ and nevertheless exhorts us to work out our salvation with fear and trembling; cf. von Hartmann, *Religionsphilosophie* I 569 f. There are therefore two rocks upon which the Christian order of salvation has always run the danger of shipwreck: antinomianism on the one side and Pelagianism on the other.

Pelagianism not only conflicts with the decrees of God, vol. II, 349–357, but also detracts from the person and work of Christ. In proportion as it expands the activity of the human being in the acquisition of salvation, it contracts that of Christ. For it is clear that if faith, conversion, and perseverance lie wholly or partly within the human being’s power and are his work, if the decision about actually being saved ultimately, when it comes down to it, lies in human hands, then Christ can at most have acquired only the possibility of being saved. He has then indeed opened the opportunity, but whether anyone, or few, or many, or all will make and continue to make use of that opportunity ultimately depends upon human beings themselves. God left them free and placed the decision in their hands. It then follows that Christ has in fact by no means accomplished everything, but that the principal thing—that is, what decides whether one is actually saved—must still be done by the human being. Christ thus descends from the unique place he occupies in the work of salvation; he comes to stand on one line with all those prophets and teachers through whom God has instructed and educated human beings. His work becomes related to, and inserted among, all those preparatory and pedagogical activities that God has bestowed upon the human race; the gospel of grace differs only in degree from the *lex naturae*. And the human being himself, although helped and supported by all that educative work of God, is summoned to self-activity. It depends upon him whether he will seize the opportunities God offers him and thus become partaker of salvation. The Pelagian conception of the order of salvation erases the specific difference between Christianity and the pagan religions, comprehends them all in one process, and can at most honor the Christian religion as first among its equals. It lapses into paganism and has salvation acquired by the human being’s own wisdom and strength. And in doing this it also undermines the assurance of faith. Paul testifies of the Gentiles that they are without Christ and therefore also without God and without hope in the world, Eph. 2:12. From works of the law there is no justification and also no assurance of salvation. The more closely someone examines himself and his good works, the more he comes to the sorrowful discovery that even his best works are imperfect and stained with sin. He must therefore content himself either with an appeal to God’s love, which overlooks what is lacking and accepts the will for the deed, or with the authority of church and priest, and surrender himself to false security; he never has or obtains certainty for himself. Indeed, because grace, insofar as it is granted and necessary to him, remains not only resistible but also always amissible, he is exposed at every moment to the danger of losing again what he has and falling away from the hope of salvation. From this standpoint no fixed course, no development of the Christian life, is possible. It is even wholly uncertain what the result of world history will be, whether a church and a kingdom of God will come. The government of the world, as regards the principal thing—that is, its eternal final destiny—rests in human hands. The errors of this rationalistic Pelagianism are plainly evident, but they are also present when it clothes itself in pietistic or Methodist garb. Pietism and Methodism, like so many other attempts at reformation of life in Protestant churches, were justified over against dead orthodoxy. They originally intended nothing other than to awaken sleeping Christendom; they did not wish to introduce a change into the confession of the Reformation but only to apply it in life. Yet in an understandable reaction they often went too far in this endeavor and swung to the opposite extreme. They too gradually shifted the center of gravity from the objective to the subjective work of salvation. In this it is essentially immaterial whether one makes salvation dependent upon faith and obedience or upon faith and experience. In both cases the human being himself moves to the foreground. Even though Pietism and Methodism did not deny the acquisition of salvation by Christ, they nevertheless left it unused and brought it into no organic connection with the application. It was, so to speak, dead capital. The official activity of the exalted Christ, the Lord from heaven, receded into the shadows behind the experiences and inward states of the subject. In Pietism, instead of being directed to Christ, the human being was directed to himself. He had to travel a long road, meet all kinds of demands and conditions, and test himself by many and varied marks before he might believe, appropriate Christ to himself, and be assured of his salvation. Methodism did indeed try to concentrate all this—conversion, faith, assurance—into one indivisible moment, but it systematized this method, though greatly abbreviated, in the same way as Pietism. In both there is a failure to recognize the activity of the Holy Spirit, the preparation of grace, and the connection between creation and re-creation. Therefore neither arrives at a genuinely Christian life. Whether this life withdraws pietistically from the world or enters the world aggressively in Methodist fashion, it is always something separate, something standing dualistically alongside natural life and therefore not working organically upon family and society and state, upon science and art. With or without the uniform of the Salvation Army, Christians are a peculiar kind of people who live not in but outside the world. The Reformation antithesis between sin and grace has more or less made way for the Roman Catholic antithesis between natural and supernatural. Puritanism has been exchanged for asceticism. The essence of sanctification lies in abstention from things indifferent.

On the other side stands antinomianism. Over against Pelagianism it defends an important truth which, precisely in order to overcome antinomianism, we must acknowledge not halfway but wholly and completely. It is true that Christ has accomplished everything and that the human being has nothing to add to his sacrifice for salvation and cannot contribute even the least thing. But antinomianism (not to be confused with antineonomianism proper in England and in the Netherlands) uses this truth only to gain entry for an entirely different doctrine. For most certainly, Christ has accomplished everything. But does that mean that not only we, but also Christ, have nothing more to do after he has suffered and died? Certainly not, for Christ was also raised and glorified; through and after his resurrection he was appointed Son of God in power, Lord from heaven, life-giving Spirit. In the state of exaltation much still remains for Christ to do. He must also apply and distribute to his church the salvation he acquired, and for that purpose he sent out his Spirit so that the Spirit would regenerate the whole church and lead it into all truth. Antinomianism fails to recognize this application of the work of salvation; in principle it denies the personality and activity of the Holy Spirit. At bottom, according to the law that two extremes meet, it agrees with Pelagianism. But precisely because it is inwardly driven by an interest other than that of Christ’s perfect sacrifice, it proceeds still further and also denies and opposes objective satisfaction. By his suffering and death Christ, namely, did not acquire eternal salvation but only made known God’s love; reconciliation and justification are already from eternity. As in Pelagianism, Christ here descends to the rank of a prophet and teacher. But while Pelagianism arrives at these errors through its deistic principle, antinomianism proceeds in principle from pantheism. It resembles as two drops of water the philosophy of Gnosticism, of Spinoza, and of Hegel. God is essentially one with the human being; he is reconciled from all eternity; wrath and justice are human conceptions. Only, on account of his finitude and limitation, the human being feels himself far from God; he thinks that God is far from him, that God is angry over sin and demands satisfaction. And that is a mistaken thought that the human being forms of God. God is eternal love, eternally reconciled, eternally one with the human being. The whole of redemption thus consists in this, that through the preaching of the prophets the human being is better informed and enlightened, that he puts off the delusion of God’s wrath and retributive justice, and that he acknowledges God as his Father and himself as God’s child. Nothing other than this illumination is necessary for redemption; in this it consists; it comprises nothing other than faith. No conversion, no contrition, no sorrow over sins, no dread of hell, no fear of judgment, no prayer for forgiveness, no sanctification, and no good works are necessary. These are all Pelagian errors that detract from the objective facts of God’s grace and reconciliation. At a low, legalistic standpoint one still feels the need of them, just as at that level one still has satisfaction effected through Christ’s sacrifice and speaks of God’s wrath and justice. But those are religious representations, symbolic forms of expression, which have their value for the common people but which at the standpoint of the πνευματικοι, the philosophers, make way for the pure idea and the adequate concept. Like Pelagianism, antinomianism too ends with a total failure to recognize the essence of Christianity, sinks back into paganism, and places redemption from sin in rationalistic enlightenment or moralistic improvement of the human being. Both deny, in Arian or in Sabellian form, the confession of the Trinity.
