## 2.

Preceded by John the Baptist, Jesus appears in the New Testament with the proclamation of the kingdom of heaven. On the one hand, this kingdom is presented as a treasure kept in the heavens and distributed as a reward to the righteous, Matt. 6:20; 13:43; 19:21; 25:46. In order to partake of it, another, better righteousness than that of the Pharisees is necessary, Matt. 5:20. It must be sought before all things, Matt. 6:33, and purchased at the cost of everything, 13:44–46; 19:21; Mark 9:43–47; 10:28, 29. But this conception passes over into another. The eschatological significance of the kingdom of God gives place to the religious-ethical. For work and reward here bear no proportion to each other. The kingdom of God in the eschatological sense is indeed called a reward, but in value it so far surpasses every work that every notion of reward falls away, Matt. 19:29; 20:13–15; 25:21; Mark 10:30; especially Luke 17:10. The righteousness required for entering the kingdom is itself a good of that kingdom, Matt. 6:33, as is also the forgiveness of sins, Matt. 26:28; Luke 1:77; 24:47, and so forth. And this kingdom with all its goods—forgiveness, righteousness, eternal life—is near and comes by God’s will, Mark 1:15; 9:1. It is a gift that he grants by grace, Matt. 25:34; Luke 12:32. And he grants it not to the righteous but to tax collectors and sinners, Matt. 9:13; to the lost, Matt. 18:11; to the poor, and so forth, Matt. 5; to little children, Matt. 18:3; Mark 10:15. The kingdom of heaven is already theirs here on earth, Matt. 9:15; 11:11; 13:16, 17; 23:13; Mark 10:15; Luke 17:21. Thus no righteousness of one’s own is necessary to partake of that kingdom, only repentance, μετανοια, _resipiscentia_, _mentis mutatio_, change of mind, and faith, πιστις—that is, accepting and trusting the gospel of the kingdom as a gift of God to the lost, Mark 1:15, and thus trust in God, Mark 11:22; in Jesus’ word and power, Matt. 8:10; 9:2; Mark 4:40; in Jesus’ person as Messiah, Matt. 27:42; Mark 9:42; John 1:12; 2:11; 6:29; 17:8; 20:30; Acts 9:22; 17:3; 18:5, and so forth. But this μετανοια and πιστις are themselves again gifts of God’s grace, Matt. 11:25, 27; 15:13; 16:17; Luke 10:22; John 6:44, 65; 12:32, so that only those who are of the truth, John 8:43, 47; 12:39; 18:37; who are given by the Father to the Son, 6:37 f.; 17:2, 6, 9; 10:26; 11:52; who are already regenerated, 1:12, 13; 8:47, come to faith.

In the apostolic proclamation all this is worked out much more broadly. The relation between the objective acquisition and subjective application of salvation then comes more clearly to light. When Jesus has died and been raised, it becomes clear to his disciples that the kingdom he proclaimed, with all its goods of forgiveness, righteousness, and eternal life, was acquired through his suffering and death; and that precisely for this he was raised and glorified by the Father, so that he might apply these benefits to his own. Application is inseparable from acquisition. It is one work assigned to the Mediator, and he will not rest until he can deliver the whole kingdom, completed, to the Father. Yet however indissolubly acquisition and application of salvation are connected, there is a distinction. Christ brought about the former on earth, in the state of humiliation, through his suffering and death. He accomplishes the latter from heaven, in the state of exaltation, through his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity at the Father’s right hand. Therefore he also exercises this application of salvation through the Holy Spirit. Through his exaltation he himself became Lord from heaven, life-giving Spirit, 1 Cor. 15:45, 47; 2 Cor. 3:17. The Spirit who had equipped him from his conception onward and had been poured out upon him more and more has now become the principle of his entire glorified life. He laid aside all natural, earthly life. He is now the Lord from heaven, the life-giving Spirit. The Spirit of God has become his Spirit, has become the Spirit of Christ. Connected with this is the word in John 7:39 that the Spirit was not yet, because Christ was not yet glorified. This cannot mean that the Holy Spirit did not yet exist or work before Christ’s glorification, for already in the Old Testament there was no power and no gift except through the Holy Spirit, vol. II, p. 230. Christ himself was anointed with that Spirit without measure, John 3:34; and Elizabeth and John the Baptist too are said to be filled with that Spirit, Luke 1:15, 41. The meaning can be no other than that although previously the Spirit also granted many gifts and worked many powers, only after Christ’s glorification did he personally come to dwell permanently in the church of believers as in his temple. It is with the Holy Spirit as with Christ himself. The Son of God was anointed from eternity as Mediator. He already worked as prophet, priest, and king in the days of the Old Testament. Yet he first became flesh in the fullness of time. So too there is a fullness of time for the Holy Spirit. When Christ completed his work, he not only himself became life-giving Spirit, but the Spirit of God, of Father and Son, also became fully the Spirit of Christ, whom he sends out, through whom he as Lord from heaven executes his work on earth, who takes his place, takes everything from what is his, glorifies him, and causes him himself to dwell spiritually in the church. For the Holy Spirit brings about not distance but the most intimate fellowship between Christ and his church. He is indeed distinct from Father and Son, ἀλλος παρακλητος, John 14:16, specially named alongside both, Matt. 28:19; 1 Cor. 12:4; 2 Cor. 13:13; Rev. 1:4. But he is also one with them in essence and can therefore cause believers to partake fully of their fellowship. His activity by no means consists only in communicating Christ’s benefits. If through his suffering and death Christ had acquired only the forgiveness of sins, it would be enough for the Holy Spirit to empower the proclamation of this gospel, John 15:26, 27; Acts 5:32; 1 Cor. 2:4; 2 Cor. 4:13; 1 Thess. 1:5, 6; 1 Pet. 1:12; convict the world of wrong, John 16:8–11; work faith in hearts, 1 Cor. 2:5; 12:3; Eph. 1:19, 20; 2:8; Col. 2:12; Phil. 1:29; 1 Thess. 2:13; and assure believers of their childship, Rom. 8:15, 16. But this objective, judicial benefit of forgiveness is not the only one. It is supplemented by the ethical and mystical benefit of sanctification. Christ not only takes away the guilt of sin but also breaks its power. One died for all so that those who live should no longer live for themselves but for Christ, 2 Cor. 5:15. In the satisfaction of the law, which is the power of sin—that is, in forgiveness—the power of sin is also broken in principle. Where there is righteousness, there is also life. Romans 3–5 is followed by Romans 6–8. Christ not only died, but also rose and was glorified. He is and remains the Lord from heaven, the life-giving Spirit, who not only died for the church but also dwells and works within it. This fellowship between Christ and the church is now brought about and maintained by the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit is therefore not only the one who works faith and assures of childship, but he is also author of a new life. And faith is not merely acceptance of a testimony of God but also the beginning and principle of a holy walk, 2 Cor. 5:17; Eph. 2:10; 4:24; Col. 3:9, 10. In and through the Spirit Christ himself comes to his own, John 14:18; lives in them, Rom. 8:9–11; 2 Cor. 13:5; Gal. 2:20; Eph. 3:17; Col. 3:11; just as conversely believers through that Spirit are, live, think, and act in Christ, John 17:21; Rom. 8:1, 9, 10; 12:5; 1 Cor. 1:30; 2 Cor. 5:17; Gal. 3:28; 5:25; Eph. 1:13; Col. 2:6, 10; παντα και ἐν πασιν Χριστος, Col. 3:11; cf. Deissmann, _Die neut. Formel ἐν Χρ. Ιησου_, 1892, who wrongly, however, always understands the formula in a local sense. And not only Christ but God himself too comes through that Spirit to make his dwelling in them and fills them with his fullness, so that in the end he may be all in all, John 14:23; 1 Cor. 3:16, 17; 6:19; 15:28; 2 Cor. 6:16; Eph. 2:22. And through fellowship with the person of Christ, the Holy Spirit also brings about fellowship with all his benefits: with his σοφια, 1 Cor. 2:6–10; δικαιοσυνη, 1 Cor. 6:11; ἁγιασμος, ibid.; Rom. 15:16; 2 Thess. 2:13; ἀπολυτρωσις, Rom. 8:2, 23. He assures believers of their childship, Rom. 8:14–17; Gal. 4:6, and of the love of God, Rom. 5:5. He frees them from the law and causes them to appear together in the world as one church, living by a principle of its own, standing under a head of its own, Acts 2; 2 Cor. 3; Gal. 4:21–6:10; above p. 217. He joins believers into one body, 1 Cor. 12:13; leads them to one Father, Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6; Eph. 2:18; brings all to the confession of Christ as Lord, 1 Cor. 12:3; makes them one in heart and soul, Acts 4:31, 32; Gal. 5:22; Phil. 2:1, 2; and causes them together to grow to a mature man in Christ, 1 Cor. 3:10–15; Eph. 4:1–16; Col. 2:19. He is the author of regeneration, John 3:5, 6; Titus 3:5; life, John 6:63; 7:38, 39; Rom. 8:2; 2 Cor. 3:6; illumination, John 14:17; 15:26; 16:13; 1 Cor. 2:6–16; 2 Cor. 3:12; 4:6; Eph. 1:17; 1 John 2:20; 4:6; 5:6; gifts, Rom. 12:3–8; 1 Cor. 12:4 f.; renewal and sanctification, Rom. 8; Gal. 5:16, 22; Eph. 3:16; sealing and glorification, Rom. 8:11, 23; 2 Cor. 1:22; 5:5; Eph. 1:13, 14; 4:30.
