## 3.

That faith in Christ was the way to salvation was naturally established in the Christian church from the beginning. Whoever does not believe in the blood of Christ is condemned, Ignatius, _Smyrn._ 6. We are justified not by ourselves, by our wisdom or piety or good works, but by faith, Clement, _1 Cor._ 32. Justin says that no one is saved except through the merits of Christ, who took the curse upon himself and made satisfaction for all, _Dial._ c. 95; who redeems all who repent and believe, ibid., c. 100; and he repeatedly speaks of a grace that precedes our works, enlightens us, and leads us to faith, _Dial._ c. 119; _Apol._ I 10. Irenaeus not only binds salvation to faith in Christ, for example, _adv. haer._ IV 2, 7; V 19, 1, but also says that the Holy Spirit was sent to work the will of the Father in human beings and to renew them, and that this Spirit is as necessary as rain and dew to make the land fruitful, III 17; cf. V 10, 2. Even Origen testifies that the human will is of itself incapable of turning to God, _nisi divino vel juvetur vel muniatur auxilio_. God is the _prima et praecipua causa operis_, _de princ._ III 1, 18; cf. 2, 5. The moral corruption of the human being and the necessity of the grace of the Holy Spirit are expressed even more strongly by the Latin fathers Tertullian, Cyprian, and Ambrose, whose statements Augustine therefore also invokes, _c. duas epist. Pelag._ IV 8–10. Thus the first says: _haec erit vis divinae gratiae, potentior utique natura, habens in nobis subjacentem sibi liberam arbitrii potestatem_, _de an._ 21. From Cyprian, _Test._ III 4, come the words repeatedly quoted by Augustine: _in nullo gloriandum quando nostrum nihil sit_. Ambrose already knows an inward grace that acts upon and prepares the will: _a Deo praeparatur voluntas hominum_; the fact that God is honored by the saints is God’s grace, _in Lucam_ I 10. Yet in the earliest period the doctrine of the application of salvation was developed very little and was in part already directed early on into wrong channels. Although here and there there are individual _testimonia veritatis evangelicae_, on the whole the gospel was soon understood as a new law. Faith and conversion were indeed generally regarded as the necessary way to salvation, but ultimately they depended on human freedom. Salvation had indeed been objectively acquired by Christ, but in order to partake of it, the free cooperation of the human being was necessary. As a rule, faith was nothing more than conviction of the truth of Christianity, and conversion soon acquired the character of a penance that made satisfaction for sins. Sins committed before baptism were indeed forgiven in baptism, but those committed after baptism had to be made good through penance. Although _poenitentia_ was also still frequently regarded as heartfelt sorrow over sin, the emphasis nevertheless fell increasingly upon the outward acts in which it had to manifest itself, such as prayer, fasting, giving alms, and so forth, and these good works were understood as a _satisfactio operis_. The whole of soteriology was externalized. What counted as the way of salvation was not the application of salvation by the Holy Spirit to the heart of the sinner, but the performance of so-called good, often entirely arbitrary works. Following Christ consisted in imitating and copying the life and suffering of Christ, which were vividly portrayed before the eyes; martyrs, ascetics, and monks were the best Christians. Cf., besides the works on the history of dogma by Münscher–von Coelln, Hagenbach, Schwane, Harnack, and so forth, Vossius, _Hist. Pelag._ 1. 3; Wiggers, _Aug. u. Pelag._ I 440 f.; Suicerus, s.v. πιστις, ἀναγεννησις, μετανοια; Landerer, _Das Verhältniss von Gnade und Freiheit in der Aneignung des Heils_, _Jahrb. f. d. Th._ 1857 S. 500–603; Wörter, _Die chr. Lehre über das Verhältniss von Gnade u. Freiheit von d. ap. Zeiten bis auf Aug._, Freiburg 1856; Dr. H. Wirth, _Der Verdienstbegriff in der Chr. Kirche. I Der Verdienstbegriff bei Tert._, Leipzig 1892.

Pelagius departed much further from the doctrine of grace than any of his predecessors; he abandoned the Christian foundation upon which they all still stood and revived the self-sufficient principle of paganism, particularly of Stoicism. For not only did he sever every connection between Adam’s sin and ours, so that neither guilt nor pollution nor even death was a consequence of the first transgression, but Christianity too lost its absolute significance: salvation was not bound to Christ but could also be obtained through the _lex naturae_ and the _lex positiva_. There could therefore be no question in Pelagius of a _gratia interna_, of a regenerating grace of the Holy Spirit that not only enlightened the understanding but also bent the will. He did indeed speak of grace, but by it he understood only: a) the _posse in natura_, the gift of the ability to will, which God granted to every human being, _gratia creans_; b) the objective grace of the preaching of the law or the gospel and of the example of Christ, which addressed the human understanding and instructed the human being concerning the way of salvation, _gratia illuminans_; and c) the forgiveness of sins and future salvation, which would be granted to the human being who believed and performed good works. Grace in the first-mentioned sense thus belonged to all human beings; grace in the second sense was not absolutely necessary but served only to make it easier for the human being to acquire salvation. It was not a _gratia operans_ but only an _adjutorium_ for the human being; it was also not granted to all, but only to those who had made themselves worthy of it by the good use of their natural powers; it was neither a _gratia praeparans (excitans)_ nor a _gratia irresistibilis_, which would rather be a _fatum sub nomine gratiae_; and finally it was not necessary and was not given by God for every good deed, _ad singulos actus_, but only for some. Many good works were performed by the human being without any grace, Wiggers I 220 f. Semi-Pelagianism moderated this system and taught that through Adam’s sin the human being had become not spiritually dead but nevertheless sick, that his freedom of the will was not lost but nevertheless weakened, and that the human being therefore needed the assistance of divine grace in order to do good and obtain salvation. But this grace, which enlightens the understanding and supports the will, may never be detached from the freedom of the will that remained in the human being, but must always be considered in connection with it. Grace and will work together in such a way that grace, according to God’s intention, is universal and intended for all, but actually benefits only those who make good use of their freedom of the will. _Nostrum est velle, Dei perficere._ Grace may sometimes precede, as in Paul’s case, but as a rule the will is first; the beginning of faith and perseverance in it are a matter of the will; grace is necessary only for the increase of faith; God helps those who help themselves. There is no _gratia operans, irresistibilis_, and even a _gratia praeveniens_ is as a rule denied, Wiggers, II 359 f.
