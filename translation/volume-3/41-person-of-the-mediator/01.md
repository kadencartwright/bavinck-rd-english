# § 41. The Person of the Mediator

## 1.

The covenant of grace is also distinguished from the covenant of works in that it has a mediator—not of union but of reconciliation—in order to restore the broken fellowship between God and the human being. Holy Scripture does not stand alone with this doctrine of mediation either, but is supported and confirmed on every side by the analogies of it that occur in the religions among the nations. In general, the words and deeds of great persons have the richest significance for the history and life of peoples; but this applies particularly in the religious domain. Nearly everywhere there are sacred persons who bring about and maintain fellowship with God for others. Prophets act as interpreters of the deity and make its will known. Priests represent human beings in their approach to God, convey their sacrifices and prayers, and distribute God’s blessing to them. Kings are frequently regarded as sons of the gods, as bearers of their wisdom and power. History does not tell us the origin of all these sacred persons; but their universality proves that here we are dealing with a phenomenon that is not accidental but is connected with the essence of religion itself and with a profound need of humanity. Many historical religions are moreover connected with the names of particular founders, who are later elevated above the rank of ordinary human beings or even, as in Buddhism, entirely deified. Apotheosis and incarnation occur in nearly all religions. The distinction Tiele makes between theocratic and theanthropic religions cannot, even by his own acknowledgment, be strictly maintained and is only a difference of degree, _Inleiding tot de godsdienstwet._, Amsterdam, 1897, pp. 141 f., 221 f. Finally, in all religions there is not merely a general expectation that evil will one day be conquered by good, but that expectation is repeatedly also connected with a particular person—for example, with Krishna in Indian religion and Balder in Norse religion. The myth of Heracles, Plato’s well-known statement about the righteous person in _Rep._ VII, and Virgil’s fourth eclogue are also remarkable in this respect. In its most beautiful and noble expressions, paganism unconsciously points forward to Christianity; cf. vol. I, pp. 238–240, and further Lamennais, _Essai sur l’indifférence_ III 408 s.; Tholuck, _Lehre v. d. Sünde_, 4th appendix; R. Ch. Trench, _The Hulsean Lectures for 1845, 1846_, 4th ed., Cambridge, 1859, p. 153: “Christ the Desire of All Nations, or the Unconscious Prophecies of Heathendom”; Pfleiderer, _Religionsphilos._³, 1896, p. 714 f.

But what among the pagans was no more than an unconscious and indefinite longing became in Israel the object of a firm faith and ardent hope. God gave his promises to that people. He did not allow humanity to begin its history without the hope that the woman’s seed would one day triumph over the serpent’s seed. Indeed, it was Christ himself who as Angel of the covenant guided God’s people in the Old Testament day, through Israel prepared his own coming in the flesh, and by his Spirit caused his own image to be drawn in history and prophecy, John 5:39; 1 Pet. 1:11; Rev. 19:10. The Old Testament therefore does not contain a few isolated messianic texts gathered atomistically in a Christology of the Old Testament. Rather, the entire Old Testament dispensation, with its persons and events, its offices and institutions, its laws and ceremonies, is a pointing and a movement toward fulfillment in the New Testament. There is a “_Symbolik der Schöpfung_,” a typology in nature that, as Jesus’ parables show, attains its realization in him and in his kingdom. There is an unconscious expectation and hope in the religion and history of the nations that comes to its truth in Christianity. There is a direct and intentional preparation for and foreshadowing of the λογικη λατρεια in the institutions and events of the Old Testament. Temple and altar, priest and sacrifice, Zion and Jerusalem, prophet and king—all are patterns and shadows of a higher, spiritual, true reality. And especially the kingship acquired such a typical significance under Israel. The theocratic king, embodied especially in David with his humble origin, rich experience of life, noble nature, deep feeling, poetic aptitude, great gifts, undaunted courage, and brilliant victories, Smend, _Altt. Rel. Gesch._ 58, was a son of God, 2 Sam. 7:14; Ps. 2:6, 7; Ps. 89:27; the anointed one _par excellence_, Pss. 2:2; 18:51, for whom all kinds of physical and spiritual blessings were desired, Pss. 2:8 f.; 21; 45; 72, and who was even addressed as Elohim, Ps. 45:7. The king is the bearer of the highest, the divine, dignity on earth. In David the theocratic king found his purest image; therefore the kingship will also remain in his house, 2 Sam. 7:8–16. This promise of God to David is then the foundation and center of all subsequent expectation and prophecy. Prophecy, which is added to typology as explanation, looks from the past and present toward the future and depicts the expected descendant of David ever more clearly in his person and work. The opinion, for example, of Paul Volz, _Die vorexilische Jahweprophetie und der Messias_, Göttingen, 1897, that the messianic idea is foreign to the preexilic prophets, is refuted by the facts and can be maintained only by arbitrary criticism. As the kingship in Israel and Judah corresponded less and less to its idea, prophecy took up the promise of 2 Sam. 7 and clung to it, Amos 9:11; Hos. 1:11; 3:5; Mic. 5:1, 2; Isa. 9:5, 6; 11:1, 2, 10; Jer. 23:5; 30:9; 33:17, 20–22, 26; Ezek. 34:23, 24; 37:22–24. This anointed king will come forth from David’s house when it has fallen into lowliness, been thrust from the throne, and become like a felled stump, Isa. 11:1, 2; Mic. 5:1, 2; Ezek. 17:22. God will cause him to spring up as a shoot for David’s house, Jer. 23:5, 6; 33:14–17, so that he even bears the name Branch, Zech. 3:8; 6:12. But despite his humble birth, he will nevertheless be the genuine, true, theocratic king. Although coming from little, despised Bethlehem, where the Davidic royal house has its origin and retreats when driven from rule, Mic. 5:2; cf. 3:12; 4:9, 14, the Messiah will nevertheless be a Ruler over Israel whose goings forth and origins as Ruler, from God, are from of old, from the days of eternity, vol. II, p. 243. He is given by God, is an eternal king, bears the names Wonderful Counselor, mighty God, cf. 10:21; Deut. 10:17; Jer. 32:18, everlasting Father (for his people), Prince of Peace, Isa. 9:5, 6. He is anointed with the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, counsel and might, knowledge and fear of the Lord, 11:5; is laid as a tested, precious cornerstone in Zion, 28:16; is a righteous, victorious, meek, and therefore donkey-riding king who does not take pride in his power but is upheld by God, Jer. 33:17, 20, 22, 26; Zech. 9:9 f.; whom the people call and acknowledge as YHWH our righteousness, Jer. 23:6; cf. 33:16, where Jerusalem is so called as the city in which YHWH causes his righteousness to dwell; who will be a hero like David and whose house will be like God, like the Angel of God who once went before Israel’s army at the exodus, Zech. 12:8; cf. Mal. 3:1; who will rule eternally, establish a kingdom of righteousness, peace, and prosperity, and extend his dominion over the Gentiles too, to the ends of the earth, Pss. 2; 45; 72; Ezek. 37:25; Zech. 6:13; 9:10, and so forth. This glorious image of the Messiah is then further completed by the fact that this future king is also depicted as prophet and priest. These features do not indeed come to the foreground, for in the kingdom of God the Spirit of God will be poured out upon all, Joel 2:28; Zech. 12:10; 13:2 f.; Jer. 31:34, and all the people will be priestly and holy to the Lord, Isa. 35:8; Joel 3:17; Zech. 14:20, 21. Nevertheless, the Messiah is also presented as a prophet upon whom the Spirit of the Lord rests in a special measure and who will bring good news to Israel and the Gentiles, Deut. 18:15; Isa. 11:2; Isa. 40–66; Mal. 4:5. And he will unite the priestly and royal dignity in himself, Jer. 30:21; Zech. 3; 6:13; Ps. 110. In the servant of the Lord in Isaiah, all three offices clearly come to light. He is priest, who through his suffering atones for the sins of his people; he is prophet, who, anointed with God’s Spirit, proclaims the acceptable year of the Lord; he is king, who is glorified and enjoys the fruit of his labor. How deeply this messianic expectation penetrated Israel is shown to us by the Psalms. Many proceed from the Davidic kingship and are messianic in the narrower sense, Pss. 2; 18; 20; 21; 45; 61; 72; 89; 132. Others speak only of God or YHWH as King, Pss. 10; 24; 29; 44; 47; 48; 66; 68; 87; 93; 95–100; 145–150.
