## 3.

This apostolic testimony concerning Jesus the Christ was too comprehensive to be immediately assimilated into Christian consciousness and expressed in a clear formula that excluded every error. Among the apostolic fathers there is therefore as yet no question of this, although they assign Christ an entirely unique place and call him by all kinds of glorious and exalted names, vol. II, p. 250. Through the errors of Ebionitism and Gnosticism arising on the left and right, Christian thought was first awakened to appropriate the apostolic testimony in its many-sidedness and to bring the relation of Christ both to God and to humanity into clarity. Ebionitism indeed regarded Jesus as the Messiah and sometimes also believed that he was conceived in a supernatural manner and equipped at his baptism with a divine power, but otherwise it saw in Jesus nothing but a human being from David’s line, anointed with God’s Spirit and appointed as king over an earthly kingdom to be established at his return. Gnosticism, despising matter and ascribing the creation of the world to a δημιουργος, also made a sharp separation in Christ between the divine and the human. The highest aeon, namely Christ, had descended from heaven and united himself for a time with the earthly man Jesus, or brought a psychic body with him from heaven, or temporarily assumed an apparent body in order to free humanity from the bonds of matter. Harnack indeed thinks that the acknowledgment of Jesus as a human being chosen by God and equipped with the Spirit was not Ebionite but Christian, _D. G._ I 245, and also says that the doctrine of the two natures of Christ was originally Gnostic, I 220, 516. Yet he must concede that in the oldest tradition Jesus was also confessed as the Son of God and was never regarded as a ψιλος ἀνθρωπος, I 153–168; cf. Loofs in Herzog³ 4, 18 f. There may have been different conceptions of how one and the same subject could simultaneously be Son of God and human being; but from the beginning Christ was acknowledged in this way by all. This confession had to lead, and under Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian did lead, cf. vol. II, pp. 250–256, to the doctrine of the two natures. The expression δυο οὐσιαι χριστου first occurs in a fragment of Melito whose authenticity is disputed, Harnack, _D. G._ I 165; wrongly, according to Loofs, Herzog³ 4, 31. Irenaeus does not yet have the formula of the two natures but clearly teaches that Christ truly is the Son, the Logos, and God himself; that as such he became human; and that this incarnate Logos is an indissoluble unity. He is _vere homo et vere deus_, _Adv. haer._ IV 6, 7. It is one and the same Christ who created the world and who was born and died, III 9, 3; 16, 6; 19, 1, and so forth. Tertullian teaches the same and speaks even more strongly of two substances in Christ, _carnea_ and _spiritalis_; of two _conditiones_, _divina et humana_, _De carne Chr._ 5; and assumed in him _duplicem statum, non confusum sed conjunctum in una persona, Deum et hominem verum_, _Adv. Pr._ 27. Soon afterward in the West the formula was used for this doctrine of Christ that he was _una persona_ with _duo naturae_. Augustine regularly expresses himself in this way: _ita vero inter Deum et homines mediator apparuit, ut in unitate personae copulans utramque naturam, et solita sublimaret insolitis et insolita solitis temperaret_, _Ep. ad Volus._ 3. In the East, however, the terminology, as in the doctrine of the Trinity, long remained unsettled and therefore liable to all kinds of misunderstanding. The words οὐσια, φυσις, ὑποστασις, προσωπον still lacked strict definition and were therefore used interchangeably. Even Cyril still frequently designates the human nature of Christ as ὑποστασις instead of φυσις and then nevertheless speaks again of one nature in Christ, μια σεσαρκωμενη φυσις. The union of both natures was designated by Gregory Nazianzen and Gregory of Nyssa as a μιξις, ἀνακρασις, and by Cyril as a ἑνωσις φυσικη or κατα φυσιν. And that which was brought about by the hypostatic union was repeatedly called not εἱς but ἑν, Schwane, _D. G._ II 294 f., 341 f. The _Epistola dogmatica_ of Pope Leo the Great to Flavian, however, contributed greatly to promoting clear insight and precise usage also in the East in the doctrine of Christ, Halm, _Bibl. der Symbole u. Glaubensregeln_³ 321–330. And at Chalcedon, after the rejection of Nestorianism, Patripassianism, Eutychianism, and so forth, the confession of Christ was established as of one and the same Son and Lord: τελειον τον αὐτον ἐν θεοτητι και τελειον τον αὐτον εν ανθρωποτητι, θεον αληθως και ἀνθρωπον αληθως τον αυτον,..... ἑνα και τον αὐτον χριστον, υἱον, κυριον, μονογενη, ἐν δυο φυσεσιν (according to the original reading; not ἐκ δυο φυσεων) ἀσυγχυτως, ἀτρεπτως, ἀδιαιρετως, ἀχωριστως γνωριζομενον, Hahn, ibid., 166.
