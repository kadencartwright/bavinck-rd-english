## 13.

Thus in Christ God and the human being are united with one another. Scripture does not speak in the language of later theology but materially contains the same thing confessed by the Christian church in its doctrine of the two natures. Pauline Christology, says Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 75, _ist allerdings ein erster Ansatz zur kirchlichen Lehre von der Doppelnatur_. For according to Scripture, the Word who was with God and himself God became flesh, ὁ λογος σαρξ ἐγενετο, John 1:14. He who was ἀπαυγασμα της δοξης και χαρακτηρ της ὑποστασεως του θεου partook of our flesh and blood and became like us in all things, Heb. 1:3; 2:14. God sent his own, only-begotten Son into the world, who was born of a woman, Gal. 4:4. He who existed ἐν μορφῃ θεου, ἑαυτον ἐκενωσε μορφην δουλου λαβων, Phil. 2:7. Though from the fathers κατα σαρκα, he is nevertheless ὁ ὠν ἐπι παντων θεος εὐλογητος εἰς τους αἰωνας, Rom. 9:5. Although the image of the invisible God, firstborn of all creation, Creator of all things, he is nevertheless also πρωτοτοκος ἐκ των νεκρων, Col. 1:13–18. David’s son, he is at the same time David’s Lord, Matt. 22:43. Walking about on earth, he is also always still εἰς τον κολπον του πατρος, John 1:18; ὁ ὠν ἐν τω οὐρανῳ, John 3:13; existing before Abraham, John 8:58. In a word, the fullness of deity dwells σωματικως in him, Col. 2:9. At every moment in Scripture divine and human predicates are ascribed to the same personal subject: divine and human being, omnipresence and limitation, eternity and time, creative omnipotence and creaturely weakness. What is all this other than the ecclesiastical doctrine of the two natures united in one person? Part of modern theology, however, feels a deep aversion to this doctrine of the two natures, finds it the summit of absurdity, and replaces it with _Doppelseitigkeit_, Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 65, 94; with _Doppelheit von Gesichtspunkten_, Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 129; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 513; with the doctrine of two successive states before and after the resurrection, Schultz, _Gottheit Christi_ 417; or with that of idea and appearance, Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ II 12, and so forth. It is clear that in these conceptions the teaching of Scripture concerning the person of Christ is not at all given its due. Although it is true that Christ, especially in Paul, first through his resurrection enters into full Sonship, Rom. 1:4; becomes Lord from heaven and life-giving Spirit, 1 Cor. 15:45; 2 Cor. 3:17; and receives a name above every name, Phil. 2:9, Paul thereby does not in the least deny that even before his incarnation Christ already had a personal, divine existence, 2 Cor. 8:9; Phil. 2:6; Col. 1:15–17, and was God’s own Son, Rom. 1:3; 8:32; Gal. 4:4. The doctrine of the two natures is something entirely different from the doctrine of two sides of, or two states in, the life of Christ. In Scripture Jesus bears all those glorious predicates not because on the one hand as a human being he dedicated himself perfectly to God and was therefore truly human, and on the other hand as that same human being perfectly revealed God’s love to us, Schultz, _Gottheit Christi_ 338, but because he truly was God and human in one person. Precisely according to the strict monotheism of Holy Scripture, a human being, however high he may stand in the religious and ethical sense, nevertheless cannot and may not bear those divine predicates that Scripture ascribes precisely to Christ. It accordingly becomes convincingly clear that those who replace the doctrine of the two natures with that of the two sides or the two states by no means can or dare speak of Christ as Scripture does. The names God, Son of God, Logos, Image of God, firstborn of all creation, and so forth are therefore all deprived of their scriptural meaning and understood in an entirely different sense. Modern theology, assuming in Jesus only a human nature, can honor him with the names Scripture ascribes to him neither according to his religious side as revelation of God’s love nor in the state of exaltation, in which he supposedly became Lord. Even if its exegesis were correct, it would give it not the slightest gain for its own Christology. For Christology no longer exists if Jesus is only a human being and not also the eternal and only Son of God.

In order to maintain all the data of Scripture concerning the person of Christ, theology gradually came to the doctrine of the two natures. It did not propose this by way of hypothesis, nor did it intend by it an explanation of the mystery of Christ’s person. In it theology only summarized, unimpaired and undiminished, the whole teaching of Scripture concerning Christ, and thereby maintained it against the errors that appeared on the left and right in Ebionitism and Gnosticism, Arianism and Apollinarianism, Nestorianism and Eutychianism, adoptionism and Monothelitism, and in more recent times in the teaching of Rothe and Dorner concerning the God-man who is becoming and in the teaching of Gess and others concerning kenosis. Nestorianism arose from the Antiochene school, had its preparation in Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia, and was then developed by Nestorius in such a way that the eternal, natural Son of God was distinguished from and another than the Son of David born of Mary. After all, there is a distinction between the temple and the one dwelling in it; between him who was in the form of God and him who walked about in the form of a servant; between the God and the human being in Christ, both of whom exist personally. Mary therefore cannot be called θεοτοκος, and the human being born from her is not the eternal Son of God but his adopted son (adoptionism). The union of God and human being in Christ is not natural but moral, like that between husband and wife in marriage; not a ἑνωσις but a συναφεια. God’s indwelling in Christ differs from that in believers not specifically but by degree, and it increases as the man Jesus advances in virtue and increasingly becomes organ and instrument, temple and garment of deity, as he becomes more θεοφορος; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ II 317 f. Related to this in the most recent period is Dorner’s doctrine, according to which the Logos gradually communicates himself more to the human being and grows more intimately together with him as the latter develops morally under his influence, _Chr. Gl._ § 102 f. This whole conception is condemned by Scripture. It ascribes all kinds of very different predicates to Christ, but always to one and the same subject, the one undivided “I” who dwells in him and speaks from him. In particular, it also says that the Logos did not dwell in a human being but became flesh, John 1:14. What someone has become, that he is. If the Son of God became human, then he himself is human. Many things can be predicated of one person, but never another person. It can be said of many human beings that they are one in one respect or another; but it can never be said of one person that he is the other person. Husband and wife are one flesh, but the husband is never the wife or conversely. Thus if the human being in Christ were another subject than the Logos, Scripture could never have said that the Logos became flesh and therefore is flesh. In Nestorius, therefore, the union between God and human being in Christ is not personal and natural but moral. They always remain two distinct subjects, however much they may increasingly become one morally. And even this moral union is not the point of departure but the final goal and result. It is not even, as in Origen, the fruit of the merit of the preexistent soul, which did not fall but acquired union with the Logos in its preexistence, _De princ._ II 6, 3; _C. Cels._ VI 47. Rather, it gradually comes about in the earthly life of Jesus and depends upon all kinds of conditions. It is distinguished only by degree from God’s union with other human beings. That is, Christ loses his entirely unique place. He is only a human being in whom God reveals himself more than in others. The idea of the God-man is lost and the work of redemption undermined. Nestorianism is akin to deism and Pelagianism.

Chalcedon therefore rightly declared that the union of the divine and human natures in Christ was ἀδιαιρετος and ἀχωριστος. But over against the other tendency it maintained just as firmly that it was ἀτρεπτος and ἀσυγχυτος. Against Nestorius, Cyril defended that the union in Christ was not moral but a ἑνωσις κατα φυσιν or καθ’ ὑποστασιν, a ἑνωσις φυσικη. But because he did not yet distinguish the words προσωπον, ὑποστασις, φυσις as clearly as later theologians, he hesitated to speak of δυο φυσεις in Christ and also said that Christ became one ἐκ δυο φυσεων, that he is μια φυσις, and that the divine and human natures are united in him εἰς ἑν τι, εἰς μιαν οὐσιαν. This could be misunderstood and was misunderstood by Eutyches, according to whom both natures, through and after the union, each lose their particular properties and are changed and fused into one new divine-human nature; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ II 351 f. The more recent doctrine of κενωσις is related to this Monophysitism. It was prepared by the Lutheran doctrine of the _communicatio idiomatum_, above p. 245; was further developed by Zinzendorf, Plitt, _Zinzendorfs Theologie_ II 166 f.; and then in this century, under the influence of the philosophy of Schelling and Hegel, which adopted the idea of becoming in God, was warmly defended by many as the only possible explanation of the person of Christ. It entails that at his incarnation the Logos divested himself of all his divine properties, including the immanent—or at least of his transient—properties, descended to the standpoint of a pure potency, and then from there in union with the human nature gradually developed once more into a divine-human person, above p. 252. But this theory is just as unacceptable as Dorner’s. Scripture knows only one person, one subject, one Christ, but nevertheless ascribes to him a twofold kind of properties, divine and human. He is Logos and became flesh; according to the flesh he is from Israel and nevertheless God above all, to be praised, and so forth, above p. 239. Whether, more in the manner of the past, one lets the human nature be changed into the divine; or, more in the manner of the present, lets the divine empty itself down to the human; or lets both natures wholly or in part flow together into a third, into a _mixtum quid_—in every case the boundary between God and the human being is erased in pantheistic fashion and the idea of the God-man is falsified. The Monophysite and kenotic doctrine conflicts with the immutability, with all the properties, with the being of God, and likewise also with the nature of the creature and of finite humanity. A deity or a divine property that is such only _potentia_ and not _actu_ is inconceivable. And a human being who through development can make the divine nature his own ceases to be a creature and passes from time into eternity, from finitude into infinity. Indeed, the whole thought of a God-man in whom the union of the two natures is replaced by their mingling is a _monstrum_ of which no one can form any conception. Such a one cannot be the Mediator between God and human beings because he is neither. He does not truly bring about the union, reconciliation, and fellowship of God and the human being because through his mingling of the two natures he is distinct from both and is a _tertium genus_.
