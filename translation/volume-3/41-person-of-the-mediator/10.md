## 10.

According to the teaching of Holy Scripture, this Son of God became human through conception by the Holy Spirit and birth from the virgin Mary. Supernatural conception was already denied in ancient times by the Jews, Eisenmenger, _Entd. Judenthum_ I 105 f.; by the Ebionites; by Cerinthus, Carpocrates, Celsus, cf. Moor III 722; by deists and rationalists in the previous century, such as Morgan and Chubb, cf. Bretschneider, _Entw._ 567; by more recent critics such as Strauss, Bruno Bauer, Renan, and others; and most recently by Harnack, _Das apost. Glaubensbekenntniss_, Berlin, 1892. His judgment that the words “conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary” formed no part of the original proclamation of the gospel occasioned a serious controversy, in which many aligned themselves with him, such as Achelis, _Zur Symbolfrage_, Berlin, 1892; Herrmann, _Worum handelt es sich in dem Streit um das Apostolikum?_, Leipzig, 1893; Hering, “Die dogm. Bedeutung und der relig. Werth der übernat. Geburt Christi,” _Zeits. f. Th. u. Kirche_ of Gottschick, 1895, pp. 58–91; Lobstein, _Die Lehre v. der übernat. Geburt Christi_, 2nd ed., 1896; while others resolutely opposed him, especially Wohlenberg, _Empfangen vom h. Geist, geboren von der Jungfrau Maria_, 1893; Cremer, _Zum Kampf um das Apostolikum_, Berlin, 1892; Theodor Zahn, _Das apost. Symbolum_, Erlangen and Leipzig, 1893. This controversy acquired still more importance through the discovery by Mrs. Lewis and her sister Mrs. Gibson of a Syriac translation of the Gospels in a palimpsest at Saint Catherine’s Monastery on Sinai in the summer of 1892, _The Four Gospels in Syriac, Transcribed from the Sinaitic Palimpsest_, by the late Robert L. Bensley and by J. Rendel Harris and by F. Crawford Burkitt, with an introduction by Agnes Smith Lewis, Cambridge, 1894. For Matt. 1:16 reads as follows in this Syriac translation: Joseph, to whom the virgin Mary was betrothed, begot Jesus, who is called Christ; cf. _Theol. Tijdschr._, May 1895, pp. 258 f.; _Gids_, July 1895, pp. 88–104. But even if this Syriac translator thereby denied the supernatural conception of Christ, this fact would stand entirely alone in the history of the gospel among the Syrians and would have remained without influence. Tatian’s Syriac Diatessaron, the Syriac gospel fragments published by Cureton in 1858, and all later Syriac editions of the Gospels clearly teach it. Further, in Matt. 1:16 the newly discovered translation calls Mary a virgin and betrothed to Joseph. Verse 18 reads the same as in our Greek text and thus teaches conception by the Holy Spirit, as does verse 20; and the same is the case with Luke 3:23. There is not the slightest proof that the Syriac translator intended by his text of Matt. 1:16 to deny conception by the Holy Spirit, Theodor Zahn, _Theol. Lit. Blatt_, 1895, col. 28. But further, supernatural conception by the Holy Spirit was certainly a component of the original proclamation of the gospel. The Old Roman Symbol, which certainly existed before the middle of the second century and probably already toward the end of the first century, already contained the words _qui natus est de Spiritu Sancto et Maria virgine_ (τον γεννηθεντα ἐκ πνευματος ἁγιου και Μαριας της παρθενου), later somewhat modified for clarification. The opponents of supernatural conception, including Cerinthus, a contemporary of John, never appealed to the claim that this doctrine had arisen later. In their time it must therefore have been part of the universal Christian faith. There is no ground whatsoever, and it is moreover strange, with Harnack to regard the heretics Cerinthus and others as the pure bearers and preservers of the faith of the first Christian church. Further still, this doctrine is found in the letters of Ignatius, _Ad Smyrn._ I 1–3; _Ad Eph._ VII 1–2, who died a martyr around the year 117, and in the recently discovered _Apology_ of the philosopher Aristides of Athens, which he presented to Emperor Hadrian in the year 125. It must all the more have been part of the apostolic proclamation because the pagan fables of sons of gods would otherwise certainly have deterred Christians from a doctrine that seemed so closely related to them. There is nowhere any trace of an influence of those pagan fables upon the origin of the gospel narrative of supernatural conception, as Usener, _Religionsgesch. Untersuchungen_, I, _Das Weihnachtsfest_, Bonn, 1880, p. 69 f., and Hillmann, “Die Kindheitsgesch. Jesu nach Lukas,” _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1891, p. 192 f., assumed. Harnack, _Theol. Lit. Z._, 1889, no. 8, therefore explains it solely from Jewish data, particularly from an incorrect exegesis of Isa. 7:14; cf. also Weiss, _Leben Jesu_ I 217 f. Finally, it indeed occurs directly in the New Testament only in Matthew and Luke, but the point at issue in these narratives of Matthew and Luke is the teaching of all evangelists and apostles. Jesus, first, is genealogically a son of David. He was universally regarded as such by the crowd that continually surrounded him and by all his disciples, Matt. 1:1, 20; 9:27; 12:23; 15:22; 20:30, 31; 21:9, 15; 22:42–45; Mark 10:47; 11:10; 12:35–37; Luke 1:27, 32, 69; 18:38, 39; 20:41–44; John 7:42; Acts 2:30; 13:23; Rom. 1:3; 9:5; 2 Tim. 2:8; Heb. 7:14; Rev. 3:7; 5:5; 22:16. Further, he is the Holy One, who never committed or knew any sin, Matt. 7:11; 11:29; 12:50; Mark 1:24; Luke 1:35; John 4:34; 6:38; 8:29, 46; 15:10; 17:4; Acts 3:14; 22:14; Rom. 5:12 f.; 1 Cor. 15:45; 2 Cor. 5:21; Heb. 4:15; 7:26; 1 Pet. 1:19; 2:21; 3:18; 1 John 2:1; 3:5. And finally, he who was born of Mary is the Son of God in an entirely unique sense, Matt. 1:23; 4:3; 8:29; 14:33; 16:16; 26:63; Mark 1:1; 3:11; 5:7; 14:61; 15:39; Luke 1:32, 35, and so forth. In order that this might be obtained—that a son of David, born from his line, should nevertheless be free from all sin and be God’s Son—supernatural conception was necessary. For this it occurred, and therefore it also has high religious and ethical significance. This is not the case, however—to dispose briefly of this point here—with the _theologoumenon_ that Mary remained a virgin during and after the birth. Mary’s virginity _in partu_ and _post partum_ is not yet encountered among the church fathers before Nicaea. Tertullian, _De carne Christi_ c. 23; Origen, homily 14 on Luke; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV 66, do not yet know the _virginitas in partu_, and Tertullian, _De carne Christi_ 7, also denies the _virginitas post partum_. The _virginitas in partu_ first occurs in the apocryphal _Gospel of James_ c. 19 and was also already known to Clement of Alexandria as the opinion of some, _Strom._ VII c. 16. But after Nicaea the _virginitas_ of Mary, both _in partu_ and _post partum_, in connection with her _Gottesmutterschaft_ (θεοτοκος, _deipara_), is taught ever more clearly by Epiphanius, Jerome, Gregory of Nyssa, Ephraim, Ambrose, Augustine, and others, and defended against the Apollinarians, Helvidius, Jovinian, and Bonosus; cf. Bellarmine, _De sacr. euch._ III c. 6; Petavius, _De incarn._ XIV c. 3 sq.; Lehner, _Die Marienverehrung_, p. 120 f. The fifth ecumenical council, can. 6, adopted from Epiphanius the title ἀειπαρθενος for Mary, and the Lateran Synod of 649 established in can. 3 her virginity also during and after the birth of Jesus; cf. _Cat. Rom._ I 4, 8. The _semper virgo_ was also included in _Art. Smalc._ I 4; _Form. Conc._ II 7, 100 and 8, 24; Zwingli, _Expos. Chr. fidei_ 5. Nevertheless, Lutherans and Reformed taught that the birth of Jesus took place in the ordinary manner and that Mary’s _virginitas post partum_, although plausible on grounds of piety, was no article of faith and in no case was assumed by Mary by way of a vow. Scripture does not teach in a single word that Mary gave birth to Jesus _utero clauso_. That she bound herself by vow to virginity cannot be derived from Gen. 3:15; Isa. 7:14; Luke 1:34, or from so-called types, Judg. 6:36; 11:29; Dan. 2:34; Ezek. 44:2. And that she later bore no more children is difficult to maintain over against Matt. 1:18, 25; 12:46; 13:55; Mark 3:31; 6:3; Luke 2:7; 8:19; John 2:12; 7:5; Acts 1:14; 1 Cor. 9:5. Cf. Calvin on Luke 1:34; Polanus, _Synt._ VI c. 17; Rivet, _Apol. pro S. Virgine Maria_, _Op._ III 601–744; Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._ II 4, c. 3; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIII 10; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 10, 12; Moor III 563, 716; Quenstedt, _Theol._ III 401.
