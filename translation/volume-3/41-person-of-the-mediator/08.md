## 8.

A third and final preparation of the incarnation is the history of revelation from paradise onward. The incarnation did not take place immediately after the fall; rather, many centuries elapsed from the first sin to the coming of Christ in the flesh. When Scripture speaks of the fullness of time, Eph. 1:10; Gal. 4:4, it points out that this was no accident or arbitrariness but was so determined by God in his wisdom. The incarnation first had to be prepared in preceding history through all kinds of means and along all kinds of ways. Just as the incarnation presupposes generation and creation, so now another presupposition and preparation is added, namely revelation. It is especially John in his prologue who brings to light for us this preparation of the incarnation in preceding history. Not only was the Logos in the beginning with God and himself God; and not only were all things made through him. But from the moment of creation this Logos also communicated his life and light to creatures. For in him was life, and the life was the light of human beings. Even after the fall this revelation did not cease. On the contrary, the light of that Logos shone in the darkness and enlightened every human being coming into the world. He revealed himself particularly in Israel, which he had chosen for himself as an inheritance and, as Angel of the Covenant, guided and blessed. He continually came to his own in theophany, prophecy, and miracle. Thus the Son prepared and made ready the entire world, Jews and Gentiles together, for his coming in the flesh. World and humanity, land and people, manger and stable, Bethlehem and Nazareth, parents and blood relatives, nature and environment, society and civilization—it is all a moment in the fullness of the times in which God sent his Son in the flesh. It was the Son himself who, immediately after the fall, as Logos and Angel of the Covenant made the world of Gentiles and Jews ready for his coming. He had been coming from the beginning of the ages and finally came into humanity for good and, through his incarnation, made his dwelling in it. Cf. Baldensperger, _Der Prolog. des vierten Evangeliums_, Freiburg: Mohr, 1898. The incarnation connects with preceding revelation, both general and special. It stands or falls with it. For if God was able to reveal himself as Scripture testifies concerning both the Gentile world and Israel, the possibility of incarnation is naturally enclosed in this; and if the latter were not possible, neither could the former be maintained. Revelation rests upon the same thought as incarnation—that is, upon the communicability of God, both within the being of God to the Son (generation) and outside the being of God to creatures (creation). This whole preparation of the incarnation in the preceding centuries now concentrates, as it were, and completes itself in the election and gracious endowment of Mary as the mother of Jesus. Mary is blessed among women. She received an honor that fell to no other creature. In unmerited grace bestowed upon her, she rises far above all human beings and angels. Rome has rightly maintained this; whoever denies it does not take the incarnation of God seriously. Only, without any ground, the Roman Catholic Church proceeded from this acknowledgment to the doctrine of the _immaculata conceptio B. Virginis_; see above, pp. 211 f. What guided it here and brought it to this is not the authority of Scripture or tradition. Neither is it the attempt thereby to explain and guarantee the sinlessness of Jesus, for this rests causally in his divine nature and instrumentally in conception by the Holy Spirit. Rather, the driving force for this dogma again lies for Rome in the hierarchical idea. The nearer a creature stands to God, the more it must also partake of his nature and of all his properties; the more it must share in θεωσις, in _deificatio_. Now no creature stands above Mary. She is united with God in the closest manner. She is θεοτοκος, _Deipara_; she carried God’s own Son beneath her heart; God himself dwelt in her. Whether there are proofs from Scripture and tradition is not the main point. The Roman Catholic cannot conceive the mother of the Savior otherwise than as above all the children of human beings and above all the angels of heaven, pleasing to God, pure and spotless. Was she not connected with the God-man, and thus also with the divine Person of the Word, with holiness itself, through the closest and tenderest relations? Did not God, through the very fact of her election to so high a dignity and so intimate a familiarity, most clearly show that he loved this pure virgin above all creatures? And therefore, apart even from the proofs from Scripture and tradition, is it not very _plausible_ that God endowed the holy virgin above all creatures with his gifts of grace? The Savior’s honor demanded that his chosen mother remain free from every trace and even shadow of sin. It is _fitting_ that a creature who had to associate so intimately with God, who stood in so intimate and tender a relation to him, should remain preserved from the stain of the least sin. It is _fitting_ that the house of the Lord was holy. Thus Bensdorp in _De Katholiek_ CXII, 1897, pp. 429, 445, 447. The doctrine of the immaculate conception and the sinlessness of Mary fits within the hierarchical system of the Roman Catholic church and theology, and therefore was gradually adopted within it. Even the doctrine of her assumption is a question of time. Consequently, the predicates ascribed to Mary increase more and more in number and quality. She is daughter of the Father, bride of the Son, temple and organ of the Holy Spirit, _complementum_, _Ergänzung der Dreieinigkeit_, _die instrumentale und theilweise die meritorische Ursache unserer ewigen Auserwählung_, the principal reason for natural and supernatural creation, the wise worker of every miracle, the almighty ruler of the church equipped with unlimited dominion, as God rules the world, and so forth. See, for example, Wörnhart, _Maria die wunderbare Mutter Gottes und der Menschen_, Innsbruck, 1890, pp. 13, 19, 244, 289; also Petavius, _De incarn._ XIV; Scheeben, _Dogm._ 69, 93; a Jesuit, Salazar, in Rivet, _Op._ III 681. Mariolatry in Rome entirely displaces true Christian worship of God. Professor Schoeler, _Das vatik. Bild_, Gütersloh, 1898, sees this superstition typically expressed in a mural at the Vatican that depicts the Madonna placed high in the center, while Father and Son are seated at the right and left as instruments of her almighty will. The Reformation opposed this deification of a human being. It would not be surprising if, from fear of such idolatry, it had not always shown Mary the honor due to her. But that is not the case either, although it was naturally cautious in its praise. Among all Protestants who confess the incarnation of the Word, Mary too stands in high honor. She was chosen and prepared by God to be the mother of his Son. She is the favored one among women. She was desired by Christ himself as his mother, who conceived him by the Holy Spirit, carried him beneath her heart, nursed him at her breast, instructed him in the Scriptures—in whom, in a word, the preparation of the incarnation was completed.
