## 6.

The doctrine of Christ is not the point of departure but is indeed the center of the whole of dogmatics. All other dogmas prepare for it or are derived from it. In it, as the heart of dogmatics, the whole religious-ethical life of Christendom beats. It is the μυστηριον εὐσεβειας, 1 Tim. 3:16. From this every doctrine of Christ must proceed. But if Christ is the incarnate Word, then the incarnation is also the central fact of the whole history of the world; then it must have been prepared from the times of the ages and continue to operate afterward and throughout all eternity. Schelling rightly says that it must be difficult for someone, _einer Persönlichkeit, die ihm nicht eher als von dem Augenblick an bekannt ist, wo sie in menschlicher Gestalt erschienen ist, die für ihn eine bloss historische ist, es muss ihm ungemein schwer fallen, einer solchen Persönlichkeit nachher eine vormenschliche, ja vorweltliche Existenz zuzuschreiben; er wird sich natürlich geneigt finden, diess nur als eine Vorstellung anzusehen, mit der im weiteren Fortgang die Person des grossen Religionsstifters umgeben und verherrlicht worden_, _Werke_ II 4, p. 35. This becomes entirely different when, like every fact of revelation, the person of Christ too is considered first of all theologically. In the first place, the incarnation then has its presupposition and foundation in the trinitarian being of God. In deism and pantheism there is no place for an incarnation of God. In the former, God is separated from world and human being in an abstract manner; in the latter, God loses himself in the creatures and has no being and life of his own. It is therefore entirely natural when from this standpoint the incarnation is rejected as absurd. The Socinians openly acknowledged this and made their exegesis subservient to this _dictum rationis_, _Cat. Rac._, qu. 98, 111. They called the incarnation a human phantom and a monstrous dogma and considered it more possible that a human being should become an ass than that God should become human, Fock, _Der Socin._ 525. Spinoza likewise said that the incarnation of God was as absurd as that _circulus naturam quadrati induerit_, _Ep._ 21; cf. also Bretschneider II 195; Wegscheider § 128; Schleiermacher § 96; Strauss II 153, and so forth. Only the theistic and trinitarian confession of God’s being opens the possibility for the fact of the incarnation. Here God remains who he is and can nevertheless communicate himself to others. If, according to Vinet’s saying, one must first possess oneself in order to give oneself, then absolute love can be conceived only as the perfection of a triune divine being. Only then is there a distinction between essence and person, and therefore fellowship of the human being, through the person, with the being of God, without that being being identified with or flowing over into the human being. In a word, the Trinity makes it possible for there to be a mediator who himself partakes of both the divine and the human nature and thus joins God and the human being to each other. However far the theosophy of Böhme and Schelling therefore went astray when it tried to derive the incarnation from the being of God, nevertheless the trinitarian being is the presupposition and condition of the incarnation of God, Dorner, _Gl._ II 394.

It is therefore also important to maintain that not the divine nature as such but specifically the Person of the Son became human. The Patripassianism of Praxeas, Hermogenes, Noetus, Beron, Beryllus, Sabellius, cf. M. Vitringa V 52; Dorner, _Entw._ I 518, was condemned by the church in every age, for example at the Synod of Aquileia, and no longer occurs in this form. But in its fundamental thought it belongs to all pantheistic systems, especially those of Hegel, Schelling, von Hartmann, and others, which conceive the absolute not as being but as becoming and cause the divine to pour itself out and become finite in the world. The world and humanity with all its sorrow and misery are then a moment in God’s life; the history of revelation is then the history of God’s suffering; cf. also A. von Oettingen, _Das göttliche noch nicht_, Leipzig, 1895. Although, as will shortly appear, an element of truth may be acknowledged in this, Holy Scripture nevertheless always ascribes the incarnation to the Son, John 1:14; Phil. 2:6; Heb. 2:14, 15. The Reformed even preferred to say that the person of the Son became human rather than, with the Lutherans, that the divine nature in Christ became human. By this they did not wish to deny that the fullness of deity dwelt bodily in Christ, Col. 2:9, that the Son partakes of the same divine being with the Father and the Spirit, and thus that in this sense the divine nature too had assumed our flesh. But over against every mingling they nevertheless emphasized that the person of the Son, in whom the divine nature existed in a proper manner, had assumed the human nature. The difference is certainly not of great significance, as Mastricht V 4, 15; Moor III 480, observe. Yet it is noteworthy that many Lutherans prefer to express themselves by saying that the divine nature in the person of the Son became human; this is undoubtedly connected with their fundamental thought. The Reformed, however, preferred the expression that the person of the Son was united with the human nature immediately, and the divine nature in him mediately, Zanchi VIII 47; Polanus VI c. 13; _Synopsis_ XXV 8; Alting, _Loci Comm._, p. 74; Maresius, _Syst._ IX 12; M. Vitringa V 51, 63. So too it had formerly been taught by the fathers and confessed by the church. The 6th Synod at Toledo, 638, declared: although the whole Trinity cooperates in the incarnation, since all works of the Trinity are _inseparabilia_, _solus (Filius) tamen accepit hominem in singularitate personae, non in unitate divinae naturae, in id quod est proprium Filii, quod non commune trinitati_, Hahn, _Bibl. d. Symbole u. Glaubensregeln_³ 237; cf. also Anselm in Dorner II 376; Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 5; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 3, art. 4; Bellarmine, _Contr._ I, lib. 3, c. 8; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ III 1, c. 4, qu. 1, and so forth. Questions such as were treated in scholasticism on _Sent._ III, dist. 1—whether the Father and the Holy Spirit too could have become human—therefore need not detain us. The Father could not be sent, for he is first in order and exists from himself. The Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son, follows him, and is sent by him. But the Son is the one designated for the incarnation. In the divine being he occupies the place between Father and Spirit, is Son and Image of God by nature, was already Mediator in the first creation, and as Son could restore us to be children of God, Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 1; Thomas, _S. Th._ III, qu. 3, art. 8; Petavius, _De incarn._ II 15; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorz._ III 180 f.; Turretin, _Th. El._ XIII, qu. 4; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 266; Dorner, _Gl._ II 394. Yet although the incarnation subjectively and terminatively is proper to the Son alone, _originaliter, principiative, quoad efficientiam_ it is nevertheless a work of the whole Trinity. Christ is sent by the Father and conceived by the Holy Spirit. Reformed theology already gave expression to this in its doctrine of the _pactum salutis_. The entire work of re-creation is not merely a decree of God; it rests upon the free, conscious counsel of the three persons. It is an _opus personale_, not _naturale_. The Father is eternally in the Son the Father of his children; the Son is eternally their Surety and Mediator; the Holy Spirit is eternally their Comforter. The foundations of the covenant of grace were laid not first after the fall, not even first at creation, but in eternity. And the incarnation is not an accidental decree that arose later; it was purposed and determined from eternity. There was no time when the Son was not. Neither was there a time when the Son did not know and was not prepared to assume human nature from Adam’s fallen race. The incarnation was prepared from eternity. It rests not in the being but in the persons. It is not a necessity as in pantheism, but neither is it arbitrariness or accident as in Pelagianism.
