## 14.

For the present, if theology wishes truly to be scriptural and Christian theology, it can do no better than maintain the doctrine of the two natures. In doing so, it may deeply impress upon itself the deficiency that clings to its language, particularly also in the doctrine of Christ. But all other attempts thus far made to formulate the Christological dogma and bring it nearer to our consciousness detract from the richness of Scripture and the honor of Christ. And theology must guard these in the first place. Whatever objections have been brought against the doctrine of the two natures, earlier and later, it nevertheless has this advantage: it neglects none of the data of Holy Scripture, maintains Christ’s name as the only Mediator between God and human beings, and in the end also gives the clearest and most lucid conception of the mystery of the incarnation. For this incarnation does not stand isolated in history. It is indeed essentially distinguished from all facts and occupies an entirely unique place, but it nevertheless stands in close connection with everything that took place before, alongside, and after it. As appeared earlier, it has generation, creation, revelation, and inspiration as its preparation and presupposition. Finally, it must still be added here that it also stands in connection with the essence of religion. Religion is fellowship with God. Without it a human being cannot be a true, complete human being. The image of God is no _donum superadditum_ but belongs to his nature. This fellowship with God is a _unio mystica_. It far exceeds our comprehension. It is a most intimate union with God through the Holy Spirit. It is a _unio personarum_, an indissoluble and eternal covenant of God and the human being that is described far too weakly by the designation _ethical_ and is therefore designated as mystical. It is so intimate that it changes the human being according to God’s image and causes him to partake of the divine nature, 2 Cor. 3:18; Gal. 2:20; 2 Pet. 1:4. If this fellowship of God and human being is now truly understood, not as an imagination but as true reality, then its kinship and analogy with the incarnation is evident. Whoever truly confesses generation, creation, immanence, revelation, inspiration, religion—that is, whoever confesses God’s communicability—has in principle also acknowledged incarnation. Not in the sense that the incarnation would flow naturally from the essence of religion, but in this sense: acknowledgment of the one removes every right to deny the other. For if incarnation is impossible either from God’s side or from the human being’s, then religion too cannot truly consist in fellowship between God and the human being. But religion in this sense was disrupted by sin. There is no true, blessed fellowship between God and the human being. Therefore the union established in Christ between divine and human natures had to bear an entirely peculiar character. It could not be identical with the religious relation between God and the human being, for it had precisely to be once more the beginning, the principle, the objective realization of true religion. A covenant cannot be improvised; it must be made with a people in its king or representative. And so too, in order to make his fellowship with humanity a reality again, God united himself with it in Christ as its Head. Therefore Christ is not an individual alongside others but Head and Representative of humanity, the second and last Adam, the Mediator between God and human beings. The union of divine and human natures in Christ is therefore essentially distinguished from God’s indwelling in his creatures and in believers. It is not a _unio personarum_ but a _unio personalis et substantialis_. It is no moral union that finds its image in marriage, no agreement of disposition and will, no fellowship of love alone. Rather, it is a ἑνωσις φυσικη, as Athanasius, _C. Apoll._ I 8, and Cyril, _Anathem._ 3, called it. This does not indicate that the union was necessary and flowed naturally from one or both natures. Rather, it is so called because it is not moral in character but is a union of the natures in the person of the Son, a _unio naturarum_, not _naturalis_ but _personalis_, Petavius, _De incarn._ III 4; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIII 6, 3. And the result of this union is neither a new nature nor a new personality, but only the person of Christ as Christ. He who was in the form of God from now on also existed in the form of a human being.

By virtue of its entirely unique nature, this union cannot be conceived otherwise than as a union of the person of the Son with an impersonal human nature. For if the human nature in Christ had an independent personal existence, no union other than a moral one would have been possible. Then Christ would not be personally God either, but merely a human being living in close fellowship with God. Then too Christ would have been a particular human being with an individuality of his own, but not the second Adam, head of the human race. He had to be not an individual alongside others. His work did not consist in bringing one single human being, with whom he united himself, back into fellowship with God. Rather, he had to assume the seed of Abraham, be Head of a new humanity and firstborn among many brothers. For this he had to assume an impersonal human nature. This is not to be understood with Anastasius of Sinai and others as though Christ assumed human nature in its universality, in the sense of the Platonic idea, for this does indeed exist in humanity but not by itself, in a numerical unity, Schwane, _D. G._ II 369. On the contrary, the human nature in Christ was certainly individual to this extent: through definite properties he was thereby distinguished from all human beings, although he partook of the same nature. Yet Christ is not therefore an individual alongside others, for the human nature in him had no independent personal existence alongside the Logos but was from the beginning so prepared by the Holy Spirit for union with the Logos and for his work that in that Logos it could represent the whole human race and be the Mediator of God for all human beings of every family, station, and age, of every century and place. Cf. Gregory of Nyssa, Hilary, Basil, Ephraim, Apollinaris, and others in Dorner, _Entw._ I 958 f.; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ III 3, 6, 11; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 4, art. 4; Petavius, _De incarn._ V 5; Moor III 776; Pictet, _Christ. Godg._ I 552; Schneckenburger, _Zur kirchl. Christol._ 74 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 405; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 295.

It is precisely this distinction between nature and person, however, that encounters the greatest objection both in the Trinity and in the doctrine of Christ, and is therefore also the cause of most errors in both doctrines. Neither Nestorius nor Eutyches could allow this distinction, and therefore inferred from the two natures to two persons or from the one person to one nature. Christian theology, however, was led to this important distinction in the doctrine of God and of Christ. In God there was one nature and three persons; in Christ, one person and two natures. The unity of the three persons in the divine being is in the fullest sense _naturalis_, συνουσιωδης, _coessentialis_. The unity of the two natures in Christ is _personalis_. This distinction is also philosophically justified. Nature and person are not related, as in Hegel’s philosophy, as essence and appearance, as _potentia_ and _actus_, so that nature—essentially one in all creatures—develops by immanent power into person in the human being. Rather, nature is the substratum, the _suppositum_, that by which something is what it is, _principium quo_. And person is the subject, not of a nature in general but of a rational nature, _individua substantia rationalis naturae_, _principium quod_. Person is that which exists in and for itself, which is owner, possessor, and lord of the nature, a _complementum existentiae_, _sustentans et terminans existentiam naturae_; the subject that through the nature with all its rich content lives, thinks, wills, acts, and so forth; through which the nature becomes something existing for itself and is not an accident of another, Schwane, _D. G._ II 369 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. d. Vorzeit_ III 71 f.; Schaezler, _Das Dogma v. d. Menschw. Gottes_ 3 f. Now in this manner the human nature in Christ is united with the person of the Son. The Son does not first become person in and through the human nature, for he was person from eternity. He needed neither creation nor incarnation to come to himself and become personality, spirit. But incarnation does entail that the human nature formed in and from Mary did not exist for a single moment in and for itself but from the very first moment of conception was united with and taken up into the person of the Son. The Son created it into himself: _in se ipso creavit et creando assumsit_. Yet this human nature is not therefore incomplete, as Nestorius and even now Dorner, _Entw._ II 1225, claims. For although it was not enclosed within itself by a personality and egohood of its own, it was nevertheless personal from the beginning in the Logos, who as subject in and through it, with all its constituent parts, faculties, and powers, lived, thought, willed, acted, suffered, died, and so forth. That it did not receive an independent, separate _complementum existentiae_ in a self-subsistent egohood flowed not from a defect but precisely from the fact that it had to be the human mode of existence of the Logos. _Naturae assumptae non deest propria personalitas propter defectum alicujus, quod ad perfectionem humanae naturae pertineat, sed propter additionem alicujus, quod est supra humanam naturam, quod est unio ad divinam personam_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 4, art. 2 ad 2.

Therefore the human nature in Christ is not coordinated with the Logos through a personality of its own but is subordinated to him. Both natures are and remain ἀλλο και ἀλλο, but not ἀλλος και ἀλλος. It is always the same person, the same subject, the same “I,” who lives and thinks, speaks and acts through the divine and the human nature. The human nature is the tabernacle in which the Son came to dwell; the garment that he himself prepared and put on; the μορφη in which he appeared to us; the instrument and organ that he sanctified for himself and that he employs with divine wisdom for his office and work. _In Christo humanitas quasi quoddam organum divinitatis censetur_, Thomas, _Comp. Theol._ c. 212; cf. _S. Theol._ III, qu. 19, art. 1; Petavius, _De incarn._ IV 8, 6; Turretin, _Theol._ XIII 6, 7. There is nothing in this that deserves our contradiction. Without thereby detracting from the creature’s own activity, we confess that the whole world is an instrument of God’s revelation, a garment in which he simultaneously reveals and conceals himself from us. The church and every believer desire nothing higher than to stand in God’s service with soul and body and all their powers and to be an instrument in his hand. What objection could there then be to the human nature in Christ being, in a much richer measure, in the absolute sense, the glorious, willing organ of his deity? The union of the divine and human natures in Christ rises far above all our speaking and thinking. Every comparison fails us, for it is without any parallel. But then it is also the μυστηριον εὐσεβειας into which the angels desire to look and which the church adoringly admires.
