## 4.

Thereby the boundaries were sharply and clearly drawn within which the ecclesiastical doctrine of Christ could be further developed. But it was far from the case that agreement was thereby obtained, either earlier or later. The question, “What do you think of the Christ?” has elicited all kinds of answers in Christian life and also in theology. Throughout all the ages it keeps the minds and hearts of human beings divided. Even within the churches that together accept the Chalcedonian creed, despite all agreement there is still considerable difference. In the theology of the East the fundamental thought remained that God himself had to become human so that the human being might partake of the divine nature, immortality, eternal life, θεωσις. The consequences of sin are much more physical than ethical in nature, and therefore mysticism always runs alongside rationalism in the Greek church. This mysticism had to lead to the result that, although Christ’s human nature was indeed acknowledged, the strongest emphasis nevertheless fell upon his deity, upon the entrance of the divine nature into the human, upon the unity of the two, upon the essence of Christ more than upon his historical appearance, upon his incarnation more than upon his satisfaction. In the person of Christ, therefore, what mattered especially was his divine being, which was communicated in human form and so received and enjoyed by the human being. Accordingly, there was least of all a felt need for a strict distinction between the two natures. It is lacking in Athanasius and the Cappadocians, and even still in part in Cyril. The Greek reading of the Chalcedonian creed is a correction of the original text; the expression ἐν δυο φυσεσιν was replaced by ἐκ δυο φυσεων. Monophysitism was a power in the East. However much it was condemned and suppressed, it nevertheless continually resurfaced in the doctrine of the Theopaschites, the Aphthartodocetists, the Actistetes, and the Monothelites. It contributed to erasing the boundary between the divine and the human and to making the churches in the East increasingly ripe for perishing in paganism; cf. vol. II, pp. 292, 293.

The West, by contrast, distinguished strictly between Christ’s divine and human natures. The terminology of _una persona_ and _duo naturae_ had been settled since Tertullian. Emphasis fell more upon the ethical than upon the physical, more upon satisfaction, suffering, and death than upon incarnation. Yet here too not every danger of deifying Christ’s human nature was avoided. The East took almost no notice of the West and in particular did not undergo the influence of Augustine at all. But conversely, the West did adopt the theology and particularly the mysticism of the East. Through this the thought of a deification of the human also penetrated the Latin church and theology. Mystical contemplation, the doctrine of the _donum superadditum_, and transubstantiation all rest upon the thought that the finite can participate in the infinite. This could not remain without influence upon Christology, for the human nature was united with deity more closely than any other creature. If Adam through the _donum superadditum_, believers through _gratia infusa_, and mystics through contemplation already partook of the divine nature and were in a certain sense deified, cf. vol. I, pp. 220 f., 276 f.; II, pp. 152 f., 518, 522 f., then this had to be the case in much stronger measure still with the man Christ. Entirely in agreement with John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ III 3, 7, 17, 19; cf. Dorner, _Lehre v. d. Person Christi_ II 267 f., scholastic and Roman Catholic theology therefore taught that each nature in Christ indeed remains itself and that the communication of divine properties to the human nature is not to be conceived as real, but that the divine nature nevertheless wholly penetrates and suffuses the human, as heat does iron, and causes it to partake of divine glory, wisdom, and power (περιχωρησις, θεωσις). From this it is then inferred that Christ as a human being already possessed the _scientia beata_, the _visio Dei_, here on earth. Jesus was already on earth a _comprehensor ac viator_, walking by sight and not by faith. In his case there can and may be no question of faith and hope. Further, all the gifts of which Christ’s human nature was capable were bestowed on him not gradually but immediately, all at once at his incarnation. As a human being he indeed remained finite and limited. In the state of his humiliation he also had all kinds of defects (susceptibility to suffering and death) and affections (feelings of sorrow, hunger, cold, and so forth). But at his incarnation he nevertheless received all at once all the wisdom of which his human nature was capable. His growth in it, Luke 2:52, is to be understood not objectively but subjectively: it appeared so to others. He also prayed not from need but only for our sake, to give us an example. Jesus was really never a child; he was immediately a man. And because his human nature has been so glorified and deified by the divine, it too has a right to and is an object of adoration. Indeed, every part of Christ’s human nature, especially the Sacred Heart, is worthy of divine veneration. Cf. Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 9, 13 sq., 23; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 7–15, 25, 33, 34; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ IV 6; Bellarmine, _De Christo_ III, IV; Petavius, _De incarn._ X, XI; _Theol. Wirceb._ IV 260 sq.; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 1–261; Kleutgen, _Theol. d. Vorz._ III 213 f.; Pesch, _Prael._ IV 86 sq., 111 sq.; Jansen, _Prael._ II 669; C. von Schaezler, _Das Dogma v. d. Menschw. Gottes_, 1870, p. 164 f.; Simar, _Dogm._ 420 f.

We encounter the same fundamental thought in Lutheran theology. Here, however, it is worked out and applied differently. Greek and Roman Catholic theology indeed taught a communication of divine gifts, but not of divine properties, to the human nature. It also taught a true and essential communication of the divine properties, but then not to the human nature as such, rather to the hypostasis of both natures. Luther, however, taught that even in the state of humiliation Christ’s humanity was where the deity was and that both natures were united and mingled not only in the person but also mutually with their properties, _vereinigt und vermischt_, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 392 f. Roman Catholic theologians could therefore unanimously oppose Lutheran Christology and especially the doctrine of ubiquity, Bellarmine, _De Christo_ III 9; Petavius, _De incarn._ X 7–10, and so forth. Yet here, as in the doctrine of the Lord’s Supper, there is an affinity. The Lutherans expressly teach that the two natures in Christ are never mingled or changed into one another but that each remains itself eternally, retains its essential properties, and never receives the properties of the other nature as its own, _Form. Conc._, ed. Müller, 675, 676. Neither do they say that all divine properties are communicated in the same sense and measure to the human nature. The quiescent properties of infinity and so forth were not themselves bestowed on it directly, but only through the mediation of the other properties. The operative properties, however, such as omnipresence, omnipotence, and omniscience, became the portion of the human nature directly and immediately. Indeed, it was frequently ascribed not merely _multivolipraesentia_ or _ubiquitas_ in Chemnitz’s sense but specifically _omnipraesentia_, ibid., 685 sq. And materially Roman Catholic and Lutheran theology agree in this: they elevate the human nature above the limits set for it and dissolve Christ’s human development, together with the state of his humiliation, into appearance. In Lutheran theology this has come out very clearly. At the incarnation a distinction was made, not temporally but nevertheless logically, between the _incarnatio_ (_assumtio carnis_) and the _exinanitio_ (_conceptio in utero_). Of the former, the Logos alone is the subject; and it consists in his making the human nature, finite in itself, capable of the indwelling of the fullness of deity and communicating to it the divine properties named above. But in this way, not only the distinction between divine and human nature but also that between the state of humiliation and the state of exaltation threatened to disappear entirely. It was therefore assumed that in the second moment, in the _exinanitio_, of which not the Logos but the God-man was the subject, he had in a certain sense laid aside again the properties first communicated. But there was great disagreement over the nature of this _exinanitio_, and even a protracted controversy was conducted between the Giessen and Tübingen theologians (1607–1624). According to the Tübingen theologians, Christ laid aside only the _public_ use of those properties. He indeed retained them and also used them, for the distinction between _potentia_ and _actus_ does not apply to the divine properties. A potential omnipresence, omniscience, and so forth is absurd. But in the state of his humiliation Christ used these communicated properties only in a latent, hidden manner (κρυψις χρησεως). The state of exaltation is nothing other than a visible display of what already existed invisibly from the hour of conception onward. In this way, however, Christ’s whole human development—his growth in knowledge and wisdom, his hunger and thirst, his suffering and death—became mere appearance. The Giessen and later Lutheran theologians therefore preferred to say that at the moment of the _exinanitio_ Christ had laid aside every use of the communicated properties (κενωσις χρησεως). He indeed continued to retain them, but only _potentia_, not _actu_; only at his exaltation did he also take them into use. Cf. Chemnitz, _De duabus naturis in Christo_, 1570; Gerhard, _Loc._ IV c. 11–14; Quenstedt III 79 sq.; Hollaz 765 sq.; Buddeus, _Inst. theol._ IV c. 2; and further Dorner, _Entw._ II 771–847; _Gesch. der prot. Theol._ 569 f.; Frank, _Theol. der Concordienformel_ III 165–396; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ § 26; idem, _Zur kirchl. Christologie. Die orthodoxe Lehre vom doppelten Stande Christi nach luth. und ref. Fassung_, 1868; Sartorius, _Christi Person und Werk_ I³ 520 f.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV 1, p. 243 f.; Herzog¹, article “Doppelter Stand Christi”; Herzog², article “Kenotiker und Kryptiker”; Herzog³, article “Comm. idiom.”

From the beginning the Reformed were in a much more favorable position. They overcame in principle the Greek, Roman Catholic, and Lutheran mingling of the divine and human in the doctrine of Christ too. Although maintaining the unity of the person most strictly, they also applied to Christ’s human nature the principle _finitum non est capax infiniti_ and upheld it not only in the state of humiliation but even in that of exaltation. Thus Reformed theology made room for a purely human development of Christ, for a successive communication of gifts, and for an essential distinction between humiliation and exaltation. Yet in doing so it seriously avoided the Nestorianism of which it was always accused. And it did so because in Greek, Roman Catholic, and Lutheran theology the emphasis always fell upon the incarnation of the divine being, of the divine _nature_. If that nature does not become flesh, the work of salvation, fellowship with God, appears to be endangered. But Reformed theology places in the foreground that the _person_ of the Son became flesh. Not the substance but the _subsistentia_ of the Son assumed our nature. In that person the unity of the two natures, despite their strict distinction, is immovably established. As in the doctrine of the Trinity, of the human being as the image of God, and of the covenants, so here too in the doctrine of Christ the Reformed thought of personal, conscious life as the richest and highest life comes very clearly to the foreground.
