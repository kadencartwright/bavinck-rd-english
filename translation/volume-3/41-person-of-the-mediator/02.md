## 2.

Even after prophecy had died out, the messianic expectation remained alive in the heart of Israel’s people. In the apocryphal literature we do find the expectation of Israel’s future deliverance and dominion, but apart from a few indications the Messiah does not occur in it, 1 Macc. 2:57; 4:46; 9:27; 14:41. Philo too has nothing about the Messiah. In general, the self-righteousness of Judaism was unfavorable to the messianic expectation; after all, Israel had the law, was righteous by keeping it, and therefore had no need of a deliverer. At most there was room for an earthly king who would recompense the Jews according to their merit and bring them to dominion over the peoples of the world. The Messiah became solely a political person. But the messianic expectation nevertheless remained alive among the people and repeatedly came to the fore, especially in times of oppression. It was maintained and nourished by the reading of the Old Testament; numerous passages were interpreted messianically, as the LXX proves. The Jews even found 456 messianic promises in the Scriptures: 75 in the Pentateuch, 243 in the Prophets, and 138 in the Hagiographa. Above all, the apocalyptic literature of Enoch, the _Psalterium Salomonis_, Baruch, the Sibyl, and 4 Ezra took up the messianic expectation again and elaborated it. It was generally expected that he would appear at the end of the עֹלָם הַזֶּה, after times of distress, the so-called messianic woes, and Elijah or another prophet such as Moses or Jeremiah had preceded him. He was usually designated Messiah, Son of Man, Elect One, and Son of David, a few times also Son of God, and was conceived as a human being who had already existed beforehand and was hidden with God, who would emerge from Bethlehem, was righteous, holy, and equipped by God with many gifts, and would establish the kingdom of God on earth. In its main features this agrees with the expectation found in popular circles according to the testimony of the New Testament, Luke 1:38, 74; 2:25, and so forth. Schürer, _Neutest. Zeitgesch._ 613; Weber, _System_ 333; Baldensperger, _Das Selbstbewusstsein Jesu im Lichte der Mess. Hoffnungen seiner Zeit_, Strassburg, 1888, 2nd ed., 1892; _Herzog_² 9, 653; Marti, _Gesch. d. isr. Rel._ 289; Holtzmann, _Lehrb. der neut. Theol._ I 68; Ludwig Paul, _Die Vorstellungen vom Messias u. vom Gottesreich bei den Synopt._, Bonn, 1895.

In the midst of these expectations Christ himself appears, and in his preaching he immediately attaches himself to them. The kingdom of God that was predicted and expected by the prophets, in which God is king and his will shall be everyone’s delight, which by origin and nature is a heavenly kingdom and is even now present in the heavens, Matt. 6:10—that kingdom also comes to earth; it is near, Mark 1:15. But while building upon those expectations, Jesus immediately introduces a great modification into them. From Jewish tradition he goes back to Scripture, and by the kingdom he understands not primarily a political but a religious-ethical dominion. The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, Mark 12:26, the God of Israel, Matt. 15:30, whom Jesus acknowledges and confesses as his God, is certainly also and above all King, Matt. 5:35; 18:23; 22:2, the Lord of heaven and earth, Matt. 11:25, but he is also the Father in heaven who wishes to rule in his kingdom as a father over his children. His kingdom is also a family, a congregation, Matt. 6:4, 6, 9; 7:11; Mark 3:34, 35; and these two thoughts of the kingship and fatherhood of God do not impair but promote each other. Further, there is no entrance into that kingdom through Pharisaic law-keeping but through repentance, faith, and regeneration, Matt. 18:3; Mark 1:15; John 3:3, and therefore it is open precisely to the poor, the lost, the tax collectors, and sinners, Matt. 5:3; 9:11, 12; 11:5, 28–30; Luke 19:10. That kingdom, which on the one hand must be sought before all else and presupposes a righteousness other and better than that of the Pharisees, Matt. 5:20; 6:33; 13:44–46, and is then presented as a reward kept in the heavens, Matt. 5:12; 6:20; 19:21; 20:1–7; 24:45, is nevertheless on the other hand, with its entire content—forgiveness of sins, Matt. 9:2; 26:28; Luke 1:77; 24:47; righteousness, Matt. 6:33; eternal life, Matt. 19:16; 25:46; Mark 8:43—a gift that far surpasses all work and merit, Matt. 19:29; 23:12; 24:47; 25:21; 25:34; Luke 6:32 f.; 12:32, 37; 17:10; 22:29. Insofar as salvation is not yet fully enjoyed here, the kingdom is indeed still future; but insofar as it is already initially planted in hearts here through regeneration, forgiveness, and renewal, it is present, Matt. 11:11; 12:28; 23:13; Mark 4:26–29; 10:15; Luke 10:18; 17:21. Schmoller, _Die Lehre vom Reiche Gottes in den Schriften des N. T._, Leiden, 1891, and others, such as Joh. Weiss and Gunkel, have wrongly denied the latter. For Jesus, the kingdom of God is not only a good that lies ready in heaven and is bestowed as a reward upon righteousness, and thus only a religious concept; but it is also initially realized on earth in the benefits of repentance, faith, regeneration, and renewal. It gradually grows and permeates everything; it is at the same time an ethical concept. That is, Jesus adopts the concept of the kingdom of God as it was developed in an eschatological sense in Scripture and especially later in apocalyptic literature. But he connects with it the thought, later neglected by Judaism, that although the kingdom of God in the eschatological sense will first be realized at the end of days by an act of God intervening in the world, it must nevertheless be preceded and prepared by a religious-ethical renewal, by the kingdom of God in this sense. Among the prophets of the Old Testament these thoughts go together and are interwoven. They know only one coming of the Messiah. The kingdom of God is the sum of all spiritual and natural benefits; it brings at the same time repentance and return (the restoration of Israel as a people and kingdom); it is at once an ethical and a religious concept. But Jesus distinguishes between these. The kingdom is present in a religious-ethical sense; it comes in an eschatological sense. The one idea of the kingdom of God is realized in two great moments. The one coming of the Messiah divides into a twofold coming, for salvation and for judgment, for preparation and for completion. _Das Messiaswerk wird Heilswerk, es entwindet sich der Eschatologie und mündet ein in die Soteriologie_, Baldensperger, _Das Selbstbew. Jesu_ 114. Leaving aside the question of how much time, in the consciousness of Jesus and that of the apostles, would elapse between his present and his future kingdom, the fact is established that the two are also temporally distinct. Cf. Holtzmann I 215–225, and further the literature on the kingdom of God found in Frédéric Krop, _La pensée de Jésus sur le royaume de Dieu d’après les évangiles synoptiques avec un appendice sur la question du fils de l’homme_, Paris: Fischbacher, 1897, p. 7 s. Cf. also _A. Ritschls Idee des Reiches Gottes im Lichte der Gesch., kritisch untersucht von Dr. Rich. Wegener_, Leipzig: Deichert, 1898.

Jesus did not arrive at this distinction because his work, begun hopefully in Galilee, later remained without result and he could then remain faithful to his calling in no other way than by revealing his messiahship and the program of suffering at the same time, Holtzmann, I 284. For from the beginning Jesus’ place in that kingdom, whose gospel he preached, was perfectly clear and distinct to him. Apart from the indication in the baptism by John, Matt. 3:11 f.; John 1:26 f., this is clearly evident from the fact that Jesus appeared at once with the names Son of Man and Son of God. Jesus—as is now quite generally acknowledged, especially since Baldensperger’s important study on _Das Selbstbewusstsein Jesu_, Strassburg, 1888, 2nd ed., 1892—consciously derived the former name from Dan. 7:13, in order to indicate by it both that he was the Messiah, without whom the kingdom of God could not come, and that he was the Messiah in an entirely different sense than his contemporaries imagined in their earthly-minded expectations. Thus the name Son of Man is not a symbol for the future kingdom of God (Hoekstra), nor a designation of Jesus as the true, ideal human being (Herder, Schleiermacher, Neander, Lange, Ebrard, Thomasius, Godet, Beyschlag, and so forth), or as the humble, weak human being (Grotius, de Wette, Ewald, Baur, Strauss, Kuenen, Schenkel, Stier, Nösgen, and so forth), but specifically designates his messianic dignity, exalted above all, in the sense in which he himself understood it. Now, just as earlier the rationalist Paulus, _Comm. über das N. T._ on Matt. 8:20, and Uloth in the _Godg. Bijdragen_ 1862, so in most recent times Lagarde, Wellhausen, Brandt, Oort, and especially Lietzmann have claimed that Jesus never called himself בר נוש in Aramaic, or used it only to designate himself in the third person as the human being; that the Aramaic words were later wrongly translated by ὑιος του ἀνθρωπου, were understood in Christian apocalyptic literature, in connection with Dan. 7:13, as referring to the Messiah, and in that meaning were then put into Jesus’ mouth. But this hypothesis is very improbable. It lacks any explanation of when and why the Aramaic expression was rendered in this way in Greek, was understood in a messianic sense, and without any reason was put into Jesus’ mouth; cf. Schulze, _Die Religion Jesu und der Glaube an Christus_, Halle, 1897, pp. 7–13; Fréd. Krop, _La pensée de Jésus sur le royaume de Dieu_, 124 s.; Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_, Leipzig, 1898, p. 78. Rather, it is probable that the expression בר נוש already designated the Messiah in Jewish apocalyptic literature, or that Jesus himself intentionally borrowed this title from Daniel in order to designate himself clearly as the Messiah and yet in a different sense from what his contemporaries thought. In any case, Jesus calls himself this where through his humble life, Matt. 8:20; 11:19; through his service, Matt. 20:28; through his seeking and saving of the lost, Matt. 18:11; through his forgiveness of sins, Mark 2:10; through his power over the Sabbath, Mark 2:18; through his suffering, Mark 8:31; 9:12, 31; 10:33; and through his return, Mark 13:26; 14:62, he establishes, extends, and completes the kingdom of heaven on earth. Jesus did not first appear with this claim and this name toward the end of his life. He was conscious of his messiahship from the first moment of his public activity and began that activity by virtue of this consciousness. Already at the age of twelve he knew that he had to be in his Father’s affairs, Luke 2:49. In the baptism by John he received the divine sign and seal of his calling, Luke 3:21. And he immediately appeared with the name Son of Man, long before the event at Caesarea Philippi, Mark 2:10, 28. From the beginning he assigns himself a special and altogether unique place in the kingdom of God, performs works that presuppose his messiahship, and demands an honor that belongs to him only if he is the Messiah, Matt. 5:11; 10:18, 32, 37; 12:6, 41; 19:29. But it is true that he uses the name Son of Man sparingly in the first period, and that he employs it all the more frequently when, after the event at Caesarea, he can connect the program of suffering with his messiahship. Jesus had to train his disciples so that they acknowledged him as Messiah and yet did not transfer to him all those earthly, political expectations connected with the messianic idea in Jesus’ time. Literature on the expression Son of Man in Oort, _De uitdrukking ὁ υἱος του ἀνθρωπου in het N. T._, Leiden, 1892, p. 5 f.; H. Lietzmann, _Der Menschenson_, Freiburg: Mohr, 1896; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 246; H. Appel, _Die Selbstbezeichnung Jesu, Der Sohn des M._, Stavenhagen, 1896. See also _Theol. Tijdschr._, Nov. 1894; May 1895; _Theol. Stud. van Dr. Daubanton_, and so forth, May 1895; Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_, 1898, p. 75 f.; Fréderic Krop, _La pensée de Jésus_, and so forth, pp. 118–132. This means that Jesus’ messianic self-consciousness cannot be explained historically or psychologically. It is present immediately when Jesus appears; it cannot be derived from the influence of apocalyptic literature, which Baldensperger undoubtedly overestimates both in general and also in relation to Jesus. He therefore also finds himself compelled to go farther, namely, to Jesus’ religious consciousness, to his God-consciousness, and to say that at the baptism his messiahship became immediately conscious to him together with his God-consciousness. Then he perceived God’s nearness as never before; then he heard inwardly within himself the voice: “You are my Son,” Baldensperger, _Das Selbstbew. Jesu_, p. 160. To a certain extent this is correct. Jesus’ consciousness that he was the Messiah flowed from the knowledge that he stood in an altogether unique relation to God. He called himself Son of Man but also Son of God; cf. vol. II, 243. In the Old Testament first the people of Israel, then the king and especially the Messiah were designated in this way. The name Son of God perhaps still has this theocratic meaning in the mouths of the demon-possessed, Matt. 8:29; the Jews, Matt. 27:10; the high priest, Matt. 26:63; and even the disciples in the first period, John 1:50; 11:27; Matt. 16:16. But Jesus puts another and deeper sense into this name. He is Son of God not because he is Messiah and King, but he is the latter because he is the former, because he is Son of the Father. God is his Father, Luke 2:49; he is the one Son whom the Father loved and whom he sent at last, Mark 12:6. At the baptism, Matt. 3:17, and later at the transfiguration, Matt. 17:5, God calls him his beloved Son, in whom he has all his good pleasure; and in Matt. 11:27 Jesus says that everything necessary for carrying out God’s εὐδοκια has been handed over to him and that only the Father knows the Son and the Son the Father. This Sonship is the source of all his life, thought, and action. In this consciousness he places himself above the ancients, Matt. 5:18 f.; above Jonah and Solomon, Luke 11:31, 32; even above the angels, Mark 13:32. Knowing that he stands in an altogether unique relation to the Father and is king of the kingdom of God, he pronounces people blessed, Matt. 5:3 f.; Luke 10:23; forgives sins, Mark 2:20; demands that everything be abandoned for his sake, Matt. 5:11; 10:18, 22, and so forth, and connects entrance into eternal life with this. The Synoptics already contain in germ everything later taught by the apostles and also by the Christian church concerning the person of Christ. It is true that before Jesus’ resurrection the disciples did not yet have a correct understanding of his person and work. The Gospels themselves tell us this. Hence Jesus in his teaching also took account of the capacity of his disciples, gradually trained them in the knowledge of his Sonship and his messiahship, and left much to the instruction of the Spirit, John 16:12. But the resurrection already caused a wondrous light to dawn upon the person and work of Christ. From then on he was regarded by all the disciples as _ein himmlisches Wesen_; the teaching of Paul and John concerning the being of Christ encountered no opposition from any of the disciples, Weiszäcker, _Das apost. Zeitalter_² 16, 110. What they add to it is nothing new, but only expansion and development. Jesus is truly human, become flesh and come in the flesh, John 1:14; 1 John 4:2, 3; from the fathers as far as the flesh is concerned, Rom. 9:5; Abraham’s seed, Gal. 3:16; from the tribe of Judah, Heb. 7:14; from David’s line, Rom. 1:3; born of a woman, Gal. 4:4; Heb. 2:14; human in the full, true sense, Rom. 5:15; 1 Cor. 15:45; 1 Tim. 2:15; who was tired, thirsty, sorrowful, and joyful as we are, John 4:6 f.; 11:33, 38; 12:27; 13:21; Heb. 4:15; who was under the law, Gal. 4:4; learned obedience unto death, Phil. 2:8; Heb. 5:8; 10:7, 9; suffered, died, and was buried, and so forth. But this same human being was at the same time free from all sin, Matt. 7:11; 11:29; 12:50; John 4:34; 8:29, 46; 15:10; Acts 3:14; 2 Cor. 5:21; Heb. 4:15; 7:26; 1 Pet. 1:19; 2:26; 1 John 2:1; 3:5. He has also risen, been glorified, and is seated at God’s right hand, Acts 2:34; 5:31; 7:55, and so forth. He already existed before his incarnation, John 1:1; 17:5; 1 Cor. 10:4, 9; Heb. 11:26; was then in the form of God, Phil. 2:6; the firstborn of all creation, Col. 1:15; higher than the angels, Heb. 1:4; the one through whom God created everything and in whom everything holds together, John 1:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; Col. 1:16; Eph. 3:9; Son of God in an altogether unique sense, John 1:14; 5:18; Rom. 8:3, 32; Gal. 4:4; and himself God, John 1:1; 20:28; Rom. 9:5; 2 Thess. 1:1; Titus 2:13; Heb. 1:8, 9; (1 John 5:20); 2 Pet. 1:1. Cf. the articles “Christologie” and “Schriftlehre” by Kähler in _Herzog_³ 4, 4, with the literature cited there.
