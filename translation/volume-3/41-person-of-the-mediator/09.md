## 9.

Yet although in his incarnation Christ connects himself with the preceding revelation and prepared his own coming through nature and history, he is no product of the past, no fruit of Israel or of humanity. To a certain extent it is true of every human being that he cannot be explained completely from his parents and environment. Therefore Kuenen, _G. v. I._ II 506 f.; _Volksgodsd. en Wereldgodsd._ 158, 193, after indicating the conditions and materials for Christianity, could also acknowledge that the person of Christ had not thereby been comprehended. But this applies to Christ in a different and higher sense than Kuenen intended. For according to Scripture, in Christ that Word became flesh which in the beginning was with God and was itself God. At all times and from all sides this deity of Christ has been denied and opposed. But 1º Scripture teaches nothing else; see above, pp. 231, 238. As Ch. de la Saussaye once said, we are accustomed to the superlatives of Holy Scripture and often no longer understand the force of its expressions. But if a human being spoke of himself as Jesus consistently did, and if others honored a human being as prophets and apostles honor Christ, everyone would regard it as insane fanaticism or dreadful blasphemy. Not in a single passage but repeatedly, Scripture attributes to Christ a personal, eternal preexistence, John 1:1; 8:58; 17:5; Rom. 8:3; 2 Cor. 8:9; Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:6; divine Sonship in a supernatural sense, Matt. 3:17; 11:27; 28:19; John 1:14; 5:18; Rom. 8:32; the creation and preservation of all things, John 1:3; 1 Cor. 8:6; Eph. 3:9; Col. 1:16, 17; Heb. 1:3; Rev. 3:14; the securing of all salvation and blessedness for each and every one, Matt. 1:21; 18:11; John 1:4, 16; 14:6; Acts 4:12; 1 Cor. 1:30; kingship in the church, Matt. 3:2; 5:11; 10:32, 37; John 18:37; 1 Cor. 11:3; Eph. 1:22; Col. 1:18; dominion over all things, Matt. 11:27; 28:18; John 3:35; 17:2; Acts 2:33; 1 Cor. 15:27; Eph. 1:20–22; Phil. 2:9; Col. 2:10; Heb. 2:8; and judgment over the living and the dead, John 5:27; Acts 10:42; 17:31; Rom. 14:10; 2 Cor. 5:10. It directly and unambiguously calls him by the name God, John 1:1; 20:28; Rom. 9:5; 2 Thess. 1:12; Titus 2:13; 2 Pet. 1:1; Heb. 1:8, 9; cf. Cremer, s.v. θεος. 2º To be sure, every opposition to the deity of Christ begins by appealing to Scripture against the confession. But this illusion lasts only a very short time. Impartial exegesis soon shows that the church's doctrine has far more ground in Scripture than was originally expected. One then finds oneself compelled to go back again from the Christ of apostolic proclamation to the Jesus of the Synoptics and to criticize the latter until all the supernatural has disappeared from him. The deity of Christ can then be declared a fruit of profound theological or philosophical speculation, originally wholly foreign to the church; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 522; Schultz, _Gottheit Christi_ 417 f., 438, 468. This, however, always lasts only as long as one values and has an interest in presenting one's own faith as original, pure Christianity. As soon as that standpoint is abandoned, impartiality regains its voice and vindicates the ecclesiastical confession. _Negari nequit, in dictis eorum (scriptorum s.) semina quaedam doctrinae ecclesiasticae vere inesse_; Wegscheider § 128. _Es ist unleugbar, dass auch dasjenige, was die Kirchenlehre über die göttliche Natur Christi lehrt, Stützpunkte im N. T. besitzt_; Nitzsch, ibid., 518, 521; cf. also Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 353, 418; Baldensperger, _Der Prolog. des vierten Ev._ 4 f. With this, the explanation of this dogma from later theological or philosophical speculation again collapses. 3º As the study of Scripture does, so also research into the history of dogma repeatedly sets the seal of truth upon the ecclesiastical confession of Christ's deity. The development of christological dogma displays a logical course that in the end is observed and acknowledged by every investigator; v. Hartmann, _Die Krisis des Christ._ 6. Simple was the faith with which the church entered the world. It knew but one thing: that in Christ God himself had come to it and received it into his communion. That stood firm; it did not allow this to be taken from it; it defended this against every kind of opposition and formulated it clearly and distinctly in its confession. In the doctrine of Christ's deity it maintained the absolute character of the Christian religion and the reality of its communion with God. In Christianity Christ occupies an entirely different place than Buddha, Zarathustra, and Mohammed do in their religions. _Christus ist nicht der Lehrer, nicht der Stifter, er ist der Inhalt des Christenthums_; Schelling, _Werke_ II 4, p. 35; cf. v. Hartmann, _Die Krisis des Christ._ 1880, p. 1; Strauss, _Dogm._ II 174. Therefore, judged by the teaching of Scripture and the faith of the church, in the end men such as Irenaeus, Athanasius, and Augustine are repeatedly vindicated over against their opponents. Everyone counts agreement with them an honor; no one likes to be named after Arius, Pelagius, or Socinus. 4º It is clear that the Christian religion, that is, true communion between God and man, can be maintained only by confessing the deity of Christ. For if Christ is not truly God, then he is only a human being. And however high he may then be placed, neither in his person nor in his work can he be the content and object of Christian faith. Whether Christ himself stood in unbroken communion with God (Schleiermacher), was the first to express the unity of God and man (Hegel), perfectly realized sonship with God in himself (Lipsius), or revealed God's love and founded the kingdom of God (Ritschl), now that Christianity exists, it is nevertheless independent of him. He is its founder, his historical significance remains great, and his example continues to exert influence, but he himself stands outside the essence of Christianity. Arian christology, the moral christology of rationalism, Kant's symbolic christology, Hegel's ideal christology, de Wette's aesthetic christology, and Feuerbach's anthropological christology—none of them leaves a place for Christ in dogmatics. _Wird die Persönlichkeit Christi als eine menschliche, wenn auch noch so sehr idealisirte, festgehalten, so kann nicht sie selbst als Persönlichkeit die erlösende Kraft für den Gläubigen sein_; v. Hartmann, ibid., 15. 5º Ritschl and his followers nevertheless still try to retain for Jesus, though he is only a human being, the title God, because for the church he occupies the place of God and possesses the value of God. Earlier the Socinians attempted this in the same way. They opposed the deity of Christ as strongly as possible and said that in those few passages of Scripture where Christ was called God, such as John 1:1; 20:28; Rom. 9:5, he was so called on account of his rank, dignity, and dominion, to which he had been exalted especially after his resurrection; _Cat. Rac._ qu. 94–190. Through divine power Christ does those same things _quae ipsius Dei sunt, tanquam Deus ipse_; ibid., 120, 164. _Hoc nomen Deus non est nomen substantiae cujusdam proprium vel personae, sed auctoritatis, potentiae_, just as angels and authorities in Scripture, according to Jesus' own words, John 10:34, are also sometimes called gods; Socinus on John 1:1. The same thing is now taught by Ritschl and his school. The name God is indeed used for Christ in Scripture and in the church, but it is then a name of office, not of essence. This position, however, is entirely untenable. Angels and authorities are indeed sometimes called God in Scripture, but there the metaphorical, official sense is clear and obvious to everyone. With Christ the case is entirely different. Personal, eternal existence is attributed to him; of him it is said that he was God, existed in the form of God, was the radiance of God's glory, the only-begotten Son of God, the image of the invisible God, indeed, God over all, blessed forever—who could possibly understand this of a human being who received the honorary title God solely because of the office he bore and the work he did? Furthermore, when the Christian church called Jesus God, it never intended thereby a name of office but always a name of essence. When one begins to use that same word and that same name in an entirely different sense, one deliberately gives occasion for misunderstanding and confusion and makes oneself guilty of dishonesty toward the church. Further, if Christ is not God in essence, then he may not be so named and worshiped either. Even if one says that he perfectly revealed God's love, that he wholly took into himself God's plan for the world and humanity, and that he fully made God's will his own, but none of this, from a scriptural, Christian standpoint—or logically and philosophically either—justifies giving the human being Jesus the designation God. To be one with God in a religious and ethical sense is something entirely different from being so in a metaphysical sense. A _Werthurtheil_ is untrue if it does not have its ground in a _Seinsurtheil_. Finally, the designation and worship of Jesus as God, while he is only a human being, is a pantheistic confusion of Creator and creature, a pagan idolatry, a return to Roman Catholic creature-worship, and directly contrary to the essence of Christianity and the principle of Protestantism. If Jesus, though only a human being, may be invoked and worshiped as God, then in principle the Roman Catholic veneration of Mary, the saints, and the angels, and all pagan idolatry, is thereby also justified. While denying that God can become man, one teaches at the same time that a human being can indeed raise himself to the rank and dignity of God; Schultz, _Gottheit Christi_ 386 f., 389, 407, 411, 454, 463. Incarnation is impossible, but apotheosis can very well exist. God's becoming human is absurd, but a human being's becoming God is supposed to be rational—as though what arises from development could ever be God. _Το γαρ προκοπτον οὐ θεος_; Greg. Naz., in Schaezler, op. cit., 56; and cf. further Fock, _Der Socin._ 538 f.; Lipsius, _Theol. Jahresbericht_ X 378; Pfleiderer, _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._ 1889, p. 168 f.; Dieckhoff, _Die Menschw. des Sohnes Gottes. Ein Votum über die Theol. Ritschl’s_, Leipzig, 1882; Stählin, _Kant, Lotze, Albr. Ritschl_, Leipzig, 1888, p. 165 f.; Hoensbroech, _Christ u. Widerchrist. Ein Beitrag zur Vertheidigung der Gottheit Christi u. zur Char. d. Unglaubens in der prot. Theol._, Freiburg, 1892.
