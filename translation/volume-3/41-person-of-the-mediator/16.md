## 16.

The confession concerning Christ finds its practical application in the worship rendered to him, in the _honor adorationis_. For those who with Arius deny Christ’s deity, or with Nestorius separate it from the human nature, or with Socinus see in Jesus only a human being, every ground for the worship of the Mediator falls away. Yet they still try to maintain it in all kinds of ways. Nestorius said that the man Jesus too might be worshiped as confessor and defender of the divine honor: _separo naturas, conjungo reverentiam_, Schwane, _D. G._ II 349. Socinus defended it on the ground that Christ was exalted to be Lord and had received all power and thus could help us in need and misery. Many of his party, however, such as Dávidis, Francken, and others, opposed it because God alone might be worshiped and Christ’s power was in any event limited. Socinus therefore saw himself compelled to distinguish between the worship of God as _prima causa_ and that of Christ as _causa secunda_ of our salvation, Vitringa V 252; Fock, _Der Socin._ 538 f. The Remonstrants too derived the ground for worshiping Christ especially from the fact that he was Mediator, King, Lord, and as such had received from the Father an _honor adorationis_ that was indeed _verus_ and _divinus_ but nevertheless not _supremus_, _Apol. Conf._ c. 2, p. 39; c. 3, p. 50; c. 16, p. 153; Arminius, _Op._ 436; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ V 18; Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV 35; VII 27, 6; cf. against this _Censura in conf. Rem._ c. 2, 3, 12; Trigland, _Antapol._ c. 44; Heidanus, _Causa Dei_ V 12; Vitringa V 268. The defense of the worship of Christ by Ritschl and his school materially agrees with that of Socinus. Christ, as a human being in relation to God, may indeed be honored but not worshiped; but Christ, considered religiously, as the revelation of God, as the person in whom the church has God, is indeed to be worshiped, for then we do not worship him alongside God but worship God in him. This worship of Christ then belongs further in the public worship of the church but not in the prayer of private persons. The latter is unhealthy, makes Christ into a subject distinct from God, cultivates a sentimental tendency, and leads to the deification of a creature, Schultz, _Gottheit Christi_, 1881, p. 706 f. As among the Socinians, so among the followers of Ritschl there are some who entirely reject worship of Christ and indeed call him an object of faith and an example for imitation, but not an object of religious veneration, Chapuis, _Revue de théol. et de philos._, November 1895, pp. 560–586; idem, “Die Anbetung Christi,” in Gottschick’s _Zeits. f. Theol. u. Kirche_, 1897, pp. 28–79. Socinianism and Ritschlianism thus end in Unitarianism, which deprives Christ of all religious veneration. Insofar as they nevertheless try to maintain a worship of the man Christ, they are related to and offer support to those who, though acknowledging Christ’s deity, nevertheless also derive a ground for worship from his human nature. To these belong in the first place the Monophysites, against whom the fifth ecumenical council, c. 9, in Denzinger, n. 180, determined that the worship of Christ rests not upon a συγχυσις της θεοτητος και της ἀνθρωποτητος but must be directed to God, the incarnate Logos with his flesh. But scholastic and Roman Catholic theologians too gradually came to venerate Christ’s human nature religiously in itself. Their point of departure was the general teaching of the church fathers that the person of Christ, in the undivided unity of his two natures, as the incarnate Word, as God and human together, had to be honored with one worship. From this it was inferred that Christ’s human nature itself, in itself, _in se_, might and must also be an object of divine veneration (λατρεια), though not on account of itself, _per se_ and _propter se_, but on account of its hypostatic union with the Son of God. To this was then further added that although the human nature is to be worshiped in itself and is thus the _objectum materiale_ of _adoratio_, it nevertheless is always an _objectum partiale_ and not _totale_. That is, whoever worships it always worships with it him who united himself with that human nature. And so it thereby became permissible and possible to make not only Christ’s human nature in itself but even a part of it, such as the Sacred Heart of Jesus, an object of worship. For the worship is directed not to that heart alone but to the whole Christ, who caused his divine love to dwell in that heart. Finally, many theologians also said that Christ’s human nature may be the object not only of λατρεια on the ground of its hypostatic union with the Logos but also of δουλεια on the ground of the many gifts bestowed upon it without measure, and then more specifically the object of ὑπερδουλεια, according to the hierarchical rule: _adoratio est diversa pro diversitate excellentiae, quae est ejus objectum formale_. Cf. John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ III c. 8; Lombard and other commentators on _Sent._ III, dist. 9; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 25; Bellarmine, _De imag. sanct._ II c. 12 sq.; Petavius, _De incarn._ XV c. 1–4; _Theol. Wirceb._ IV 330; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ IV 274; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 51 f.; Pesch, _Prael. theol._ IV 97 sq.; Simar, _Dogm._ 411. By virtue of their doctrine of the communication of divine properties to the human nature, the Lutherans too teach that Christ’s human nature is to be worshiped, Gerhard, _Loc._ IV § 23; Quenstedt, _Theol._ III 199–208; V 353; Buddeus, _Inst. theol._, p. 771 sq. And finally, among the Moravians the man Christ has almost entirely taken God’s place. He is not the mediator but the substitute of God and almost the only object of religious veneration, Plitt, _Zinzendorfs Theol._ III 20 f.; Römheld, _Theol. sacrosancta_, 1888.

Entirely in agreement with their doctrine of Christ, the Reformed now said that the Mediator was indeed the object of worship but that the ground for this worship lay in his divine nature. Yet there was still some difference of opinion among them. All agreed that Christ must also be worshiped and honored as Mediator, but some thought that the ground for this worship lay solely in Christ’s deity, Voetius, _Disp._ I 520 sq.; II 304 sq.; Maccovius, _Coll. theol._ I 369 sq.; _Macc. Redivivus_ c. 91; Hoornbeek, _Socin. confutatus_ I 36 sq.; Mastricht V 2, 26, and so forth. Others judged that the _objectum formale_ was not only Christ’s deity but also his mediatorship, Ames, _De adoratione Christi_; Walaeus, _Loci Comm._, _Op._ I 389; Trigland, _Antapol._ c. 46; Alting, _Theol. problem._ XII 20; Cloppenburg, _Op._ I 461; Bucanus, _Inst._ XXXV, qu. 9; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV, qu. 18; Hottinger, Heidanus, Burman, Heidegger, Moor III 829; Vitringa V 247. Now Scripture leaves no doubt whether Christ, just as he may be the object of our faith and trust, John 14:1; 17:3; Rom. 14:9; 2 Cor. 5:15; Eph. 3:12; 5:23; Col. 1:27; 1 Tim. 1:1, and so forth, may also be the object of our religious veneration and worship, John 5:23; 14:13; Acts 7:59; 9:13; 22:16; Rom. 10:12, 13; 1 Cor. 1:2; 2 Cor. 12:8; Phil. 2:9; Heb. 1:6; Rev. 5:12, 13; 22:17, 20; cf. Loofs in Herzog³ 4, 21; Theodor Zahn, _Die Anbetung Jesu im Zeitalter der Apostel_, Stuttgart, 1885; also in _Skizzen aus dem Leben der alten Kirche_, 1898, pp. 271–308. Nevertheless, according to Scripture the ground for worshiping Christ can be derived from nothing other than his deity. For the word of Scripture stands firm: You shall worship the Lord your God, and him alone shall you serve. That is the commandment that in principle condemns all pagan and Roman Catholic deification of creatures. If Christ’s royal power or also his mediatorship is still advanced as ground for religious worship, then this commandment of God is weakened and violated. As Mediator, King, Priest, and Prophet, Christ is not _absolute summus_ but subordinated to God. In that capacity he has another glory and another power than that of which he partakes as Son with the Father and the Spirit. In that capacity he is not the _causa efficiens_, which God alone is, but the _causa instrumentalis_ of our salvation. Therefore, if the ground for worshiping the Mediator lies not only in his deity but also in his mediatorship, then the ground in fact also lies in his human nature, for as Mediator Christ cannot be conceived without it. Then Father and Spirit, who are not Mediators, have one ground for their worship less than the Son, and he thus comes to stand above the Father and the Holy Spirit. Then there are two grounds for adoration, one derived from deity, another from something else—that is, from something creaturely—and then in principle the Roman Catholic distinction between λατρεια and δουλεια has been introduced and the deification of creatures legitimized. Christ is therefore most certainly to be worshiped as our Mediator, just as God is also honored and invoked as Creator and so forth, but the ground for this lies solely in his deity. He is not God because he is Mediator, but he is Mediator because he is God, with the Father and the Spirit the one and only God, to be praised above all forever. The dignity and works of the Mediator can and may be motives for worship, just as all kinds of benefits compel us to worship God. They can also be called grounds for worship insofar as the divine being works and reveals itself in them. But deity alone is the foundation of worship.
