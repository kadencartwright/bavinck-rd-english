## 7.

Besides in the Trinity, the incarnation further has its presupposition and preparation in creation. Creation gives existence to finite, limited beings. It is absolutely impossible for God to create something equal to him in essence and itself God. Thus God eternally conceived finite creatures and also gave them existence within the necessary boundaries of space and time. In those creatures he has, as it were, limited his eternal thoughts, his infinite omnipotence. Specifically, the creation of the human being in God’s image is a presupposition and preparation for the incarnation of God. Under the influence of the pantheistic doctrine of identity, and also in connection with the Lutheran _communicatio idiomatum_, more recent theology has greatly misused this. In place of the old _finitum non est capax infiniti_, it set the rule _homo divinae naturae capax_, pointed to the kinship of God and the human being, erased the distinction between the two, and proceeded from the thought that the incarnation was necessary for the perfection of both, Dorner, _Entw._ II 1227. This is forbidden by Christian theism. Nevertheless, the human being is akin to God, his image, his son, his offspring; and therefore the incarnation of God is possible, Thomas, Bonaventure, and others on _Sent._ III, dist. I. Thus questions whether God could also assume the nature of a stone, a plant, or an animal, _Sent._ III, dist. 2—as Ockham answered affirmatively, Stöckl, _Philos. d. M. A._ II 1620—are out of place. And when God then creates the human being in his image, dwells and works in that human being by his Spirit, exercises influence upon his heart and mind, speaks to him, and gives himself to be known and understood by him, that is a descent of God to, an accommodation to his creature, an anthropomorphizing of God, and thus in a certain sense and to that extent an incarnation of God. With and in creation the possibility of revelation and also of incarnation is given. Whoever considers the incarnation impossible must, on reflection, also come to deny creation. Whoever accepts the latter has in principle lost the right to oppose the former. Earlier, vol. II, pp. 310, 401, it appeared that the possibility of creation is given with the generation of the Son. If God were incommunicable, he could have given life neither to the Son nor to any creature. Now it must be added that if God was able to create and to reveal himself to beings essentially distinct from him, then he must also be able to become human. For the incarnation is certainly distinguished from every other revelation, but it is nevertheless also related to it; it is its summit, crown, and completion; cf. Athanasius and Gregory of Nyssa in Harnack, _D. G._ II 167. All revelation works toward and groups itself around the incarnation as the highest, richest, most perfect revelation. Generation, creation, and incarnation stand in close connection, even though the following does not flow necessarily from the preceding. But there is more. Creation itself is already to be conceived infralapsarianly, and Adam was already a type of Christ. This is unacceptable from the standpoint of those who think that God proceeded to creation without counsel and decree and at creation passively waited to see what the human being would do. But Scripture teaches otherwise. In the creation of Adam, God already reckoned with Christ. Creation itself in this sense already prepared for the incarnation. The world was so created that, falling, it could be raised again; humanity was so organized under one head that, sinning, it could be gathered again under another head; Adam was so appointed as head that Christ could immediately take his place; and the covenant of works was so ordered that, when broken, it could be healed in the covenant of grace. It has therefore wrongly been thought that the incarnation of God’s Son would have taken place even without sin. Among the church fathers this view does not yet occur clearly; cf. only Tertullian, _De resurr. carnis_ 6; _Adv. Prax._ 12. But in scholasticism this question was vigorously discussed. Besides the Trinity, the incarnation was an article of faith even before the fall and necessary to bring the human being to his supernatural goal; cf. vol. I, p. 518; II, p. 528. The question was therefore answered affirmatively by Rupert of Deutz; Duns Scotus, _Sent._ III, dist. 7, qu. 3; Alexander of Hales; Albert the Great; John Wessel; Catharinus; Pighius; Suárez; cf. also Bellarmine, _De Christo_ V c. 10, who does not decide; further by Osiander; Socinus, _Praelect. theol._ c. 10; and then by many more recent theologians: Steffens, Göschel, Baader, Nitzsch, Martensen, Liebner, Lange, Rothe, Schöberlein, Ebrard; cf. Dorner, _Entw._ II 1243–1260; _Chr. Gl._ I 642; many English theologians who try to explain everything through the incarnation, such as Westcott, _Christus Consummator_, London, 1886, pp. 99 f.; Illingworth, “The Incarnation in Relation to Development,” 5th essay in Gore’s _Lux Mundi_; also Van Oosterzee, _Christol._ III, pp. 85 f.; _Dogm._ II 107, 495, and so forth. It is understandable how, by reasoning, one arrived at this opinion. A fact such as the incarnation of God cannot be accidental and cannot have its cause in sin as a contingent and arbitrary act of the human being. Sin may have been able to modify God’s plan, but it could not destroy it. The incarnation must therefore have been established apart from sin; sin only caused that incarnation to have to occur for the redemption of the sinner. To this is then added that religion before and after the fall cannot differ essentially. If a mediator is now necessary, then such a mediator was also necessary in religion before the fall. Christ’s person and work by no means consist entirely in atoning for sins and acquiring salvation. He is not only mediator but also head. He is not a means but also an end, _Selbstzweck_, 1 Cor. 15:45–47; Eph. 1:10, 21–23; 5:31, 32; Col. 1:15–17. He does not exist only for the church; the church also exists for him. The predestination of Christ to glory precedes that of the human being. These considerations contain so much truth that the agreement found by the hypothesis of God’s incarnation apart from sin cannot surprise us. If Pelagian freedom of the will is taught and sin is thus for God an accident and disappointment, there is no better means of reconciling free will and God’s plan than by saying that the incarnation was nevertheless determined and modified only in something subordinate. From the standpoint of Augustine, and still more specifically of Reformed theology, however, there is no need for this entire hypothesis. There is only one plan and decree of God. With a view to God’s counsel there is no room for another reality than the existing one. However much sin entered the world through the will of the creature, it was nevertheless incorporated into God’s counsel from eternity and is for him not contingent or unforeseen. In that eternal counsel the incarnation because of sin also has a place. It does not depend upon the human being but only upon God’s good pleasure. More still, even apart from sin the Son was _mediator unionis_ for the human being. Many Reformed acknowledged this with Calvin, _Inst._ II 12, 4, 6. Because this was not correctly understood by Quenstedt, _Theol. did. pol._ I 110; cf. Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ II 190, he could count Zanchi, Bucanus, and Polanus among the advocates of incarnation apart from sin. The intention was only that religion in the covenants of works and grace was essentially one and the same and that salvation must therefore always consist in fellowship with the triune God. Only Comrie, through his strictly maintained supralapsarianism, came to the doctrine that the predestination of the man Christ preceded that of the fall, vol. II, p. 339. But otherwise most theologians adhered to Scripture, which always and only connects Christ’s incarnation with sin and sees in it the greatest proof of God’s compassion, Matt. 1:21; 9:13; 20:28; Luke 1:67; 2:30; John 1:29; 3:16; Rom. 8:3; Gal. 4:4, 5; 1 Tim. 3:16; Heb. 2:14; 1 John 3:8, and so forth. The opposite opinion also leads so easily to the thought that incarnation in itself is fitting and necessary for God—that is, to the pantheistic doctrine of God’s eternal self-revelation in the world.
