## 12.

Through this conception by the Holy Spirit and birth from Mary, the Son of God became a true and complete human being. But his humanity has been assailed no less vigorously than his deity. Because of their dualism, the Gnostics could not acknowledge it and therefore said that the aeon Christ had assumed only a phantom body (Saturninus, Marcion); or that he had brought a glorious, spiritual body with him from heaven and had merely passed through Mary as water through a pipe (Valentinus, Bardesanes); or that at his descent to earth he had indeed formed a body for himself from the elements and had suffered in it, but at his return to heaven had dissolved and relinquished it (Apelles); or that only temporarily, at the baptism, had he descended upon the human Jesus and had left him before the passion (Cerinthus); or that before the passion he had switched places with Simon of Cyrene and had handed him over to death on the cross (Basilides); or that Christ, the _Jezus impatibilis_, who had already descended from the world of light to Adam and appeared on earth only in a phantom body, must be carefully distinguished from the _Jesus patibilis_, who was the son of a poor widow and an emissary of the devil and, because he set himself against Christ, was crucified by the latter (Mani). All these ideas propagated themselves in the medieval sects and, under the influence of pantheistic mysticism, kabbalistic theosophy, and more recent philosophy of nature, acquired a new and widespread dominion among the Anabaptists in the century of the Reformation. They too taught that Christ could not assume his human nature from Mary, from Adamic humanity, because he would then necessarily have had to be a sinner. Rather, he assumed it from himself from eternity, thus brought his body with him from heaven, and passed through Mary as through a channel (Hofmann, Menno Simons; cf. the related conception of an eternal corporeality of God in the Kabbalah with its _Adam Kadmon_, in Swedenborg, Dippel, Oetinger, and Petersen); or from eternity he formed this heavenly, invisible corporeality for himself from the eternal virgin, the divine Eve, the wisdom of God, dwelt with it already immediately after the fall in Adam, Abel, and so forth, and then made it visible and mortal through the conception and birth from Mary (Weigel); or already at creation he assumed that glorious human nature from Adam before the fall, who then still had a subtle, heavenly corporeality, in order later to clothe it from Mary with a weak, mortal humanity (Ant. Bourignon, Poiret, Barclay); or he formed his human nature from Mary, but not from fleshly Mary but from regenerated Mary, who through her union with divine wisdom had received a holy, divine element within herself and had regained the virginal nature of Adam before the fall (Schwencfeld and others). Cf. the previously cited works of Dorner, Schwane, Harnack, and so forth. These ideas seem very strange to us. Yet in other forms they express nothing other than what more recent philosophy since Kant and Hegel has presented with its separation of the ideal and the historical Christ. The ideal Christ is the eternal Logos, absolute reason, the one substance that eternally realizes itself in the world and cannot pour out its fullness in a single human being but assumes human nature in humanity as the Son of God. But the historical Jesus is not the true, essential Christ. In the process of incarnation he indeed forms an important but nevertheless only transitory moment. He was a weak, mortal, sinful human being who did reveal the idea of the true Christ but by no means coincides with him or is one with him; cf. Runze, _Dogm._ § 78.

Now it requires no long argument to show that Holy Scripture stands directly opposed to this. Promised under the Old Testament as the Messiah who would come forth from a woman, from Abraham, Judah, and David, he is conceived in the fullness of time in Mary, ἐν αὐτη, Matt. 1:20, by the Holy Spirit and born from her, ἐκ γυναικος, Gal. 4:4. He is her son, Luke 2:7; the fruit of her womb, Luke 1:42; according to the flesh from David and Israel, Acts 2:30; Rom. 1:3; 9:5; a partaker of our flesh and blood and like us in all things except sin, Heb. 2:14, 17, 18; 4:15; 5:1; a true human being, the Son of Man, Rom. 5:15; 1 Cor. 15:21; 1 Tim. 2:15; growing up as a little child, Luke 2:40, 52; hungry, Matt. 4:2; thirsty, John 19:28; weeping, Luke 19:41; John 11:35; troubled, John 12:27; sorrowful, Matt. 26:38; angry, John 2:17; suffering, dying, and so forth. For Scripture it is so firmly established that Christ came in the flesh that it calls the denial of this antichristian, 1 John 2:22. And it teaches not only that Christ assumed a true human nature but also that he assumed a complete one. Arius denied this and said that the Logos, who after all was a creature, could not become human but had merely appeared in human form and for that purpose had assumed only a body, not a soul. Apollinaris, by contrast, wished precisely to maintain that Christ was not merely a human being enlightened by the Logos, an ἀνθρωπος ἐνθεος, as Paul of Samosata said, but that he himself was God and his work divine; and thus he arrived at the doctrine that the Logos had assumed an animate human body without _pneuma_ and himself supplied the place of that _pneuma_; cf. the article “Apoll.” in _Herzog_³ 1, 671. But Scripture says clearly that Jesus was a complete human being and ascribes to him all the constituent parts of human nature: not only a body, Matt. 26:26; John 20:12; Phil. 3:21; 1 Pet. 2:24; flesh and blood, Heb. 2:14; bones and side, John 19:33, 34; head and hands and feet, Matt. 8:20; Luke 24:39; but also a soul, Matt. 26:38; spirit, Matt. 27:50; Luke 23:46; John 13:21; consciousness, Mark 13:32; will, Matt. 26:39; John 5:30; 6:38, and so forth. Apollinarianism was therefore condemned at all times by the Christian church and theology; they understood the interest at stake here: _totus enim Christus totum assumpsit me, ut toti mihi salutem gratificaret; quod enim inassumptibile est, incurabile est_, Damasc., _de fide orthod._ III 6; Lombard, _Sent._ III 2; Petavius, _de incarn._ V c. 11. The denial of the true and complete human nature always proceeds from a certain dualism. The σαρξ, matter, is sinful by nature and therefore cannot be a constituent part of the true Christ. He has therefore derived his substance not from the sensible, material world but from the invisible, heavenly essence in God, in himself, in divine wisdom, in the unfallen Adam, or in regenerated Mary. The connection between this ideal Christ and the historical Jesus can therefore be only accidental and mechanical. No true unity comes about, and thus neither does a true fellowship between God and the human being. God and world, creation and re-creation, nature and grace, the eternal and the temporal, the heavenly and the earthly, remain eternally alongside and opposed to each other. Among the Gnostics and the Anabaptists all this is clear. But however strange it may sound, this same dualism is also characteristic of more recent pantheistic philosophy, which so gladly adorns itself with the name of monism. For it is an axiom of this philosophy that the idea cannot fully pour itself out in one individual; that is, no unity, no fellowship between God and the human being is possible. In order to enter into fellowship with God, the individual must lose itself, efface its personality, and sink like a wave into the ocean of the all. God and the human being, eternity and time, and especially holiness and finitude stand opposed to each other; the finite is defective and imperfect in its nature; sin is necessary. Hence Hegel, in Dorner, _Entw._ II 1115 f.; Strauss, _Gl._ II 164; _Leben Jesu_ 1835, II 716–718; Baur, _Dreieinigkeit_ III 963 f.; and others cannot maintain the sinlessness of Jesus. A finite individual cannot be perfect and enjoy full fellowship with God. Now this pantheism thinks it can compensate in another way for what it first took away. It ascribes to the whole what it denies to the parts: not a single human being but humanity is the true Christ, the Son of God; it enjoys the highest unity and fellowship with God; in it God assumes human nature. But this compensation is only apparent and gives nothing. Not only does humanity exist only in the individuals, and is the whole nothing other than the sum of the parts, but it is also not true that the whole, that humanity, stands nearer to God than the individual human beings. Goethe did indeed say, _willst du ins Unendliche schreiten, so geh’ ins Endliche nach allen Seiten_; but the endless is altogether different from the infinite, eternity altogether different from the inexpressible sum of all moments of time, and perfection altogether different from the totality of all imperfections. Even if pantheism exchanges the individual for humanity and the part for the whole, it thereby advances not a single step. It leaves the transition from the infinite to the finite, from eternity to time, from God to the world, completely unexplained and offers as explanation nothing but words and images, vol. II, 392. If God cannot become human in one, then neither can he become human in all. Over against this dualistic and atomistic view Scripture now places the organic view. In one, God comes to all, not in appearance but in truth. There is one Mediator between God and human beings, the man Christ Jesus. But therefore his true and complete human nature matters just as much as his deity. If one essential constituent in the human nature of Christ is excluded from true unity and fellowship with God, then there is an element in creation that remains dualistically alongside and opposed to God. Then there is an eternal ὑλη. Then God is not the Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth. Then the Christian religion is not truly catholic. _Quod enim inassumptibile est, incurabile est._
