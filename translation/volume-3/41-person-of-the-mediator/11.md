## 11.

Entirely different is the case with supernatural conception.
This is of the highest significance for the person of Christ and therefore also of religious importance.
Scripture ascribes Jesus’ conception to the Holy Spirit or to the power of the Most High.
The Holy Spirit, who is the author of all physical, psychic, and pneumatic life, vol. II, 230, is understood in Matt. 1:18, 20, as appears from the preposition ἐκ, to be the _causa efficiens_ of that conception, while in Luke 1:35 the conception is ascribed to the power that will proceed from God, the Most High, and come upon Mary.
It is clear from this that the activity of the Holy Spirit in this conception did not consist in pouring some heavenly, divine substance into Mary, but in a manifestation of power that made her womb fruitful, in an overshadowing as with a cloud; cf. Ex. 40:34; Num. 9:15; Luke 9:34; Acts 1:8.
If the old controversy between the spermatists and the ovists were decided in favor of the latter—which, however, is by no means yet the case; Schäfer, _Die Vererbung_, Berlin, 1897, pp. 74–80—then this activity of the Holy Spirit would thereby also be clarified physiologically.
However this may be, that which is begotten in Mary, το ἐν αὐτῃ γεννηθεν, is ἐκ πνευματος ἁγιου, from the Holy Spirit as _causa efficiens_; it has its cause in the activity of the Holy Spirit and not in the will of man or of the flesh.
Besides making Mary’s womb fruitful, the Holy Spirit also brought about the sanctification of the child that would be born from her.
It is a ἁγιον that is born from her; it will be called Son of God, Son of the Most High, Luke 1:35.
It is important now to observe that this activity of the Holy Spirit with respect to Christ’s human nature by no means stands by itself; although it began at conception, it did not end there; it continued throughout his whole life, even into the state of exaltation.
In general, the necessity of this activity can already be inferred from the fact that the Holy Spirit is the author of all life in creatures and specifically of the religious-ethical life in the human being.
The true human being, who bears God’s image, cannot be thought of for a single moment without the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, vol. II, 540.
Further, Christ’s human nature had to be prepared for union with the person of the Son, that is, for a unity and fellowship with God such as no other creature has ever been deemed worthy of.
If the human being in general can have no fellowship with God except through the Holy Spirit, then this applies all the more strongly to Christ’s human nature, which had to be united with the Son in an altogether unique manner.
This special union, which far surpasses and essentially differs from God’s immanence in his creatures, God’s revelation in the prophets, and God’s indwelling in his people, already makes an altogether special activity of the Holy Spirit _apriori_ probable and even necessary.
But Holy Scripture also teaches this expressly.
Prophecy had already announced that the Messiah would be anointed with the Holy Spirit in a special sense, Isa. 61:1, and the New Testament relates that Christ received that Spirit without measure, John 3:34.
Not only was he conceived by him, but that Spirit descends upon him at his baptism, Matt. 3:16; fills him entirely, Luke 4:1, 18; leads him to the wilderness and to Galilee, Matt. 4:1; Luke 4:14; and gives him the power to cast out demons, Matt. 12:18; to offer himself to God without blemish, Heb. 9:14; just as he became David’s Son in the way of the flesh, so through the resurrection to be appointed Son of God in power, as Lord, with a glorified body, Rom. 1:4; to vindicate himself as such before the eyes of all, 1 Tim. 3:16; to depart from the earth and ascend into heaven, 1 Pet. 3:19, 22; and to reveal himself to his own as the life-giving Spirit, the one to whom the Spirit belongs and who works through the Spirit, 1 Cor. 15:45; 2 Cor. 3:17, 18; cf. Gloël, _Der H. Geist_, Halle, 1888, p. 113 f.

Placed in this context, conception by the Holy Spirit acquires a rich significance.
According to his human nature, from the first moment onward and throughout his whole life, even into the state of exaltation, Jesus had to be formed to be the Messiah, the Christ, the Son of God, the Son of the Most High.
He could become this only κατα σαρκα, in the way of the flesh, through birth from a woman, Rom. 1:3; 9:5; Gal. 4:4, but at the same time, in order to be this, he could not be the fruit of the will of man or of the flesh.
What is born of flesh is flesh.
Jesus might then perhaps have been David’s son and heir of his earthly kingdom, but not David’s Lord, the Messiah, the Son of God, who is appointed king in the kingdom of God.
Yet that is what he was.
Paul does indeed say that through the resurrection he was appointed Son of God and Lord, Rom. 1:4, but this does not exclude that he was already Son of God at the time of and even before his conception, Rom. 1:3, any more than the υἱοθεσια expected by believers in the future, Rom. 8:23, excludes their already possessing it now, Rom. 8:15.
Christ was the Son of God from eternity; he was with God in the beginning; he is the firstborn of all creation.
Thus he could not be begotten and brought forth by the will of man; he himself was the acting subject who prepared a body for himself in Mary’s womb through the Holy Spirit.
In the Old Testament he is therefore promised especially as the seed of the woman, Gen. 3:15; as the one given by God, Isa. 9:5; as the one raised up by the Lord, Jer. 23:5, 6; 33:14–17; as a shoot from the felled trunk of Jesse, Isa. 11:1; as the son of an עַלְמָה, in general a young woman and especially an unmarried one, Gen. 24:43; Ex. 2:8; Ps. 68:26; Song 1:3; 6:8; Prov. 30:18, 19, who will bear the name Immanuel, Isa. 7:14.
In the Old Testament nothing further is said about the manner of his conception; only that he will be the son of a woman and at the same time a gift and revelation of God.
This was thus fulfilled in Christ.
Leaving aside the difficult question whether Luke gives Mary’s genealogy and whether Mary herself was also of David’s line, cf. Luke 1:30–33, 35, 48, Jesus was a descendant of David not through Mary but through Joseph.
Although he was not from Joseph, through Mary, who was betrothed to Joseph, he was in civil and legal terms the son of Joseph, Luke 2:27, 41, 48, and inherited from him the rights to David’s throne.
Therefore Joseph was also admonished by God to take Mary to himself as his lawful wife, to act as head and father of the household, and as such to give the child the name Jesus, Matt. 1:18–21.
Thus Christ became David’s Son and at the same time remained David’s Lord.
This exclusion of the man from Christ’s conception also brought it about that Christ, because he was not included in the covenant of works, remained free from original guilt and therefore could also, according to his human nature, be preserved from every stain of sin before and after his birth.
As subject, as an ego, he was not from Adam but was the Son of the Father and had been chosen from eternity as the head of a new covenant.
Not Adam but God was his Father.
As a person he did not proceed from humanity but he himself came to it from outside and entered into it.
And because he thus remained free from all original guilt according to God’s righteous judgment, he could be conceived by the Holy Spirit and through that Spirit remain free from every stain of sin.
Conception by the Holy Spirit was not the deepest ground and final cause of Jesus’ sinlessness, as many claim—Ebrard, _Dogm._ II 4–10; Rothe, _Ethik_ § 533 f.; Müller, _Sünde_ II 535—but it was the only way in which he, who already existed as a person and had been appointed head of a new covenant, could now also, in a human manner, in the flesh, be and remain what he was: the Christ, the Son of God, the Son of the Most High.
Cf. Polanus, _Synt._ XI 13; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ II 109 f.
