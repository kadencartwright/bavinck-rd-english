## 15.

Yet an important objection is continually raised against the doctrine of the two natures: that it does not do justice to Christ’s human nature and makes a human development impossible for him. Roman Catholic and Lutheran christology do indeed give occasion for this objection. For in earlier dogmatics the union of the two natures in Christ entailed three consequences: _communicatio idiomatum_, _apostelesmatum_, and _charismatum_. The first held that in the incarnation both natures, with all their properties, were communicated to the one person and the one subject, who can therefore be designated by divine and human names. Thus one may say that the Son of God was born, suffered, and died, Acts 20:28; 1 John 1:7, and also that the man Christ Jesus exists from eternity, descended from heaven, and so forth, John 3:13. The second was understood to mean that the proper mediatorial or redemptive works all bear a divine-human character; that is, they all have as their efficient cause the one undivided personal subject in Christ; they were accomplished by Christ with the cooperation of both his natures and with a twofold ἐνεργεια; and yet in their result they again form an undivided unity, since they are the work of one person. The third indicated that from the first moment of its existence Christ’s human nature was adorned with all kinds of glorious and rich gifts of the Holy Spirit. An important difference arose concerning these three _effecta unionis_; see above, pp. 242–246. The Lutherans understood the _communicatio idiomatum_ to mean that the properties of both natures were communicated not only to the one person but that those of the divine nature were also communicated to the human nature. Through the union, the human nature was elevated to divine omnipotence and omnipresence; _Symb. Bücher_, ed. Müller, 679, 680. It received _singulares, excellentissimas, maximas, supernaturales, impervestigabiles, ineffabiles atque coelestes praerogativas majestatis, gloriae, virtutis ac potentiae_, which are not merely created and finite gifts, ibid., 685, but divine and infinite ones, such as _vivificare, omnipotentia, omniscientia, omnivolipraesentia_, 686, 689, 691, 692. It is clear that this understanding of the communication of properties deprives the communication of gifts of its entire significance. What need is there still for gifts if divine properties are communicated? Lutheran christology still speaks of gifts, ibid., 686, but in fact does not know what to do with them; cf. Dorner, _Entw._ II 914–916, and it no longer even has a place for Christ’s anointing with the Holy Spirit; Schneckenburger, _Zur kirchl. Christol._ 30. On the other hand, Roman Catholics do teach a communication of gifts and also oppose the Lutheran doctrine of the _communicatio idiomatum_, but they say that by virtue of the _unio hypostatica_, immediately at the first moment of its conception, Christ’s human nature _uberrimam Spiritus Dei copiam atque omnem charismatum abundantiam accepit_; _Cat. Rom._ I 4, 4. Both Lutheran and Roman Catholic christology thereby harbor a docetic element. The purely human development of Christ does not receive its due here. In reaction, during this century people swung to the other extreme and failed to acknowledge the Lord’s deity. The Reformed, however, understood the communication of gifts in such a way that a human development of Jesus remained possible. Although he was the second Adam, Christ was nevertheless different from Adam. Adam was created as an adult, received paradise as his dwelling place, and was not subject to suffering and death. Christ, however, was conceived in Mary by the Holy Spirit and born as a helpless infant. He was not placed in a paradise but came into a world that lies in evil. He was exposed to temptation from every side. He bore a nature susceptible to suffering and death. His was not the human nature of Adam before the fall; rather, God sent his Son _ἐν ὁμοιωματι σαρκος ἁμαρτιας_, that is, in such flesh as was like the _σαρξ ἁμαρτιας_ in form and appearance, Rom. 8:3.

Through this beautiful doctrine of the _communicatio charismatum_, Reformed theology was better able than any other to maintain the true, genuine humanity in Christ alongside his deity. Its value for this is incalculable. 1º Among the Reformed there was still a difference over whether the incarnation in itself, apart from the state of sin in which it took place, was already an act of humiliation. On the one hand, one could appeal to the fact that the distance between God and man is so great that the assumption of human nature as such is already a humiliation. But on the other hand, it could be argued in reply that Christ would then still be in the state of humiliation now, while he is glorified at the Father’s right hand; _Heraut_ 284, 285. The dispute is easily ended by saying that the incarnation in itself, as such, always is and remains an act of condescending goodness, but is not in the narrower sense a stage in the state of humiliation. It was such a stage because it was a becoming-flesh, the assumption of a weak human nature. After all, there was no dispute that Christ assumed a weak human nature subject to suffering and death, and that this was an act of humiliation for the Son of God. Scripture therefore establishes this beyond all doubt, Isa. 53:2, 3; John 1:14; 17:5; 1 Cor. 2:8; 2 Cor. 8:9; Phil. 2:7, 8, and so forth. 2º As soon as we try, however, to give an account of what is included in that weak human nature, the question becomes more difficult. Jesus certainly had all kinds of needs for food, drink, rest, sleep, and so forth, but these also belonged to Adam before the fall. Conversely, it is difficult to think that according to his human nature Jesus was in himself susceptible to sickness, illness, and death. He himself had the power to lay down his life, John 10:17, but was not of himself, without his will, subject to death. From ancient times opinions on this matter were therefore divided. There was disagreement about Jesus’ outward appearance. Some, such as Origen, Chrysostom, Jerome, and others, ascribed to him a beautiful appearance with an appeal to Ps. 45:3. Others, such as Justin, Clement, Tertullian, and so forth, thought that bodily he had no form or glory, Ps. 22:7; Isa. 53:2, 3. Still others took a middle position between these two conceptions or refrained from judgment; cf. Vitringa V 501; Walch, _Bibl. theol._ III 439; Menzel, _Symbolik_ 1855 I 177 f.; Schotel, _Iets over de uitwendige gedaante van Jezus Christus_, Bosch, 1852; Riehm, _Handw._ I 724, and the article “Christusfiguren” in Herzog³. And so too, regarding susceptibility to suffering and death, the Aphthartodocetists and the _phthartolatri_ stood opposed to one another. Now there is no doubt that the Son of God assumed a human nature that was susceptible to suffering and death; otherwise these would have been appearance and not reality. Even Roman Catholics and Lutherans must acknowledge this. Furthermore, on the other hand, it is also true that Christ assumed this human nature entirely voluntarily and, considered in the abstract, always retained the right to lay aside this weak human nature or to change it into a glorious one elevated above all suffering and death. To that extent, suffering and dying always remained his free act. He had and retained the power to take up and lay down his life. No one could cause him any pain or take his life unless he himself gave the power to do so. He determined the hour and the power of darkness. But given this will of his, suffering and dying were natural for him, given with the character of the human nature he assumed. Hilary, in Schwane, _D. G._ II 269, and others have often presented it precisely the other way around and viewed Christ’s pain, suffering, and so forth as the unnatural, the miracle, and Christ’s miracles precisely as the natural. Although this can be understood in a good sense, it nevertheless does not take sufficient account of the fact that Christ assumed the weak human nature and was therein distinguished from Adam. From this it may not be inferred that Christ was also susceptible to all kinds of sickness and illness. For he did take upon himself the general consequences of sin—suffering and death—which now belong to human nature, but not every particular illness that flows from particular circumstances, a weak physical constitution, or an irregular or imprudent way of life; Thomas, _S. Th._ III, qu. 14; Moor III 591; IV 26; Ebrard § 417. The human nature in Christ was also much more richly unfolded than in Adam. For in the _status integritatis_ there was no occasion for many affections—anger, sorrow, compassion, mercy, and so forth. But Christ not only visited us with the inward movements of God’s mercy; in his human nature he opened to us that rich world of feeling which did not yet exist in Adam and could not exist in him. 3º In Christ there was human knowledge, an intellectual development, an increase in wisdom and knowledge. The Arians and Apollinarians, according to whom the Logos takes the place of the _pneuma_ in Christ, could not assume human knowledge in Christ. The Monophysites also had to oppose it, but they could just as well teach Christ’s omniscience if the human nature had been absorbed into the divine as, in the opposite case, ignorance (Themistius, head of the Agnoetae), with an appeal to texts such as Matt. 20:32; 21:19; Mark 5:9; 13:32; Luke 2:52; 8:30; John 11:34; Schwane, _D. G._ II 366. Over against these parties, the church fathers came more and more to regard Christ’s human knowledge as perfect from the first moment and incapable of increase; and from them this opinion passed into scholastic and Roman Catholic christology, and also into Lutheran christology. They thereby came into conflict with the clear statements of Scripture mentioned above and had to resort to a docetic interpretation. The true ground for this doctrine is therefore only the _convenientia_ that God surely had to grant this gift of knowledge to a human nature so closely united with him; Kleutgen, _Theol._ III 251. The passages of Scripture to which appeal is made, such as John 1:14; 2:24, 25; 6:64; 13:3; Col. 2:3, 9, do not prove the contrary because they concern the whole Christ and not specifically his human nature, and much less this nature in its historical development. The Reformed said in reply, first, that the _scientia infusa_ and _acquisita_ in Christ were not immediately complete but gradually increased and expanded; and second, that here on earth Christ was not a _comprehensor_ but a _viator_, that he walked by faith and hope and not by sight, and that the _scientia beata_ was not yet his portion here on earth. Naturally, faith in Christ was not, as it is in us, a resting upon the grace and mercy of God. Faith acquired this peculiar feature only through the state of sin in which we find ourselves. By nature, faith in Adam and in Christ is nothing other than clinging firmly to the word and promise of God, holding fast to the Invisible One. And Jesus too did that, Matt. 27:46; Heb. 2:17, 18; 3:2. That faith and that hope in Christ were not wavering and vacillating either but firm and strong. They sustained him, who awakens and completes faith in us, in temptation and caused him, for the joy awaiting him as the reward for his labor, to endure the cross and despise the shame, Heb. 12:2. Thus there was an increase in wisdom and knowledge in Christ. The human consciousness in him, although having the same subject as the divine consciousness, knew that subject, that “I,” only to a very limited extent—indeed, _ὁλον_ but not _ὁλως_. Behind our limited consciousness there still lies in us a world of being. So behind Christ’s human consciousness lay the depth of God, which could shine through that human consciousness only gradually and always in a limited manner; Gomarus, _Op._ I 196; Voetius, _Disp._ II 155 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIII 12, 13; Moor III 804; M. Vitringa V 246; Heppe, _Dogm. d. ev. ref. K._ 315; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 281, 307, 329; Kuyper, _Het werk v. d. H. Geest_ II 281 f.; C. Lucassen, _Der Glaube Jesu Christi_, _Neue Kirchl. Zeits._ VI 1895, pp. 337–347. From this it may not be inferred, however, that Jesus could err in various domains. This is certainly taught today in wide circles, specifically in order to evade Jesus’ authority in matters concerning the Old Testament. But in this way one assails Christ himself. For it is indeed true that Jesus gave no instruction in any human science and did not come to earth for that purpose. He came to make the Father known to us and to accomplish his work. But for that purpose he also had to know that Father in his revelation and works, and therefore also to know whether or not the Old Testament was God’s Word. This was not knowledge of a purely scientific but of a religious nature and was of the highest importance for the faith of the church. Whoever attributes error to Jesus in this respect comes into conflict not only with his divine nature but also with his prophetic office; cf. volume I 333; Herzog¹ 21, 204; Herzog² 6, 27; Voigt, _Fundamentaldogm._ 527 f.; Tholuck, _Das Alte Test. im N._, 4e Aufl., 1854; Caven, _Presb. and Ref. Review_, July 1892; Schwartzkopff, _Konnte Jezus irren?_, Giessen, Ricker, 1896; idem, _Die Irrthumslosigkeit Jesu Chr. und der chr. Glaube_, ibid., 1897. 4º In Christ there was a moral development. Theodore of Mopsuestia, Nestorius, and all who proceed from Christ’s human nature assume that he perfected himself through all kinds of struggle and temptation. Jesus was not positively holy; he did not bring with him the _non posse peccare_. On the contrary, such an innate holiness is impossible and ethically worthless. But Jesus was a human being who brought with him the possibility of sinning, yet through moral exertion and struggle actually kept himself free from all sins, developed himself ethically to the highest standpoint, and made himself worthy of union with God; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ II 319, 325, and so forth. This position rests upon a failure to take Christ’s deity into account in actual fact. It proceeds from the mistaken idea that there is no virtue other than that acquired through struggle, and at most it attains only a historical, factual sinlessness, which is insufficient for Jesus as Mediator. The attempts to prove Jesus’ sinlessness historically, such as those of Ullmann, _De zondeloosheid van Jezus_, Tiel, 1851; Dorner, “Ueber Jesu sündlose Vollkommenheit,” _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ VII 1862, pp. 49–107; the article “Sündlosigheit Jesu” in Herzog¹, suppl.; Gouda Quint, _De zondeloosheid des Heeren_, 1862; Schaff, _Jezus Christus, het wonder der geschiedenis_, 1866; van Oosterzee, _Leven v. Jezus_ I 569 f.; _Dogm._ § 93; Chapuis, “La sainteté de Jésus,” _Revue de théol. et de philos._, Juillet, Sept. Nov. 1897, and so forth, are inadequate; Philippi, _K. Gl._ IV 161. Historical certainty that others shortly thereafter make uncertain again is not enough for us. However worthy of appreciation it is that so many from whom, according to their principles, something else might be expected still have a deep sense of Jesus’ moral perfection—such as Daub, Marheineke, Rosenkranz, Vatke, Schleiermacher, Beyschlag, Hase, Schenkel, Lipsius, and so forth—nevertheless, faith that does not rest upon the testimony of Scripture is unstable and is therefore continually burdened and oppressed by all kinds of philosophical and historical objections. Scripture, however, causes us to recognize in Christ not only an empirical sinlessness but also a necessary incapacity for sin. He is the Son of God, the Logos who in the beginning was with God and was himself God. He is one with the Father and always accomplishes his will and work, and so forth. For whoever confesses this of Christ, the possibility of sinning and falling is an _Ungedanke_; Holtzmann, _Neut. Th._ II 446; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ II² 178. Therefore, over against Arians, Pelagians, and Nominalists such as Scotus, Biel, Durandus, Molina, and so forth, Christian theology maintained that Christ could not sin. For then either God himself would have had to be able to sin—which is blasphemy—or the union of the divine and human natures is regarded as breakable and is in fact denied; Augustine, _Enchir._ c. 36, 40, 41; Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 12, and the commentaries of Thomas and Bonaventure; Moor III 692; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 330; Philippi IV 161 f., and so forth. But this does not eliminate the essential difference that exists between the holiness of God and the holiness of Christ as man. Attending to this, Jesus could say that no one is good—goodness itself—except God alone, Matt. 19:16, 17; Mark 10:17, 18; Luke 18:18, 19. The goodness or holiness of Christ according to his human nature is not divine and original but bestowed, _infusa_; and therefore it too had to manifest, maintain, and confirm itself in the way of struggle and temptation. The _bonitas infusa_ does not exclude the _bonitas acquisita_. The latter presupposes the former: no good fruits except from a good tree. But the excellence of the tree must nevertheless become evident in the soundness of the fruits. And so Christ too had to manifest his innate holiness through temptation and struggle. These are not rendered superfluous or futile by the _non posse peccare_. For although actual temptation could come to Jesus only from without and not from within, he nevertheless had a human nature that shrank from suffering and death. Thus throughout his entire life he was tempted in all kinds of ways—by Satan, his enemies, and even his disciples, Matt. 4:1–11; Mark 1:13; Luke 4:1–13; Matt. 12:29; Luke 11:22; Matt. 16:23; Mark 8:33. And in those temptations he had to maintain himself through struggle. The _non prosse peccare_ was no compulsion but was ethical in character and therefore also had to manifest itself in an ethical manner. 5º The same applies to Christ’s power. Although omnipotent as the Son of God, he was nevertheless limited as regards the power of his human nature. The Monophysites do not distinguish these two but allow the two natures, the two wills, and the distinct powers to merge into one another. But Scripture and the church distinguished between them and have the two natures so joined that in the one divine-human work each nature does what belongs to it. And therefore the working of miracles, the forgiveness of sins, the granting of eternal life, and all that belongs to the work of the Mediator pertain not only to his deity but also to his humanity. Therefore Christ, precisely as the Son of Man, as Messiah, ascribes to himself this power of forgiveness and judgment, Matt. 9:2–8; John 5:27. Power goes out from him at a touch, Luke 6:19. His flesh is the bread that gives life to the world, John 6:51. The Father has given all things into his hand, John 3:35; 13:3; 17:2. No one can snatch the sheep from his hand any more than from the Father’s hand, John 10:28–30. Just as the Father does, he hears prayer, John 14:13; cf. 16:23; sends the Spirit, 15:26; cf. 14:26; and grants eternal life, 10:28; 17:2. But none of this excludes his power, as human power, from being capable of increase. He was born as a weak and helpless infant. He needed food and drink. He was tired from the journey and sat down by the well, John 4:6. Even when working miracles he was dependent upon the faith of the people, Matt. 13:58. In the garden he was strengthened by an angel, Luke 22:43. Only after the resurrection does he say that all power in heaven and on earth has been given to him, Matt. 28:8; Mark 16:20; Luke 24:19. Then he receives back the glory that he already had as Son with the Father, John 17:5, and causes his human nature also to share in it. Through the resurrection Christ also as man became Lord over the living and the dead, received a name above every name and power over all creatures, Matt. 28:18; Col. 2:3, 9; Phil. 2:9; Heb. 2:7, 8.
