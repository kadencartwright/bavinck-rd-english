## 5.

All these developments of the doctrine of Christ proceed from and move within the boundaries of the _symbolum Chalcedonense_.
But by no means could all Christians assent to this confession.
At all times there were those who deviated to the right or to the left and followed in the footsteps of ancient Ebionism or Gnosticism.
On the one side stand Arianism, Nestorianism, Socinianism, Deism, Rationalism, and so forth; cf. vol. II 260–264; and on the other side Patripassianism, Sabellianism, Monophysitism, medieval Pantheism, Anabaptism, Theosophy, and so forth; cf. vol. II 264–268; and further Petavius, _de incarnatione_ I c. 1: _synopsis haeresum omnium, quae cath. in carnationis fidem oppugnarunt_; Forbesius à Corse, _Instruct. hist. theol._, lib. 2, 3, 5, 6; M. Vitringa V 46 sq.; Baur, _Lehre v. d. Dreiein. u. Menschw. Gottes_, 1841–43; Dorner, _Entw. der Lehre v. d. Person Christi_², 1851–54; Réville, _Histoire du dogme de la divinité de Jésus-Christ_, 1869; Münscher-v. Coelln, _D. G._; art. “Christologie, Kirchenlehre” by Loofs in Herzog³ 4, 16.
Especially in modern theology the thought has become dominant that the doctrine of the two natures may indeed have suited Greek theology and the Greek church but has now lost all religious value for us, that it has also fallen irrevocably under the criticism of Socinianism and Rationalism, and that it therefore ought now to be altered in an entirely new, religious-ethical sense.
This new Christology has its principal representatives in Kant, Schleiermacher, and Ritschl.

Kant could not accept the biblical and ecclesiastical doctrine of Christ, because he denied the knowability of the supernatural and, inferring the _du kannst_ from the _du sollst_, had no need of a Redeemer.
For Kant, Christ could therefore remain only an example of morality and a teacher of virtue.
Everything further that Scripture and church said of this Christ had only symbolic value.
The ecclesiastical Christ is the symbol of humanity pleasing to God; this humanity is the true, only-begotten, well-beloved Son for whose sake God created the world.
The incarnation of Christ symbolizes the origin of true moral life in the human being; his vicarious suffering means that the moral human being within us must atone for the evil of the sensuous human being; faith in Christ indicates that for salvation the human being must believe in the idea of humanity pleasing to God.
In a word, the historical human being Jesus is no mediator or savior, but everything the church confesses of that person applies fully to the idea of humanity; _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 69 f.
Kant began in modern philosophy, like the ancient Gnostics, to separate the historical Christ from the ideal Christ; and others continued this.
Fichte proceeded from the thought that God and the human being were absolutely one.
But Christ was the first to recognize this unity in himself and express it clearly; that is his great historical significance; through him thousands came to this recognition, to this communion with God.
But even if this was historically so, it does not thereby follow that the human being cannot attain that communion by himself and can do so only through Christ.
If Jesus returned, he would be completely satisfied that Christianity reigned in hearts even if his person were entirely forgotten.
Only the metaphysical, eternal truth—the recognition of unity with God—saves; the historical is a self-contained, passing fact; _Anweisung zum seligen Leben_, especially 6e Vorl.
In Schelling’s first period, the absolute is not an immutable being but an eternal becoming that therefore comes to revelation in the world as its _logos_ and son.
Theology believes that Christ is the only-begotten and incarnate Son of God.
That is incorrect; God is eternal and cannot have assumed human nature at a definite moment in time; Christianity as a historical fact has passing significance.
But the idea remains eternal; the world is the Son of God; the incarnation of God consists in this: that the absolute, in order to be itself, comes to revelation in a world, in a multiplicity of individuals, in a rich history, in a historical process.
The world is therefore God himself in his becoming; the incarnation of God is the principle of all life and history; the finite is the necessary form for God’s becoming manifest; everything must be understood from the idea of the incarnation.
And this is also the esoteric truth of Christianity; the historical clothing is only a temporary form for this eternal idea; _Vorles. über die Methode des akad. Studiums_, 1803, _Werke_ I 5, S. 286 f.
Hegel likewise said that what theology presented symbolically in images was converted by philosophy into concepts; Christ is not the only God-man, but the human being is essentially one with God and also becomes conscious of this at the highest standpoint of his development; _Religionsphilos._, _Werke_ XII 235 f.
Proceeding from these philosophical premises, Marheineke, Rosenkranz, Göschel, Daub, Conradi, and others still sought to maintain the incarnation of God in Christ, but Strauss drew the consequences and said in his _Leben Jesu_, 1835, II 716 f., 734 f., that the idea does not pour out its fullness into one specimen but only into a multiplicity of individuals; humanity is the incarnate God, conceived by the Holy Spirit, living without sin, rising from the dead, ascending into heaven, and so forth.
In modern dogmatics that proceeds from this philosophical thought, no place therefore remains for Christ other than as a religious genius, a teacher of virtue, a prophet who understood religion most deeply, revealed God’s love most clearly, and expressed the unity and communion of God and the human being most plainly; but the person of Christ himself stands in fact outside the essence of Christianity; Strauss, _Dogm._ II 214 f.; _Alte u. neue Gl._², 24 f.; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ § 116 f.; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 128; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ II² 580 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._² § 588; Scholten, _Initia_ c. 4, p. 171; Kielstra, _Het godsd. leven_, 1890, p. 39.
Not without reason, with a view to this modern theology, von Hartmann spoke of a crisis and a _Selbstzersetzung des Christendoms_; _Die Krisis des Christ. u. die moderne Theologie_, 1880; _Die Selbstzersetzung des Christ. u. die Religion der Zukunft_³, 1888.
And Dr. Boekenoogen, “Christol. Beschouwingen,” _Theol. Tĳdschr._, March, May, Sept., Nov. 1892; Nov. 1897, attempted in vain still to maintain the doctrine of Christ in modern dogmatics as a symbolic imagining of religious ideas.

Schleiermacher took another direction.
Although decisively rejecting the ecclesiastical doctrine of Christ, he nevertheless attempted to avoid the error of speculative philosophy, namely, seeking the essence of Christianity in an abstract idea and detaching it from the historical person of Christ.
To this end he proceeded from the experience of the church, from the Christian consciousness whose content is reconciliation and communion with God.
The ultimate cause of this can be found nowhere other than in the founder of the Christian church, in whom the consciousness of God must therefore have been present in absolute strength.
And this strength of his God-consciousness was the being of God in him, so that he is the _religiöse Urbild_ of humanity, sinless, perfect, the highest product of the human race, and at the same time brought forth by a creative act of God as the perfect subject of religion.
What matters in the first place is not his teaching but his person, not what he did but what he was, not his ethical example but his religious life; _Chr. Gl._ § 91 f.
By thus seeking the realization of the religious idea not in humanity but in the person of Christ, Schleiermacher exercised a powerful influence and once again secured a place for Christology in dogmatics.
First, Schleiermacher’s influence became noticeable in the attempt, over against Kant, Fichte, and Hegel, to maintain in Christ a wholly special and entirely unique revelation of God.
If the God-consciousness in Christ was absolute and had never been disturbed by any sin, God must also have dwelt in him in an entirely unique manner.
Naturally, this could be attempted in different ways according to how one otherwise thought about the Trinity.
Those who reject the ontological Trinity assume in Christ a special manifestation of God, a perfect indwelling of God, the realization of God’s eternal world-thought or idea of humanity: Rothe, _Ethik_ § 533 f.; Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ I 437–556; Schenkel, _Dogm._ II 2, S. 717, 724; Ewald, Göschel, Redepenning, Beyschlag, and others.
Others do acknowledge an ontological Trinity but conceive the relation of the Son to the Father in a subordinationist manner and therefore arrive at an Arian Christology: Thomasius, Kahnis, Gess, Keerl, Hofstede de Groot, Doedes, and others.
Still others coordinate the Son with the Father and therefore approach the ecclesiastical doctrine: K. I. Nitzsch, Twesten, Müller, Martensen, Sartorius, Liebner, Lange, Voigt, Philippi, Vilmar, Ebrard, Oosterzee, and others.
Next, through Schleiermacher an interest previously uncommon arose in modern Christology concerning the human, historical development of the person of Christ.
The doctrine of the _communicatio idiomatum_ was therefore virtually surrendered; the human nature of Christ was pushed to the foreground; the doctrine of the two states was changed into a life of Jesus, and that life was investigated in its preparation, development, and influence.
The history of Israel, of the classical world, and especially of Jesus’ own time therefore became very popular (_Neutest. Zeitgesch._ by Hausrath, Schürer, and others); further, the incarnation had to be regarded not as contingently necessitated by sin but as flowing from the idea of God and given with creation itself (Steffens, Göschel, Baader, Nitzsch, Martensen, Liebner, Lange, Rothe, Ehrenfeuchter, and others); and then the development of Jesus as a human being had to be traced through its historical course until he became the second Adam, the head of humanity, the _Centralindividuum_ (Rothe, Liebner, Schneckenburger, Martensen, Lange, Neander, Kahnis, Beyschlag, Keerl).
Finally, in modern Christology that still held fast to the confession of Christ as God-man, the endeavor arose to maintain the unity of these two in another and better way than had been done in the confession of Chalcedon and in ecclesiastical dogmatics.
For this purpose, the idea of becoming was applied partly to God himself but then especially to the God-man.
Schelling began this in his second period; cf. vol. II 304 f.
The Son was indeed in a certain sense eternal in the Father, but as generated by the Father, as Son outside (_praeter_) the Father, he first appears in creation.
Even then, however, the Son does not yet exist as an actuality but as a _Potenz_ that can and must actualize itself.
Through human sin, however, the world becomes an _aussergöttliches Sein_, and the Son, who was generated for the sake of that world and remains bound to it, acquires a being independent of the Father not inwardly but outwardly; _Werke_ II 3, S. 317 f.
He was in an intermediate state, ἐν μορφῃ θεου; he becomes Christ, remains bound to the fallen world that the Father leaves to him, leads it back to the Father himself by way of self-annihilation and obedience, and thus at the end of the world himself becomes Son in the perfect sense; II 4, S. 35 f.
This thought concerning the becoming of the God-man exercised great influence in theosophical circles, for example, upon Baader, Steffens, and others.
Even Rothe and Dorner adopted the thought that God or the Logos came to dwell in the historical person of Jesus in the same measure as he developed himself into a religious personality, into spirit; the incarnation of God increases with the human being’s becoming God.
In another yet related manner, an explanation of the God-man was attempted through the doctrine of the κενωσις, that is, through the supposition that in the incarnation the Logos emptied himself of all or some attributes to the level of human development and then gradually regained them in the way of development; cf. Gess, _Die Lehre v. d. Person Christi_, 1856, S. 281 f., 309 f.; Liebner, _Die Chr. Dogm. aus d. christol. Princip_, 1849, and _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1858; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 409–445; Sartorius, _Lehre v. d. heiligen Liebe_ II² 21; Lange, _Dogm._ II 594 f.; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 350 f.; Schöberlein, _Prinzip u. System der Dogm._, 167 f.; Martensen, _Dogm._ § 133; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ II 1, S. 20 f.; Delitzsch, _Bibl. Psych._², 326 f.; Kahnis, _Dogm._ II 83; Frank, _Chr. Wahrheit_ II 137 f.; Kübel, _Ueber den Unterschied zw. der posit. u. d. liber. Richtung in der mod. Theol._², 1893, S. 124; H. Schmidt, “Zur Lehre v. d. Person Christi,” _Neue Kirchl. Zeits._ VII, 1896, S. 972–1005, especially 982 f.; Godet, _Comm._ on John 1:14; Grétillat, _Exposé_ IV 180 s.; Recolin, _La personne de J. C. et la théorie de la kenosis_, Paris, 1890; Saussaye, cf. my _Theol. van Ch. d. l. S._, 44; Oosterzee, _Chr. Dogm._ II 494; Kähler, Herzog³ 4.

Finally, Ritschl’s Christology agrees with Schleiermacher’s; only, adhering more closely to Kant’s philosophy, it places more emphasis on the work than on the person of Christ and assigns a larger place to the ethical in the essence of Christianity.
Ritschl too rejects from the doctrine of Christ all metaphysics and everything condemned by natural science and historical criticism, for example, preexistence, supernatural conception, resurrection, ascension, and return.
In this respect Christ is an ordinary human being.
But his distinctive character lay in his vocation, in the work he performed, namely, establishing a kingdom of God.
As an ethical person, Christ stands high above all human beings; his will was perfectly one with the will of God, with the plan and purpose that God had proposed to himself with respect to the world and humanity.
But for this reason Christ also has great religious significance; in him God himself, his grace and faithfulness, his will and intention for humanity, came to revelation; Christ showed us and confirmed by his death that the kingdom of God is the destiny of all human beings, that his will must become the will of the whole of humanity.
In this consists Christ’s royal power, his dominion over the world; and in this also consists his deity.
Christ is not God in a metaphysical sense, but in his case the name God indicates his rank and standing; it is not a name of essence but a name of office; Christ may be called God because with respect to us he occupies the place of God and has the value of God; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 358 f.; Kaftan, _Wesen der chr. Rel._, 1881, S. 295 f.; Id., _Brauchen wir ein neues Dogma_, 1890, S. 49–72; Id., _Dogmatik_, 1897, S. 404 f.; Schultz, on Rom. 9:5, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ XIII, 1868, S. 462–507; _Die Lehre v. d. Gottheit Christi_, 1881; Gottschick, _Die Kirchlichkeit der s. g. kirchl. Theologie_, 1890, S. 207; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, 513; Lobstein, _Etudes Christologiques_, Strasbourg, 1892; Chapuis, _La transformation du dogme christol. au sein de la théol. moderne_, Lausanne, Bridel, 1893.
