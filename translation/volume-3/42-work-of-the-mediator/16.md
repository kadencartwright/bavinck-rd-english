## 16.

Understood in this way, the doctrine of _satisfactio vicaria_ still has to be defended only against the objection that such substitution is impossible in the moral domain. First, however, it must be observed in reply that the idea of substitution is deeply grounded in human nature in the moral domain as well, has been embodied among all peoples in priesthood and sacrifice, and has also been expressed in many ways in poetry and mythology. Origen already compared Christ in his death with those who, according to classical traditions, died for their fatherland in order to deliver it from plague or other disasters, for it appears to lie in hidden laws within the nature of things that the voluntary death of a righteous person for the common good breaks the power of evil spirits; _C. Cels._ I 31. Christian theology therefore often cited the examples of Codrus, Curtius, Cratinus, Zaleucus, Damon and Phintias, and the hostages in order thereby to illuminate the vicarious suffering of Christ. Naturally, these examples have no value other than to show that the idea of substitution occupied a large place in the thought-world of the Greeks and Romans. The same is true of tragedy, whose fundamental idea can certainly not always be rendered by _Schuld und Sühne_ but can indeed be rendered by _Leidenschaft und Leid_. In many tragedies the death of the hero is not an actual atonement for committed sin, but it is nevertheless always a deliverance necessitated by some mistake, error, or the like, and therefore ultimately reconciles us and grants satisfaction. But even understood in this way, tragedy proclaims a great truth: all human greatness walks along abysses of guilt, and satisfaction comes only when the noble and great perish in death. The downfall of Orestes, Oedipus, Antigone, Romeo and Juliet, Max and Thekla, Iphigenia, and so forth reconciles us with them and with their race; _alle menschlichen Gebrechen sühnet reine Menschlichheit_ (Goethe). And so it is often in history too: the last, noble Constantine, fighting and dying for his people and land, is an atonement for the horrors of the Byzantine emperors, and Louis XVI, innocent in comparison with his predecessors, expiates in his death the sins of his house. If the history of families and lineages were known to us, it would furnish us with many similar examples. In _de mortuis nil nisi bene_ we all honor the reconciling power of suffering and death. Indeed, all life and joy here on earth are the fruit of pain and death. Everything lives from the death of another. The grain of wheat must die in order to bear fruit. What one has sown is reaped by another. In the pain of childbirth, and sometimes in dying, the mother gives life to her child. Every birth, including in the domain of thought and art, is out of darkness into light. A few labor, struggle, and suffer, and others enjoy the fruit of their work. We all live from the treasures of preceding generations acquired through exertion. All the noble goods of humanity were won through struggle and suffering by a few for all. Love especially bears a vicarious character; here on earth it is scarcely conceivable otherwise than as fellow suffering, _συμπαθεια_. Whoever loves most suffers most. The mother suffers for, in, and with her child; the father bears grief in his heart over the wandering of his son. Nature and humanity teach that there are _stellvertretende Kräfte_. Cf. above, p. 135, and further Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ X 24; Turretin, _De satisf._ 51; Petrus de Witte, _Wederl. der Soc. dwal._ II 221 f.; Bushnell, _Vicarious sacrifice_, 1866; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 622; de Maistre, _Soirées de St. Petersbourg_, _éclairc. sur les sacrifices_.

All these examples and lines of reasoning are undoubtedly suited to elucidate Christ’s vicarious suffering to some extent. Over against the individualism and atomism that tear humanity apart and know nothing of the mysticism of love, they are of exceptional value. Yet they are not able to explain Christ’s suffering. Many do stop with these examples and try to understand Christ’s suffering as a natural consequence of his entrance into our sinful communion, for example Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 104, 4; Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ § 876; Lange, _Dogm._ II 840, 841; H. Schultz, _Der Begriff des stellvertr. Leidens_, Basel, 1864, and so forth. But in this way Christ’s sacrifice does not receive its due. Human sympathy was indeed a cause of deep, painful suffering for Christ, and above all for him as the holy and merciful High Priest, Matt. 8:17; 9:36; 14:14, and so forth, but it is not the only and principal cause, any more than hunger and thirst, persecution by his enemies, temptation by Satan, and abandonment by his disciples were. For then the suffering would have been only suffering and not punishment for Christ, and he himself would have been no more than a witness, a martyr, a sufferer, differing from others only in degree. But Christ himself regarded his suffering as a punishment laid upon him by God for our sins, Matt. 20:28; 26:28; 27:46, and the whole of Scripture teaches that he was made sin for us and became a curse, 2 Cor. 5:21; Gal. 3:13. Those go a step further who explain Christ’s suffering realistically from the place he occupies in the human race. For he is not an individual alongside others but _Centralindividuum_. He assumed not a human person but human nature. That nature bore sin and lay under the curse, and thus with that nature Christ also took its guilt and punishment upon himself. Just as Adam could therefore be our representative because he was the progenitor of the whole human race, so Christ is the substitute for the church, which as his body is born from him as the Head and is one with him. And just as, for example, we are punished on our back for what we have done wrong with our hand, so Christ was punished for our sins because he is one with us; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 48, arts. 1, 2; _Suppl._, qu. 13, art. 2; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 57 f., 533 f.; Dale, _The atonement_, lect. 10; Scott Lidgett, _The spiritual principle of the atonement_, ch. 7; Sartorius, _Lehre v. d. h. Liebe_ II 67 f.; Bilderdijk, _Opst. v. godg. en zedek. inhoud_ I 1–15, and so forth. This realistic-mystical conception of Christ’s substitution is in itself entirely correct and is also clearly taught by Scripture. For believers themselves have been crucified, died, been buried, raised, and seated in heaven with Christ, Rom. 6–8; Gal. 2:20; Eph. 2:6; Col. 2:11; 3:3, and so forth; cf. Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 114–121. Since Christ is not only reconciler but also redeemer, and had not only objectively to remove the guilt of sin but also subjectively to break the power of sin, this mystical union of Christ and believers forms an essential and indispensable component in the work of salvation. Nevertheless, it is not the only and the first relation that exists between Christ and his own. In Scripture it is built upon the federal relation: Rom. 6–8 follows Rom. 3–5. When it is detached from this relation, it loses the foundation upon which it must rest, begins to seek its support in pantheism, which changes re-creation into a process, and increasingly shifts objective reconciliation into subjective redemption. Shedd, for example, already makes the imputation of Christ’s righteousness depend upon regeneration and faith; ibid., 534; cf. Dale 422, and so forth. Only when Christ is regarded first of all as Head of the covenant and has taken the place of his own in a federal, legal sense can this mystical union be maintained in its scriptural significance together with the objective reconciliation of Christ’s sacrifice. The covenant of grace precedes the person and sacrifice of Christ. For that covenant does not begin after Christ has accomplished his work—with the Holy Spirit and the benefits of regeneration and faith—but Christ himself also stands in that covenant. He is its surety and mediator, Heb. 7:22; 8:6; 12:24. His blood is covenant blood and therefore atoning, Matt. 26:28. Indeed, still more, the covenant of grace was not first established in time, but it has its foundation in eternity, rests in the _pactum salutis_, and is in the first place a covenant of the three persons in the divine being itself. Father, Son, and Spirit are all three active in that covenant; and so far is it from first beginning in time with the activity of the Holy Spirit that it rather has its existence and stability from eternity in the counsel of the Triune God. From this Christ’s _satisfactio vicaria_ is also explained. It rests upon an ordinance, upon a free, almighty, gracious disposition of God. That absolutely does not mean that it is arbitrary and irrational. All kinds of relations in nature and humanity offer analogies for Christ’s substitution. But analogy here, just as with Adam, see above, p. 129 f., is not and cannot be identity. Both occupy a unique place in humanity. They alone are heads of the whole human race. Their influence and activity extend to all places and times. And Christ again stands above Adam. For Adam was humanity’s representative; Christ is its substitute. Adam acted in our name but took nothing from us upon himself. Christ came to us, placed himself in our place, bore our guilt and punishment, and acquired our righteousness. Adam was head of a covenant of works that was unstable. Christ is Head of a better covenant that knows no instability. Adam was a human being, though without sin, earthy from the earth. Christ was the incarnate Word, the only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth, the Lord from heaven. Adam ruined what was good; Christ restored and perfected what was ruined. As far as the covenant of grace surpasses the covenant of works and the gospel surpasses the law, so far does Christ stand above Adam. His _satisfactio vicaria_ cannot even be comprehended according to the covenant of works with its law. It is indeed not _contra legem_, for it confirms the law, but it is nevertheless _supra legem_ and very far surpasses all our thoughts. It cannot be reduced to any general rule or explained by a general law, for it is not one phenomenon alongside others but is a concrete fact, wholly unique in the history of humanity, explained by nothing and itself explaining everything, resting in a special ordinance of God. And this ordinance of God is not an isolated decree of the will but bears a covenantal character. The _satisfactio vicaria_ has its foundation in the counsel of the Triune God, in the life of the highest, perfect, and eternal love, in the unshakable covenant of redemption. According to the ordinances of that covenant, Christ takes the place of his own and exchanges their sin for his righteousness, their death for his life. _Ὠ της γλυκειας ἀνταλλαγης, ὠ της ἀνεξιχνιαστου δημιουργιας, ὠ των ἀπροσδοκητων εἰεργεσιων, ἱνα ἀνομια μεν πολλων ἐν δικαιῳ ἑνι κρυβῃ, δικαιοσυνη δε ἑνος πολλους ἀνομους δικαιωσῃ_; _Ep. ad Diogn._ 9. Cf. A. A. Hodge, _The atonement_ 198, and so forth; Hugh Martin, _The atonement_, p. 9, and so forth.
