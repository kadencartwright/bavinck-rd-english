## 5.

The work of Christ, however, like his person, was understood by others in an entirely different way.
The Ebionites saw in Christ only a prophet who by his teaching and example gives the human being strength for the struggle against sin.
For the Gnostics, Christ was an aeon of divine wisdom who had appeared on earth in an apparently human form in order through illumination and knowledge to liberate human beings from the bonds of matter and make them πνευματικοι.
Over against this speculative rationalism, the Montanists maintained the supernatural, transcendent character of Christianity, not only in the person of Christ but also in the revelation continuing through the Holy Spirit, which is still now the portion of believers and causes the Christian religion to pass from childhood into mature age.
Likewise, in the dualistic, pantheistic, apocalyptic, and libertinist sects of the Middle Ages, no other significance remained for Christ than that in his time he had revealed to human beings their true essence and destiny, whereby they themselves could now, in the age of the Spirit, become what Christ was; what Scripture teaches concerning Christ is realized in every Christian; the actual dying and rising of Christ takes place in the regeneration of every human being.
All these ideas continued to operate in the age of the Reformation and led to vigorous opposition to the doctrine of Luther and Calvin.
Not Christ for us but Christ in us, not the Word but the Spirit, was the essence of religion.
Osiander taught that Christ had brought the eternal, divine essential righteousness with him in his human nature, pours this into his own through faith, and thus justifies them, _Herzog_² 11, 125 f.
Carlstadt, Frank, Schwenckfeld, Weigel, and others saw trust in Christ’s imputed righteousness as a dangerous error; our salvation lies not in what Christ does outside us and for us but in what he does in and through us, in mystical fellowship with God, Erbkam, _Gesch. d. prot. Sekten_, 247 f., 340 f., 441 f.
This mystical conception of redemption through Christ also later found acceptance among many.
Böhme regarded God’s wrath and death as real, physical powers that Christ conquered through his death and in whose place he pours out a new divine life into humanity, Joh. Claassen, _J. Böhme, sein Leben u. seine theos. Werke_ III 31–76.
Among the Quakers, redemption does indeed have its cause in Christ, but Christ himself is nothing other than the summation of the grace and light in humanity; the proper, true redemption is that which occurs in us, Barclay, _Verantwoording van de ware Christ. Godgel._, Amsterdam, 1757, p. 154.
Antoinette Bourignon and Poiret called vicarious suffering impossible and improper; Christ came to earth not to make satisfaction for us but to preach God’s forgiving love to us and to cleanse us from sin through his teaching and example, M. Vitringa VI 51.
J. C. Dippel likewise understood the suffering and death of Christ as an example of his spiritual mediation, through which he kills the old human being in us and causes the new to arise; redemption takes place in us; an objective atonement is unnecessary, for there is no wrath in God, ibid., 56; Dorner, _Entw._ II 924.
According to Zinzendorf, Christ is the Creator and Sustainer of all things, the Jehovah of the Old Testament, who revealed God’s love in becoming so small, so poor, so humble, and in suffering so much; his suffering is not so much punishment and satisfaction to God as voluntary and loving martyrdom; his blood, his death, is thereby the source of humanity’s life; the martyr wounds in Christ’s side are the _matrix_ of the human race, the origin of the Spirit who pours himself out from Christ into all, Plitt, _Zinzendorfs Theol._ I 291; II 194 f.; Schneckenburger, _Vorles. über die Lehrbegriffe der klein. prot. Kirchenparteien_, 195 f.
Swedenborg regarded the belief that suffering on the cross was redemption as a fundamental error that, together with the doctrine of the Trinity, had brought the church to ruin.
Suffering on the cross was not redemption but was Jesus’ final temptation and, because he remained standing, the means of his glorification; then the human in him was united with his Father’s divine nature, then God truly became human, and he could approach and conquer the hostile, sensual powers of hell; redemption is a continuing subjection of hell and ordering of heaven brought about by God as a human being, Swedenborg, _Die wahre christl. Religion_², 1873, pp. 169–205.

But opposition to the church’s doctrine concerning Christ’s work came not only from the mystical side but also from the rationalistic side.
To this may be reckoned the doctrine of Stancarus that Christ is our Mediator and our righteousness only according to his human nature, _Herzog_² 14, 590 f.; and also the denial of the _obedientia activa_ by Karg (Parsimonius) and by Joh. Piscator, who first set forth his ideas in a letter in the year 1604, included in the _Epist. praest. virorum_, p. 156; cf. _Theses theol._ XV 18, 19.
Karg retracted his view in 1570, but Piscator found support from Martinius, Crocius, Pierius, Pareus, Wendelinus, H. Alting, and others, and later exercised a harmful influence upon Reformed theology through Camero, Placaeus, Capellus, and others.
But the most serious and thorough opposition to the _satisfactio vicaria_ came from the Socinians.
They do still call Christ prophet, priest, and king, _Cat. Rac._, qu. 101, but in fact they make the priestly office an appendage of the kingly office.
When Christ was on earth, he was only a prophet who, before the beginning of his public ministry, had been taken up into heaven by God (_raptus in coelum_, John 3:13, 31; 6:36, 62; 8:28; 10:28; _Cat. Rac._, qu. 195), had there been acquainted with the truth by God himself, and was thus able to perfect the law with new commandments, _Cat. Rac._, qu. 209 f., and to promise eternal life to those who kept those commandments, ibid., qu. 352, and the sanctifying power of the Spirit, qu. 361.
Christ confirmed this teaching of his by his sinless life, by his miracles, and especially by his death and resurrection, ibid., qu. 374.
His death was necessary in order to cause his followers to persevere to the end in their piety and holy walk, qu. 380, and to confirm God’s love for us clearly and plainly, qu. 383; and his resurrection served to show us by his example that those who obey God are delivered from every death and to grant Christ himself the power to bestow eternal life upon all who obey him, qu. 384.
The death of Christ therefore had an entirely different significance here than in the doctrine of the church; it formed no independent moment in Christ’s work but served only, on the one hand, to confirm his teaching and, on the other hand, to bring him to the resurrection, which first made him King and Lord in heaven, qu. 386.
And strictly speaking, he first became this through his ascension.
The resurrection still belongs to the state of humiliation, for even after it he still had a mortal body, qu. 465.
But at the ascension he received a glorified body and was exalted to King, Lord, and God.
As such he received from God the power to support in every need those who follow his example and finally to reward them with immortality.
That he _can_ do this is his kingly office; that he _wills_ to do this is his high-priestly office, qu. 476.
Thus he was not yet a priest on earth, qu. 483; his death was not the sacrifice proper but the introduction and preparation for it; he now brings the true sacrifice in heaven, just as the Old Testament high priest first accomplished atonement in the most holy place, qu. 413.
And that sacrifice consists in his accomplishing atonement, that is, delivering us from the service and punishment of sin.
From this standpoint, the Socinians had to oppose the doctrine of satisfaction as strongly as that of the Trinity and the deity of Christ; cf. especially _Catech. Rac._, qu. 388–414, and F. Socinus, _Praelect. theol._ c. 15, and _de Jesu Christo Servatore_, _Bibl. Fr. Pol._ II 121–252.
First, in their judgment it conflicts with Scripture: its advocates cannot bring forward a single certain passage for their view; Scripture says clearly that God forgives sin by grace, and forgiveness excludes satisfaction; the expression that Christ suffered for us, and so forth, has no substitutionary significance but says only that Christ suffered for our benefit, not to satisfy God but to deliver us from sin; the words redemption, atonement, and the like indicate only that Christ showed us the way to be delivered from the service and punishment of sin, but by no means that God had to be reconciled through a sacrifice, for God was graciously disposed toward us and made this known to us through Christ.
Further, satisfaction is also not necessary: righteousness and mercy in God are not in conflict with each other, nor are they attributes that belong to God by nature, but they are _effectus ipsius voluntatis_ and depend upon his will; whether God wills to punish or forgive sins is determined not at all by his nature but by his will; God can forgive sins without satisfaction just as well as, and better than, a human being; indeed, his righteousness is nullified by satisfaction because it punishes the innocent and lets the guilty go free, and his mercy loses its value if it can manifest itself only after satisfaction; God has moreover always promised forgiveness to the penitent and willed that we should imitate him in this.
Next, satisfaction is also impossible: the _obedientia passiva_ is impossible because monetary debts, but not personal moral debts, can be transferred to another; to punish an innocent person for another’s sin is unjust and cruel; and even if this were possible, that other person could at most undergo for one single human being the punishment of sin, that is, eternal death, but never for many or for all human beings; and as for the _obedientia activa_, this is still less possible, for everyone is personally obligated to keep God’s law for himself, but one can never take this over for another; moreover, the _obedientia passiva_ and _activa_ conflict with one another, for whoever fulfills the one is free from the other; Christ therefore fulfilled neither of them, for he did not suffer eternal death but rose from the dead on the third day; however severe it was, his suffering was finite; his divine nature could not make it infinite in value because it could not suffer and die, or it would make every moment in that suffering infinite and thus all the rest superfluous; moreover, what benefit could that be to us, since the human being had sinned, and how could God the Son satisfy God himself, the Father?
Finally, the doctrine of satisfaction is also harmful, since it exalts Christ with his mercy above God with his demand of satisfaction, obligates us to greater gratitude toward Christ than toward God, and also opens the door to carelessness and ungodliness; after all, satisfaction has been made for all sins, so we can then sin as much as we wish.
And the death of Christ intended precisely that we should be delivered from sin and walk in a new, godly life!

Faustus Socinus’s criticism of the doctrine of satisfaction was so sharp and complete that later opponents could do nothing but repeat his arguments.
The Remonstrants still tried to maintain Christ’s satisfaction but in fact adopted all the objections brought against it; they taught not only, with Roman Catholics and Lutherans, that Christ had made satisfaction for all human beings, but also denied that Christ suffered all the punishments God had placed upon sin, that he underwent eternal death, and that his _obedientia activa_ was substitutionary.
Even his suffering and death were no _satisfactio plenaria pro peccatis_, no _solutio debitorum_, which would after all make forgiveness on God’s side and faith on our side superfluous, but an offering, a perfect devotion of Christ to God maintained even unto death, which God regarded as sufficient for all sins.
On the ground of that offering, God now has the forgiveness of sins offered to all through the gospel so that everyone who believes may be saved; but Christ did not acquire actual salvation for his own, only the possibility of being saved for all; whether anyone is actually saved depends upon himself, _Conf._ and _Apol. Conf._ c. 8; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ III c. 18–23.
Hugo Grotius also made an attempt to justify the doctrine of satisfaction in his _Defensio fidei catholicae de satisfactione Christi_, 1617.
To this end he derived it neither from God’s will nor from his punitive righteousness but from the _justitia Dei rectoria_, that is, from the necessity for God to uphold order and law, right and righteousness, in the world and to take account of the _bonum commune_.
By conceiving this legal order, however, as a world order standing outside God, he made God’s righteousness subservient and subordinate to it; changed Christ’s satisfaction into undeserved suffering, a penal example to deter others, a measure of governmental wisdom; and left unexplained why Christ had to be God for this and how God could thus cause an innocent person to suffer and could regard his suffering as satisfaction for others.
Yet however many objections the Socinian and Remonstrant doctrine could provoke, it gradually gained the upper hand.
The supernaturalists, such as Michaelis, Storr, Morus, Knapp, Steudel, Reinhard, _Dogm._ § 107, Muntinghe, Vinke, Egeling, _Weg der zaligheid_ II 175, and so forth, although sometimes still thinking that they defended the church’s doctrine, in substance presented no other doctrine than that of the Remonstrants or of Hugo Grotius.
The same was the case in the New England Theology.
The elder Jonathan Edwards still defended the orthodox doctrine, _Works_ I 582, but Edwards Jr., Smalley, Maxey, Burge, Dwight, Emmons, and Spring presented Grotius’s governmental theory; cf. E. Park, _The Atonement: Discourses and Treatises of Jon. Edwards, Smalley, Maxey, Emmons, etc._, New York, 1860; A. A. Hodge, _The Atonement_, p. 328.
But many went still further and rejected the whole doctrine of satisfaction.
Ernesti, _de officio Christi triplici_, _Opusc. theol._, 1773, p. 413, opposed the doctrine of the three offices on the ground that the names prophet, priest, and king are unclear Old Testament representations and each office already includes the other two in itself.
In his _de obedientia Christi activa commentatio_, 1755, Töllner revived all the Socinian objections, not only against Christ’s active obedience but against the whole doctrine of satisfaction.
And with him Bahrdt, Steinbart, Eberhard, Löffler, Henke, Wegscheider, _Inst._ § 140–142, Hobbes, _Leviathan_, ch. 41, Locke, Chubb, Coleridge, John Taylor, Priestley, and others argued that Christ was not a revelation of God’s righteousness but of his love and mercy; that God punishes sin only with such natural punishments as flow from sin itself and bear a pedagogical character; that the doctrine of substitution is an absurd idea of Augustine and Anselm and does not occur at all in Scripture, or only by accommodation in Old Testament symbols; that Christ’s active obedience, if it were substitutionary, would make all faith, repentance, and moral improvement unnecessary on our side; and above all that forgiveness of sins, adoption as God’s children, and justification cannot precede moral renewal or sanctification but must follow it and depend upon it.
Religion was built upon morality; the center of gravity was shifted from the objective to the subjective; _Versöhnung_ was made dependent upon _Erlösung_; God was made a servant of the human being.
Thus the death of Christ could now be only an example of virtue and confirmation of the truth (Eberhard, Löffler); or a sacrifice for the benefit of human beings and a proof of God’s love (Schwarze); or a factual declaration by God that he wills to forgive the sins of whoever repents (Morus, Köppen, Klaiber, Stäudlin); or a means to awaken trust in God and deter from sin (Töllner, Egeling); or also to free us from the false idea that God is wrathful and punishes (Steinbart); or a symbolic representation of the moral human being’s voluntary assumption of the punishment he has deserved in his sinful condition, Kant, _Religion_, 84 f.; cf. van der Willigen, _Oordeelk. Overzigt over de versch. wijzen, op welke men zich heeft voorgesteld het verband tusschen den dood van J. C. en de zaligheid der menschen_, _Godg. Bijdr._ II, 1828, pp. 485–603; Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._, 608 f.; Baur, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_, 478 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I, c. 7, 8.
