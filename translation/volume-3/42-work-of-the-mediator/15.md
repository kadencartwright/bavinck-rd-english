## 15.

From of old, however, very weighty objections have been brought against this _satisfactio vicaria_. One person can take over another’s monetary debts and pay them for him, but sins are moral debts and attach to the person. By their very nature they cannot be taken over by another. This conflicts with God’s righteousness, which does not hold the guilty innocent or the innocent guilty. It conflicts with the nature of sin, for whoever wished to bear it for another could nevertheless never take over the most dreadful thing in sin—that is, self-accusation, repentance, pangs of conscience—but only external suffering and dying; and this would then be for him not a punishment for sin but a chastisement, a test, a martyrdom. It conflicts with reality, for Christ did not bear God’s wrath but always shared in his love and favor. He did not bear the whole punishment of sin, for he died neither spiritual nor eternal death. And even if he had borne it, then he nevertheless bore it only for one single human being, never for many, for he bore that punishment only once. Even if he was God, this cannot increase the value of his sacrifice, since his deity could not suffer, above p. 324. Many of these objections flow from misunderstanding, which must therefore first be removed. First, then, it is entirely true that Christ was never personally, for and on account of himself, the object of God’s wrath. He was never a sinner in his own person. Gnostics and Anabaptists indeed frequently distinguished between a divine, heavenly, immortal, holy corporeality and a human, earthly, unclean, mortal corporeality in Christ, above pp. 275 f. And the Antinomians understood substitution in such a way that not only the guilt and punishment but also the stain and uncleanness of sin were transferred to Christ. The exchange between Christ and the elect was so absolute that he himself is sin and they are righteousness. In Christ they were grieved over their sins, justified, regenerated. The sins they themselves commit are no longer sins, no longer trouble them in conscience, no longer need forgiveness, and are only deeds of the flesh, of the old human being, Hulsius, _De hedendaagsche Antinomianery_, 2nd ed., 1738, pp. 377 f.; Hoornbeek, _Summa Controv._, 1653, p. 704; Witsius, _Misc. S._ II 758–780. In later times these views were sometimes renewed by the Methodists, Schneckenburger, _Vorles. über die kleineren protest. Parteien_ 146; by the Irvingites, Herzog² 7, 154; by some followers of Kohlbrugge, Bula, _Die Versöhnung des Menschen mit Gott durch Christum_, 1874; Böhl, _Von der Incarnation des göttl. Wortes_, 1884; idem, _Dogmatik_ 299 f.; cf. Kuyper, _De vleeschw. des Woords_, Amsterdam, 1887, introduction and pp. 155 f. But Scripture teaches as decisively and clearly as possible that Christ personally was free from all sins, above p. 296. And the few passages appealed to for the opposite, John 1:14; Rom. 8:3; Heb. 2:14, state only that the Son of God assumed a weak nature subject to suffering and death, not that he himself was a sinner in the subjective sense. Some theologians, such as Chrysostom, Oecumenius, Luther, Marlorat, and also Calvin on Gal. 3:13, have indeed called Christ a sinner, but meant this only in the objective sense, as Paul says that Christ was made sin and became a curse, 2 Cor. 5:21; Gal. 3:13; cf. Isa. 53:12. By this the apostle does not indicate that Christ was in himself a sinner and accursed, but that he was regarded and treated by God as one who was guilty of transgressing the law and had loaded its curse upon himself. Self-accusation, repentance, pangs of conscience, confession of personal sins could therefore not occur in Christ. Spiritual death, as incapacity for good and inclination to every evil, was not suffered by him. Precisely in order to bear the sins of others and satisfy for them, he himself could and might not be a sinner. The _permutatio personarum_ that took place between Christ and his own is to be understood not in a pantheistic-physical or mystical sense but bears a legal character. Christ voluntarily entered into that relation to the law and its demands in which we stood toward it through our transgression. In the second place, the substitutive character of Christ’s obedience naturally also entails that it is equivalent, perfectly answering the demand of the law. But this equivalence was nevertheless conceived differently by the Reformation than by Rome. Duns Scotus judged that even a holy human being or an angel could have satisfied for our sins if God had seen fit, for _tantum valet omne creatum oblatum, pro quanto Deus acceptat illud et non plus_, _Sent._ III, dist. 20, qu. un., n. 9; cf. dist. 19, qu. un., n. 7. The Remonstrants later likewise taught that not _justitia Dei_ but only _aequitas_ demanded any satisfaction and that _meritum quod Christus persolvit juxta Dei Patris aestimationem persolutum est_, Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ III 21, 6, 8, 9; 22, 2; Episcopius, _Inst. Theol._ IV, sect. 5, c. 3. Directly opposite this, Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 48, art. 2, called the _passio Christi_ not only _sufficiens_ but _superabundans satisfactio pro peccatis humani generis_; cf. _Cat. Rom._ I 5, qu. 13, 2; _Theol. Wirceb._ IV 317; Billuart, _Summa S. Thomae_, part III, vol. 2, pp. 206–226; Scheeben III 206, 343, and so forth. Even the question was treated whether one drop of Christ’s blood would not have been sufficient for atonement; cf. Quenstedt, _Theol._ III 327; Dorner, _Entw. der Lehre v. d. Person Christi_ II 843. This whole conception in both Thomas and Duns Scotus rests upon a sensible, quantitative calculation of Christ’s suffering. In principle the Reformation broke with this system of calculation. This appears from the fact that it rejected both the _acceptilatio_ (from _acceptum ferre_) of Scotus and the superabundance of Thomas, Voetius, _Disp._ II 247; Mastricht V 18, 38; Moor III 1084; Alting, _Theol. probl._, problem 41; that in Christ’s work it included active alongside passive obedience; that it called Christ’s sacrifice equivalent but not identical to what we owed; that it regarded it as completely sufficient, so that in neither the Roman Catholic nor the Remonstrant manner did it need supplementation by our faith and good works. And in particular the Reformed said that Christ’s work was in itself completely sufficient for the atonement of the sins of the whole world; that if he had willed to save a smaller number it could not have been less, and if he had willed to save a larger number or all human beings it would not have needed to be greater. Sins are indeed not monetary debts, and satisfaction is not a calculation. The transfer of our sins to Christ did not take place so mechanically that they were first precisely added up from all the elect, then laid upon Christ in that sum, and each individually satisfied by him. Neither did Christ pass through all human ages and in each separately satisfy for the sins of every age, as Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ II 22, 4, and others represented it. Neither did he suffer precisely the same, _idem_, as we, nor in the same manner. For consciousness of guilt and so forth could not occur in him. He did not know spiritual death as inclination to evil. And eternal death he suffered not in form and duration but only intensively and qualitatively, as abandonment by God, Thomas, _S. Th._ III, qu. 46, art. 5; qu. 48, art. 2; Calvin, _Inst._ II 16, 12; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 12, 9, 21; Moor IV 122–133; Witsius, _Misc. S._ II 770; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 454; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ II 239; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV 2, 29; Sartorius, _Lehre v. d. h. Liebe_ II 75; van Oosterzee, _Dogm._ II 586. Even in _acceptilatio_ there lies some truth, for God’s strict right demanded that every human being personally satisfy for himself; and it was his grace that gave Christ as mediator of the covenant and imputed his righteousness to the covenant partners. Thus we do not arrive at _satisfactio vicaria_ through a quantitative calculation. In the doctrine of satisfaction we deal with magnitudes other than those that can be measured and weighed. Sin is a principle governing and corrupting the whole creation, a power, a kingdom that expands and organizes itself in many actual sins. God’s wrath is an indignation directed against the sin of the whole human race, Heidelberg Catechism 37. His righteousness is that virtue by which he cannot tolerate being denied or dishonored as God by his creatures. Therefore _satisfactio vicaria_ consists in Christ as surety and head entering into that relation to God, to his wrath, his righteousness, his law, in which the human race stood over against them. For that humanity which was given to him for atonement, he was made sin, became a curse, and took its guilt and punishment upon himself. When the Socinians say that Christ could in any event satisfy only for one human being and not for many, because he bore the punishment of sin only once, this reasoning proceeds from the same quantitative calculation as Duns Scotus’s _acceptilatio_ and Thomas’s superabundance. For although the sin that entered the world through Adam manifests itself in an innumerable series of sinful thoughts, words, and deeds; although God’s wrath is felt individually by every guilty human child; it nevertheless always is and remains the one indivisible law that was violated, the one indivisible wrath of God kindled against the sin of the whole human race, the one indivisible righteousness of God injured by sin, the one immutable, eternal God mocked by sin. And therefore Christ’s punishment is also one, but one that intensively and qualitatively is equivalent to the sin and guilt of the whole human race, atones for God’s wrath against that whole human race, fulfills the whole law, fully restores God’s righteousness, and brings God himself in all his virtues of truth and righteousness, love and grace, once more to acknowledgment in the human race. For this punishment was also laid upon him who was not merely an individual alongside others but the second Adam, head of the human race, the Son of Man and Son of God at the same time.
