## 22.

If this, then, is the great work assigned to Christ by the Father—namely, to be Savior in the full sense and bring about the whole re-creation—then it is immediately evident that the state of exaltation is just as necessary for it as the state of humiliation. They must have a poor conception of Christ’s person and work who think that the resurrection, ascension, and session at God’s right hand can be surrendered without harm to faith and life and who are content with the historical image of Jesus, which continues to live and exert influence in history in the same way as that of other great persons. Conversely, it is understandable that whoever sees in Jesus no more than an exceptionally devout human being and in his work nothing other than a religious-ethical reformation considers the whole state of exaltation worthless for Christian life and denies and opposes the facts of the resurrection and ascension. Scripture, however, proceeds from an entirely different thought. It is the crucified but also risen and exalted Christ whom the apostles proclaim. From this standpoint of exaltation they view and describe his earthly life, suffering, and death. In his cross he laid the foundations of the work that he now carries out as the exalted Mediator. The cross was his only weapon in the struggle against sin, world, and Satan. Through the cross he triumphed in the sphere of right over every power hostile to God. But therefore in the state of exaltation he also received the divine right, divine appointment, royal power and authority to execute the work of re-creation fully, conquer all his enemies, save all those given him, and complete the whole kingdom of God. On the ground of the one perfect sacrifice made on the cross, he distributes all his benefits in agreement with the Father’s will. These benefits are no physical or magical aftereffect of his earthly life and death. The history of the kingdom of God is no evolutionary process. It is the living Christ, exalted at God’s right hand, who consciously and with sovereign authority distributes all these benefits, gathers his elect, conquers his enemies, and guides world history toward the day of his parousia. He is still always active in heaven as Mediator. He not only was but still is our highest prophet, our only high priest, and our eternal king. Yesterday and today he is the same forever. In the state of humiliation he fulfilled through his life and death the condition for now, in the state of exaltation, on the ground of his perfect sacrifice, freeing God’s creation from bondage to sin and corruption and gathering all things under himself as Head. Re-creation is the continuing act of the Mediator. Christ is exalted at God’s right hand, yet always present and active upon earth—in his church, in office, Word, sacrament, in forgiveness, regeneration, faith, and so forth—as Lord from heaven. In this light the state of exaltation acquires a significance often misunderstood but of the highest importance for doctrine and life. Resurrection and ascension are as necessary for the work of re-creation as incarnation and death on the cross. Accordingly, these facts are not merely mentioned in Scripture but are repeatedly placed in the foreground and explained in their rich significance. According to the whole New Testament, Christ’s resurrection took place on the third day. In Matt. 12:40; Mark 8:31, and so forth, for the sake of the comparison with Jonah, parts of days and nights are counted as complete, M. Vitringa V 597, or are intended only as a general description of a very short time, Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_ 207 f. It consisted in the rising of that same body that was taken down from the cross and buried in the garden of Joseph of Arimathea. It was indeed changed and glorified, so that it became not a σωμα ψυχικον but a σωμα πνευματικον, Luke 24:16, 36; John 20:14, 19; 1 Cor. 15:44 f.; Phil. 3:21, but it nevertheless remained a human σωμα, Matt. 28:5, 9; Luke 24:39, 40, 43; John 20:27; 21:25; Acts 1:11; 1 Cor. 15:37 f.; Rev. 1:7. The various attempts to explain this resurrection in another way—as deception (Reimarus), apparent death (rationalists, Schleiermacher, Hase, Herder, Gfrörer), vision, whether entirely subjective (Strauss, Lang, Holsten, Hausrath, Renan) or objective, brought about by God (Keim, Schweizer, Schenkel)—have proved futile down to the present day, Steude, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1887, pp. 203–294; Nösgen, _Neut. Offenbarung_ I 637 f.; Loofs, _Die Auferstehungsberichte und ihr Wert_, _Christl. Welt_ 33, Leipzig: Mohr, 1898. After the resurrection Christ remained on earth for some time, both to convince his disciples of the truth of his resurrection through various appearances and to prepare them for their office and provide them proof that, although he would not resume his former association with them because after the resurrection he belonged no longer to earth but to heaven, John 20:17, he would nevertheless remain unchangeably the same in love toward them, 13:1, and would be with them eternally to the consummation of the world, Matt. 28:20. The appearances took place during the first eight days in Jerusalem, John 20:26, where the disciples still had to remain on account of the Passover, and ended with Jesus giving his disciples the Holy Spirit and granting them apostolic authority, John 20:22, 23. Later followed his appearances in Galilee, to which the disciples returned and where most of Jesus’ followers lived, John 21; Matt. 28:16; 1 Cor. 15:6. Matthew and John conclude their Gospels with these appearances of Jesus in Galilee and do not mention the ascension. According to Luke 24:49; Acts 1:4, the disciples had to remain in Jerusalem until they were clothed with power from on high. Luke 24 speaks of no appearances in Galilee; Acts 1:3 presupposes them. Certainly at one of the appearances in Galilee Jesus again commanded his disciples to go to Jerusalem and there await the promise of the Father. When he then appears to them in Jerusalem for the final time, he says that they must remain in Jerusalem until that promise is fulfilled. Then he leads them outside as far as Bethany, Luke 24:50, to the Mount of Olives, Acts 1:12. And there he parted from them, διεστη, and was taken up from them into heaven, ἀνεφερετο εἰς τον οὐρανον, wrongly omitted by Tischendorf in Luke 24:51; ἐπηρθη, Acts 1:9; ἀνεληφθη, Acts 1:2, 11, 22; 1 Tim. 3:16. Although not mentioned by John, Matthew, and Mark—whose Gospel according to many ends at 16:8—the ascension nevertheless stands firm on the ground of many clear testimonies of Scripture. John presupposes it, 6:62; 14:2; 20:17. Paul points to it, Rom. 10:6; Eph. 2:6; 4:9, 10; Col. 3:1, and names it in 1 Tim. 3:16. Peter mentions it, 1 Pet. 3:22; cf. Acts 2:33; 3:21; 7:56. The Epistle to the Hebrews knows it, 6:20; 9:24. Further, it underlies the New Testament doctrine of Jesus’ session at God’s right hand, Acts 2:33; 5:31; 7:56; Rom. 8:34; Eph. 1:20; Col. 3:1; Heb. 1:3; 8:1; 12:2; 1 Pet. 3:22; his intercession in heaven, Rom. 8:34; Heb. 7:25; 9:24–28; 1 John 2:1, 2; all the activities he performs from heaven upon earth, principally in his church; and the expectation of his return, Matt. 24:3, and so forth.

This exaltation has first of all the greatest significance for Christ himself. Formerly, dogmatics also ordinarily treated the question whether through his perfect obedience Christ merited anything for himself. Anselm said that Christ indeed merited reward for his death, to which he was not obligated, but ceded it to his own, _Cur Deus homo_ II 19. Most scholastics—Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 18; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 19, art. 3; qu. 48, art. 1; qu. 49, art. 6; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ IV c. 7—further most Roman Catholic theologians—Bellarmine, _De Christo_ V c. 9, 10; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ III, tr. 1, c. 14, qu. 5; idem, _Manuale Controv._ III 2, qu. 4—and very many Reformed theologians—Zanchi, _Op._ VI 121; VIII 477, 502; Piscator on Phil. 2:9; Gomarus on Phil. 2, _Op._ I 530 sq.; Cloppenburg, _Op._ I 305, 888; Rivet, _Op._ II 836; Voetius, _Disp._ II 265–267; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 14, 7; Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ XVIII 39; Moor III 600, and others—gave an affirmative answer to the question above. They then judged that the hearing of prayer, John 11:42; Heb. 5:7, and especially the whole state of exaltation—the resurrection, ascension, session at God’s right hand, and return for judgment—had to be regarded as reward for his merits, Isa. 53:11; Luke 24:26; John 17:4, 5; Phil. 2:9; Heb. 2:10; 12:2. Others, however, said that Christ merited nothing for himself but everything for us, John 17:19; 1 Cor. 1:30; 1 Tim. 1:15, and so forth, and that therefore exaltation was indeed a consequence but no reward for his humiliation, Calvin, _Inst._ II 17, 6; commentary on Phil. 2:9; Polanus, _Syst._ VI 26; Junius, _Theses theol._ 29, 11; Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._ II 7, 8; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ 45; marginal notes of the States Translation at Phil. 2:9, and so forth; and likewise the Lutheran theologians, Gerhard, _Loc._ IV 329; Quenstedt, _Theol._ III 324; Buddeus, _Inst._, p. 787; Hollaz, _Examen_, p. 748, and so forth. With Scripture in hand, however, the question posed above can be answered only affirmatively. It repeatedly presents the state of humiliation as the way and means for Christ to obtain the state of exaltation, Isa. 53:10–12; Matt. 23:12; Luke 24:26; John 10:17. The διο in Phil. 2:9 indicates not merely the _ordo_ and _consequentia_ but specifically the _causa meritoria_. Because Christ humbled himself so deeply, vv. 5–8, God therefore also exalted him so highly. The Epistle to the Hebrews especially repeatedly places strong emphasis upon this meritorious connection between Christ’s humiliation and exaltation, 1:3; 2:9, 10; 5:7–10; 10:12; 12:2. Christ himself was perfected through suffering—that is, not dedicated to God or made morally perfect, but completed, brought to full growth and maturity, made τελειος—consisting in this, that he is now crowned with honor and glory, 2:9, and has become an ἀρχηγος, a cause of eternal salvation, 2:10; 5:9. The reason many objected to speaking of Christ’s merit for himself lay in opposition to the Socinians, who let Christ attain the rank of deity only in the state of exaltation. But although this conception is incorrect, Scripture clearly says that exaltation was also of great significance for Christ and stands in a meritorious connection with his state of humiliation. Reformed theology has precisely the advantage that it can do justice to this teaching of Scripture much better than Lutheran theology. For on the Lutheran standpoint no room remains for Christ’s merit for himself or even for a state of exaltation. The Logos, assuming human nature in the first moment of incarnation, made it capable of the indwelling of the fullness of deity and of the communication of divine properties. Although the God-man in a second moment again laid aside these properties with respect to their use, or at least their public use, he nevertheless continued to possess them, above p. 245. The state of exaltation among the Lutherans can therefore be nothing other than taking again into use, or public use, the divine properties laid aside in this sense. Thus in his exaltation Christ received nothing he did not already have: _non data est Christo in exaltatione nova potentia, virtus aut majestas, quam antea non habuit, sed collata ei tantum fuit plena facultas administrandi ejus regni, quod per ipsam unionem acceperat_, Quenstedt III 368; cf. Gerhard, _Loc._ IV § 306 sq., 329; Hollaz, _Ex._ 774; Buddeus, _Inst._ 788; Schneckenburger, _Zur kirchl. Christol._ 93–114. According to the Lutherans, this resumption of the use of the divine properties took place at the moment of _reviviscentia_ or _vivificatio_, and this is therefore properly the first stage of exaltation. Gerhard, Quenstedt, and others indeed call the _descensus ad inferos_ the first stage. But because this _descensus_ specifically took place according to Christ’s human nature, in both soul and body, the _vivificatio_ must precede it; and Buddeus, _Inst._, p. 789, and others, cf. Vitringa V 573, therefore rightly give it first place in the state of exaltation. The Lutherans further teach of this _vivificatio_ that it took place not only through Christ’s divine but also through his human nature. The latter did not indeed possess the power for this of itself, but from the moment of incarnation it nevertheless possessed the divine properties, particularly also the _vis vivificans_. And thus _anima Christi, virtute divinitatis personaliter sibi communicata, corpus utpote proprium suum templum vivificavit_, Quenstedt III 435. Further, at that same moment of vivification, Christ according to his human nature once again took into use all those divine properties it had received at incarnation but had laid aside in exinanition, at least as regards their use or public use. That is, at that same moment it once more had the use of _omniscientia, omnipotentia, omnipraesentia_, and _vis vivificans_, Quenstedt III 154–198. From this it follows that the _gradus exaltationis_ among the Lutherans cannot properly be different, successive stages in exaltation. At the moment of vivification Christ’s human nature was immediately, through its union with the Logos, omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent. The _descensus ad inferos_, which the Lutherans count as part of exaltation, is a revelation of Christ’s _majestas divina_ in hell. The _resurrectio_ is merely a _resurrectionis manifestatio_ for human beings, Buddeus, p. 789. Both therefore also took place _clauso sepulcro_, just as Jesus’ appearance to the disciples, John 20:19, took place _occlusis foribus_, Quenstedt III 441. The ascension is indeed called a _vera et realis loci mutatio_ insofar as Christ visibly ascended before the eyes of his disciples, but it is nevertheless only a _visibilis_ and _localis_, by no means an _invisibilis absentia corporis Christi in terris_, for also according to his human nature Christ is and remains omnipresent, although invisibly, Gerhard, _Loc._ IV § 219; XXVIII § 24; Quenstedt III 380; Buddeus, _Inst._ 796; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV² 1, p. 185; cf. Vitringa V 601; Moor IV 246. And finally, the _sessio ad dextram Dei_ consists in Christ, specifically according to his human nature, partaking of the _divina, infinita ac immensa virtus et majestas Dei_, especially also of his omnipresence, and exercising this in his kingdom of grace and power, Quenstedt III 383–388, 443–450; Gerhard, _Loc._ IV § 218; Buddeus, p. 797. Now if one considers that all these properties were communicated to Christ’s human nature already at the moment of incarnation and that he indeed laid aside their use but never their possession, it becomes evident that according to the Lutheran conception nothing was communicated to Christ in the state of exaltation that he had not already possessed from his conception onward. At his incarnation Christ is immediately what he can become. He is at once completed, τελειος, also according to his human nature. No development is possible in him. Exaltation was already present at his conception and therefore cannot be conceived as a reward. On this point the Lutheran doctrine is related to the Roman Catholic doctrine, which makes Christ already a _comprehensor_ on earth and has all gifts of which the human nature is capable communicated to Christ already at the incarnation. It serves to defend the same religious interest, namely Christ’s bodily presence in the Lord’s Supper.

Reformed theology, however, had another conception. Over against the Socinians and also many more recent theologians, it indeed maintains that Christ did not first become prophet, priest, and king through his resurrection and was not elevated to the rank of deity then. For Scripture repeatedly testifies that in the beginning he was with God and himself God, John 1:1; 17:5; Rom. 8:3; 2 Cor. 8:9; Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:6; Heb. 1:3, and so forth, and that already from eternity he was anointed by the Father as prophet, priest, and king and as such was active in the days of the Old Testament and during his walk on earth, 2 Tim. 1:9; Titus 3:4; Heb. 13:8; 1 Pet. 1:11, 20. What Christ received for himself in the state of exaltation therefore cannot have consisted in the divine nature or rank of deity, nor in the office of prophet, priest, and king, which rests upon divine election and appointment. Rather, it consisted in exaltation itself—in resurrection, ascension, session at God’s right hand, and return for judgment—in the glory of the Mediator to which he was exalted according to both natures, Isa. 53:10–12; Luke 24:26; John 17:5; Phil. 2:9; Heb. 2:10; 12:2; cf. Voetius, _Disp._ II 277. According to Rom. 1:3, Christ κατα σαρκα—that is, in the way of the flesh, through birth from a woman, Gal. 4:4—came from David. But κατα πνευμα ἀγιωσυνης, by virtue of the Spirit of holiness who dwelt in him and guided him throughout his life, he was, from and through the resurrection, ordained and appointed by God, ὁρισθεις, cf. Acts 17:31, as Son of God in power. Birth and resurrection stand over against each other here. Through birth Christ became the seed of David, Rom. 9:5; assumed ὁμοιωμα σαρκος ἁμαρτιας, Rom. 8:3; became weak, 2 Cor. 13:4. But through the resurrection he was publicly appointed as Son of God. That does not and cannot mean that he then first received the divine nature or the rank and name of God, for the opposite is evident from Rom. 1:3; 8:3, 32; Gal. 4:4, and so forth. Rather, while at his incarnation he exchanged the μορφη θεου for the μορφη δουλου, Phil. 2:9, at the resurrection he now receives back the glory he formerly had with the Father, John 17:2; now becomes κυριος της δοξης, 1 Cor. 2:8; θεου δυναμις, 1 Cor. 1:24; receives a name above every name—that is, the name κυριος, John 20:28; Acts 2:36; 1 Cor. 12:3; Phil. 2:9, 10—and in it the κυριοτης, the right, authority, and power as Mediator, as prophet, priest, and king, to rule over all creatures, subject his enemies, gather his people, and regain fallen creation for God, Pss. 2; 72; 110; Matt. 28:18; 1 Cor. 15:21 f.; Eph. 1:20–23; Phil. 2:9–11; Heb. 1:3 f.; 1 Pet. 3:22; Rev. 1:5, and so forth. In the resurrection God publicly appointed him Son of God, Lord, King, Mediator, and said to him: You are my Son; today I have begotten you, Acts 2:36; 13:33; 17:31; Heb. 1:5. Christ truly entered a new state through his resurrection. As Mediator he is exalted above all creatures at God’s right hand. In a certain sense his divine nature too shares in this exaltation. Just as not merely Christ’s human nature but the person of the Son was the subject of humiliation, so that same person according to both natures is the subject of exaltation. For he had laid aside his μορφη θεου and concealed his divine nature behind the garment of a weak human nature. No one saw or could see in him the Only-Begotten of the Father, except with the eye of faith, John 1:14. But now, in the state of exaltation, his divine glory shines into everyone’s eyes. Whoever now sees him must confess that Christ is Lord to the glory of God the Father. Further, his human nature too shares in that exaltation. The πνευμα ἁγιωσυνης already dwelt in Christ before his resurrection, from his conception onward, for he was conceived by the Holy Spirit, Luke 1:35; was full of the Holy Spirit, Luke 4:1; received him without measure, John 3:34, and so forth; cf. Matt. 12:18, 28; Luke 4:14; Acts 1:2; 4:27; Acts 10:38. But this glory that Christ possessed inwardly could not reveal itself outwardly. He was flesh and by virtue of the weakness of the flesh was also put to death on the cross, 2 Cor. 13:4. But in death he laid aside that weakness and broke every connection with sin and death. God, who gave his own Son into death for us and therein executed judgment upon sin, raised him from the dead through his Spirit, who as πνευμα ἁγιωσυνης dwells in Christ himself and also in all believers, Rom. 8:11, so that he would now live no longer in the weakness of flesh but in the power of Spirit. He was indeed put to death in flesh but was made alive in Spirit, 1 Pet. 3:18. For God’s Spirit dwelt in Christ, even when he was flesh, as the governing power of his life, as πνευμα ἁγιωσυνης, so that Christ always let himself be led by that Spirit and remained obedient to the Father even unto death. And therefore at the resurrection that Spirit must now also reveal himself in Christ as πνευμα ζωης, who completely overcomes death in Christ and one day also in believers, Rom. 8:11. Christ is now exalted so far above every weakness of the flesh that through the resurrection he became a πνευμα ζωοποιουν, 1 Cor. 15:45. Even after the resurrection he still has a σωμα; he is the same Jesus, Acts 9:5; Rom. 4:24; 8:11; 1 Cor. 12:3; 2 Cor. 1:14; 4:5 f. He is the second and last Adam, 1 Cor. 15:45. He has the same σωμα with which he rose, but it is a σωμα πνευματικον, partaking, in place of the φθορα, ἀτιμια, and ἀσθενεια that belong to the σωμα ψυχικον, the σαρξ, of entirely different properties, namely ἀφθαρσια, δοξα, δυναμις, 1 Cor. 15:42 f.; Phil. 3:21. Indeed, in 2 Cor. 3:17 Paul says, ὁ δε κυριος το πνευμα ἐστιν. The apostle does not thereby wish to give a description of Christ’s substantial being, but he comes to this statement because he wishes to argue that Christians are free from the law. For this freedom has its ground in the fact that the Lord—that is, the exalted Christ—is the Spirit, meaning that God’s Spirit now dwells so absolutely in Christ and is so intimately one with him that all bondage is thereby ended, οὑ δε το πνευμα κυριου, ἐλευθερια. The expression πνευμα κυριου proves that at the beginning of the verse Paul does not think of an identification of Christ and the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit is the Spirit of Christ because he dwells in Christ himself and because Christ communicates himself through him to his own, v. 18. And thus Christ is now the one in whom παν το πληρωμα της θεοτητος σωματικως dwells, Col. 2:9; cf. 1:19. He is the visible εἰκων του ἀορατου θεου, Col. 1:15. Divine glory becomes manifest in his human nature and shines from his face, 2 Cor. 3:18; 4:4, 6.
