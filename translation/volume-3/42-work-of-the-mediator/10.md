## 10.

This naturally leads to the question much discussed in theology, whether this way of right was necessary for God in order to reveal his grace, or whether he could also forgive without satisfaction. Scotus proceeded from the _absolutum dominium Dei_ and considered incarnation and satisfaction determined only by God’s arbitrariness. Athanasius, Augustine, Thomas, Calvin, Musculus, Zanchi, Twisse, and others judged them not absolutely necessary but nevertheless, in agreement with God’s wisdom, supremely fitting and suitable. Irenaeus, Basil, Ambrose, Anselm, Beza, Piscator, Voetius, Turretin, Owen, de Moor, and others inclined to regard them as absolutely necessary; cf. vol. II, pp. 207–216, and Voetius, _Disp._ II 238. It is understandable that many preferred to walk the middle way rather than enthrone arbitrariness in God or limit his omnipotence through an absolute _necessitas_. Nevertheless, there is no objection to speaking here of necessity, provided it is rightly understood. The will in God is neither a formal arbitrariness separated from all the virtues and from the whole being of God, nor a choice bound by all those virtues and by that whole being and therefore unfree. God’s will is frequently for us the final ground of things, and we must rest in it even when we do not know why God acted in this way and not otherwise. But in God that will always has wise and holy reasons for acting in this way and not otherwise, for he never acts except in agreement with all his virtues, with his love, wisdom, righteousness, and so forth. And this agreement of God’s will with all his virtues is no compulsion, no limitation upon that will, but precisely the true, highest freedom. To will and act as his holy, wise, omnipotent, loving nature itself wills is for God both the highest freedom and the highest necessity. So it is also with incarnation and satisfaction. They may be called necessary, not as a compulsion imposed upon God from without and from which he cannot escape, but as acts and actions that agree with his virtues and bring them to the most glorious revelation. First, Scripture teaches that God does everything for his own sake, Prov. 16:4; Rom. 11:36. The final ground and final goal, also of incarnation and satisfaction, cannot lie in the creature, in the salvation of the sinner, but must lie in God himself. For his own sake he sent his Son into the world as an atonement for our sins, so that his virtues might thus come to revelation. And indeed there is no fact that brings these perfections of God to light as incarnation and satisfaction do. Not one virtue alone is thereby placed in the clearest light, but all together: his wisdom, grace, love, compassion, forbearance, righteousness, holiness, power, and so forth. Although ordinarily only God’s grace and righteousness are mentioned, the other virtues may not be forgotten. Christ in his person, word, and work is the highest, most perfect, most comprehensive revelation of God, his servant, his image, his Son. He has explained the Father to us. If God willed to reveal himself most gloriously, creation and re-creation, incarnation and satisfaction were necessary for that end. His virtues were already revealed in creation, but much more richly and highly still in re-creation. In his omnipotence he knows how to use sin as a means to glorify himself. Second, it is the teaching of Scripture that God as the absolutely righteous and holy One hates sin with divine hatred, Gen. 18:25; Ex. 20:5; 23:7; Pss. 5:6, 7; Nah. 1:2; Rom. 1:18, 32. There is an absolute opposition between God and sin, which necessarily comes out in his reacting against it with all his virtues. He does not will sin; he has no delight or pleasure in it; he hates it and is wrathful against it. Sin cannot exist without being hated and punished by God. God cannot deny himself. He cannot acknowledge the right of sin. He cannot give Satan equal rights with himself. Precisely because he is the absolutely Holy One, he must hate sin. Properly speaking, everyone concedes this. Even if it is said that God can forgive sin without satisfaction and as the highest love must also forgive, nevertheless everyone still acknowledges that God _forgives_ sin—that is, that by grace he does not punish it but possesses the right and power to punish it. Even Mr. Chavannes found little agreement at the meeting of modern theologians for his proposition that the expression “forgiving love” is a _contradictio in adjectis_ and means nothing, _Bijbl. v. d. Herv._, June 28, 1895. Third, God is most certainly Father of human beings, but this name by no means expresses the whole relation in which God stands to his creatures. He is also Creator, Sustainer, Governor, Sovereign, Lawgiver, Judge, and so forth, and it is one-sided and leads to error if one beholds the full revelation of God in one of these names while passing over the others. Thus in relation to sin God is not merely a creditor and an offended party who can remit the debt and forget and forgive the offense. Rather, he is himself the Giver, Protector, Avenger of the law, personal righteousness, and as such he cannot forgive sin without satisfaction, Heb. 9:22. In that capacity he cannot annul the right of the law, for what is at issue here is not a personal, private right that one can renounce but righteousness—that is, the virtues and honor of God himself. One could indeed appeal against this to the right of pardon frequently exercised by earthly government. But this right of pardon is granted to it only because it is fallible and in many cases imposes a punishment that is too severe or even undeserved. Nothing like this can occur in God. He is righteousness itself, does not need to restore _justitia_ through grace, neither annuls it through grace, but causes them to come to revelation together in the cross of Christ. Fourth, the moral law as such is no arbitrary, positive law but is grounded in the nature of God himself. Neither is it an impersonal power independent of God and resting in itself, so that Christ satisfied not God but its demand; cf. Dale, _The Atonement_, lecture 9. Rather, it is the expression of his being. God, in maintaining the law, maintains himself, and conversely. It is therefore inviolable and unbreakable. Throughout all Scripture it bears that character. Our own conscience testifies to it. And the whole so-called moral world order with its phenomena of responsibility, sense of duty, guilt, remorse, anxiety, pangs of conscience, punishment, and so forth is built upon this inviolability of the moral law. Christ therefore did not come to dissolve the law but to fulfill it, Matt. 5:17, 18; Rom. 10:4. He maintained its majesty and glory. And faith does not annul the law but establishes it, Rom. 3:31. Fifth, sin bears many characters in Scripture. It is lack of understanding, foolishness, guilt, pollution, uncleanness, shame, and so forth. It is compared to a monetary debt and, from another viewpoint, is also an offense against God. But in Scripture it also occurs as a crime, _crimen_, an offense against righteousness, a violation of God’s majesty that brings us under his judgment, Rom. 3:19, and makes us worthy of death, Rom. 1:32. In this character it demands punishment, and without satisfaction there is no forgiveness. Only in the way of right can it be completely overcome, both as regards its guilt and pollution and as regards its power and dominion. Sixth, it may still be added that sin is so great that, rather than leave it unpunished, God punished it in his dear Son Jesus Christ with the bitter and shameful death of the cross. If righteousness could have been obtained in another way, Christ would have died in vain, Gal. 2:21; 3:21; Heb. 2:10. To summarize everything: incarnation and satisfaction took place so that God would once more be acknowledged and honored as God by his creatures. Sin was a failure to acknowledge God and all his virtues, a turning toward and worshiping of the creature. But in Christ God once more revealed himself, restored his sovereignty, justified all his virtues, glorified his name, and maintained his deity. In the work of redemption too, God was concerned first of all with himself, with his own glory. Cf. Voetius, _Disp._ I 372; II 238 sq.; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 18, 34; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV 10; idem, _De satisf. Christi_, disp. 1 and 2; Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ XIX 80 sq.; Heppe, _Dogm._ 341; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 427; Martensen, _Christl. Ethik_ II² 155 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 614; Ch. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 487; A. A. Hodge, _The Atonement_, p. 48; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 378; Hugh Martin, _The Atonement_, Edinburgh, n.d., p. 172, and so forth.
