## 14.

This active and passive obedience of Christ, which is taught so clearly in Scripture, cannot be maintained except by conceiving it as _satisfactio vicaria_. The opponents of this doctrine, however, frequently represent the matter as though, with the work as with the person of Christ, one must distinguish between the pure facts and the theories proposed to explain them. At first this distinction is made with a view to the ecclesiastical doctrine of _satisfactio vicaria_, but soon afterward also with a view to the teaching of the apostles. In the end nothing then remains except the bare fact of Christ’s death itself; the interpretation and appreciation of the fact are left to everyone’s arbitrariness. But this is not how the matter stands. Word and fact go together in God’s revelation. Christ is priest but also prophet. He explained his own person and work. He himself interpreted his death in his word, and Christian theology is bound to it. Thus there are not many theories—the moral, governmental, mystical, private-law, and public-law—which are proposed by theology as hypotheses to explain the facts and phenomena and, as different attempts at solution, all have an equal right to exist. Rather, the question is what in all these opinions and teachings agrees with Scripture and is grounded in it. When the question is posed in this way, scarcely any doubt is possible that _satisfactio vicaria_ is taught by Scripture. No one any longer accepts the exegesis applied by Socinians and rationalists to remove it from Scripture. And although Ritschl’s interpretation may have fascinated for a time because of its acumen, its untenability is scarcely disputed by anyone any longer; cf. Kreibig, _Die Versöhnungslehre auf Grund d. chr. Bew._, 1878; Frank, _Die Theol. Ritschls_, 1891; Pfleiderer, “Die Ritschl’sche Theologie,” _Jahrb. f. pr. Theol._, 1889; idem, _Entw. der prot. Theol._ 228 f.; Orr, _The Ritschlian Theology_, London, 1897. An impartial reading of Holy Scripture always finds in it again the ecclesiastical doctrine of vicarious satisfaction, Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ § 136; Pfleiderer, _Der Paulinismus_² 136 f.; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 68; II 97, 313 f., and so forth.

For Holy Scripture causes us to see in the whole work of Christ a fulfillment of God’s law, a satisfaction of his demand, above p. 310. As prophet, priest, and king; in his birth and death; in his life and suffering; in his words and miracles; in his speaking and acting—he always accomplishes God’s will. He came to do that will. God’s law was within his inmost being. His whole life is one perfect sacrifice, a fragrant aroma to God. This will of God is one, and Christ’s will is also one, and his sacrifice one. Yet a twofold side can be very clearly distinguished in it. For the demand placed by God upon fallen humanity was twofold: that the human being should keep the law perfectly and should also repair its transgression through punishment, above p. 220. The benefits Christ acquired for us are twofold: the forgiveness of sins and eternal life. The two are not identical. Justification does not naturally coincide with heavenly blessedness. Before his disobedience Adam was indeed righteous, but nevertheless still had to acquire eternal life in the way of works. Bearing punishment in itself is by no means one with fulfilling the law. The criminal who is punished but hardens himself under the punishment satisfies justice but by no means answers the whole demand of the law. Moreover, Christ was the second Adam. He came not only to bear the punishment for us but also to acquire for us that righteousness and life which Adam had to acquire through his obedience. He did not deliver us only from guilt and punishment and place us at the beginning, but at the end, of the way Adam had to walk. He grants us much more than we lost in Adam—not only the forgiveness of sins and remission of punishment, but also immediately in faith the _non posse peccare_ and _non posse mori_, vol. II, p. 557. Whoever believes in him is not condemned and has eternal life, John 3:16, 18. Both kinds of benefits are therefore, although never separable _in concreto_, nevertheless frequently named separately alongside each other, Dan. 9:24; John 3:36; Acts 26:18; Rom. 5:17, 18; Gal. 4:5; Rev. 1:5, 6. And so it is also with Christ’s active and passive obedience. They are distinct, but _in concreto_, in Christ’s life and death, they always coincide. Active obedience is no external addition to passive obedience, nor conversely. No single deed and no single event in Christ’s life or suffering is to be brought exclusively under the one or the other. Just as Christ is always and in everything simultaneously prophet, priest, and king, so he is also continually active in atoning for the guilt of sins and in acquiring eternal life. It is not even right to say that the forgiveness of sins was acquired only through his passive obedience and eternal life only through his active obedience. For his suffering was not only a bearing of punishment but also a fulfillment of the law; and his working was not merely fulfillment of the law but also a bearing of its punishment. His doing was suffering, and his suffering was action. It was one work that Christ accomplished, but so rich, so valuable in God’s eyes, that through it God’s righteousness was perfectly satisfied, every demand of the law was fully fulfilled, and the whole of eternal salvation was acquired. The satisfactory character of Christ’s obedience does not consist in his satisfying a vengeful deity with blood, appeasing its hatred and envy through a quantity of suffering. Rather, it lies in this: from the beginning to the end of his life he subjected his will to the whole, perfect, holy, and loving will of God and consecrated himself, with body and soul and all his powers, as a perfect sacrifice to God. But according to the teaching of Scripture, that will of God encompassed not only Christ’s life but also his suffering; and that sacrifice consisted not only in his “moral vocation” but also in his death on the cross. What God has joined together, let no human being separate. Cf. Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV 13, 11 sq.; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 18, 14; Moor III 960 sq.; Heppe, _Dogm. d. ev. ref. K._ 326, 341; Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ 104, 2; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 279 f.; III² 61.

Christ’s obedience, however, is not only a _satisfactio_; it is a _satisfactio vicaria_. On this too Scripture speaks clearly. Properly speaking, the idea of substitution is enclosed in every atoning sacrifice. In the place of the offerer, who is worthy of God’s wrath, it sets something else that can make him favorable again. In Israel’s history we already encounter the idea of substitution in Abraham, when at the command of the Angel of the Lord he does not stretch out his hand against his son but offers a ram as a burnt offering in his son’s place, Gen. 22:12, 13. In Old Testament worship, at the atoning sacrifices the laying on of hands transferred the offerer’s sins to the sacrificial animal, Lev. 16:21. The atonement itself comes about not in one but in three acts: slaughter, sprinkling of blood, and burning. Although burdened with the sins of the offerer and thus worthy of death, the sacrificial animal is nevertheless not simply killed but slaughtered. The point is not death as such, without more, for the sacrificial animal is intended to make atonement and restore the offerer to God’s favor. That favor is acquired not through the death of the sacrificial animal without more, but through the blood, the soul, the life of the sacrificial animal—burdened indeed with the offerer’s sins and therefore killed, yet in itself completely innocent—being brought and consecrated to God. Thus that blood, as the self-sacrifice of a living being that is therefore not killed but slaughtered, makes atonement for the sins of the offerer. It causes the whole animal in the burning to be a pleasing aroma to God. The offerer himself then shares fully in his favor. From beginning to end the animal took his place and thus atoned for him and restored him to fellowship with God. From this worship Isaiah borrowed the features for his portrait of the servant of the Lord. _Poena vicaria_ cannot be expressed more strongly than in Isa. 53. The servant of the Lord took our sicknesses upon himself and bore our sorrows. He was wounded for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities. The punishment that brings us peace was upon him, and by his stripes healing came to us. The Lord caused the iniquities of us all to fall upon him. For the transgression of the people the plague was upon him. He set his soul as a guilt offering. Himself righteous, without injustice or deceit, he bears the iniquities of his own. All this becomes still clearer in the New Testament. First, the expression λυτρον comes into consideration here, Matt. 20:28; Mark 10:45; 1 Tim. 2:6. From its derivation from λυειν, to loose, it is the means of loosing someone, freeing someone from captivity, and hence frequently ransom. In the Septuagint it is the translation of גְּאֻלָּה, Lev. 25:51, 52; or פִדִּוי, Num. 3:46; or כֹּפֶר, Ex. 21:30; 30:12; Num. 35:31, 32; Prov. 6:35; 13:8, which elsewhere, however, is translated by ἐξιλασμα, 1 Sam. 12:3; Ps. 49:8; or ἀλλαγμα, Amos 5:12; Isa. 43:3; or δωρον, Job 36:18. Now it is true that the substitutive and equivalent character is not naturally included in the word λυτρον. Nevertheless, it is incorrect with Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II 69, to understand the words in Mark 10:45 as meaning that Jesus’ voluntary death is a gift, a covering, a protective means through which many are preserved—not from death as the lot of all creatures, but from complete destruction and the aimlessness of life—and thus freed from the fear of death. For although the word λυτρον may not in itself yet express that the redemption price is equivalent to what is redeemed through it, nevertheless the thought is obvious that someone who has a right to something will generally not renounce it except for proper compensation. In Isa. 43:3; Prov. 21:18, it is therefore interchanged with תחת, in the place of. And above all, the redemption price Christ brought is elsewhere called a τιμη, a costly price, 1 Cor. 6:20; 7:23; 1 Pet. 1:18, 19. It is specifically said that a human being can give no ἀνταλλαγμα, redemption price, compensation, equivalent, for his soul, Matt. 16:26; Mark 8:37; cf. Ps. 49:8. And the word λυτρον is intensified in the New Testament by the preposition ἀντι: Jesus gives his life as a ransom in the place of many who indeed had to but could not do so; cf. Cremer, s.v. But besides the expression λυτρον, in the second place the whole New Testament teaching of Christ’s sacrifice comes into discussion here. The prepositions that designate the relation of that sacrifice to us and our sins—ὑπερ, περι, δια, above p. 312—do not in themselves mean “in the place of” but “for the benefit of,” “for the sake of,” “because of,” “on account of.” Yet they establish between Christ’s sacrifice and our sins such a connection that the mystical, moral, and symbolic interpretation by no means exhausts the sense of Scripture. It is naturally true that Christ also suffered and died as an example for us and that all were crucified, died, and were buried in him. But the sense of Scripture does not consist wholly in this. Indeed, the mystical and moral conception of Christ’s suffering and death cannot be maintained unless it is first acknowledged that he suffered and died vicariously for us in the legal sense. And Scripture teaches this as clearly as possible, even though it no more uses the expression _satisfactio vicaria_ than it uses those of Trinity, incarnation, God-man, and so forth. For when it says that Christ, though personally without any sin, according to God’s will took our sin upon himself and bore it; was made sin for us and became a curse; according to that same will of God was punished for it with the accursed death of the cross; and thereby, as _causa meritoria_, acquired for us atonement, forgiveness, righteousness, life, the whole of salvation—then this cannot be conceived otherwise than that he placed himself in our stead and bore our punishment. Cf. Weiss, _Lehrb. der bibl. Theol._ § 49 b., 80, and so forth; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 64 f.; II 97 f., and so forth. Concerning the preposition ὑπερ, Holwerda, _Jaarb. v. wet. Theol._, 1862, pp. 521 f.
