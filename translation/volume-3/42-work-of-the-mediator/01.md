# § 42. The Work of the Mediator

## 1.

Among all peoples we encounter some awareness of sin and a need for reconciliation and redemption. In nearly all religions there also occur sacred persons who bring about and maintain fellowship with God for others. Kings and prophets frequently act as such mediators, but the priests especially are charged with this service. The word priest comes from _presbyter_, elder, and thus originally had no hieratic sense at all. The names in other languages, such as כֹּחֵן, ἱερευς, _sacerdos_, designate priests as persons who stand before God and perform a sacred work, particularly the offering of sacrifices. Although such a priesthood occurs in nearly all religions, cf. Julius Lippert, _Allgemeine Geschichte des Priesterthums_, Berlin, 1883–4, its origin is unknown to us. According to Scripture, no special priests yet occurred in the earliest period; Abel, Cain, Noah, and the patriarchs still brought their own sacrifices, Gen. 4:3, 4; 8:20; 12:7, and so forth. Only when the awareness of sin and the consciousness of separation between God and the human being increase among the peoples does the idea of mediation arise everywhere. The principal means this priesthood employs to obtain fellowship with God is sacrifice: מִנְחָהַ, עֹלָה, זֶבַח, אִשֶּׁה, δωρον, ἱερειον, προσφορα, θυσια, τελετη, _oblatio_, _sacrificium_. The origin and essence of sacrifice too are shrouded in darkness for us. Some explain sacrifice from the disposition of human beings to make the deity favorable by one gift or another and to obtain all kinds of help from it (_do ut des_, gift theory), Saussaye, _Religionsphilos._ I 101; von Hartmann, _Religionsphil._ I 35. Many then also believe that human beings at first conceived the gods as like themselves and therefore offered them primarily foodstuffs in the sacrifices (food theory), ibid., and Siebeck, _Religionsphilos._ 279. Others uphold the mystical or sacramental theory and see the essence of sacrifice not in a gift to the gods but in fellowship with them, enjoyed by sitting together at one table and partaking of one food and drink, Marti, _Israel. Relig._³ 36; Smend, _Att. Rel._ 24; Pfleiderer, _Religionsphilos._³ 648. According to another, the symbolic theory, the sacrificer gives in the gift he offers the gods a proof of his reverence and submission, of his renunciation of everything earthly, and of his complete dedication to God, Wuttke, _Gesch. des Heidenthums_ I 127 f. Finally, there are still those who regard the destructive element in sacrifice as essential and therefore assign every sacrifice an expiatory character, Bellarmine, Vasquez, and many Roman Catholic theologians; cf. Thalhofer, _Das Opfer des alten und neuen Bundes_, Regensburg, 1870; idem, _Handbuch der kath. Liturgik_, Freiburg, 1894, I² 197 f. The question concerning the origin and essence of sacrifice becomes still more difficult when we think of bloody sacrifices and then, more specifically, of human sacrifices. How did humanity arrive at this manner of worshiping God? Which sacrifices were the oldest, bloody or bloodless? Whence the broad place and the tremendous significance assigned to blood in the cult? History gives no answer here. Scripture alone relates that sacrifice has existed from the earliest period of humanity. Before the fall it makes no mention of sacrifice. Yet there is nothing absurd in the thought that even then sacrifice in the broader sense belonged to the elements of worship, just as did prayer. For sacrifice, according to Augustine’s definition, _de civ._ X 6, is _omne opus, quod agitur, ut sancta societate inhaereamus Deo_. As such it was also fitting for the human being in the _status integritatis_. After all, he was created in God’s image in true knowledge, righteousness, and holiness; he was prophet, priest, and king, and as such had to glorify God’s name, dedicate himself with all that was his to God, and rule and govern everything according to God’s will. In the Sabbath he also received a special day for the service of God and needed definite forms of worship for this purpose. There is nothing strange in supposing that sacrifice belonged to these along with prayer. Strictly speaking, the human being can give God nothing, for the earth is the Lord’s and the fullness thereof. But God did give the earth to the human being and appointed him lord over it. And therefore the human being can offer something to God in a symbolic sense as proof of his reverence and dependence. If sacrifice belonged originally to human religion, which cannot differ in essence before and after the fall, then it is also easily explained that Cain’s and Abel’s sacrifices are spoken of soon after the fall without God expressly instituting the sacrificial cult. Protestants and Roman Catholics formerly disputed whether sacrifice rested on a positive command of God or on an inward religious impulse of the human being, M. Vitringa IV 275–280; _Herzog_² 11, 31. But if sacrifice already existed before the fall, this dispute is thereby resolved. Sin, however, did bring a great modification into sacrifice, not only in the sense that the propitiatory sacrifice was mechanically added to the existing sacrifices but also specifically in the sense that sacrifice itself changed in character. The human being after the fall fears God and hides from his face, and therefore the disposition and intention with which he sacrifices and prays have also changed. The propitiatory sacrifice (_sacrificium propitiatorium, propter offensam commissam_) comes to stand at the center and forms and supports the other sacrifices (_sacr. latreuticum, propter Dei majestatem_, praise offering; _sacr. impetratorium, propter beneficia sperata_, prayer offering; _sacr. eucharisticum, propter beneficia jam suscepta_, thank offering), Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 102, art. 3 ad 10. Among these propitiatory sacrifices, bloody sacrifices occupy the first place, and these bear a destructive and substitutionary character. What is offered to God is destroyed, slaughtered; and because the human being himself should properly sacrifice himself in this way for his reconciliation, he takes an animal or even another human being in his place. Finally, a separate order of priests gradually arises in all religions; the human being feels himself removed from God and needs a mediator who brings him into God’s presence. Thus all the sacrifices of humanity—directly in Israel, indirectly also among the Gentiles—point forward to the great, perfect sacrifice offered by Christ, the Mediator between God and human beings, on the cross of Golgotha.
