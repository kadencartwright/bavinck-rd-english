## 2.

The modification introduced into sacrifice by sin manifested itself only gradually. At first sacrifice was simply מִנְחָה, gift, Gen. 4:3, and sacrifices were brought by the offerers themselves. When sacrificial worship and priesthood had already developed among other nations, they were also regulated for Israel. Like the prophetic and royal offices, the priestly office too rests according to the law upon election by YHWH, Num. 16:7; Heb. 5:4. The task assigned to the priests is twofold. They must instruct the people of Israel, also by means of the Urim and Thummim, concerning the statutes and laws of the Lord, Ex. 28:30; Deut. 17:9; 33:8–10; Jer. 18:18; Ezek. 7:26; 44:23, 24; Hag. 2:12; Mal. 2:7; and further approach YHWH with the sacrifices of the people, Lev. 21:8; Num. 16:5, and so forth, and then bless the people on his behalf, Lev. 9:23; Num. 6:23. The sacrifices Israel had to bring were diverse. The Passover sacrifice, Ex. 12, occupies an independent place and is atoning sacrifice and burnt offering, _sacrificium_ and _sacramentum_ at the same time. The covenant sacrifice, Ex. 24:3–11, served, like that with Abraham, Gen. 15:9; Jer. 34:18 f., to confirm the covenant. It therefore consisted of burnt offerings of young bulls, whose blood, for the covering of sin and for sanctification, was sprinkled by Moses as mediator of the covenant partly upon the altar and partly upon the people, and was afterward concluded with thank offerings. The burnt offering and thank offering, Lev. 1, 3, served to maintain fellowship with God resting upon the foundation of the covenant. The sin offering and guilt offering, Lev. 4–6, presupposed that fellowship with God had been disturbed by a sin of weakness and offered in the sprinkling of the blood of the slaughtered sacrificial animal a covering of sin and restoration of fellowship with God. Atonement now came about as follows. Through the laying on of hands the offerer transferred his sin to the animal. This is frequently denied, but wrongly. In Scripture the laying on of hands always entails some transfer: of blessing, Gen. 48:13; Matt. 19:13; curse, Lev. 24:14; office, Num. 27:18; Deut. 34:9; the Holy Spirit, Acts 8:17, and so forth; and thus in bloody sacrifices, including burnt offerings and thank offerings, Lev. 1:4; 3:2, of acknowledged and confessed sin, Lev. 4:4, 15; 16:21; 2 Chron. 29:23. The sacrifice itself was called חטאת or אשׁם. Through this the sacrificial animal was now worthy of death. But because it served not only to undergo the punishment for the offerer but precisely to make atonement for his sin, killing the animal is always called slaughtering. The point was not death as death but thereby to obtain the blood that had to make atonement. God had given precisely that blood of the animal as an atonement upon the altar, because that blood was the seat of the soul, the seat of a life once more freed from sin after and through the slaughter, Lev. 17:11. When this blood then came into God’s presence upon the altar or the mercy seat, the offerer or his sin was thereby covered before the holy face of God. Or rather, it was God himself, Deut. 21:8; Jer. 18:23; Mic. 7:19, and the priest as his representative, Lev. 5:13; 10:17; 15:15, who through the sacrifice conceived as כֹּפֶר, λυτρον, ransom, covered the persons of the offerers away from their sins, or also covered those sins themselves before his face, כִּפֶּר with the preposition על or בעד. Literature on Old Testament sacrificial worship in Herzog² 11, 61; and further Smend, _Altt. Relig._ 319; Marti, _Gesch. d. isr. Rel._ 226.

It deserves notice, however, that the atoning sacrifices by no means atoned for all sins but only for some particular, unintentional sins. Excision from the midst of the people stood as punishment for sins committed with a raised hand, Num. 15:30. Although sins of error were understood very broadly, Lev. 5, 6, the atonement brought about through the atoning sacrifices nevertheless remained very limited. After all, the covenant of grace established by God with Israel did not rest upon those atoning sacrifices but preceded them and had its foundation solely in God’s promise: I am the Lord your God. The sin and guilt offerings served only to atone for unintentional transgressions that were no definite covenant breaking and to restore fellowship with God disturbed by them. This also appears from the fact that they were ordained for cases in which only Levitical uncleanness and not subjective guilt was involved, Lev. 5:2; 12:6, 7; 15:14. Thus numerous sins remained for which the law indicated no atonement through sacrifices: not only some sins committed with a raised hand, which were punished with excision, but further all kinds of spiritual and carnal sins, sins of thoughts and words, sins of pride and selfishness. No sacrifices were prescribed for all these sins. It is true that by no means only the sin and guilt offerings, but also the burnt and thank offerings, bore an atoning character, Lev. 1:3, 4; 9:7, and that on the great Day of Atonement all the sins of the people were atoned for, Lev. 16:16; 23:26–32; Num. 29:7–11. Nevertheless, it remains noteworthy that all the sins named above are not mentioned there and that the proper sin and guilt offerings provide only for particular cases. On special occasions, when the people had sinned grievously and made themselves guilty of covenant breaking, atonement was therefore obtained in an extraordinary manner: through Moses’ intercession, Ex. 32:30–35; Num. 14; Ps. 106:23; or through unusual sacrifices, Num. 16:45–50; 2 Sam. 24:25; 2 Chron. 29:8–11. The devout in Israel knew this too. They knew that the atoning sacrifices opened a way to atonement only in a very few cases, and therefore they went back behind those sacrifices and pleaded God’s mercy. And this is also what Old Testament sacrificial worship intended to teach Israel. Those few sacrifices that were prescribed did not cover the whole of life. They brought about no true atonement. They served only to awaken the consciousness of sin and were types that pointed toward another and better sacrifice. Old Testament sacrificial worship was imperfect. The priests were themselves sinners. The blood of bulls and goats could not take away sins. The sacrifices had to be endlessly repeated. Everything indicated that the ceremonial dispensation of the Old Testament had only a passing, symbolic, typical significance. And therefore, according to prophecy, another covenant is coming, one that will not be broken but will be kept by all, Jer. 31:31; another prophet, who will be anointed with the Spirit of God in a special measure and will bring good news to Israel and the Gentiles, Deut. 18:15; Isa. 11:2; Mal. 4:5; another priest, who will be appointed not after the manner of Aaron but according to the order of Melchizedek and will therefore unite the priestly and royal dignity in himself and bear both eternally, Ps. 110; Jer. 30:21; Zech. 6:13; another king, who will come forth from David’s house and will be a Ruler in Israel, Mic. 5:1, 2. And so another, better sacrifice will also come. The sacrifices of animals are not the true ones, Pss. 40:7; 50:8; 51:18; Amos 4:4; 5:21; Hos. 6:6; 8:11; Isa. 1:11; Jer. 6:19; 7:21, and so forth. The true sacrifices of God are obedience, 1 Sam. 15:22; mercy, Hos. 6:6; a broken spirit, Ps. 51:19; listening to God’s voice, Jer. 7:23. And that sacrifice will be brought by the servant of the Lord who will take Israel’s place, accomplish its work, be a covenant of the people and a light of the Gentiles, Isa. 42:6; 49:6, and set his soul as a guilt offering for the sins of his people, Isa. 53:10.

In the Old Testament this promise of the suffering servant of the Lord and that of the anointed king still run partly in parallel. Both promises are rooted in the firmness of God’s covenant. Despite Israel’s unfaithfulness and apostasy, God cannot forget his covenant. He cannot do so for his name’s sake. It is an eternal covenant that knows no wavering. And therefore Israel, however much fallen into misery on account of its sin, nevertheless receives once more a king from David’s house. This king will be of humble birth, Isa. 11:1, 2; Mic. 5:1, 2; Ezek. 17:22. He will be not only king but also priest, Jer. 30:21; Zech. 3:1; 6:13; Ps. 110; bring righteousness for his people, Jer. 23:6; make sacrifices superfluous, Isa. 60:21; Jer. 24:7; 31:35; Ezek. 36:25, 27; and make all into priests, Isa. 61:6. Alongside this now runs the other promise that this righteousness will be acquired for Israel only by the way of suffering. Sacrificial worship symbolized the necessity of the atoning sacrifice. History showed in so many examples—in Moses, David, Job, the prophets, and in that small company of faithful ones who did not bow the knee to Baal—that the best suffer the most, that those who champion God’s cause and are righteous to that extent must enter glory through suffering. And in and after the exile, Israel as a congregation became the servant of the Lord who, living in need and misery and distressed on every side, would nevertheless be delivered by the Holy One of Israel, Isa. 41:8 f. But Israel too is not the true servant of the Lord and itself needs deliverance, Isa. 41:14; 42:19 f. Where could the prophet have found, either in the past or the present, the Israel or an individual person, a prophet or a martyr, who was so wonderfully equipped by YHWH, who remained so unwaveringly faithful, who in proclaiming the truth to the Gentiles underwent the most severe suffering and the most shameful death? Modern exegesis labors in vain to find among individual devout persons in Israel, among the prophets, or even in Jeremiah or another sufferer, the figure for Isaiah’s portrait of the servant of the Lord. Israel itself, with all its devout persons and prophets, places itself in Isa. 53 over against that servant of the Lord, acknowledges that it despised him for his suffering, and confesses that precisely for their transgressions he was wounded and for their iniquities crushed. Although it is not directly stated, the servant of the Lord can be none other than the Messiah, who after all will also be priest and bring righteousness for his people. Cf. Delitzsch on Isa. 42 f.; Oehler in Herzog² 9, 649, and the literature cited there; also Smend, _Altt. Rel._ 257. It is a difficult question whether Jewish theology before Jesus’ coming already caused these two lines in prophecy to coincide and thus expected a suffering Messiah; cf. Wünsche, _Leiden des Messias_, Leipzig, 1870; Dalman, _Der leidende u. sterbende Messias der Synagoge_, 1888; Weber, _System_ 344 f.; Schürer, _Neut. Zeitgesch._ 597; Baldensperger, _Selbstbew. Jesu_ 121 f.; Oehler in Herzog² 9, 670; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 65 f. But even if this question is answered affirmatively, the expectation of a suffering Messiah was nevertheless not part of popular belief. Jesus’ disciples show themselves entirely incapable of accepting it, Matt. 16:22; Luke 18:34; 24:21; John 12:34.
