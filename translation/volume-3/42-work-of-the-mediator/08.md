## 8.

This threefold office of Christ encounters objection among many, however, because one cannot be distinguished from another, Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 520 f.; III² 386 f.; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ II² 158, 202; Kaftan, _Dogm._ 486, 531. It must indeed be conceded that no single activity of Christ can be limited exclusively to one office. His words are a proclamation of law and gospel and thus point to the prophetic office. But he speaks as one having authority, and everything obeys his command, Mark 1:22; 4:41; Luke 4:32, and so forth. He himself calls himself king, come into the world to bear witness to the truth, John 18:37. His miracles are signs of his teaching, John 2:11; 10:37, and so forth, but also a revelation of his priestly mercy, Matt. 8:17, and of his royal power, Matt. 9:6, 8; 21:23. In his intercession not only his high-priestly but also his prophetic and royal office comes out, John 17:2, 9, 10, 24. His death is a confession and example, 1 Tim. 6:13; 1 Pet. 2:21; Rev. 1:5, but also a sacrifice, Eph. 5:2, and a display of his power, John 10:18. Dogmatics has therefore also been at a loss concerning what from Jesus’ life and works could be brought under each office in particular. Under the prophetic office were usually treated teaching, predicting, and performing miracles; under the priestly office, sacrificing, interceding, and blessing. But for the royal office in the state of humiliation there then remained nothing more than the homage of the wise men from the East, the entry into Jerusalem, the appointment of the apostles, and the institution of the sacraments—isolated facts that in part could just as well be brought under the other offices. Indicating the distinction in the state of exaltation became still more difficult. For Jesus’ prophetic activity indeed continues in the teaching of his church through Word and Spirit, but as king he too governs and protects his church through both; and his high-priestly intercession is no entreaty but a royal willing, John 17:24. It is therefore an atomistic conception that detaches particular activities from the life of Jesus and brings some of them under the prophetic and others under the priestly and royal office. Christ is the same yesterday and today forever. He does not merely perform prophetic, priestly, and royal activities, but is himself in his whole person prophet, priest, and king. And everything he is, speaks, and does reveals this threefold dignity. In one activity his prophetic, and in another his priestly or royal office, may indeed come out more clearly for us. His prophetic office may indeed come more to the foreground in the days of the Old Testament and in his walk on earth, his priestly office more in his suffering and death, his royal office more in his state of exaltation. But in reality he bears all three offices simultaneously and always exercises all three at the same time, both before and after his incarnation, in both the state of humiliation and that of exaltation. Speaking of three offices in Christ is therefore no arbitrariness, no eastern figure of speech that can be surrendered without objection. Neither can one office be reduced to either of the other two. Separation is impossible, but distinction certainly exists. In order to be Mediator, to be complete Savior, he had to be appointed by the Father to all three offices and equipped by the Spirit for all three. For the idea of the human being already contains this threefold dignity and activity within itself. He has a head in order to know, a heart in order to give himself, a hand in order to govern and lead. Correspondingly, in the beginning he was equipped by God with knowledge and understanding, with righteousness and holiness, with dominion and glory (blessedness). Sin, which corrupted the human being, affected all his faculties and was not only ignorance, foolishness, error, falsehood, blindness, darkness, but also unrighteousness, guilt, moral corruption, and further still misery, death, and ruin. Therefore Christ, both as Son and Image of God for himself and also as Mediator and Savior for us, had to bear all three offices. He had to be prophet in order to know and reveal the truth of God; priest in order to dedicate himself to God and offer himself to God in our place; king in order to govern and protect us according to God’s will. Teaching, reconciling, and leading; _discere, acquirere_, and _applicare salutem_; wisdom, righteousness, and redemption; truth, love, and power—all three are necessary for our complete salvation. He is prophet in his relation from God to us; priest in his relation from us to God; king in his relation as head to humanity. Rationalism acknowledges only his prophetic office, mysticism only his priestly office, chiliasm only his royal office. But Scripture sees all three in him, always and simultaneously: our highest prophet, our only priest, our eternal king. He is king, but he rules not by the sword but by his Word and Spirit. He is prophet, but his word has power and comes to pass. He is priest, but he lives by dying, conquers by suffering, and is almighty through his love. He is always everything together, never the one without the other, mighty as a king in his speaking and acting and full of grace and truth in his royal dominion.
