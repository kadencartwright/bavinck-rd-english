## 6.

In modern theology there has been no lack of attempts to modify the doctrine of Christ’s office and work in light of the many objections brought against it, yet still to maintain it in a new form. In general it was guided in this by the effort to transform the juridical doctrine of satisfaction, in which Christ fulfilled the demand of God’s justice in our place, into that of a personal, religious-ethical activity of Christ by which he brought about a change not in God, who is always love, but in us—whether more in our understanding, our heart, or our will. Schelling and Hegel, however, still ascribed only a passing value to Christianity as a historical phenomenon. That God becomes human in a particular person and then suffers and dies for the sin of others is inconceivable. But understood symbolically, it contains profound truth. The person and work of Christ are the historical clothing of the idea that the world, as son of God, proceeding from him and returning to him, must necessarily suffer and thus enter into glory. The world and humanity, subject to time and finitude, are the suffering and dying God; Schelling, _Werke_ I 5, p. 386–305. God’s reconciliation is a necessary, objective moment in the world-process: God reconciles himself with himself and returns from alienation to himself; Hegel, _Werke_ XII 249–256. Reasoning further in this way, von Hartmann said: God cannot redeem me, but I can redeem God, _nur durch mich kann Gott erlöst werden. Das reale Dasein ist die Incarnation der Gottheit, der Weltprocess die Passionsgeschichte des fleischgewordenen Gottes und zugleich der Weg zur Erlösung des im Fleische Gekreuzigten_; _Das sittl. Bewustsein_², 1886, p. 688. In modern theology, which does not go that far but is nevertheless governed in principle by the philosophy of Hegel and Kant, the person of Christ therefore receives no higher significance than that of liberator from legalism, historical starting point of the Christian religion, _urbildliche_ realization of sonship with God and of the unity of God and man, herald of God’s fatherly love, and example for the church; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 128, 133; Schweizer, _Chr. Gl._ § 115, 125 f.; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ § 802, 815 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 619 f.; cf. v. Hartmann, _Die Krisis d. Christ. in der mod. Theol._, 1880; Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 410 f.; Hoekstra, _Godg. Bijdr._, 1866, p. 273 f.; idem, _Grondslag, wezen en openb. van het godsd. geloof_, p. 201 f.

The person and work of Christ occupy a higher position in Schleiermacher. Although he taught something entirely different from what the church confessed, even while conforming to its terminology and to the doctrine of the three offices, he was nevertheless concerned to secure for Christ a permanent place in Christianity. In Schleiermacher, _Erlösung_ comes about through the mystical union of the human being with Christ. For Christ always shared in communion with God and was thereby holy and blessed. As such he came to us, entered into our communion, came to share in our suffering, suffered with us in priestly fashion, and maintained his holiness and blessedness amid the deepest pain, even to the death of the cross. Having thus first entered into our communion, he then receives us into his communion—not only through his word and example but through the mystical operation that proceeds from his whole person—and by his redeeming activity causes us to share in his holiness and by his reconciling activity in his blessedness. _Erlösung_, that is, regeneration and sanctification, precedes _Versöhnung_, the forgiveness of sins, just as in Rome and rationalism; _Chr. Gl._ § 101–104. This mystical substitution theory remained the fundamental thought in the doctrine of reconciliation of the _Vermittelungstheologie_; Nitzsch, _Syst. d. chr. Lehre_ § 80, 134–136; Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 541–558; Schoeberlein, article “Versöhnung” in Herzog¹ and _Princip u. Syst. d. Dogm._ 182 f., 647 f.; Lange, _Dogm._ II 813–908; Martensen, _Dogm._ § 156–169; also Hofmann in his _Schriftbeweis_, who was therefore opposed by Philippi, Thomasius, Delitzsch, and others; cf. Weiszäcker, _Um was handelt es sich in dem Streit über die Versöhnungslehre?_, _Jahrb. f. d. Th._ 1858, pp. 155–188. Ritschl’s doctrine of reconciliation is also related. This doctrine cannot be brought into connection with God as judge, with his retributive justice or wrath, or with a legal order of reward and punishment, for that is a juridical, Pharisaical conception of the relation between God and man that has no place in religion. God is essentially love, and his righteousness consists in his unchangeable will to bring human beings to blessedness and to realize a kingdom of God in them. But humanity is now far from God because of sin. It is bowed down under the consciousness of guilt, has the feeling that it exists in a state of punishment, is afraid and does not dare approach God, and does not live in communion with God. Therefore Christ came to reveal God’s love to us. The doctrine of the three offices meets with an objection in Ritschl. The word “office” belongs only in a legal community; in a moral community of love it is better to speak of a vocation. The three offices in Christ also cannot be kept separate from one another but run into one another. If one wishes to retain them, the royal office stands in the foreground. Christ was appointed king in order to found a church, a kingdom of God on earth, and to this end, as prophet, he revealed God and, as priest, offered himself to God for his own. For Christ stood in an entirely unique relation to God. He made God’s purpose—namely, to unite human beings into a kingdom of God—his life-task. His entire life, suffering, and dying must be understood from this perspective, as a moral vocation that he fulfilled, not as suretyship or mediation, as a substitution for us. Jesus was absorbed in that moral life-task, devoted himself to it wholly, and remained faithful to it even to death. By living in this way, he accomplished God’s will, realized God’s purpose for humanity, revealed his love, grace, and faithfulness, and at the same time, by holding fast to this life-task even to death, sacrificed and consecrated himself to God for the benefit of his own. Through this moral obedience Christ exerted no influence upon God, as though God had been changed from wrathful to favorable, nor did he effect that believers were ransomed from the power of Satan or of death. But he did secure that all who, like Christ, make God’s will their own may, in communion with him, put off the consciousness of guilt, unbelief, distrust, and the mistaken idea that God is wrathful and punishes; that in spite of their sins they may believe in God’s love and faithfulness and glory in the forgiveness of sins (justification); and that afterward they themselves may also enter positively into the new relation in which God stands to them and may lay aside all hostility toward God; _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III, especially chs. 6–8; and further Herrmann, _Der Verkehr des Christen mit Gott_, 1886, p. 93 f.; Schultz, _Grundriss d. ev. Dogm._ § 44–46; Kaftan, _Wesen der chr. Rel._ 246 f.; _Dogm._ p. 446 f.; Haering, _Zu Ritschl’s Versöhnungslehre_, Zurich, 1888; idem, _Zur Versöhnungslehre_, Göttingen, 1893; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 504–513; Ziegler in Gottschick’s _Zeits. f. Th. u. K._, 1895; Riebergall, ibid., 1897, pp. 461–512; Ecklin, _Der Heilswert des Todes Jesu_, Basel, 1888; Kühl, _Die Heilsbedeutung des Todes Christi_, Berlin, 1890.

Similar attempts at a mystical or ethical reconstruction of the doctrine of satisfaction were also made in other countries. Here in the Netherlands the Groningen theologians opposed the ecclesiastical doctrine and saw in Jesus’ death—which was caused by human beings, suffered by Jesus, and permitted by God—especially in connection with his resurrection, a revelation of God’s love, of Jesus’ perfection, and of human sin that causes human beings to recoil from their wickedness, teaches them to admire Jesus’ greatness, and teaches them to believe God’s love; Hofstede de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._ 181. In his doctrine of reconciliation Chantepie de la Saussaye adopted the ideas of Schleiermacher and the _Vermittelungstheologie_; cf. my _Theol. van Ch. d. I. S._ 48. In England Thomas Erskine, _The unconditional freeness of the gospel_, 1828, and Dr. John Mc Leod Campbell, _The nature of the atonement_, 5th ed., 1878, taught that Christ did not acquire God’s love but revealed it and was thus God’s representative to us. But he was also our representative before God, in that, entering into our communion, he suffered the deepest pain over sin as guilt before God and as the source of all misery, pronounced amen to God’s judgment upon sin, and as it were confessed it in our place, and thus as our Head acquired a righteousness and life that are not juridically imputed to us but are indeed mystically and ethically communicated to us. Horace Bushnell, _Vicarious sacrifice grounded in principles of universal obligation_, ed. New-York, 1866, sought to explain Christ’s suffering entirely from the vicarious character inherent in all love. Maurice, _Doctrine of sacrifice_, 1854, Kingsley, F. Robertson, and others saw in Christ’s perfect self-surrender to the Father’s will the essence of the reconciliation he brought about; after all, all salvation for the human being flows from the offering of oneself. Cf. Orr, _Chr. view_ 346; Pfleiderer, _Entw. der prot. Theol._, 1891, p. 460 f.; Clemen, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1892, p. 644 f. In France the following are especially worthy of consideration for this mystical or ethical conception of the doctrine of reconciliation: Pressensé, _Essai sur le dogme de la Rédemption_, _Bulletin Théol._, 1867; Durand, _Etude sur la Rédemption_, _Revue de théol. et de philos._, 1889; Ménégoz, _Péché et Rédemption_; Bovon, _Etude sur l’oeuvre de la Rédemption_, 1893 s. Almost no one left the ecclesiastical doctrine unchanged, but in the main it was still presented by Tholuck, _Lehre v. d. Sünde u. v. d. Versöhner_, 1823; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV 2; Thomasius, _Christi Person und Werk_ II; Vilmar, _Dogm._ § 48; Böhl, _Dogm._ 361 f.; Bula, _Die Versöhnung des Menschen mit Gott durch Christum oder die Genugthuung_, Basel, 1874; Koelling, _Die satisfactio vicaria, d. i. die Lehre v. d. stellvertr. Genugthuung des Herrn Jesu_, I, Gütersloh, 1897; Gess, _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ 1857, pp. 679–752; 1858, p. 713 f.; 1859, p. 467 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 612 f.; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 396 f.; idem, _Die Lehre v. d. stellvertr. Genugthuung_, 1857; Frank, _Chr. Wahrheit_ II² 157 f.; Kähler, _Wiss. v. d. chr. Lehre_, p. 363 f.; idem, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_, Leipzig, 1898; Ch. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 480; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 378; Arch. Alex. Hodge, _The atonement_, Philadelphia, 1867; Dale, _The atonement_, 18th ed., London, 1896; Crawford, _The doctrine of H. Scr. resp. the atonement_, Edinburgh and London, 1871; Candlish, _The atonement, its reality, completeness and extent_, London, 1861; Hugh Martin, _The atonement in its relations to the covenants, the priesthood, the intercession of our Lord_, Edinburgh: Hunter, n.d.; George Smeaton, _The doctrine of the atonement as taught by Christ Himself_, Edinburgh: Clark; Lyttelton, “The atonement,” in Gore’s _Lux mundi_, 1892, pp. 201–229; John Scott Lidgett, _The spiritual principle of the atonement as a satisfaction made to God for the sins of the world_², London, 1898; Merle d’Aubigné, _L’expiation de la croix_, 2nd ed., Paris, 1867; Bois, _La nécessité de l’expiation_, _Revue théol. de Montauban_, 1888; idem, _Expiation et solidarité_, ibid., 1889; Grétillat, _Exposé de théol. syst._ IV 300 s.; and so forth.
