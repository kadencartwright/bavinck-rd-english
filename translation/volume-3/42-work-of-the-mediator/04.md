## 4.

The history of the doctrine of Christ’s work displays a different character than that of the dogma of the Trinity and of Christ’s person. No definite controversy was conducted over it that led to a sharp and clear formulation. Scripture too was so many-sided in its description of that work, and in the history of theology all kinds of conceptions of Christ’s work arose that contained a core of truth; cf. Bähr, _Die Lehre der Kirche vom Tode Jesu in den drei ersten Jahrh._, 1832; Baur, _Die christl. Lehre v. d. Versöhnung in ihrer gesch. Entw._, 1838; Dorner, _Entw. d. Lehre v. d. Person Christi_², 1851; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ II³ 113 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._, vol. I; article “Versöhnung” in Herzog¹ by Schoeberlein, in Herzog² by H. Schmidt; and further the works on the history of dogma by Hagenbach and others. The apostolic fathers follow the usage of Holy Scripture and say only that Christ suffered and sacrificed himself out of love for us, _Barn._ 7; Ignatius, _Ad Eph._ 1; Polycarp, _Ad Phil._ 1; _Diogn._ 9. Soon, however, an attempt was made to give a somewhat fuller account of Christ’s work. And then different conceptions immediately occur alongside one another. From the beginning Christ was regarded not only as prophet but also as king and priest. Sometimes these three offices are also expressly named alongside one another. Eusebius speaks of Christ as μονον ἀρχιερεα των ὁλων και μονον ἁπασης της κτισεως βασιλεα και μονον προφητων αρχιπροφητην του πατρος, _Hist. eccl._ I 3, and related statements also occur in Lactantius, Gregory of Nyssa, Augustine, _De civ._ X 6, and so forth; cf. Krauss, “Das Mittlerwerk nach dem Schema des munus triplex,” _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1872, pp. 595–655. This does not alter the fact that one or another conception sometimes comes one-sidedly to the foreground. The emphasis then falls upon the fact that Christ is the Logos who appeared on earth to reveal the full truth to human beings and to give them an example of virtue, Justin, _Apol._ I 10; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ V 1; Tertullian, _De orat._ 4; Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ V 12; Origen, _De princ._ III 5, 6; _C. Cels._ I 67, 68; Athanasius, _De incarn._ 16. Or sin is felt more as power than as guilt and, correspondingly, Christ’s work is conceived more as deliverance than as atonement. God became human in order to deliver human beings from sensuality, mortality, and the dominion of demons and to make them like God, partakers of eternal life and immortality, Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ III 16, 6; 20, 2; IV 2; V 1; Athanasius, _De incarn._ 7; Gregory of Nyssa, _Catech. magna_ 17–26, and so forth. Another well-known conception, embraced by many despite the contradiction of Gregory Nazianzen, _Orat._ 42, 48, was that in his death Christ surrendered himself to Satan as a ransom, bait, or trap and thus conquered him by guile and delivered human beings from his dominion, Origen on Matt. 20:28; Gregory of Nyssa, _Orat. cat._ 22–26; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ III 1, 27, and so forth. And finally, from the beginning there already occurs the thought that in his suffering and death Christ offered himself to God for us and in our place in order to acquire atonement, forgiveness, sanctification, and the whole of salvation. We already find this thought expressed very beautifully in the _Epistle to Diognetus_ c. 9; cf. Clement of Rome, _I Ad Cor._ 7; Barnabas c. 5, 6, 7. According to Justin Martyr, Christ not only became human in order to partake of our suffering and bring healing, to put an end to the disobedience that had entered the world, and to conquer the power of Satan and death. His death is also a sacrifice for all sinners who wish to repent, the Passover slaughtered for all, the cause of the forgiveness of sins, _Dial._ c. 40, 111; cf. Semisch, _Justin der Märtyrer_ II 416 f. Irenaeus says much more clearly that Christ, who through his incarnation stands in fellowship with us and entered fully into our condition, V preface; cf. III 18, 7; V 16, 2, through his suffering and death reconciled us to God, III 16, 9; restored us to the favor of God against whom we had sinned, reconciled the Father for us, made good our disobedience through his obedience, and granted us the forgiveness of sins in faith, V 17, 1. A similar conception of Christ’s suffering as a sacrifice for our sins that acquired righteousness and life for us also occurs in Origen, Athanasius, Cyril, Gregory of Nyssa, and John of Damascus, Thomasius II 125 f. In the West too this conception was adopted and further developed. Tertullian saw in religion a legal relation in which the human being is subject to God’s law and must make satisfaction to God for transgressions through _poenitentia_. As in the Trinity, so in the doctrine of penance Tertullian brought various terms into use—_deo offenso satisfacere, deum iratum placare, reconciliare, deum promereri_, and so forth—which were applied not yet by him himself but already by Cyprian, Hilary, Ambrose, and others to Christ and his sacrifice, Harnack, _D. G._ III 15 f.; cf. I 524. Augustine enumerated various fruits of Christ’s sacrifice, all of which amount to this: on the one hand it delivered us from guilt, pollution, death, and the devil, and on the other hand it granted us illumination, life, and salvation, Kühner, _Augustinus Anschauung von der Erlösungsbedeutung Christi_, Heidelberg, 1899, p. 18. Alongside the ethical, mystical, and redemption theories, the juridical or satisfaction theory also occurs in him. Christ is _mediator, reconciliator, redemptor, salvator, medicus, pastor_, and so forth. He is _sacerdos_ and _sacrificium_ at the same time. He is the true and only sacrifice for sins, _De civ._ X 6; _Enchir._ 33, 41. Himself without guilt, he took over our punishment in order thereby to pay our guilt and put an end to our punishment, _C. Faust. Man._ XIV 4. _Delicta nostra sua delicta fecit, ut justitiam suam nostram justitiam faceret._ His _maledictio_ is our _benedictio_: _Christus de te sibi habebat carnem, de se tibi salutem, de te sibi mortem, de se tibi vitam, de te sibi contumelias, de se tibi honores_, _Enarr. in Ps._ 60.

The conceptions of Christ’s suffering that we encounter among the church fathers return in scholasticism. Anselm’s work _Cur Deus homo_, however, gave the satisfaction conception a predominance over all others. What was new in Anselm did not consist in his regarding Christ’s death as a sacrifice for our sins. But whereas previously it had usually been said that incarnation and satisfaction were not absolutely necessary, Anselm sought a ground for arguing the opposite. He found this in the fact that sin must always be followed by _aut poena aut satisfactio_, and that if God wills to forgive and preserve humanity, no one other than a God-man can bring that _satisfactio_ to God and restore his honor to him. But because Christ was God-man, his entirely voluntary death was of such great value that he not only delivered from punishment but additionally also merited. And because he did not need this merit himself, he ceded it to humanity, in whose place he had restored honor to God. No one adopted this conception of Anselm unchanged. The absolute necessity of Christ’s incarnation and satisfaction was usually denied. Duns Scotus stood entirely on the other side, denied the infinity of guilt and the infinity of Christ’s merit, denied that Christ’s sacrifice was sufficient in itself, taught that it was reckoned sufficient by God, and reduced incarnation and satisfaction to pure arbitrariness, to the _dominium absolutum_ in God; cf. vol. II, p. 211. But Thomas too did not consider them absolutely necessary and called them _conveniens_, _S. Th._ III, qu. 1, art. 1, 2; qu. 46, art. 1–3. Further, one theologian, namely Abelard, opposed the whole doctrine of satisfaction and set against it the claim that Christ’s incarnation and suffering were not a revelation of God’s righteousness but only of his grace and love; that from the beginning to the end of his life Christ taught us by his word and example and thereby awakens in us a love that frees from sin and makes us God’s children; and that in this lies the redeeming and reconciling power of Christ’s person and work, Herzog² 1, 16. Various elements in Anselm’s conception were later rejected by all, such as the wholly private-law character he gives to satisfaction; the conception of sin as insult and satisfaction as restoration of honor; the one-sided emphasis he places upon Christ’s death while failing to appreciate his life; the opposition he makes between _poena_ and _satisfactio_; the mechanical connection he establishes between _satisfactio_ and _meritum_, between Christ’s merit and the reason it benefits humanity. But this does not alter the fact that Anselm’s doctrine nevertheless acquired lasting significance in later theology in its essential elements: as satisfaction for the guilt of sin made to God’s righteousness in order thereby to acquire righteousness and life for us. The redemption brought by Christ was first and most clearly conceived by Anselm as a deliverance not in the first place from the consequences of sin, from death and the power of Satan, but first of all from sin itself and its guilt. Christ’s redemption became in the first place a reconciliation of God and the human being. Nevertheless, this came far less into its own in scholastic and Roman Catholic theology than in Protestant theology. Thomas does not limit satisfaction mainly to death with Anselm but extends it to all Christ’s suffering and his whole obedience, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 46. He also explains the transfer of Christ’s merit to his own better than Anselm does from the fact that Christ is the head and the church his body, ibid., qu. 8. Yet in considering Christ’s suffering he does not bring it to unity but successively conceives it as _meritum, satisfactio, sacrificium, redemptio_, ibid., qu. 48, and then names as its fruits _liberatio a peccato, a potestate diaboli, a poena peccati, reconciliatio, aditus paradisi_, ibid., qu. 49. Atonement does not yet stand in the foreground here, and it is first brought fully about by Christ’s awakening us to imitation as a _forma virtutis et humilitatis_, moving us to love through his love and grace, and freeing us from sin in faith. The objective and subjective sides of atonement, forgiveness and renewal, are not sufficiently distinguished from each other, ibid. 48, art. 1; 49, art. 1; and further Lombard and other commentators on _Sent._ III, dist. 18, 19; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ IV 7, 9. Later this also remains so among many Roman Catholic theologians, Petavius, _De incarn._ XII, XIII; _Theol. Wirceb._³ IV 295 sq.; Deharbe, _Verklaring der kath. geloofs- en zedeleer_, Utrecht, 1888, II 267 f. Others, however, place the whole work of Christ under the concept _redemptio_ or _satisfactio_ (and _meritum_), or also treat it in the schema of the three offices, _Cat. Rom._ I c. 3, qu. 7; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ IV, 1839, p. 309; Liebermann, _Instit. theol._⁸ II 264; Dieringer, _Kath. Dogm._⁴ 465; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 309–454; Pesch, _Prael. dogm._ IV 198 sq., 256 sq.; Jansen, _Prael. theol._ II 708.

Originally the Reformation had no other doctrine of Christ’s work than Rome and defended Christ’s satisfaction tooth and nail against Socinian opposition. Yet by virtue of its principle it placed that satisfaction under a new viewpoint and in another connection. Since it had learned to know sin first of all as guilt, atonement came to the foreground in Christ’s work. Sin was of such a nature that it provoked God’s wrath. In order to appease that wrath, in order to satisfy God’s righteousness, satisfaction by the God-man was necessary in the first place. He brought it about by placing himself in our stead as Surety of the Covenant, taking upon himself the full guilt and punishment of sin, and submitting himself to the whole demand of God’s law. Christ’s work therefore consists not so much in _humilitas_, not only in his death, but in his whole obedience, both active and passive. And he accomplished this work in his threefold office—not only as prophet by teaching us, giving us an example, and awakening us to love, but also as priest and king. Calvin first set this out in the _Catech. Genev._, afterward in the edition of the _Institutes_ of 1539, and from him it was gradually adopted by Reformed, Lutheran, and Roman Catholic theologians. The objective and subjective sides of atonement were thereby clearly distinguished. Christ accomplished everything. All benefits lie objectively enclosed in his person. Because Christ through his sacrifice satisfied God’s righteousness, he objectively changed the relation between God and the human being and thereby also all the human being’s other relations to sin, death, Satan, and world. The first benefit is therefore the forgiveness of sins, and as a consequence also deliverance from pollution, death, law, and Satan. Christ is the only Mediator between God and human beings, the all-sufficient Savior, the highest prophet, the only priest, the true king. Cf. Theol._ VI c. 27–29; Maccovius, _Coll. theol._ I 228 sq.; Voetius, _De merito Christi_, _Disp._ II 228–284; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV; idem, _De satisfactione Christi_, 1691; Owen, various treatises in vol. X of his _Works_, Edinburgh: Clark, 1862; Moor III 842 sq.; M. Vitringa V 315 sq.; VI 5 sq.
