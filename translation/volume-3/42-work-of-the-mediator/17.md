## 17.

Christ accomplished this obedience throughout the state of his humiliation. The formal treatment of the doctrine of the two states arose among the Lutherans in order to reconcile the _communicatio idiomatum_ with Jesus’ humiliation, but was soon also adopted by the Reformed, Olevianus, _Subst. foed._ II 5; Polanus, _Synt._ VI c. 13; Junius, _Theses Theol._ c. 29; _Synopsis pur. theol._ c. 27, 28, and so forth. Since Schleiermacher’s criticism, _Chr. Gl._ § 105, however, it has either been entirely surrendered or significantly modified. Those who deny Christ’s preexistence and resurrection no longer have any interest in this doctrine either, Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ § 824 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 567 f. Others who accept these testimonies of Scripture have frequently transformed it into a description of the gradually completed incarnation of the Logos, or of Christ’s divine-human development and perfection. His humiliation is then conceived as _eine stete Erhöhung seines inneren Lebens_, which naturally had exaltation as its consequence, Martensen § 139 f.; Dorner § 104; Lange II 635; Rothe, _Ethik_ § 533 f. The doctrine of the two states is replaced by a biography of Jesus, which is, however, impossible with a view to the sources and impermissible with a view to the person, and therefore always ends in a false opposition between the historical Jesus and the apostolic Christ, Strauss, _Leben Jesu_, 1864, § 1; Weiss, _Leben Jesu_ I 180; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III¹ 3, 63; Kähler, _Der sogen. histor. Jesus und der geschichtliche, bibl. Christus_, 1892; idem, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_ 68 f.; Kuyper, _Enc._ III 158. No separation can be made between a _vita Christi_ and an _officium Christi_, as Ebrard wishes, _Dogm._ § 408. His whole life stood in the service of the office to which he was appointed by the Father and sent into the world. The dogma of the apostolic proclamation is the only key to unlock the gospel tradition of Christ, the only means to provide us with a living image of the Savior of the world, Kähler, _Versöhnung_ 69. Christ did not live for himself for a single moment, Rom. 15:3, but always for his church: to give it an example, Matt. 11:29; John 13:14–16, and so forth; to serve it and give his life as a ransom for many, Matt. 20:28; to communicate to it his grace and truth, his light and life, John 1:16; 6:33 f.; Col. 3:4. The incarnation itself was already a κενωσις, consisting in the fact that he who was ἐν μορφῃ θεου ὑπαρχων οὐχ ἁρπαγμον ἡγησατο το εἰναι ἰσα θεῳ—that is, who existed in the form of God, in the same manner as God, and did not regard this as something robbed or usurped—nevertheless renounced this divine mode of existence and assumed the μορφη δουλου, so that he truly became like a human being and was found in form as a human being, Phil. 2:7, 8; 2 Cor. 8:9; cf. Weiffenbach, _Zur Auslegung der Stelle Phil. 2:5–11_, Karlsruhe and Leipzig, 1884. In the exchange of the μορφη θεου for the μορφη δουλου, the divine mode of existence for the human, consisted his κενωσις, _exinanitio_. And as soon as this had taken place, his ταπεινωσις, _humiliatio_, began, consisting in his being and remaining obedient to God unto death. Christ’s whole life from conception to death was therefore a humiliation as a consequence of his obedience, an ever deeper entrance into fellowship with our sin and an ever further removal from heavenly joy. His circumcision, Luke 2:21, served as proof that he was truly human and Abraham’s seed; that as such he stood in fellowship with our sin and had to receive the sign of cutting away that sin; and that God was his God and he was God’s Son. His baptism, which as the Holy One he no more needed for himself than circumcision, Matt. 3:14, took place because it was fitting for him as Mediator πληρωσαι πασαν δικαιοσυνην, to satisfy the whole right of the law and bring about the entire, full righteousness that the law demanded of him; because as such, standing in fellowship with sinners, he had to receive the sign and seal of his fellowship with God as the Son in whom the Father had all his good pleasure; and because he had to be anointed and equipped with the Holy Spirit and thus inaugurated into his public appearance as the Christ who alone can baptize with the Holy Spirit and with fire, Matt. 3:11–17; cf. parallels; Acts 10:38; Bornemann, _Die Taufe Christi durch Joh. in der dogm. Beurteilung der christl. Theologen der vier ersten Jahrh._, Leipzig, 1896. The temptation, which took place immediately after baptism and then continually recurred down to Gethsemane, cf. ἀχρι καιρου, Luke 4:13; John 12:27; Matt. 26:39; Heb. 4:15; 5:7; 1 Pet. 2:23, had as its purpose that Christ, who had just received the sign and seal of his fellowship with God and the gifts of the Holy Spirit, should maintain this fellowship over against every enticement of Satan and the world; as the second Adam should not break the covenant with God but maintain and confirm it for himself and his own; and as the merciful High Priest, tempted in all things as we are, should come to our aid in our weaknesses and temptations. All the words Christ spoke and works he did during his life are an execution of God’s will, John 5:19 f.; 6:38, and have as their purpose to make known the name, virtues, counsel, and good pleasure of God in both law and gospel, Matt. 11:27; John 1:18; to show his priestly mercy to all the poor, sick, and lost, Matt. 8:17; 11:5; to prove his royal power over Satan, world, sin, and all their operations, Luke 10:18; John 12:31; 14:30; 16:33; 18:37. Christ’s suffering, which begins with his incarnation but is completed in the _passio magna_, is the will and command of the Father, Matt. 26:39, 42; John 10:17, 18; proof of his absolute obedience, Phil. 2:8; Heb. 5:8; and for his own an example for their life, 1 Pet. 2:21; a ransom for their sins, Matt. 20:28; 26:28; a conquest of the world, John 16:33; Col. 2:15. His condemnation, not only by the Sanhedrin but also by the secular Roman judge Pontius Pilate, took place so that he would not die secretly by assassination or in a riot but, according to the verdict of the best and most virtuous system of justice then existing, after proper investigation, publicly, be put to death by the way of right; and so that his personal innocence, Matt. 27:18–24; the ground of his condemnation, namely his confession that he was the Son of God and the Messiah of Israel, Matt. 26:63; 27:11; the will of God, Acts 2:23; 4:27, 28; and the character of his death as dying for others, Matt. 20:28, might thereby come clearly and indisputably to light before the eyes of all. Death by crucifixion, _crudelissimum teterrimumque supplicium_, and ordinarily applied only to slaves and serious criminals, had this significance: that Christ, condemned in the name of the law to the most dreadful and shameful punishment, satisfied the strictest demand of the law; as one hanged became a curse to God, but thereby also took away from us the curse of the law, Deut. 21:23; Gal. 3:13, and completely delivered us from every evil to which the law condemns us on account of our sins. The cross is therefore the center and core of the gospel, 1 Cor. 1:23; 2:2; Gal. 6:14. The blood Christ shed—_non infirmitate, sed potestate mortuus est_, Augustine, _De nat. et gr._ 26—proves that he voluntarily consecrated his life to God, that he brought it as a sacrifice, and thereby brought about atonement and peace, Matt. 26:27; Acts 20:28; Rom. 3:25; 5:9; Eph. 1:7; Col. 1:20; Heb. 9:12, 22. Finally, Christ’s burial too has a special significance. It is repeatedly mentioned, Isa. 53:9; Matt. 12:40; 27:59, 60; Luke 11:29; 23:53; John 19:40–42; Acts 13:29; 1 Cor. 15:3, 4. It is not only proof that he truly died and thus also rose from the dead. Its significance lies especially in this: that Christ, although surrendering his spirit into the hands of his Father, who received him into paradise, Luke 23:43, 46, nevertheless remained three days in the state of death, belonged to the realm of the dead, and thus fully bore the punishment of sin, Gen. 3:19. He was not abandoned to that state of death, Hades; his flesh saw no corruption, for he was raised on the third day. Nevertheless, he did remain in Hades, Matt. 12:40; Acts 2:27, 31.

There are no other passages of Scripture that speak of Christ’s burial or of his stay in the state of death. For the descent εἰς τα κατωτερα της γης, Eph. 4:9, in view of the contrast, points to his descent to earth through his incarnation; cf. Meyer, ad loc. And 1 Pet. 3:19–22 in no case speaks of what Christ did between his death and resurrection, but either of what he did after his resurrection or before his incarnation. The words ζωοποιηθεις δε πνευματι indicate that Christ, who was killed because he partook of a _sarkic_ body, nevertheless was raised again because the πνευμα belonged to him, so that his life after the resurrection was not a _sarkic_ but a pneumatic life. The _descensus ad inferos_, τα κατωτατα, Hades, not hell, Gehenna, in the Greek, Roman Catholic, or Lutheran sense therefore has no support in this text. But the thought soon arose in the Christian church that Christ went to Hades in the time between his death and resurrection. The recently discovered _Evangelium Petri_, vv. 41, 42, has a voice from heaven say to Jesus, ἐκηρυξας τοις κοιμωμενοις, to which the answer reads γυμναι: the infernal powers are stripped bare. According to Hermas, _Sim._ IX 16, 5–7, after their death the apostles continued their preaching, specifically to those who had previously fallen asleep in righteousness; cf. also Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ VI 6, 45, 46. And Tertullian, _De an._ 55; Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ I 27, 3; IV 27, 2; V 31, 1–2, teach that Christ descended _apud inferos_ or _in ea quae sunt sub terra_ in order to cause the believers of the Old Testament to share in the benefits of his work. According to Rufinus, who died in 410, the symbol of the church at Aquileia also had this article: _crucifixus sub Pontio Pilato et sepultus descendit in inferno_. Probably from here this article passed into various confessions: that of the Synod at Sirmium, 359; Nice, 359; Constantinople, 360; Toledo, 633; into the _Symbolum Quicumque_; and so also into various later forms of the so-called Apostles’ Creed. Besides the many works on the history of dogma by Harnack, Hagenbach, and others, cf. the works on the Apostles’ Creed by Caspari; Harnack, article “Apost. S.” in Herzog²; Zahn, _Das ap. Symbol_, Erlangen, 1893; Kattenbusch, _Das apost. Symbol_ I, 1894, p. 103 f.; idem, _Christl. Welt_, 1889, nos. 27, 28; idem, _Zur Würdigung des Apostolikums_, _Hefte zur Chr. Welt_ no. 2, Leipzig, 1892, p. 29; Hahn, _Bibl. der Symb. u. Glaubensregeln der alten Kirche_³, 1897. The Greek church, _Conf. Orthod._, qu. 49, understands by the _descensus ad inferos_ that Christ with his divine nature and with his soul went to Hades, freed the souls of the holy forefathers, and transferred them with the thief on the cross to paradise. The Roman Catholic church, _Cat. Rom._ I c. 6; cf. Bellarmine, _De Christo_ IV 6–16; Petavius, _De incarn._ XII 19, 20; XIII 15–18; Scheeben III 298 f., and so forth, teaches that Christ truly, with his soul, descended _ad inferos_ after his death and remained there as long as his body rested in the grave, in order to free the souls of the devout who there, _sine ullo doloris sensu, beata redemptionis spe sustentati, quieta habitatione fruebantur_, but nevertheless lacked the vision of God, and in order as victor to strike down the demons and rob them of the spoil of souls. The Lutheran church, _Symb. B._, ed. Müller, 550, 696, confesses that Christ according to both natures, with soul and body, went to hell after the burial, conquered the devil, destroyed the power of hell, and took away all power and might from the devil. The theologians expanded this to say that on the morning of the third day, after the _vivificatio_, the _resurrectio interna_, and before the _resurrectio externa_, Christ went with soul and body to hell and there, through a _praedicatio non evangelica sed legalis, elenctica, terribilis_, made known to Satan and all condemned spirits his victory over death and Satan, Frank, _Theologie der Concordienformel_ III 397–454; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ 277, 288 f. The Reformed confessions are not unanimous and understand by the descent into hell the infernal sorrows Christ suffered on the cross, Calvin, _Inst._ II 16, 8–12; _Cat. Genev._ 1; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 44; and so Beza, Danaeus, Pareus, and others; or Christ’s actual going with his soul alone to hell to make his power known there, _Repet. Anh._ 9, and likewise Zanchi, Aretius, Alsted; or Christ’s going to hell with both soul and body for the same purpose, _Coll. Lips._ 9; or the state of death in which Christ remained for three days, Westminster Catechism in Niemeyer 55, and so Olevianus, Perkins, Ames, Molinaeus, Broughton, Vossius, Bochart, Pearson, Schultens, Vriemoet, and others. Some tried to combine these different views, Sohnius, _Op._ III 311; Witsius, _Verkl. van het apost. symbool_, 18 § 9 f.; Burman, _Synopsis theol._ V 21 § 13–14; Mastricht V 13, 5; _Synopsis pur. theol._ 27, 32; Heidegger, _Corpus theol._ 18, 32, and so forth. The great diversity of opinions explains why many could attach no good meaning to the article and rejected it entirely, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 99, 1, or also seized upon this article to give an ecclesiastical stamp to their doctrine of a continuing proclamation of the gospel and of a missionary institution in the place of the intermediate state; cf. especially Güder, _Die Lehre v. d. Erscheinung Jesu Chr. unter den Todten_, 1853; Spitta, _Christi Predigt an die Geister_, Göttingen, 1890. In fact, the matter stands as follows with this article: 1º the expression “descended into hell,” insofar as it may have been derived from texts such as Acts 2:27, 31; Eph. 4:9, historically acquired an entirely different meaning than that contained in those texts; 2º the Greek and Roman Catholic explanation of this article, that Christ supposedly went to Hades to free the devout of the Old Testament, finds not the slightest support in Scripture; 3º the Lutheran conception that Christ made his power known to Satan is indeed, as will appear later, grounded in positive statements of Scripture, but cannot be regarded as a correct explanation of the words “descended into hell,” since in that case they would not belong to the state of humiliation but to that of exaltation, which first begins with the resurrection; 4º the more recent opinion that Christ supposedly went to hell to preach the gospel to all who did not hear it here on earth likewise for the same reason cannot be regarded as a correct explanation of this article of faith; 5º 1 Pet. 3:19 says at most—although it will later be shown that even this is not the correct opinion—that after his resurrection Christ proclaimed the gospel to Noah’s contemporaries, but gives absolutely no right to extend the proclamation of the gospel to all or to other lost persons; and 6º the words “descended into hell,” in agreement also with what Rufinus says, that they are an expansion of Jesus’ burial, are best understood of the state of death in which Christ as Mediator remained for three days in order to bear the punishment of sin to the end and deliver us from it. Thus the whole life of Christ culminated in his death as the complete satisfaction of God’s righteousness. And by that one sacrifice he has perfected forever all those who are being sanctified.
