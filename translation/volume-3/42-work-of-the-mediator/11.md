## 11.

The Socinians and their kindred spirits, however, very seriously opposed this necessity of satisfaction. Their arguments all amount to this: that right and grace, satisfaction and forgiveness, and therefore further also law and gospel, Old and New Testaments, creation and re-creation, and so forth, conflict with and exclude one another. According to them, the Christian religion is the absolutely spiritual and moral religion, freed from all natural and sensible elements. God is to be conceived not as Judge but as Father. Punitive righteousness, holiness, hatred, and wrath against sin are not virtues in God; love alone is. Old Testament religion still stood on the standpoint of law and conceived the relation of the human being to God as a legal relation. Pharisaism drove this to an extreme, and Paul still employed this Pharisaic usage and did not oppose the Pharisaic combination of fulfillment of the law, righteousness, and reward. Nevertheless, Paul derives the impossibility of fulfilling the law not from the human being’s sinfulness but from the nature of the law itself, which serves not to give life but to increase sin. Thus he nevertheless conceives the Christian religion purely as _Erlösungsreligion_, as grace, forgiveness, faith. And that is indeed what it is. It is solely religious-ethical and no longer has cosmic, juridical, metaphysical components. It is solely doctrine of salvation, and everything else—the doctrine of God, the human being, sin, the world, and so forth—must be revised and reworked from this standpoint, Christologically and soteriologically. The state is the sphere of right, but religion and right stand diametrically opposed to each other. Cf. above, pp. 322 f.; and further Wegscheider § 142; Hofstede de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._ 181; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 46 f., 57 f.; Schweizer, _Gl. der ev. ref. K._ § 63 f.; _Christl. Glaub._ § 94 f.; Pfleiderer, _Paulinismus_² 86–110, 150–159; Holtzmann, _Neutest. Theol._ II 108 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 8–14, 223–265; Kaftan, _Dogm._ 544 f.; von Hartmann, _Religionsphilos._ I 546 f. This entire opposition, however, is false and has always been rejected in Christian theology as Marcionite. First, it is untrue that righteousness and grace (love) would be an opposition in God. Not only does the whole of Scripture also ascribe to God the virtues of righteousness, holiness, wrath, and hatred against sin, vol. II, pp. 195 f.; grace even presupposes righteousness in God and cannot be maintained without it. For grace is that virtue in God by which he for one reason or another renounces his right. If, therefore, as the righteous and holy One, he does not have the right to punish, there can be no question of grace in him. Likewise, the highest love in God—that is, the forgiving love revealed in Christ—is no longer love if, according to God’s righteous judgment, sin deserved no punishment. Whoever denies right also denies grace. Second, right and religion (morality) are by no means an opposition. Religion and morality are themselves a right that God has over us. In his law he demands that the human being love him above all and his neighbor as himself. Religion and morality are not thereby debased or externalized but, on the contrary, maintained in their true significance. God wills that the human being should love him with all his heart, all his understanding, and all his strength. He claims the whole human being for his service of love. Religion and morality could not exist if they were not rooted in God’s right over his creature. The moral order is borne in this sense by the legal order. The sense of right is rooted still more deeply in the human being than religious and ethical consciousness. Even in those who have cast off all religion and morality, it frequently still comes powerfully to life. And it is indeed true that the perfect human being loves God and neighbor spontaneously and according to the prompting of his holy nature. But this changes nothing about the fact that precisely this service from love agrees with God’s law and is prescribed in it. The moral law is certainly a demand and a right of God, even though it claims the whole human being and desires service in spirit and truth. For just as religion and morality are rooted in a right of God, so conversely that right of God is no abstract, formal arbitrariness but is grounded precisely in God’s nature. The legal order itself in turn bears an ethical character and finds confirmation and support in the moral order, vol. II, p. 201. Third, the assertion is therefore incorrect that the moral world order first became legal through sin, Kaftan, _Dogm._ 370, 545. Through sin it did indeed become legal for the human being in the sense that love toward God and neighbor now stands outside and over against him as a commandment to which he cannot answer, which he transgressed, and whose punishment he deserves. But it did not become legal through sin in the sense that it would not rest upon a right and law of God. Lutheran theology did indeed teach this, vol. II, pp. 556, 558, but Reformed theology judged otherwise and better. The law is certainly the norm, grounded in God’s being even apart from sin, for the entire moral world order. The latter cannot even be conceived without a law. All religion and morality presuppose a law. Where there is no law, neither is there transgression. Whoever detaches the moral world order from the law makes it arbitrary objectively and subjectively and digs away the foundation beneath its feet. Fourth, whoever places right and grace dualistically over against each other in this way comes into conflict with Scripture at every point. The _status integritatis_, as the life of the human being created in God’s image in agreement with the moral law, can no longer be maintained. Sin is no guilt and deserves no punishment except in the sinner’s consciousness. The law has not an eternal but only a temporary, passing, pedagogical significance. The Old Testament as the religion of law no longer concerns us. The doctrine of God’s righteousness and of satisfaction in Christ is a Jewish, Pharisaic intrusion into the theology of the New Testament. Forgiveness of sins is a subjective coming to insight that they are no guilt and deserve no punishment. Sanctification is moral, autonomous self-development. The church is only a religious-ethical community and has nothing to do with right and law. Under the appearance of conceiving the Christian religion more purely, it is robbed of its heart and core. Finally, fifth, if grace, love, and forgiveness are nevertheless wholly unmerited and all presuppose voluntary renunciation of a right, then the question can certainly still be asked with Socinus, _De Christo Servatore_ I 1, whether God cannot renounce that right without demanding satisfaction. God is after all slow to anger, forbearing and compassionate, and in Scripture frequently forgives without any satisfaction being mentioned, provided sincere repentance is present, Deut. 30:1–3; Jer. 3:13, 14; 18:8; Matt. 18:24 f.; Luke 15:11 f. In this question, if it is truly meant seriously, there is then no longer a disagreement _de jure_ but only _de facto_. God had the right to demand satisfaction; grace and forgiveness presuppose it. There is disagreement only over whether God demanded that satisfaction. It will shortly appear that suffering and death truly bear a satisfactory character. Over against this reality, the question of the possibility whether God could have renounced his right to satisfaction is of very subordinate significance. But in any event it is impermissible to oppose satisfaction with forgiveness, as though one excluded the other. For not only are they connected with each other in Scripture, Lev. 4:31; Rom. 3:24–26; Heb. 9:22, but they are also placed in this relation to each other: satisfaction precisely opens the way to forgiveness. In monetary debts satisfaction indeed eliminates forgiveness, since what matters there is not at all the person who pays but only the sum paid. But with moral debts it is entirely different. These are personal and must be punished in the guilty person himself. If a substitute is admitted here, then the admission of such a substitute and the acceptance of his merits for those of the guilty is nevertheless always an act of grace. Christ gives satisfaction to God, but God grants forgiveness to us. Forgiveness is grace not with a view to Christ but with a view to us. Christ’s satisfaction opens the way for God to forgive sins by grace and justify the ungodly without injury to his right. If sin is of such a nature that right and righteousness, law and truth, suffer not the slightest harm even if it is not punished, then the grace of forgiveness is not great either. But if sin is so great that, rather than leave it unpunished, God punished it in his dear Son Jesus Christ with the bitter and shameful death of the cross, then the riches of God’s grace, the power of his forgiving love, come to light. Then too the human being finds rest and comfort for his accusing conscience in that satisfaction and can rejoice without any fear in the forgiveness of his sins, for the complete satisfaction guarantees absolute, irrevocable, eternal forgiveness.
