## 19.

Intensively, the work of Christ is of infinite value, but extensively too it reaches out to the whole world. Just as the world was the object of God’s love, John 3:16, so Christ came not to condemn that world but to save it, John 3:17; 4:42; 6:33, 51; 12:47. In him God reconciled the world, all things in heaven and on earth, to himself, John 1:29; 2 Cor. 5:19; Col. 1:20, and gathers them together into one in this dispensation, Eph. 1:10. The world, created through the Son, is also destined for the Son as its heir, Col. 1:16; Heb. 1:2; Rev. 11:15. From this Origen inferred that through his suffering and death Christ redeemed the whole world—not only all human beings but also all other rational beings, namely the fallen angels, and further all creatures, _de princ._ I 6. Christ οἰ μονον ὑπερ ἀνθρωπων ἀπεθανεν, ἀλλα και ὑπερ των λοιπων λογικων, _in Joann._ I 40. He did indeed die only once, at the consummation of the ages, Heb. 9:26, but the power of his death is sufficient for the redemption not only of the present world but also of the world that existed earlier or will exist later, and not only of human beings but also of the heavenly spirits, _de princ._ II 3, 5; _Hom. in Lev._ I 3; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ I² 254. This universalism, however, was always rejected by the Christian churches, for example, Constantinople, 543, can. 7, 12. And in fact they have always been particularistic to this extent: they restrict τα παντα in Col. 1:20 and do not extend it to the fallen angels. Nevertheless, from this and other passages where the word world or all is connected with Christ’s sacrifice, Isa. 53:6; Rom. 5:18; 8:32; 1 Cor. 15:22; 2 Cor. 5:15; Heb. 2:9; 1 Tim. 2:4, 6; 2 Pet. 3:9; 1 John 2:2, the conclusion has been drawn that Christ had made satisfaction for all human beings, head for head, and that the _satisfactio vicaria_ therefore had to be understood as universal. The church fathers before Augustine commonly speak very universalistically about God’s will to salvation and Christ’s atonement, cf. Petavius, _de incarn._ XIII c. I, 2. But the actual question did not yet exist at that time and could not yet arise then, because on God’s side one assumed only a _praescientia_ and on the human side placed the emphasis upon freedom of the will, though weakened by sin, vol. II, 319. In the Pelagian controversy, however, it had to come up for discussion, and Augustine was the first who clearly taught particular satisfaction. Augustine indeed repeatedly says with Scripture that God wills the salvation of all; that if one died for all, all died; that those who perish did not wish to believe; and that whoever believes does so voluntarily, Petavius, ibid., c. 3. He also says that Christ is an atonement for the whole world and has reconciled all things, heavenly and earthly, with one another, Kühner, _Augustins Anschauung v. d. Erlösungsbedeutung Christi_, 1890, p. 62. But all this proves absolutely nothing against this other teaching, expressed by Augustine just as clearly and distinctly, that God’s will to salvation and the atonement in Christ are limited to the _praedestinati_. First, this already follows in general from Augustine’s doctrine of predestination and grace, vol. II, 320. If the number of those who will be saved was eternally and immutably determined by God and to them alone the grace of faith and perseverance is granted, then between these teachings the proposition can no longer be maintained that Christ made satisfaction for all human beings, head for head. Second, Augustine always understands 1 Tim. 2:4 in a restricted sense. He does indeed give various explanations of that text, sometimes that _omnes qui salvi fiunt nisi ipso volente non fiunt_, _Epist._ 107; _de civ._ XIII 23, and then that by all human beings are to be understood all the _praedestinati_ who were chosen from all peoples and classes of the human race, _Enchir._ 103; _de corr. et gr._ 14; _c. Jul._ IV 8. But wherever he expressly explains the text, he understands it in a restricted sense; cf. Petavius, _de Deo_ XI 7, 10; _de incarn._ XII 4. And third, he repeatedly connects the person and work of Christ only with the elect. Through Christ, God called the _populum credentium_ to adoption, _Conf._ IX 1. Through his resurrection Christ called _nos praedestinatos_ to a new life, and through his blood redeemed _justificandos peccatores_, _de trin._ IV c. 13. _Omnis, qui Christi sanguine redemptus est, homo est, non tamen omnis, qui homo est, etiam sanguine Christi redemptus est_, _de conj. adult._ I 15. _Non perit unus ex illis, pro quibus Christus mortuus est_, _Epist._ 102 _ad Evod._; cf. Wiggers, _August. und Pelag._ I 313; Vitringa VI 147. Although semi-Pelagianism soon gained the upper hand, many nevertheless remained faithful to Augustine on this point too. Appealing to 1 Tim. 2:4, the Semi-Pelagians said that God embraces all human beings with equal love and distributes to all an equal measure of grace. Prosper answered that _omnium cura est Deo, et nemo est, quem non aut evangelica praedicatio aut legis testificatio aut ipsa etiam natura conveniat; sed infidelitatem hominum ipsis adscribimus hominibus; fidem autem hominum donum Dei esse fatemur, sine cujus gratia nemo currit ad gratiam_. And as concerns the particularity of satisfaction, Christ was indeed _pro totius mundi redemptione crucifixus, propter veram humanae naturae susceptionem et propter communem in primo homine omnium perditionem; potest tamen dici pro his tantum crucifixus, quibus mors profuit_, _Resp. ad cap. cal. Gall._ 8, 9. However moderately this was expressed, all the followers of Augustine—Prosper, Lucidus, Fulgentius, and so forth—nevertheless agreed in this: that although God cares for all human beings and grants them all kinds of benefits, he nevertheless does not will the salvation of all in the same way; that he does not grant all the same measure of grace; and that although Christ died in a certain sense for all, he nevertheless died _efficaciter_ only for those whom his death actually benefits. But the Synod of Arles, 475, forced Lucidus to retract his doctrine that Christ had died only for those who are actually saved. And the Synod of Orange, 529, said only that _omnes baptizati_ can fulfill what is necessary for salvation; cf. Petavius, _de incarn._ XIII c. 5–7; Schwane, _D. G._ II² 571–582. In the 9th century the question came up again. Gottschalk taught that Christ _baptismi sacramento (reprobos) luit, non tamen pro eis crucem subiit neque mortem tulit neque sanguinem fudit_. Lupus said that Christ did not die for all human beings but did die for all believers, including those who later lose faith again. Remigius made a similar distinction. And the Synod of Valence, 855, spoke in the same spirit. It rejected the view that Christ had shed his blood _etiam pro illis impiis, qui a mundi exordio usque ad passionem Domini in sua impietate mortui aeterna damnatione puniti sunt_, and confessed that this price was paid only for those _de quibus ipse Dominus noster dicit: sicut Moyses exaltavit serpentem in deserto, ita exaltari oportet Filium hominis, ut omnis qui credit in ipso non pereat sed habeat vitam aeternam_, can. 4. Scholasticism too remained in the main faithful to Augustine. 1 Tim. 2:4 must not be understood as though God willed what does not happen, as though he willed that those too should be saved who in fact are not saved. Rather, this text means _nullum hominum fieri salvum nisi quem salvum fieri ipse voluerit_; or that all kinds of human beings from all peoples and estates will be saved; or else, more in the sense of John of Damascus, that God wills the salvation of all, namely _voluntate antecedente, conditionale_, that is, if they themselves will and come to him. But then this will was _magis velleitas quam absoluta voluntas_, Lombard, _Sent._ I, dist. 46, 2, and also on 1 Tim. 2:4; Bonaventure, ibid., art. 1, qu. 1; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 19, art. 7; qu. 23, art. 4 ad 3. And as regards satisfaction, scholasticism did indeed teach that it was _superabundans_ and was made _pro peccato humani generis, totius humanae naturae_, and so forth, Thomas, _S. Th._ III, qu. 46, art. 1. But it argued for the possibility of satisfaction chiefly from the fact that Christ was the head and believers the members of his body and therefore repeatedly connected satisfaction only with believers. Thus Thomas says, _caput et menbra sunt quasi una persona mystica et ideo satisfactio Christi ad ommes fideles pertinet sicut ad membra_, III, qu. 48, art. 2; _quia ipse est caput nostrum, per passionem suam liberavit nos tanquam membra sua a peccatis, quasi per pretium suae passionis, sicut si homo per aliquod opus meritorium quod manu exerceret, redimeret se a peccato quod pedibus commisisset_, III, qu. 49, art. 1. And in III, qu. 79, art. 7, he says: _passio Christi prodest quidem omnibus quantum ad sufficientiam, sed effectum non habet nisi in illis qui Christi conjunguntur per fidem et charitatem_. That this _efficacitas_ does not depend upon the human being’s free will but upon God’s election and Christ’s sacrifice itself is evident in general from Thomas’s doctrine of _gratia_ and specifically also from the fact that in III, qu. 48, art. 6, he says: _passio Christi efficienter causat salutem humanam_. Cf. also Lombard: _Christus electos tantum sicut se dilexit eorumque salutem optavit_, _Sent._ III, dist. 31, 4. _Christus se trinitati obtulit pro omnibus, quantum ad pretii sufficientiam, sed pro electis tantum quantum ad efficaciam, quia praedestinatis tantum salutem effecit_, ibid., III, dist. 20, 3. Later this same view was still defended in the Roman Catholic Church by Bajus, Jansenius, and Quesnel, and even also by Tapper, Estius, Sonnius, and others; cf. Rivet, _Op._ III 438; M. Vitringa VI 155–159. But semi-Pelagianism penetrated ever more deeply into Rome’s church, and as a result it became the almost universal doctrine, especially after Trent, that God wills the salvation of all _voluntate antecedente_, that Christ made satisfaction for all, and that the _voluntas consequens_ takes account of the good or bad use human beings make of their freedom and of grace, vol. II, 325; and further Trent VI c. 2 and 3; _Cat. Rom._ I 3, 7; Innocent X, _in damn._ 5 _propos. Jans._; Bellarmine, _de sacrif. missae_ I 25; _de poenit._ I 2; _de amiss. gr._ IV 4; Becanus, _Manuale Controv._ III 1, qu. 1; Petavius, _de incarn._ XIII c. 14; _Theol. Wirceb._ IV 322; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 356; Jansen, _Prael. theol._ II 748, and so forth. And in substance the Greek church agrees with this, John of Damascus, _de fide orthod._ II c. 29; _Conf. orthod._, qu. 34, 47; the Lutherans, _Symb._, ed. Müller, p. 781; Gerhard, _Loc._ VII c. 6; Quenstedt III 311–324; Hollaz, _Ex._ 745; Buddeus, _Inst. theol._ 824; the Remonstrants, _Conf._ and _Apol. conf._ VIII 10; Arminius, _Op._ 153; Episcopius on 1 John 2:2; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ IV c. 3–5; and further the Mennonites, Quakers, Moravians, Methodists, and so forth.

The Reformed therefore stand fairly nearly alone with their doctrine of particular satisfaction. Moreover, they were by no means unanimous among themselves and gradually diverged ever farther from one another. Various confessions speak moderately and generally, for example, the Anhalt Confession, in Niemeyer, pp. 635, 639; the March Confession, ibid., 650, 651; the Leipzig Confession, ibid., 662; the Thorn Confession, ibid., p. 674. The Anglican Confession is silent about it. The Heidelberg Catechism does not express it directly either, although qu. 37 has wrongly been cited as a proof for universal satisfaction; cf. M. Vitringa VI 136. The French, Dutch, and Scottish confessions teach it only _per consequentiam_. In the Canons of Dort it is clearly taught, but it is said at the same time that Christ’s sacrifice was _infiniti valoris et pretii, abunde sufficiens ad totius mundi peccata expianda_, II 3; and further it still occurs in the Westminster Confession VIII 1, 5, 8; _Cat. major et minor_; _Cons. Helv._ 13; _Walch. art._ 1693. Nor was there unanimity among the theologians. Some thought it was indeed good to say that Christ’s sacrifice would have been sufficient for all human beings if God had willed to make it _efficax_ for all, but that one could not really say it was sufficient for all. For if Christ had not died _efficaciter_ for all, then neither had he died _sufficienter_ for all; and if one nevertheless began to speak in this way, one gave occasion for misunderstanding and prepared the dangerous distinction between _voluntas antecedens_ and _consequens_, between _gratia sufficiens_ and _efficax_, Beza, Piscator, Twisse, Voetius, _Disp._ II 251 sq. Others, however, spoke with Augustine and the scholastics in such a way that Christ had died _sufficienter_ for all and _efficaciter_ only for the elect, Calvin on 1 John 2:2; H. Alting, _Probl. theol._ 174; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV, qu. 14; Mastricht V 18, 21, 39; Moor III 1035–1075, and so forth. At the Synod of Dort, the foreign delegates spoke as broadly as possible about the _dignitas_ and _sufficientia_ of Christ’s sacrifice. The English theologians even said that in a certain sense Christ had died for all: _sic Christus pro omnibus mortuus est, ut omnes et singuli, mediante fide, possint virtute_ ἀντιλυτρου _hujus remissionem peccatorum et vitam aeternam consequi_, thesis 3. In England there was, over against the strictly Reformed school of Twisse, Rutherford, and others, a more moderate tendency represented by Davenant, Calamy, Reynolds, Arrowsmith, Seaman, and others, and especially by Richard Baxter. Their view agreed materially in every respect with that of the French theologians Cameron, Testard, Amyraut, and others. There was a preceding decree according to which Christ had made satisfaction for all conditionally, on condition of faith, and another subsequent, particular decree according to which he had so made satisfaction for the elect that in due time he also grants them faith and infallibly leads them to salvation, vol. II, 341. In the Scottish church, the doctrine of particular satisfaction repeatedly came up for discussion in connection with the universal offer of grace. It was even one of the causes of the separation of the Erskines in 1733. To avoid the neonomian tendency, which made faith a legal condition and therefore allowed the gospel to be intended only for definite, qualified persons, the so-called Marrowmen (James Hog, Thomas Boston, Ralph and Ebenezer Erskine, Alexander Moncrieff, and others; cf. vol. I, 128) taught that Christ’s sacrifice possessed a _legal, federal sufficiency_ for all human beings and grounded the universal offer of grace upon it. Some went still further, distinguished between a universal and a special grace, an external and an internal covenant (Thomas Mair, 1754); or taught universal satisfaction (James Fraser, †1698, but his treatise _On Justifying Faith_ was first published in two parts, 1722 and 1749); or even wholly denied satisfaction (Dr. M. Gill, _Practical Essay on the Death of Christ_, 1786). In this century particular satisfaction once again became the subject of a protracted controversy in Scotland. In 1820 the United Associate Synod of the Secession Church issued a declaration in which it did indeed condemn universal satisfaction but nevertheless taught that Christ’s sacrifice was sufficient for all and had brought all into a _salvable state_. This declaration met with objection from both the right and the left. Some denied that all had a right to Christ and opposed universal satisfaction in every sense: Palaemon in his _Letters upon Theron and Aspasio_, Symington, Haldane, and especially Dr. Marshall. Others decidedly taught universal satisfaction: William Pringle, John Mc Leod Campbell, James Morison, Robert Morison, A. C. Rutherford, and John Guthrie. The two professors Balmer, †1844, and Brown were even accused by Marshall but nevertheless acquitted by the synod in 1845; cf. Andrew Robertson, _History of the Atonement Controversy in Connexion with the Secession Church from Its Origin to the Present Time_, Edinburgh, 1846. Likewise in other countries the doctrine of particular satisfaction was weakened in the spirit of Grotius or Amyraut or was also decisively rejected: in England by Daniel Whitby, whom Jonathan Edwards opposed; in America by the Edwardean or New England theologians Emmons, Taylor, Park, Fiske, and others; in Germany by P. Volckmann and others; in our country especially by Venema; cf. Ypey, _Gesch. der chr. kerk in de 18e eeuw_ VII 133 f. At present the doctrine of particular satisfaction has been almost universally abandoned, also by Van Oosterzee, § 111, 6; _Leer der Zal._ no. 66; _Heid. Cat._, p. 176; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 430. By contrast, it is still defended by Ch. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 544–562; A. A. Hodge, _The Atonement_, pp. 347–429; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 464–480; W. Cunningham, _Historical Theology_, 2nd ed., Edinburgh: Clark, 1864, II 323–370; Robert S. Candlish, _The Atonement, Its Reality, Completeness and Extent_, London, 1861; Hugh Martin, _The Atonement_, Edinburgh: Hunter, n.d.; Kuyper, _Dat de genade particulier is_. And in recent times it again found support in Ritschl, according to whom the correlate of Christ’s sacrifice is not the individual human being, nor yet all human beings, but specifically the congregation. It alone possesses justification and is full of forgiveness of sins; in Scripture it is always presented as the object of the operations that proceed from Christ, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 47–67; 205 f.; 305 f.; II² 216 f.; III² 103 f.
