## 7.

The whole confession of Christendom is concentrated in this: Jesus is the Christ. Jesus himself appeared with this claim, that he was the Christ. Through this faith Christians came to stand as a distinct sect over against both Jews and Gentiles; after the name of Christ the believers at Antioch were first called Christians. The name Jesus—יְהוֹשׁוּעַ and הוֹשֵׁעַ, 3 p. imp. hi., alongside יְהוֹשִׁיעַ, Ps. 116:6, a. v. ישע, to save, to help, and thus “he,” namely YHWH, “will help,” later shortened to יֵשׁוּעַ, Ιησους, Jhesus, Jesus—was indeed given by Joseph to his son at the angel’s express command, Matt. 1:21, because he was the perfect Savior, Acts 4:12; Heb. 7:25, but it was nevertheless a common name in Israel, Num. 13:16; 1 Sam. 6:14, 18; 2 Kings 15:30; 23:8; Hos. 1:1; Hag. 1:1; Zech. 3:1; Ezra 2:2, 6; Neh. 7:7, 11, 39; 10:24, and so forth; Acts 7:45; Heb. 4:1; Col. 4:11. By contrast, his office, his dignity, comes to expression in the name Christ. The Jesuits name themselves after the name Jesus and thereby arrogate to themselves a name that belongs to no one, for Christ alone is Savior; but they have therefore also frequently brought upon themselves the judgment that they become Christians all the less as they become Jesuits all the more. The believers, however, name themselves after the name Christ, for they are anointed with him as prophets, priests, and kings. Among the other names that designate his office and work, special consideration is due to μεσιτης, 1 Tim. 2:5; Heb. 8:6; 9:15; 12:24, _mediator, sequester, intercessor, interventor_, because in the covenant of grace he unites and reconciles God and the human being with each other through his person and work. In the century of the Reformation, a dispute of no small importance arose concerning this mediatorship of Christ. Over against Osiander, who regarded the essential righteousness of God in Christ as the _materia_ of our justification, Stancarus, professor at Königsberg, defended the proposition that Christ was mediator only according to his human nature and could therefore also justify us only on the ground of the righteousness acquired in his human nature, _Herzog_² 14, 590. In support of this he could appeal to Augustine, _de civ._ XI 2; _Conf._ X 43; Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 19; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III, qu. 26, art. 2, all of whom said that Christ was mediator only _in quantum homo, nam in quantum Deus non mediator sed aequalis Patri est_. These theologians were valued far more highly by Stancarus than the Reformers: _plus valet unus Petrus Lombardus, quam centum Lutheri, ducenti Melanchtones, trecenti Bullingeri, quadringenti Petri martyres et quingenti Calvini; qui omnes si in mortario contunderentur, non exprimeretur una uncia verae theologiae._ Lutherans and Reformed opposed this as strongly as possible and maintained that Christ was mediator according to both natures, that as such he was appointed from eternity, and that he had already fulfilled the office of mediator in the days of the Old Testament. _Die symb. Bücher der ev. luth. K._, ed. Müller, 622, 684; Gerhard, _Loc._ IV 325; Quenstedt III 273; Calvin, in two letters, _C. R._ XXXVII 337–358; the ministers of the church of Zurich, in two letters, _Herzog_² 14, 592; Polanus, _Synt. theol._, p. 430; Turretin, _Th. El._ XIV, qu. 2. The Roman Catholics maintained that Christ was mediator only according to his human nature, but they nevertheless had to acknowledge that Christ, in order to be mediator, had to be both God and human, that both natures constituted the _suppositum_ and the _substantia mediatoris_, and thus could still only assert that Christ accomplished the mediatorial works—offering, suffering, dying—in his human nature, that this nature alone was therefore the _principium quo_, the _principium formale_ of the mediatorial works, Bellarmine, _de Christo_ V c. 1–8; Petavius, _de incarn._ XII c. 1–4. But this separation between the person and the work of the mediator cannot be maintained. What is distinctive about the mediatorial works is precisely that they were performed by the one person with both natures. This does not mean, as Petavius imputes to Lutherans and Calvinists, ibid. XII 4, 9, that they ascribe the mediatorial work to the divine nature of Christ _per sese et ab humana divulsa_. The Son is not mediator according to his divine nature as such, as one with and equal to the Father and the Spirit, but he is mediator according to that divine nature κατ’ οἰκονομιαν, _quatenus secundum eam voluntaria illa dispensatione gratiae se submiserit_, Polanus, p. 430. Scripture therefore also repeatedly names Christ according to his divine nature as the subject of humiliation, John 1:14; 2 Cor. 8:9; Phil. 2:6. The church fathers repeatedly express themselves in the same spirit, Petavius XII 1. Augustine, Lombard, and Thomas meant nothing other than that Christ was and could be mediator not according to his divine nature as such, _ab humana natura divulsa_, but only as the incarnate Son of God; and in the end the Roman Catholic theologians themselves had to defend this again against Stancarus.

Behind this, however, there lurks yet another difference. If Christ is mediator only according to his human nature in the sense of Bellarmine and Petavius, then he was not mediator before his incarnation either, but first began to be mediator at the moment of his conception, and thus the Old Testament was in fact deprived of a mediator, Petavius, _de incarn._ XII 4, 10. But although the Son first became flesh in the fullness of time, he was nevertheless chosen and appointed as mediator from eternity. It cannot be objected to this that David, Solomon, and others were also chosen from eternity as kings and in that sense anointed. For there is this great difference: David, Solomon, and others, and all the elect, existed before their conception only in the idea and decree of God, but the Son was in the beginning with God and was himself God. His election as mediator did not take place apart from him; it bears the character of a _pactum salutis_. The work of redemption is a common work of the Father, Son, and Spirit and immediately after the fall began to be carried out by all three persons. In the same sense in which the Father was from eternity the Father of his children and the Holy Spirit was from eternity the Comforter of believers, the Son was appointed from eternity as mediator and appeared as mediator immediately after the fall. Already under the Old Testament he was active as prophet, priest, and king. The fallen world was immediately given to the Son as mediator for reconciliation and redemption. And in the fullness of time he who was already mediator was revealed in the flesh, 1 John 1:2; 3:5, 8. This explains why the royal office comes to the foreground in Christ. As such he is especially portrayed and predicted in the Old Testament, and with that title he especially appears among his people. But this kingship of Christ is very different from that of earthly rulers. It has its type only in the theocratic, Davidic kingship, which is essentially different from that of other kings. It is a kingship in God’s name, subordinated to God’s will, directing everything toward God’s glory. It is no kingship of violence and weapons but rules and governs in an entirely different, far better way. It rules through Word and Spirit, through grace and truth, through justice and righteousness. The king is therefore at the same time prophet and priest; his power stands in the service of truth and righteousness. Because Christ’s kingship bears an entirely distinctive character, many object to speaking of his royal office or use the title king for Christ in a figurative sense, Wegscheider § 144; Scholten, _L. H. K._ I⁴ 369 f. Indeed, many consider it better to speak in Christ’s case not of office but of personal vocation, for office belongs within a legal community, and Christ’s kingdom is a kingdom of love and not of law, Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 402. In fact, an office is distinguished from a profession, trade, and position in that it presupposes an authority, rests upon appointment by that authority, and serves that authority with one’s person, not for pay. But precisely for this reason the word office is the one that should be used with respect to Christ. For he did not assume the dignity of mediator for himself, but God chose, called, and appointed him, Ps. 2:7; 89:19–21; 110:1–4; 132:17; Isa. 42:1; Heb. 5:4–6. The name Christ is not a professional name but an official name, a title, a dignity, to which Jesus has a claim because he was chosen by God himself. Under the Old Testament the kings especially were anointed, Judg. 9:8, 15; 1 Sam. 9:16; 10:1; 16:13; 2 Sam. 2:4; 5:3; 19:10; 1 Kings 1:34, 39; 2 Kings 9:1; 11:12; 23:30, probably with the sacred anointing oil, 1 Kings 1:39; Ps. 89:21. Anointed one was the name of the theocratic king, Ps. 20:7; 28:8; 84:10; 89:39, and so forth. Anointing with oil was the symbol of divine consecration, of being equipped with the Spirit of God, 1 Sam. 10:1, 9, 10. And Christ received this anointing not in an outward, symbolic sense but in a spiritual, real sense, Isa. 11:2; 42:1; 61:1; Ps. 2:6; 45:8; 89:21; Luke 4:18; John 3:34; Acts 4:27; 10:38; Heb. 1:9, at his conception by the Holy Spirit, Luke 1:35, and at his baptism by John, Matt. 3:16; Mark 1:10; Luke 3:22; John 1:32. And as real as his anointing, of which that under the Old Testament was only a shadow, was his kingship also. The theocratic, Davidic kingship was only its weak type and received its essential fulfillment in Christ’s kingship. Christ is king not in a lesser but in a greater, in a far truer sense than David and Solomon. For it belonged to the idea of the theocratic king that he had to be a man after God’s heart, had to be bound to God’s law, and might not exalt himself as a despot above his brothers, Deut. 17:14–20. Israel’s kings did not keep this royal law, and so prophecy expected another, better king who, himself the Lord’s anointed and God’s servant, would rule his people through truth and righteousness and would conquer his enemies. Christ appears as such a king. He establishes a kingdom of God that now exists only spiritually and morally but is destined one day also to manifest itself outwardly and bodily in the city of God, from which all the ungodly are banished and where God will be all in all. And because he is such a king, a king in the true, full sense, his kingship therefore also includes the prophetic and priestly offices.
