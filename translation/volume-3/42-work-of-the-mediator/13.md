## 13.

While Piscator and many others excluded the _obedientia activa_ from satisfaction, more recent theology does not do justice to the _obedientia passiva_. After Socinianism and Rationalism had subjected the church’s doctrine of satisfaction to sharp criticism, many in the present sought still to maintain it by recasting it in a mystical or ethical sense, above, p. 327 f. The conception is then principally this: Christ’s suffering and death were not necessary to satisfy God’s justice, nor were they demanded by God as a sacrifice for our sins, but they were the consequence of his life in obedience to God’s will, the proof that in his religious-ethical vocation he remained faithful to God unto death, the historically necessary result of his holy life in the midst of a sinful world. Over against such views, which wholly detach the suffering and death of Christ from his person and set them up by themselves as _Etwas Sachliches_, this conception possesses an undeniable right. After all, Christ is the faithful witness, Rev. 1:5; he testified the good confession under Pontius Pilate, 1 Tim. 6:13; his suffering and death are not a fate but an act; he has power to lay down his life as well as to take it up, John 10:18. But this conception itself is nevertheless burdened by objections no less serious. 1º Already this is significant: the mystical and ethical theories speak of a vocation in Jesus’ case and no longer of an office. Herein already lies the entire difference. If Christ practiced a vocation, then by his own aptitude, education, and so forth he developed himself for it; he stands not above but alongside us; he has only outstripped us in a religious and ethical sense; and we can and must conform ourselves as much as possible to his example. If, by contrast, Christ was anointed to the office of prophet, priest, and king, then he was appointed by God, received from him a work to do, and stands with authority above us in order to teach us, to represent us before God’s face, and to govern us according to his will, Martin, _The Atonement_ 105. 2º A consequence of this view is that the suffering and death of Christ remain totally unexplained. Schleiermacher derived them from the fact that Christ as the holy one entered into our sinful community. He made them consist in _Mitgefühl mit menschlicher Schuld und Strafwürdigkeit_ and saw in his death a consequence of his _Eifer für seinen Beruf_, _Chr. Gl._ § 104, 4. Ritschl similarly says that it is fidelity to his vocation that qualifies Christ as a sacrifice. His death was no sacrifice but an unintended consequence of the conflict between him, the holy one, and sinful humanity, _das Accidens seiner positiven Treue im Berufe_, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 525; cf. 415 f.; 441 f.; II 334 f.; Kaftan, _Dogm._ 565–569; Kühl, _Die Heilsbedeutung des Todes Christi_ 190 f. The Groningen theologians saw in Christ’s death something done by human beings, suffered by Jesus, and permitted by God, and understood the δει in Matt. 16:21; Mark 8:31; Luke 9:22 in an ethical sense, Pareau and H. de Groot, _Lineam. theol. christ._, ed. 3, p. 153; H. de Groot, _De Gron. Godg._ 181. A theory that declares the suffering and death of Christ accidental is thereby already fully judged. 3º Scripture, after all, says clearly that Jesus’ suffering and death were determined beforehand and necessary. In more recent times, indeed, the attempt is made to show that Jesus first appeared among his people cheerfully and with great expectations and first saw the necessity of his death from the day at Caesarea Philippi onward, Matt. 16:20 f., as a result of the hostility that gradually developed against his person; for example, Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ I 284–295. But Kähler rightly calls this a legend. The messianic consciousness that Jesus possessed from the beginning of his ministry, as appears from his baptism by John; the name Son of Man, which he ascribed to himself with clear consciousness and a definite intention; his application to himself of Isaiah’s prophecy, Luke 4:21; the prediction that he as the bridegroom would be taken away from his disciples, Mark 2:20; his comparison of himself with Jonah, Matt. 12:40, and with the serpent in the wilderness, John 3:14; the proclamation of the kingdom of heaven in a spiritual sense, of self-denial and cross-bearing, of the hatred and hostility of the world, Matt. 5; 10; 11:16 f.; 12:25 f., prove abundantly that in Jesus’ consciousness death stood fixed from the beginning as the goal of his life. Thus prophecy had instructed him, Nösgen, _Gesch. d. neut. Offenbarung_ I, 1891, p. 395 f.; Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_, 1898, p. 159 f. The day of Caesarea Philippi brought only this change: Jesus openly proclaimed to his disciples that he was the Messiah, that he would be delivered up, and that he would rise on the third day, Matt. 16:21; Mark 8:31; Luke 9:22. And he adds that this _must_ be so, not because he was ethically obligated to die or could remain faithful to his vocation only in this way—for there is no ethical obligation to be delivered up, killed, and even raised—but because it had been so determined in God’s counsel and foretold in Scripture, Matt. 26:54; Luke 22:22; 24:26, 44, 46; John 3:14; 7:30; 8:20; 10:18; 11:9; 12:23; 17:1; 20:9; Acts 2:23; 4:28; 1 Cor. 15:3; cf. Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 45. 4º The two texts Matt. 26:39 and Heb. 5:7 are by no means in conflict with this. According to Heb. 5:7 he did not pray for deliverance from death as though he had not acknowledged it as necessary, for it says clearly that his prayer was heard by God despite his death. Rather, Christ prayed that he might not perish in death but might be rescued from it and raised and glorified. He came precisely to do God’s will through his dying and sanctified us through that will, Heb. 10:5–10. This sheds light on Matt. 26:39–42. Jesus prays there not according to his will, which he precisely submits to that of the Father, but according to the inclination implanted in human nature to flee its own destruction, marginal notes of the States Translation. He dreads death as death, but prays precisely that God may strengthen him to do God’s will in his dying, that God’s will may take place not only upon him but through him. 5º The suffering and death of Christ occupy such a place in Scripture that they cannot be understood as the _accidens_ of his vocational fidelity. The greatest portion of the Gospels is devoted to their description. And they are described not as a martyrdom but as a judgment of God, as the will of the Father, as the sacrifice of a priest. He does not die for his faith but is judicially condemned because he claims to be the Son of God, the Messiah. He does not meet death joyfully but is troubled, sorrowful, amazed, anguished unto death, and in severe struggle, so that his sweat became like drops of blood, Matt. 26:37, 38; Mark 14:33; Luke 22:44; John 12:27. _Si la vie et la mort de Socrate sont d’un sage, la vie et la mort de Jésus sont d’un Dieu_ (Rousseau). In the preaching of the apostles, the cross of Christ is the center and the cause of all the benefits of salvation. Far more than on the life, the emphasis falls on Christ’s death, and that death is always connected with our sins. Christ died on account of sins and thereby acquired righteousness and life for his own. Although that suffering and death may not be detached from the person, they were nevertheless willed as such by God and accomplished by the Son. In Christ’s life they form not an accidental element, rendered necessary only by circumstances, but an essential, indispensable constituent. Through them in the first place atonement for sins, righteousness, and salvation were acquired. 6º It is indeed objected against this that God grants us the benefits of the covenant of grace not _propter_ but only _per Christum_; so already formerly Socinus and others, and now, for example, Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 20; II 426 f.; Gottschick, “Propter Christum,” _Zeits. f. Theol. u. Kirche_, 1897, pp. 352–384. The Lutherans even took pleasure in accusing the Reformed that by virtue of their doctrine of predestination they had to deny the satisfaction of Christ and could regard him only as the _causa instrumentalis_ of salvation. After all, the Reformed themselves also said that Christ was not the _fundamentum_ and _causa electionis_, vol. II, 380. The elect were already objects of God’s love before Christ was designated as their mediator and were chosen not for but in and unto Christ, Gerhard, _Loc._ XVI, _de justif._ § 36; Schneckenburger, _Zur kirchl. Christol._, 1848, p. 45 f.; idem, _Vergl. Darst._ II 264 f.; Schweizer, _Gl. d. ev. ref. K._ II 379, 389. But this accusation is false. Christ is most certainly the proof and revelation of the Father’s love. Logically our election to salvation precedes his election as mediator; thus God was not first moved by Christ to love sinners. But God’s love consists precisely in this: that he causes the salvation he wills to grant sinners to be acquired for them in time by his own Son and thus grants it to them in accordance with his justice. God’s grace therefore does not nullify the satisfaction and merit of Christ but is precisely the ultimate ground for that merit, _quia mero beneplacito mediatorem statuit, qui nobis salutem acquireret_, Calvin, _Inst._ II 17, 1. _Imo quia prius diligit, postea nos sibi reconciliat_, ibid. 16, 3. _Jam nos diligenti reconciliati sumus_, ibid. 4. And these two, God’s love and Christ’s satisfaction, therefore had to and could go together because as creatures we were at the same time the object of his love and as sinners the object of his wrath, ibid. 3. Thus it is sheer grace by which God forgives sins. He does so for his own sake, for the sake of his great name, Isa. 43:23–25; Ezek. 36:21; Eph. 1:17; 1 John 2:12. But this conflicts so little with God’s forgiving our sins for Christ’s sake that God’s name was first revealed to us in Christ. That God forgives sins and grants life solely by grace, for his own sake and not for anything that is in us—this Christ proclaimed to us; this he acquired for us. Forgiveness by grace, as a disposition eternally present in God and preceding the election and sending of Christ (_amor benevolentiae_), was nevertheless first made possible in time through Christ’s sacrifice (_amor complacentiae_). The benefits themselves were acquired by Christ, even though the inclination to grant them to the elect had been present in God from all eternity. Christ was made sin so that we might be the righteousness of God in him. He was born of a woman and born under the law so that he might redeem us from the curse of the law and we might receive adoption as children. As Adam is the cause of sin and death, so Christ is the source of righteousness and life, Matt. 20:28; 27:28; John 1:18; 15:13; Rom. 5:12 f.; 1 Cor. 15:21, 22; 2 Cor. 5:19–21; Gal. 3:13; 4:4; 1 John 4:9. Understood in this way, the expression that God forgives us sins and grants eternal life _propter Christum_, for Christ’s sake, is scriptural and taught by all Christendom, including the Reformed: Calvin, _Inst._ II 16 and 17; commentary on John 15:13; Rom. 5:10; 2 Cor. 5:19; 1 John 4:19, and so forth; Belgic Confession, arts. 21, 22, 23; cf. Scholten, _L. H. K._ I 20; Heppe, _Dogm. d. d. Prot._ I 190; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 37; Petrus de Witte, _Wederl. der Soc. dwalingen_ II 170 f.; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 18, 20, 41 f.; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ IV 40; X 30; Turretin, _de satisf._, p. 7; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 265 f. Finally, 7º, the mystical and moral theories come into conflict at every point with Scripture and with the Christian religion. The former considers the essence of religion to lie in the mystical unity of God and the human being. This was disrupted by sin but restored by Christ, and indeed not so much by what he does as by what he is, more by his person than by his work, more by his birth than by his cross. How this mystical unity of God and the human being is now restored through Christ is difficult to say from this standpoint. Hegel said that this unity exists objectively but was first clearly understood and expressed through Christ. Schleiermacher represents it in this way: Christ, first entering into our sinful fellowship, afterward receives us into the fellowship of his holiness and blessedness. Mediating theology frequently expresses it by saying that Christ infuses a new divine-human life into us. All these modes of conception originate in pantheism and, without this philosophical system, are simply unintelligible. The moral theory proceeds from the thought that God demands no satisfaction but in his love gave us Christ so that through his teaching, life, and death he might assure us of God’s love, change us in our hostile disposition, and deter us from sin. And the governmental theory of Grotius also sees in God the _Rector mundi_, who personally could and would indeed forgive but, in the interest of the world order, must punish sin exemplarily as a deterrent to others. In all these theories one can indeed continue to speak of Christ as Son of God, of his priesthood and sacrifice, but then one speaks in a figurative sense, uses the words in an unusual meaning, and gives occasion for misunderstanding. After all, it is clear that in pantheism and deism there is no place for the Christ of the Scriptures. Christ’s high priesthood then changes into martyrdom, his sacrifice into an example, the Christian religion into a pedagogy, and the church into a school. Righteousness is no virtue of God but is in its place only in the state. Sin is no guilt, and punishment is no restoration of right. The human being is not so evil that he cannot be changed for the better by a moral example, by a shock or an impression; cf. Ch. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 566 etc.; A. A. Hodge, _The Atonement_, ch. 21. Objective satisfaction is converted into subjective reconciliation; the actual, true reconciliation then first takes place when the human being follows Jesus’ example and changes himself. The believers under the Old Testament did not yet have Jesus’ example; therefore they either were all lost or were saved in another way than we, and Christ is not the only name given under heaven among human beings for salvation. Above all, in all the theories mentioned, the connection Scripture establishes between the death of Christ and the forgiveness of our sins and eternal life remains unexplained. It cannot be seen how Christ’s death, as an example, can be the ground of these benefits—the reason why God for Christ’s sake forgives us sins and grants life. In fact, everything comes down to this: through his image Christ makes a deep impression upon the human being’s understanding or will or also feeling, and thus frees him from the opinion that God is angry with him because of his sin, or from the selfishness that holds him bound, or from the feeling of misery that weighs him down. But in none of these cases are true peace and rest granted to the human being. For the conscience is not thereby cleansed, and the sense of guilt is not thereby removed. There is peace only in the blood of the cross!
