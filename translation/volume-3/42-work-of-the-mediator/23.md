## 23.

But just as in the state of humiliation, so too everything Christ has become in the state of exaltation is for the good of his church. What he received as a reward for his work, for himself and for his own, cannot be separated. He is παντα και ἐν πασιν, Col. 3:11. The _pleroma_ that dwells in Christ must also dwell in the church; she is filled εἰς παν το πληρωμα του θεου, Eph. 3:19; Col. 2:2, 10. It is God who fills Christ, Col. 1:19; it is Christ who fills the church, Eph. 1:23. The church is therefore his _pleroma_, that which he perfects and, from himself (πληρουμενος), gradually, Eph. 4:10, fills with himself, and which therefore gradually becomes full and filled and thus in turn fills Christ, Eph. 1:23. The _completum_ becomes a _complementum_. For the church does not exist without Christ, but neither does Christ exist without the church. He is her κεφαλη ὑπερ παντα, Eph. 1:22; Col. 1:18, and she is his σωμα, which is formed from him and receives its growth, Eph. 4:16; Col. 2:19, and thus grows εἰς μετρον ἡλικιας του πληρωματος του Χριστου, Eph. 4:13. The union between Christ and the church is as close as that between vine and branches, bridegroom and bride, husband and wife, cornerstone and building. She may be called with him the one Christ, 1 Cor. 12:12. To perfect her, he was exalted to the Father’s right hand and continues his prophetic, priestly, and royal activity in heaven.

Just as in the days of the Old Testament and of his walk on earth, Christ is even now the only prophet and teacher of his church. There is no other master, Matt. 23:8, 10. Alongside, above, or in place of Christ, the church has no need of divination, oracle, enthusiasm, spiritism, or infallible papacy. For Christ is wisdom; all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in him, 1 Cor. 1:24, 30; Col. 2:3. And it is Christ himself who teaches his church through his Word and Spirit, so that all of them, taught by God, might be prophets and proclaim the great deeds of God, Num. 11:29; Jer. 31:33, 34; Matt. 11:25–27; John 6:45; Heb. 8:10, 11; 1 John 2:20. And so also in the state of exaltation he still remains active as priest. After completing his sacrifice on Golgotha, as high priest he entered the heavenly sanctuary—not with the blood of goats and calves but with his own blood, Heb. 9:12–14—through the tabernacle of his body, 9:11, and the veil of his flesh, 10:20. This sanctuary had its pattern in the holy of holies on Zion, 6:20; 9:12, 24. He entered to make intercession there for his own and to appear before God’s face, 7:25; 9:24. From this the Socinians attempted to infer that Christ was not yet a priest in the proper sense on earth, that his sacrifice on the cross was not the true sacrifice but only its introduction and preparation. Just as under the Old Testament the atoning act consisted not in slaughtering the sacrificial animal but in sprinkling the blood upon the altar or the mercy seat, Lev. 16:11–16, so Christ brings about atonement not on earth but in heaven; cf. Socinus, _de J. Christo Servatore_, _Bibl. Fr. Pol._ II 164; Volkelius, _de verit. relig._ III 37; Fock, _Der Socin._ 635, 646 f.; and even now W. Milligan, _The Ascension and Heavenly Priesthood of Our Lord_, London, 1892; Doedes, _Jaarb. v. Wet. Theol._, 1846, pp. 293 f., 313 f.; Seeberg, _Der Tod Christi in s. Bedeutung für die Erlösung_, 1895, pp. 14, 16 f., and so forth. This has rightly been opposed by others, for in the Old Testament sacrifice the various atoning acts are indeed separated temporally, but they nevertheless form one whole. It is the blood of a slaughtered animal that, through its shedding and sprinkling, restored the sacrificer to God’s favor and delivered him from sin and its punishment. In the Epistle to the Hebrews, therefore, as in the whole New Testament, atoning power is ascribed to the sacrifice Christ once offered on the cross, 2:17; 7:27; 8:3; 9:12, 26, 28; 10:10, 14, 18; 13:12. Through it all the benefits—forgiveness, cleansing, sanctification, perfection—were acquired. Because Christ once sacrificed himself on the cross and died, he cannot even do so a second time, for every human being dies only once. His sacrifice in death is not susceptible of repetition, 9:26–28. Christ’s entrance with his blood into the heavenly sanctuary therefore cannot, according to the view of the Epistle to the Hebrews, be understood as a sacrifice. It is indeed once said, in 9:7, that the high priest offered, προσφερει, the blood with which he entered the holy of holies for himself and the people’s transgressions. But this offering of the blood is nevertheless essentially distinct from the one that took place outside, in the forecourt; and in the Old Testament only the bringing of the blood is called this. But of Christ’s entrance into heaven with his blood it is nowhere said that it is a sacrifice. It is not and cannot be one. Through the one sacrifice he accomplished everything, 9:26–28; 10:12, 14. Christ’s entrance with his blood into the innermost sanctuary therefore has only the purpose of causing the salvation acquired through his death to avail before God’s face for our benefit. Precisely because atonement was accomplished through the shedding of blood—that is, through the sacrifice on the cross—Christ as high priest can enter heaven with his blood and appear before God’s face, 9:12–14, 26. His priestly activity in heaven therefore consists not in any sacrifice but in his intercession and appearance before God’s face on behalf of his people, 7:25; 9:24; cf. Rom. 8:34; 1 John 2:2. Yet there is truth in the view that Thalhofer, _Handbuch der kath. Liturgik_ I², 1894, pp. 223–236, derived from Heb. 8:1–4: that although Christ accomplished everything with one sacrifice, he nevertheless still continues that sacrifice in heaven in a heavenly manner and continually renews at the Father’s right hand his act of obedience on Golgotha. For the sacrifice of Christ, once made on the cross, lives on not merely in memory but in the disposition from which it proceeded. There is in heaven no repetition, no renewal, no reproduction of the sacrifice on the cross. But that sacrifice, once accomplished, continues to operate in his appearance before God’s face and in his intercession for us, 7:25; 9:24. Therefore, on the one hand, it can be said that Christ has perfected all through one sacrifice, 10:24, and, on the other hand, that he saves completely all who go to God through him, since he always lives to intercede for them, 7:25. Cf. Cloppenburg, _Op._ II 889–902; Nic. Arnoldus, _Religio Socin. Fran._, 1654, pp. 678–706; de Witte, _Wederl. der Soc. dwal._ II 152 f.; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 7, 15 sq.; Maccovius, _Coll. theol._ I 240 sq.; _Jaarb. v. Wet. Theol._ IV 18 f.; Weiss, _Bibl. Theol. d. N. T._ § 121; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 443 f.; Martin, _Atonement_ 115. Therefore too, because Christ was and is and eternally remains priest, the church on earth no longer needs a priest. All believers are priests, Rom. 12:1; 1 Pet. 2:5; Rev. 1:6. No sacrifice for sin need be brought anymore, not even an unbloody one in the Mass, for in Christ’s intercession a continual voice goes out to God from the one sacrifice accomplished on the cross—not for vengeance, as from Abel’s blood, but for grace and forgiveness, Heb. 12:24. Christ’s intercession is no longer supplication as in the days of his flesh, Calvin, _Inst._ III 20, 20, but is Christ’s steadfast and gracious will, John 17:24, to lead all his people to heavenly blessedness on the ground of his sacrifice. Thus Christ is our only priest, who remains eternally according to the order of Melchizedek, continually covers our sins with his sacrifice, always appears before the Father as our Paraclete, takes our side against all the accusations of Satan, the world, and our own heart, makes our prayers and thanksgivings pleasing to the Father, always assures us confident access to the throne of grace, and causes all the blessings of grace to come to us from his fullness, Luke 22:32; John 14:16; 17:9 f.; Rom. 1:7; 8:32 f.; 1 Cor. 1:3; 2 Cor. 1:2; Eph. 1:3; 1 Tim. 4:8; Heb. 7:25; 9:24; 1 John 2:2.

And thus Christ is and remains our eternal king. Although anointed to this office from eternity, according to his human nature he nevertheless first appeared as king at his exaltation. Then he received the name Lord, was appointed Son of God, and received all power in heaven and on earth. Christ is king in the first place over his people, in the _regnum gratiae_, Ps. 2:6; Isa. 9:5; 11:1–5; Luke 1:33; 19:21–23; 23:42, 43; John 18:33; 19:19. And he manifests this kingship in that he gathers, protects, and governs his church and leads her to eternal blessedness, Matt. 16:18; 28:20; John 10:28. But because his kingship bears an entirely different character from that of the rulers of the earth, in the New Testament he is much more frequently called the head of the church, 1 Cor. 11:3; Eph. 1:22; 4:15; 5:23; Col. 1:18; 2:19. He rules not by violence but by justice and righteousness, by grace and love, by Word and Spirit. Then too he is described in the New Testament especially as king when the conquest of his enemies is discussed. For in order that he may truly gather and protect his church and lead her to eternal blessedness, he must also as mediator have power over all creatures, Ps. 2:9; 72:8; 110:1–3; Matt. 28:18; 1 Cor. 15:24, 27; Eph. 1:22; Phil. 2:9–11; 1 Pet. 3:22; Rev. 1:5; 17:14. This does not imply that the world is positively governed through Christ, but it does imply that it stands under his power, is subject to him, and will one day, even though unwillingly, acknowledge and honor him as Lord. In particular, his power over the kingdom of Satan also belongs here. The conception of many church fathers, that Christ brought his sacrifice to Satan and by a ruse deprived him of his booty, is unscriptural. But Christ did nevertheless also triumph through his cross over the world of fallen spirits. He came to earth to destroy the works of the devil, 1 John 3:8, and fought against him throughout his life, Luke 4:13, especially in the final period, when it was the hour and power of darkness, Luke 22:53. He was the stronger, Luke 11:22, and the devil had nothing in him, John 14:30. He already saw him fall like lightning from heaven and deprived him of his armor, Luke 10:18; 11:22. Especially through the cross he triumphed over the rulers and powers, Col. 2:15; deprived him of the weapons of sin, death, and the world, John 16:33; 1 John 4:4; 1 Cor. 15:55, 56; Heb. 2:14; and cast him outside the domain of his kingdom, John 12:31. And he celebrated his triumph over the evil spirits particularly in the resurrection. This is clearly taught in 1 Pet. 3:19–22. There is no discussion there of a descent of Christ into hell to proclaim the gospel to the lost. For it says that Christ was first made alive—that is, raised—and then went to preach. There is no ground for making with the Lutherans a temporal distinction between the _vivificatio_ and the _resurrectio_ and then placing the descent into hell in that interval. Nor is there anywhere in Scripture any indication that after his resurrection, before ascending to heaven, Christ first went to hell. On the other hand, the exegesis that Christ went in the Spirit to Noah’s contemporaries and preached to them is also untenable. ἐν ᾡ clearly refers to the Christ made alive; πορευθεις, cf. v. 22, allows no other interpretation. Christ’s preaching in the Spirit to Noah’s contemporaries many centuries before is irrelevant here. The pericope contains something entirely different. Peter exhorts believers to suffer while doing good and therein to follow Christ. He suffered while doing good, for he suffered for sins, as a righteous one for the unrighteous, and indeed with this purpose: that he might bring us, the unrighteous, to God. That is suffering while doing good! And now Christ was indeed put to death in the flesh, but he was made alive and raised in Spirit—that is, since the πνευμα ἁγιωσυνης was the principle of his whole life, he was raised as Spirit. And as such, as made-alive, risen Spirit, as Lord and King, going, πορευθεις—that is, not to hell, but, as appears from v. 22, going _to heaven_—he preached to the spirits in prison. That is, his going to heaven as the risen Lord, Acts 2:36, was a κηρυγμα to the spirits in prison. What the content of that κηρυγμα was is not said and need not be said. The resurrection and ascension were themselves Christ’s rich, mighty, triumphant κηρυγμα to the spirits in prison. That Peter now has Christ bring that κηρυγμα through his ascension specifically to those spirits in prison who in Noah’s days were disobedient despite God’s patience and despite seeing the construction of the ark has a twofold reason. First, in Scripture those contemporaries of Noah were always the most ungodly of all human beings; and second, they perished and Noah with his own were saved through one and the same water. Likewise, the water of baptism through the _resurrection of Christ_ is destruction for the ungodly and salvation for believers. For Christ, who is risen and who instituted that baptism and imparts power to it, sits at God’s right hand after all angels and authorities and powers were made subject to him through the ascension. Christ suffered while doing good and conquered; let believers follow in his footsteps! And just as over all fallen spirits, so Christ as mediator also has power in his _regnum potentiae_ over all his enemies. And he will not rest until they are all placed under his feet.

When at the end of days Christ has won his church and conquered all his enemies, he will hand over the βασιλεια, the kingship, the royal office, to the Father. His mediatorial work is then completed. The work the Father assigned him is fully accomplished. God himself is then king forever and ever. A difference arose early concerning the nature of this subjection of Christ to the Father. Marcellus of Ancyra wrote a treatise on the subjection of the Lord Christ and was accused of the doctrine that Christ’s kingdom and also the union of the human nature with the Logos would come to an end, Schwane, _D. G._ II 136, 148. Marcellus was opposed by Eusebius and later by Basil. The _Nicaeno-Constantinopolitanum_ added to the confession that Christ would return to judge the living and the dead the words οὗ της βασιλειας οὐκ ἐσται τελος, _cujus regni non erit finis_, Hahn, _Bibl. d. Symbole u. s. w._ 164–166; cf. Petavius, _de incarn._ XII 18; Pesch, _Prael._ IV 84. Later the Socinians taught that Christ, whom the Father had temporarily appointed governor, would one day step down, just as a general, after having won the victory, returns his power and dominion to the ruler. From this they inferred that the Son of God, because he would one day be subjected to the Father, could not be the supreme God; cf., against this, Petavius, _de trin._ III 5; Bisterfeldius, _de uno Deo, Patre, Filio ac Spiritu Sº_ I 2, 26; Vitringa V 443–446. There was also disagreement among the Reformed. Some said that Christ’s kingship was economic and temporary, Calvin, _Inst._ II 14, 3; 15, 5; commentary on 1 Cor. 15:28; Alting, _Theol. probl. nova_ XII 36; Pareus on 1 Cor. 15; Kuyper, _Enc._ II 321. Others were of the opinion that although a change comes in the manner of governing, his kingship is nevertheless eternal, Mastricht V 8, 9; Moor III 1129; Vitringa V 443. The difference is easily resolved in this sense: the mediatorship of reconciliation, and thus to that extent also Christ’s prophetic, priestly, and royal office, comes to an end. God will be king and all in all. But what remains is the mediatorship of union. Christ remains prophet, priest, and king, as this is naturally given with human nature, enclosed in the image of God, and realized most highly and richly in Christ as the image of God. Christ is and remains the head of the church, from whom all life and blessedness flow to her eternally. Whoever wished to deny this would also have to arrive at the doctrine that the Son would one day lay aside and destroy his human nature; and for this all ground in Scripture is lacking.
