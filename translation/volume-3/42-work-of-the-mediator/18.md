## 18.

The fruit of this obedience of Christ is nothing less than the whole of salvation. Under this concept are included all the benefits that Christ has acquired for his own. God is called σωτηρ in Scripture, Luke 1:47, and so forth, but Christ too bears that name, because he saves his people from their sins, Matt. 1:21; Luke 2:11. He is the ἀρχηγος της σωτηριας, Heb. 2:10, the αἰτιος σωτηριας αἰωνιου, Heb. 5:9, and his gospel is the gospel της σωτηριας, Eph. 1:13. This σωτηρια is a deliverance from sin and all its consequences and a participation in the highest blessedness; it therefore stands opposed to θανατος, 2 Cor. 7:10; ἀπωλεια, Phil. 1:28; and ὀργη, 1 Thess. 5:9, and endures forever, Heb. 5:9. It therefore includes within itself many particular benefits, all of which are also named separately in Scripture. Foremost is the καταλλαγη, _reconciliatio_, reconciliation. According to Scripture, the sacrifice of Christ has an objective significance, one that is valid also before God. In the Old Testament the sacrifices were intended to cover the sins of the offerer before the face of God, כפר, LXX ἐξιλασκεσθαι. This atonement nowhere has God as its direct object, but it nevertheless takes place with reference to him and before his face, Lev. 1:3; 6:7; 10:17; 15:15, 30; 19:22; Num. 15:28; 31:50, and, by covering sin, is intended to avert his wrath, Num. 8:19; 16:46, and to render him gracious, ἱλασκεσθαι, ἱλαον ποιειν, _propitium reddere, placare_. Likewise, in the New Testament Christ is the ἱλαστηριον, Rom. 3:25, and the ἱλασμος, 1 John 2:2; 4:10, a merciful and faithful High Priest in matters pertaining to God, εἰς το ἱλασκεσθαι τας ἁμαρτιας του λαου, Heb. 2:17; as High Priest, by the sacrifice of his perfect obedience, he covered the sins of his people and thus averted God’s wrath and acquired his grace. It has indeed been asserted by Socinians, Remonstrants, Rationalists, and also most modern theologians—cf., for example, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ 104, 4; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II² 230 f.; Kaftan, _Dogm._ 460—that God is love, does not need to be reconciled, and is himself the author of reconciliation. But this rests partly on misunderstanding and is otherwise contradicted by Scripture. For Scripture teaches clearly, also in the New Testament, that God is wrathful over sins, Rom. 1:18; Gal. 3:10; Eph. 2:3; that he too, on his part, had to be reconciled, Rom. 5:9, 10; 2 Cor. 5:18, 19; Gal. 3:13; and that Christ was a ἱλασμος for this purpose, Rom. 3:25; Heb. 2:17; 1 John 2:2; 4:10. This is in no way inconsistent, however, with the fact that God is love and himself gave Christ as an atonement for our sins. For his wrath is not an evil passion of hatred or envy, and his righteousness is not a thirst for vengeance, but both are compatible with the highest love. Just as a mother grieves all the more over her son’s wandering the more she loves him, and just as a judge must sometimes condemn a relative or friend to whom he feels himself intimately bound as a person, so too in God wrath against sin can coexist with love toward his creatures. _Odit in unoquoque nostrum quod feceramus, amavit quod fecerat_, Bede in Turretin, _de satisf._ 86. _Diligit omnes homines quantum ad naturam quam ipse fecit, odit tamen eos quantum ad culpam, quam contra eum homines contraxerunt_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ III qu. 49 art. 4. Because of our sins we are indeed objects of God’s wrath, _verum quia Dominus quod suum est in nobis perdere non vult, adhuc aliquid invenit, quod pro sua benignitate amet_, Calvin, _Inst._ II 16, 3. And this in turn must not be conceived as though, at the moment of Christ’s sacrifice, God suddenly changed his disposition and attitude. For in God there is neither change nor shadow of turning; all his attributes are one with his being; in eternity there is neither before nor after. When Scripture speaks of God’s wrath and of his reconciliation with us, it does not speak falsely, but it does speak according to our human capacity. There is no change in the being of God, but there is change in the relation in which he stands to his creatures. Nor does he place himself in relation to the creature as if it existed in some measure outside him; rather, he himself places all things and all people in those relations to himself that he eternally and immutably wills, and precisely so—in that manner and at that moment in time—in which they take place in reality; cf. vol. II 125 f. Therefore it is also God himself who brings about reconciliation in Christ, 2 Cor. 5:19; he reconciles himself through the sacrifice of the cross; in Christ’s obedience he causes himself and all his virtues to be acknowledged and maintains himself as God before the eyes of all his creatures. Because Christ is truly God and also had to be truly God for this divine work, it is God himself who through the cross reconciles all things to himself. In Christ he places the church in such a relation to himself that it beholds him, the immutable one, no longer in his wrath but in his grace. _Jam diligenti nos sibi reconciliavit. Quia prius diligit, postea nos sibi reconciliat._ Cf. Augustine, _de trin._ V 16; _Enchir._ 33; Lombard and others on _Sent._ III dist. 19, 6; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III qu. 49 art. 4; Calvin, _Inst._ II 16, 2–4; Turretin, _de satisf._ p. 49, 86, 87; Moor III 448–450; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ IV 2, 263; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 612; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ II 181 f., 194 f.; Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Vers._ 362 f.; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 401, and so forth; A. A. Hodge, _The atonement_, ch. 9, and so forth.

Thus, through the sacrifice of Christ, a relation of reconciliation and peace has been established between God and humanity. As ἱλασμος, Christ has _gesühnt_ sin and thereby _versöhnt_ God. The distinction between ἱλασμος and καταλλαγη does not consist in the former being objective and the latter subjective. The καταλλαγη too is an objective relation between God and the world, established by God himself, 2 Cor. 5:18, 19. But in ἱλασκεσθαι Christ as Mediator is the subject: through his sacrifice, by covering sins, he averts God’s wrath and acquires his grace. In καταλλασσειν, however, God himself is the subject, 2 Cor. 5:19; by giving Christ as ἱλαστηριον, he establishes a relation of peace between himself and the world. He is no longer wrathful; that which made him our ἀντιδικος, namely, sin, has been covered by Christ’s sacrifice; in Christ he established such a relation that we no longer have him against us; he laid aside his enmity because its cause, sin, was removed through the death of Christ, and now stands toward the world in a relation of friendship and peace. Thus καταλλαγη is the _reconciliatio, Versöhnung_ brought about by _expiatio, placatio, Versühnung_. This καταλλαγη is the content of the gospel; all has been accomplished, God is reconciled, and there is nothing more for us to do. The whole διακονια κης καταλλαγης consists in the invitation to human beings: καταλλαγητε τῳ θεῳ; you too, on your part, lay aside enmity, enter into that relation of peace in which God, through the sacrifice of Christ, has placed himself toward sinners, and believe the gospel, Rom. 5:9, 10; 2 Cor. 5:18–21; cf. Eph. 2:16; Col. 1:20–22; Cremer, s.v. ἱλ. and καταλλ.; Philippi on Rom. 5:10; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 99 f. This already passes judgment on the whole conception that separates satisfaction and reconciliation and has the latter come about only when a person believes and repents. Human beings do not reconcile themselves with God, as though they, with and alongside God, were the subject of reconciliation; rather, God reconciled the world to himself without its assistance, apart from it, without its having contributed the least thing to it or needing to contribute anything. Human beings only receive reconciliation as a gift, Rom. 5:11, and accept it through faith, 2 Cor. 5:20. But all kinds of benefits flow from this one benefit of reconciliation acquired by Christ. Nor could it be otherwise. When the relation between God and the world has been set right, then in due time everything is set in order: the relation between heaven and earth, angels and human beings, human beings among themselves, and also the relation of human beings to sin, death, the world, Satan, and so forth. In the sphere of right, the case has been decided. God is in the right, and therefore sooner or later he will everywhere, in every domain and before all creatures, be acknowledged as in the right. Right is on his side, and one day it will be recognized by all, willingly or unwillingly. Thus in the καταλλαγη, God’s relation of peace in Christ toward the world, all kinds of other benefits are enclosed. The fruits of Christ’s sacrifice are not restricted to any one domain; they are not limited, as so many today suppose, to religious-ethical life, to the heart, the private chamber, the church, but extend to the whole world. For although sin may be powerful, the gracious gift is not like the trespass; the grace of God and the gift by grace abound exceedingly, Rom. 5:15. The benefits that come to us from God’s καταλλαγη in Christ are too many to name; see above, p. 312 f. They may be classified as: _juridical_, namely, forgiveness of sins, Mark 14:24; Heb. 9:22; justification, Rom. 3:24; 4:25; 5:9; 8:34; 1 Cor. 1:30; 2 Cor. 5:21; adoption as children, Gal. 3:26; 4:5, 6; the right to eternal life and the heavenly inheritance, Rom. 8:17; 1 Pet. 1:4; and also redemption, ἀπολυτρωσις, Eph. 1:7; Col. 1:14; Heb. 9:15, which, however, sometimes has a broader meaning, Rom. 3:24; 8:21, 23; 1 Cor. 1:30; Eph. 1:14; 4:30; 1 Pet. 1:18, 19; _mystical_, consisting in being crucified, buried, raised, and seated in heaven with Christ, Rom. 6–8; Gal. 2:20; Col. 3:1–13; _ethical_, namely, regeneration, John 1:12, 13; being made alive, Eph. 2:1, 5; sanctification, 1 Cor. 1:30; 6:4; washing, 1 Cor. 6:11; cleansing, 1 John 2:9; sprinkling, 1 Pet. 1:2; in body, soul, and spirit, 2 Cor. 5:17; 1 Thess. 5:23; _moral_, consisting in following Christ, who left us his example, Matt. 10:38; 16:24; Luke 9:33; John 8:12; 12:26; 2 Cor. 8:9; Phil. 2:5; Eph. 2:10; 1 Pet. 2:21; 4:1; _economic_, namely, the fulfillment of the Old Testament covenant, the inauguration of a new covenant, Mark 14:24; Heb. 7:22; 9:15; 12:24; freedom from the law, Rom. 7:1 f.; Gal. 2:19; 3:13, 25; 4:5; 5:1, and so forth; the cancellation of the certificate of the law, the demolition of the dividing wall, and the reconciliation of Jew and Gentile and of all other oppositions existing within humanity into unity in Christ, Gal. 3:28; Eph. 2:11–22; Col. 2:21; _physical_, namely, victory over the world, John 16:33; over death, 2 Tim. 1:10; Heb. 2:15; over hell, 1 Cor. 15:15; Rev. 1:18; 20:14; over Satan, Luke 10:8; 11:22; John 14:30; Heb. 2:14; 1 Cor. 15:55, 56; Col. 2:15; 1 Pet. 3:22; 1 John 3:8; Rev. 12:10; 20:2, and so forth. In one word: the whole re-creation, the complete restoration of the world and humanity burdened with guilt, corrupted, and shattered by sin, is the fruit of Christ’s work. Objectively, in principle, and in the sphere of right, he brought about that re-creation through his cross. Then the καταλλαγη between God and the world was established. And therefore, in due time—for everything proceeds in a fixed order—Christ will one day present the church to the Father without spot or wrinkle, deliver the kingdom to God, and God will be all in all, 1 Cor. 15:22–28.

In theology this wealth of Christ’s benefits has always been recognized. Through Christ, Clement of Alexandria said, the earth has become a πελαγος ἀγαθων. Christ was spoken of as _mediator, redemptor, reconciliator, liberator, dispensator, salvator, medicus, dominus, pastor, rex_, and the work accomplished by him was designated as θεοποιησις, θειωσις, _deificatio, vivificatio, salvatio, liberatio, redemptio, restitutio, purgatio, regeneratio, illuminatio, resurrectio_, and so forth. Sometimes attempts were also made to classify all these benefits to some extent, as in the familiar lines: _Propitians, purgans, redimens, ut victima, sponsor Salvavit, sic jura Dei verumque requirunt_, or under the headings _expiatio, remissio, consummatio_, and so forth; cf. Athanasius, _de incarn._ 54; Gregory of Nazianzus, _Or._ 2; Eusebius, _Dem. Ev._ IV 21; Augustine, _de pecc. mer._ I 26; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III qu. 48, 49; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ IV 1; Petavius, _de incarn._ XII c. 6, 7; Polanus, _Synt._ VI c. 18; Voetius, _Disp._ II 229 sq.; Mastricht V 18, 22; Turretin, _de satisf._ 317; M. Vitringa VI 121. Yet in most cases no logical order or regular treatment was achieved. The conception of Christ’s work as _satisfactio_ and _meritum_, which arose after Anselm, could not make up for this lack. The two are distinguished only logically, for the same work of Christ is _satisfactio_ insofar as he brought his sacrifice to God and met his demand, and it is _meritum_ insofar as Christ thereby acquired salvation for us from God; Thomas, III qu. 48 art. 1 and 2; Petavius, _de incarn._ XII 9; Perrone, _Prael._ IV 311; Calvin, _Inst._ II 17 1; Voetius, _Disp._ II 228; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 18, 14, 20; Quenstedt III 225; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 283, and so forth. Both concepts also place the work of Christ too one-sidedly under the category of work and merit; the far more important question is what Christ “merited” and how this is related to his sacrifice. In modern times, therefore, these terms have been almost entirely abandoned, and the fruit of Christ’s work has instead been designated as _Erlösung_ or _Versöhnung_. The former designation gained favor especially through Schleiermacher. He does indeed also speak of a new creation brought about by Christ, but for him this coincides with _Erlösung_, _Chr. Gl._ § 89, 1, 2; and by this he understands the communication of Christ’s sinless perfection, § 88, the reception of believers into the power of his God-consciousness, § 100, which takes place neither magically nor empirically but mystically, through creative divine activity, through Christ’s self-revelation in his church, § 100, 2, 3. Alongside this, Schleiermacher then also posits a reconciling activity of Christ, consisting in his receiving believers into the fellowship of his undisturbed blessedness and causing them, by means of their communion of life with him, to share in the forgiveness of sins, § 101. Ritschl, by contrast, summarizes the operation of Christ under justification and reconciliation. The fruits of his life do not consist in an _Umstimmung_ of God from an angry judge into a gracious Father, II 208 f., 217 f.; III 439; nor in actual ransoming, II 221; nor in deliverance from death, II 86; nor yet in mystical dying and rising with Christ, for Rom. 6 is strong symbolism and supplies no material for a dogma, II 226 f. Rather, through his whole person and life Christ assures and guarantees to us that God is love; despite our sins, he admits us to fellowship with God, III 506 f. The justification Christ grants us is not remission of guilt and punishment or imputation of his righteousness, but removal of the consciousness of guilt and thereby of the separation between us and God, III 51, 61—the removal of the thought that sin prevents fellowship with God, III 60. The consequence and effect of this justification is reconciliation. This consists in the person who accepts by faith the justification that is properly a good of the church now also entering subjectively into that new relation to God, laying aside enmity on his part, and placing himself in a relation of peace with God; it is an ethical change within us, II 230 f., 342 f.; III 74–76; _Unterricht_ § 37. There is a great difference between Schleiermacher and Ritschl: for the former the person, and for the latter the work, of Christ stands in the foreground; for the first, the subjective change of the human being comes about along a mystical path, through communication of life, and for the second along an ethical path, through teaching and example; the first benefit for Schleiermacher is _Erlösung_, the communication of Christ’s sinless perfection, and for Ritschl the objective, synthetic justification, which is first of all a good of the church. But despite this difference the agreement is still greater: for both, Christ is the sinless human being who stands in a special fellowship with God; both deny passive obedience in Christ’s work; his suffering and death are only the necessary consequence of his fidelity to God, maintained to the end; for both, the connection between Christ’s work and its fruits in the believer, in the church, remains unclear; both shift the center of gravity of redemption and reconciliation from the objective work of Christ to the subjective change of believers. And even though Ritschl appears to have justification as an objective benefit of the church and as a synthetic judgment precede faith, in fact, for him too, justification is made dependent on subjective reconciliation—not for the church as a whole but for each individual; cf. _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ 1888 S. 21, 22; Kübel, _Ueber den Unterschied der posit. u. der liber. Richtung in der mod. Theol._² 1893 S. 150; Kähler, _Zur Lehre v. d. Versöhnung_ 32 f. Above all, however, both agree in restricting the operation of Christ’s life and suffering to the religious and ethical domain. Now it is true that, even with this restriction, neither one clearly shows how the person and work of Christ can bring about this change in the religious and ethical domain. On the other hand, however, it is understandable to some extent that, if the fruit of Christ’s work is restricted to this domain, his work and person are also conceived as Schleiermacher and Ritschl conceive them; for what the former says is true: _die eigenthümliche Thätigkeit und die ausschliessliche Würde des Erlösers weisen auf einander zurück und sind im Selbstbewusstsein der Gläubigen unzertrennlich eins_, _Chr. Gl._ § 92. On this conception it is understandable that passive obedience is denied, that Christ’s deity consists merely in God’s being in him, and that resurrection, ascension, and so forth are unnecessary; a pantheistic-mystical or deistic-moral operation of Christ’s person and work is then sufficient for salvation.

But this is truly not in agreement with the teaching of Holy Scripture; it is a failure to recognize the person of Christ and a diminution of his work. Christ is not merely an ἀνθρωπος ἐνθεος, but the eternal and only-begotten Son of God, the radiance of his glory and the express image of his being, himself God, praised above all forever. And the fruit of his life and death is not merely some magical or moral influence that goes out from him into the world, but is nothing less than the ἀποκαταστασις παντων, Acts 3:21, the ἀνακεφαλαιωσις των παντων ἐν τῳ Χριστῳ, τα ἐπι τοις οὐρανοις και ἐπι της γης, Eph. 1:10. This work of re-creation has its principle and origin in the perfect sacrifice of Christ, or rather in the καταλλαγη that God established in Christ between himself and the world. God does not wish to prevail through superior force. It would have been easy for him to destroy the whole world in his wrath and call another world and humanity into existence, Num. 14:12. But God exalts himself in judgment and sanctifies himself in righteousness, Isa. 5:16. Sin is not a physical but an ethical power. Satan’s ungodly power over the world resides in sin, and sin has its power in the law, 1 Cor. 15:56. Thus it pleased God to overcome this power in a moral manner, along the way of right and righteousness. Not through violence or force, but through the cross, which canceled the certificate of debt of the law, God triumphed over the rulers and powers, stripped them of their armor, and publicly exposed them to shame, Col. 2:15. And now, therefore, because God has overcome sin and all its power along the way of right, he is free, even with his adversaries themselves as judges, to justify the ungodly, Rom. 4:5; no one can bring a charge against God’s elect, Rom. 8:33. It is God who justifies and who, in the justification of the one who is of faith in Jesus, himself proves to be righteous, Rom. 3:26. God can do this without prejudice to—indeed, to the glory of—his righteousness, because Christ died and was raised, Rom. 8:34. On the ground of that sacrifice he can wrest the world and humanity from sin, extend his kingdom, gather all things under Christ as head, and one day be all in all. No one, not even Satan, can object to this; in the end everyone will have to acknowledge that God is righteous and that Christ is the lawful, rightful Lord, Phil. 2:11. Nor, then, is there a physical or magical connection between the benefits Christ acquired and his person and sacrifice, as if some substance of divine-human life, divine nature, and so forth were poured out from him into us, as the theosophists imagine it. But in Christ God paved for himself the royal way of right in order to display the riches of his grace toward his fallen creature. From the objective καταλλαγη established by God himself, apart from the world, all the aforementioned benefits flow to humanity for Christ’s sake: justification, sanctification, complete redemption, the whole re-creation. And because this work of re-creation is so great, greater even than that of creation and preservation, the one to whom this work was entrusted had to be not only a true and righteous human being but also stronger than all creatures, that is, at the same time truly God. The same one through whom God created the world could alone be the Mediator of re-creation, Bonaventure, _Brevil._ IV c. 1.
