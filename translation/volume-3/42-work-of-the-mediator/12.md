## 12.

The work that Christ accomplished for his own consisted in general in his absolute and perfect obedience to God’s will, Matt. 3:15; 20:28; 26:42; John 4:34; 5:30; 6:38; Rom. 5:19; Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:7, 8; Heb. 5:8; 10:5–10, and so forth. This rich thought has often not received its due in theology. Christ’s suffering has frequently been detached from the act of obedience expressed in it and thus made an object of pious meditation. In the Christian church, the martyrs, the monks, the mendicants, and the flagellants were successively regarded as the true disciples of Jesus. Asceticism and self-torment in every form were the preeminent Christian virtues. The imitation of Christ consisted in copying and imitating deeds and states from his life, particularly from his suffering. Christ was the great sufferer, the exalted martyr, whose suffering had to be the object of contemplation and imitation; Zöckler, _Askese und Mönchthum_ I, 1897, p. 145 f.; Moll, _Joh. Brugman_ II 1–97. In Anselm, since Christ was already obligated for himself to obey God’s law, Christ’s satisfaction consisted solely in his suffering and death, which he added to his life as an _opus supererogationis_ and offered to the Father as a voluntary gift. Roman Catholic theology does not speak unanimously and unambiguously about Christ’s active obedience. Trent does mention Christ’s _sanctissima passio_, VI, ch. 7, but its theologians reject active obedience entirely or at least understand it in such a way that Christ did not fulfill God’s law in our place; Bellarmine, _De justif._ I 2; _De Christo_ V 9; Bossuet, _Gesch. der veranderingen v. d. Prot. kerken_, trans. Berends, 1829, II 340; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 321, 341. Among Protestants too, Mystics, Anabaptists, Moravians, and others hold conceptions of Christ’s suffering as _Etwas Sachliches_ that fail to do justice to his active obedience. Even Parsimonius and Piscator denied it, because Christ was already obligated to this obedience for himself, and this obedience was therefore indeed a _necessarium requisitum personale_ for our good, _nostro bono_, but no component of his satisfaction accomplished in our place, _nostro loco_; because Holy Scripture always connects our whole salvation, both the forgiveness of sins and eternal life, with Christ’s suffering and death alone; and because believers, even though they possess forgiveness and eternal life, nevertheless remain obligated to keep the law. The Lutherans saw Nestorianism in this and said that the person of Christ according to both natures was Lord of the law and therefore, even as man, was not of himself subject to the law for his own person; Gerhard XVI 57, 59; Quenstedt III 284; Schneckenburger, _Zur kirchl. Christol._ 58–73; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 274. But the Reformed could not speak in this way, since Christ as a true human being was certainly obligated to keep the law and to love God above all and his neighbor as himself. Nevertheless, they rightly rejected Piscator’s opinion. For first, Holy Scripture views Christ’s entire life and work as one whole and never separates an _obedientia vitae_ that he accomplished for himself from an _obedientia mortis_ that he accomplished for us. It is one work that the Father assigned to him and that he brings to completion in his death, John 4:34; 17:4; 19:30. His service is completed in giving his soul as a ransom for many, Matt. 20:28. Even Paul, who places all emphasis upon the cross of Christ, sees in his death not the whole of his obedience but its culmination. He was born under the law, Gal. 4:4; in the likeness of sinful flesh, Rom. 8:3; did not live to please himself, _οὐχ ἑαυτῳ ἠρεσεν_, Rom. 15:3; already emptied himself at his incarnation and assumed the form of a servant; continually humbled himself and became obedient unto death, _μεχρι θανατου_, Phil. 2:7, 8; 2 Cor. 8:9. And thus it is the one _δικαιωμα_ and one _ὑπακοη_ that grants the _δικαιωσις ζωης_ to many, Rom. 5:18, 19. It is therefore wholly contrary to Scripture to restrict Christ’s work of satisfaction to his suffering, or even, as Jac. Alting did, _Op._ V 393–395; cf., however, pp. 478–480, to his suffering during the three hours of darkness on the cross. The appeal to passages such as Zech. 3:9; John 19:30; Rom. 6:10; Heb. 7:27; 1 Pet. 3:18, where it is said that Christ suffered once, upon the tree, and cried out, “It is finished,” proves nothing against this, because Christ’s entire preceding life is taken up, summarized, and completed in his suffering and death; cf. Moor III 985 sq.; Mastricht V 11, 34; 18, 29; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ II 6; _Misc. S._ II 771; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ IX 46; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ X 467–471. Rather, Christ’s entire life and work, from his conception to his death, are vicarious in character. The assumption of human nature in itself and as such does not yet bear this character, because all mediatorial works presuppose the two natures; but his holy conception and birth and all his holy works are included in the one work of Christ; Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 18, 2; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 36 and 60; Belgic Confession, art. 22; cf. _Acta Syn. Dordr._, sess. 172, 173; Voetius, _Disp._ II 282; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ I 91, 122 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 287. Second, it is certainly true that Christ as man was subject to the law for himself; only, his incarnation and his being human took place not for himself but for us. Christ never was and may never be regarded as a _persona privata_, an individual alongside and on the same level as others. From the beginning he was a _persona publica_, the second Adam, _sponsor et caput electorum_. Just as Adam sinned for himself and thereby loaded guilt and death upon all whose representative he was, so through his righteousness and obedience Christ acquired forgiveness and life for all his own. More than this, Christ as man was certainly subject to God’s law as the rule of life; even believers are never released from the law in that sense. But Christ placed himself in an entirely different relation to the law as well, namely, to it as the law of the covenant of works. Adam was not only obligated to keep the law, but in the covenant of works the law was presented to him under another _forma_ as well, namely, as the way to eternal life, which he did not yet possess. Christ, however, had eternal and blessed life through his union with the divine nature. He voluntarily relinquished it. He subjected himself to the law of the covenant of works as the way to eternal life for himself and his own. The obedience that Christ rendered to the law was therefore entirely voluntary. Not his death alone, as Anselm said, but his whole life was a self-denial, one self-offering, presented by him as Head in the place of his own. Third, the fact that believers still remain obligated to keep the law as the rule of life proves nothing against this. If this proved anything, every element of satisfaction would disappear from Jesus’ life and suffering. For believers still have to bear all kinds of suffering as a consequence of sins; they are still tempted by Satan and enticed by the world; they still sin repeatedly and must still die, and so forth; and thus Christ would have delivered them from nothing, neither from sin nor from its consequences. Jac. Alting reasoned in this way and therefore said that Christ had delivered his own only from eternal death through his hellish agonies during the three hours of darkness on the cross, but not from suffering, bodily death, and so forth. But this view of the work of redemption is undoubtedly wrong. Redemption is complete, a redemption of the whole human being in soul and body, from all sins and consequences of sin. And it has been accomplished in and through Christ’s life and death. But just as he distinguishes his kingdom into an invisible, spiritual kingdom and a visible kingdom to be established on earth one day; just as he himself once came to suffer and will soon return to judge the living and the dead; so the redemption brought about by Christ is gradually worked out and applied, first spiritually and afterward bodily. Now in this dispensation believers are spiritually delivered from every guilt and power of sin and its consequences, from the world, Satan, and death. They have the forgiveness of sins and eternal life. Law, Satan, and death can no longer rob them of these. And one day they will also be delivered outwardly and bodily from every sin and power of sin. The whole re-creation, as it will be completed in the new heaven and the new earth, is the fruit of Christ’s work. Just as it is one-sided, with Alting, to see the fruit of Christ’s work only in deliverance from eternal death, so it is equally one-sided, with Ritschl, to restrict that fruit to the Christian’s spiritual, ethical dominion over sin and the world in the _Diesseits_. The whole person of Christ, with both his active and passive obedience, is the guarantee of the whole redemption.
