## 20.

In this important controversy over the value of Christ’s sacrifice, there is complete agreement on the one hand that not all but only some human beings actually partake of Christ’s benefits, and on the other hand that Christ’s sacrifice in itself would be completely sufficient to cause not only some but all human beings to share in the forgiveness of sins and eternal life. Thus in practice the universalists all embrace the particularity of grace; and without exception all particularists confess the universality of Christ’s sacrifice with respect to its intrinsic value. Even those who have objections to the words that Christ died _sufficienter_ for all and _efficaciter_ for the elect nevertheless fully acknowledge that the _materia meriti_ of Christ is completely sufficient for the atonement of the sins of all human beings. What they intend is only this: that the _forma meriti_—that is, Christ’s merit—considered not in itself but _in ordine ad reprobos_, can be called not only not _efficax_ but also not _sufficiens_, Voetius, _Disp._ II 254. Thus the difference concerns only the question whether it was the will and _intention_ of God that Christ should bring his sacrifice for the sins of all human beings without exception, or only for the sins of those given him by the Father. Posed in this way, the question seems scarcely capable of two answers. For first, Scripture continually connects Christ’s sacrifice only with the church, whether it is designated by many, Isa. 53:11, 12; Matt. 20:28; 26:28; Rom. 5:15, 19; Heb. 2:10; 9:28; by his people, Matt. 1:21; Titus 2:14; Heb. 2:17; 7:27; 13:12; by his sheep, John 10:11, 15, 25 f.; Heb. 13:20; by his brothers, Heb. 2:11; by children of God, John 11:52; Heb. 2:13–15; by those given him by the Father, John 6:37, 39, 44; 17:2, 9, 24; by his church, Acts 20:28; Eph. 5:25; by his body, Eph. 5:23; or also by us as believers, Rom. 5:9; 8:32; 1 Cor. 5:7; Eph. 1:7; 2:18; 3:12; Col. 1:14; Titus 2:14; Heb. 4:14–16; 7:26; 8:1; 9:14; 10:15; 1 John 4:10; 1 Pet. 3:18; 2 Pet. 1:3; Rev. 1:5, 6; 5:9, 10, and so forth. Ritschl rightly called attention to this again. For although he came to it through entirely different considerations, the fact itself stands firm: in Scripture not all human beings, head for head, but the church is _das Correlat aller an den Opfertod Christi geknüpften Wirkungen_, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II² 216. Ritschl’s considerations, however, are different from those of the Reformed. The latter said: not all but the church of the elect. Ritschl says: not the individual but the church, and thus attempts to remove from Scripture the _unio mystica_, the fellowship of believers individually with Christ. But in the matter itself there is nevertheless agreement. Over against this clear, continuous teaching of Scripture, the few texts to which universalists appeal carry little weight. The expression _all_ in Isa. 53:6; Rom. 5:18; 1 Cor. 15:22; 2 Cor. 5:15; Heb. 2:9; cf. 10, proves nothing, or it proves much more than the universalists claim and would benefit Origen’s doctrine of the restoration of all things. The universalists are therefore themselves compelled to limit the word _all_ in these passages. Texts such as Ezek. 18:23; 33:11; John 1:29; 3:16; 4:42; 12:32; 1 Tim. 2:4, 6; Titus 2:11; Heb. 2:9; 2 Pet. 3:9; 1 John 2:2; 4:14, where God’s will or Christ’s sacrifice is connected with the salvation of all or of the world, carry more weight. But none of these texts conflicts with the statements named above, which limit Christ’s benefits to the church. For the New Testament is an entirely different dispensation than the Old Covenant. The gospel is not limited to one people but must be preached to all creatures, Matt. 28:19. There is no partiality with God; there is no longer a distinction between Gentile and Jew, Acts 10:34, 35; Rom. 3:29; 10:11–13. Indeed, even when Isa. 53:11, 12; Matt. 20:28; 26:28; Rom. 5:15, 19; Heb. 2:10; 9:28 speak of many for whom Christ died, the underlying opposition is not the one frequently inserted later, that not all but only many will be saved. Rather, the thought from which this speaking of many arises is entirely different: Christ did not die for a few but for many, for very many. He gives his life for many; he sheds his blood for many; he will justify many. It is not a few but many who through the obedience of one are constituted righteous. Scripture is not afraid that _too_ many will be saved. And therefore, from this same consideration, it says that God has no pleasure in the death of the wicked; that he wills all to come to repentance and be saved; that Christ is an atonement and gave his life for the world; and that the gospel must be preached to all creatures. If the universalists infer from this that satisfaction is entirely universal, then they come into conflict both with Scripture and with reality. For both teach in rivalry with each other that not all but only many become acquainted with the gospel and come to true repentance. All those passages therefore speak not of the _voluntas beneplaciti_, which is unknown to us and cannot and may not be the rule of our conduct; nor of a _voluntas antecedens_ that precedes our decision of will and directs itself according to it; but of the _voluntas signi_, which tells us how we are to conduct ourselves in the New Covenant. It gives us the right and lays upon us the duty to bring the gospel to all human beings without exception. We need no other ground for the universal offer of grace than this clearly revealed will of God. We no more need to know beforehand for whom Christ specifically died than who were elected by God to eternal life. The calling indeed rests upon a particular basis, for it belongs to and proceeds from the covenant. Yet in agreement with God’s revealed will and with the intrinsically all-sufficient value of Christ’s sacrifice, it is also directed to those outside the covenant so that they too might be taken up into that covenant and in their faith itself receive the proof of their election; cf. vol. II, pp. 216–222. In the second place, Scripture entails that Christ’s sacrifice and intercession, and likewise the acquisition and application of salvation, are indissolubly connected. The sacrifice is the ground of Christ’s intercession; the latter therefore extends as far as the former. Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ III 19, 11, accordingly acknowledges, _intercessionem non esse actum reipsa ab oblatione, quatenus in coelo peragitur, distinctum_. Thus if the intercession is particular, as it is, John 17:9, 24; Rom. 8:34; Heb. 7:25; 1 John 2:1, 2, then the sacrifice is so too. Limborch IV 4, 7, indeed appeals against this to Luke 23:34, but here Jesus does not pray for the salvation of his enemies, only for the non-imputation of that dreadful crime of which they made themselves guilty in their ignorance when they crucified the Messiah. Likewise there is an indissoluble connection between the acquisition and application of salvation. All the benefits of the covenant of grace are connected, Rom. 8:28–34, and find their ground in Christ’s death, Rom. 5:8–11. Atonement in Christ brings salvation and blessedness with it. For Christ is the head and believers are his body, which receives its growth from him, Eph. 4:16; Col. 2:19. He is the cornerstone and they are the building, Eph. 2:20, 21. He is the firstborn and they are his brothers, Rom. 8:29. Believers therefore objectively died, were crucified, buried, raised, and seated in heaven with and in him. That is, the church is no accidental, arbitrary aggregate of individuals that could just as well be smaller as larger, but forms with Christ an organic whole that lies enclosed in him as the second Adam, just as the whole of humanity proceeds from the first Adam; cf. Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ I, p. 501. The application must therefore extend as far as the acquisition of salvation. It is included in this and is its necessary effect. After all, this also lies in the nature of the matter. If Jesus is truly Savior, then he must actually save his people, not in possibility but in reality. The universalists, however, are compelled to conceive salvation itself entirely differently and to detract from the name of Jesus. In logic the rule applies: _quo major extensio, minor comprehensio_. And this rule applies in many other domains; it applies here too. Under the appearance of honoring Christ’s work, it is weakened and limited by the doctrine of universal satisfaction. For if Christ satisfied for all, the acquisition does not necessarily include the application of salvation. The latter is then something that may perhaps be accidentally added but is not naturally given with it and does not by nature flow from it. The application of salvation is then not and cannot be Christ’s work, but ultimately always depends upon the human will. It must supplement Christ’s work, apply it, cause it to pass into reality. That is, for Christ there remains only the acquisition, not of the reality but merely of the possibility of salvation; not of actual _reconciliatio_ but of potential _reconciliabilitas_. Christ acquired for God only the possibility of entering into a covenant of grace with us, of granting us the forgiveness of sins and eternal life—if, namely, we believe. The principal part of the work of salvation, that which actually causes us to become saved, remains for us to do. Christ did not establish the covenant of grace itself in his blood. He did not cause the sins of his people to be forgiven, but only declared that from God’s side there is no objection to entering into a covenant with us and forgiving us our sins if and after we for our part believe. Properly speaking, Christ therefore acquired nothing for us, but only for God the possibility of forgiving us if we fulfill the commandments of the gospel. The universalists therefore all come to diminish the value and power of Christ’s work. What they gain in quantity—and then only apparently—they lose in quality. Rome teaches that sins committed before baptism—that is, as a rule original sin—are forgiven in baptism. But after baptism _concupiscentia_ remains, which is indeed itself no sin but becomes an occasion for sinning. Sins then committed are indeed forgiven in the sacrament of penance as regards guilt and eternal punishment; but temporal punishment must be borne by the human being himself here or in purgatory, Trent VI 30; XIV c. 8, 9; can. 12–14, and so forth. The Remonstrants said that in Christ’s death God so reconciled all sinners to himself, _ut per et propter hoc ipsum λυτρον ac sacrificium in gratiam cum iis redire et ostium salutis aeternae viamque immortalitatis pandere ipsis voluerit_, _Conf._ VIII 9. The Quakers make Christ’s work consist in his offering atonement to us and making God inclined to forgiveness, Barclay, _Verantwoording_, 1757, p. 153. And all who teach a universal satisfaction must come to the same conclusion. The center of gravity is transferred from Christ to the Christian. Faith is the true reconciliation with God; cf. Kübel, _Unterschied_ 135 f. The Reformed, however, were of a different mind. _Satisfactio vicaria_ is no _fertige Grösse_ but includes in principle the whole re-creation within itself. Christ’s work is finished only when he delivers the kingdom to the Father. He did not open the possibility of being saved but continually saves on the ground of his sacrifice accomplished on the cross. He is Savior who not only died for our sins but therefore also was raised and ascended to heaven and now as the exalted Savior intercedes for his church. He sanctified himself so that his own too might be sanctified in truth, John 17:19. He gave himself for the church so that he might sanctify it and present it gloriously to himself, Eph. 5:25–27. Both are from one, namely God, Heb. 2:11, and are as it were one Christ, 1 Cor. 12:12. In and with Christ, God grants believers everything they need, Rom. 8:32 f.; Eph. 1:3, 4; 2 Pet. 1:3. Election in Christ brings all blessings in Christ with it: adoption as children, redemption through his blood, Eph. 1:3 f.; the gift of the Holy Spirit, 1 Cor. 12:3; faith, Phil. 1:29; repentance, Acts 5:31; 11:18; 2 Tim. 2:25; a new heart and a new spirit, Jer. 31:33, 34; Ezek. 36:25–27; Heb. 8:8–12; 10:16. Cf. Canons of Dort II 8 f.; the Leiden professors in _Censura Conf. Rem._ VIII 9; Trigland, _Antapologia_ c. 16; Mastricht V 18, 42; Voetius, _Disp._ II 9, 269; V 270; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ II 7; _Misc. Sacra_ II 781; Vitringa VI 126; Moor III 1086 sq.; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ II 26. In the third place, it must still be added that universalism leads to all kinds of false propositions. It introduces separation among the three persons of the divine being, for the Father wills the salvation of all, the Son satisfies for all, but the Holy Spirit limits the gift of faith and salvation to a few. It introduces conflict between God’s intention, which desires the preservation of all, and God’s will or power, which in fact does not will or cannot cause all to partake of salvation. It lets the person and work of Christ precede election and covenant, so that Christ comes to stand entirely outside them and cannot satisfy vicariously because there is no fellowship between him and us. It detracts from God’s righteousness, which causes forgiveness and life to be acquired for all and nevertheless does not distribute them. It establishes free will, which has the power to believe, can either nullify Christ’s work or not, and holds in its hands the decision—indeed, the whole result of world history. It leads to the doctrine, as the Quakers rightly observed, Barclay, _Verantw. van de Ware Christ. Godg._, p. 92, that if Christ died for all, then all must also here or hereafter be placed in a position to accept or reject him. For it would be entirely unjust to condemn and punish those whose sins were all atoned for solely because they were without opportunity to accept Christ by faith. And it comes to the proposition, in clear contradiction with the whole of Holy Scripture, that the only sin for which anyone can be lost and punished is unbelief. All other sins have after all been atoned for, even those of the man of sin, the Antichrist.
