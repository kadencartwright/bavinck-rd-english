## 21.

Although, therefore, the _satisfactio vicaria_ as the acquisition of complete salvation cannot be extended to all human beings head for head, it is not thereby asserted that it has not the least significance for those who are lost. Between the church and the world there is not merely separation and opposition. It is not the case that Christ acquired everything for the former and nothing for the latter. In rejecting universalism, it must not be forgotten that Christ’s merit has its limits with respect to the former and its value and significance with respect to the latter. In the first place, after all, it should be carefully considered that Christ as such is indeed the Recreator but not the Creator of all things. Just as the Son follows the Father, so re-creation presupposes creation, grace presupposes nature, and rebirth presupposes birth. Therefore, strictly speaking, the merits of Christ do not include the elect being born and living and receiving food, shelter, clothing, and all kinds of natural benefits. It can indeed be said that God would not have allowed the world and humanity to continue existing after the fall if he had had no other, higher purpose for them. There is indeed _gratia communis_ for the sake of _gratia specialis_; and along with salvation in Christ God also grants the elect many other natural blessings, Matt. 6:33; Rom. 8:28, 32; 1 Tim. 4:8; 2 Pet. 1:3. Nevertheless, it is wrong, with Moravians and Pietists, to erase the boundaries between nature and grace, creation and redemption, and to put Christ in the Father’s place upon the throne of the universe. Even election and the covenant of grace, both of which presuppose their objects and participants, were not acquired by Christ but precede his merits. Through his creation the Father prepares the work of re-creation and leads toward it. With his labor the Son goes deep, as far as sin reaches, back into the work of creation. Yet the two works remain distinct and must not be mingled; Voetius, _Disp._ II 271–273. In the second place, Christ did not acquire the same thing for each of his own. Before they come to faith, there is a difference among believers in sex, age, station, rank, character, gift, and so forth, and also a difference in the measure and degree of wickedness and corruption. And when they come to faith, there is a difference in the grace granted to them. Grace is given to each according to the measure of Christ’s gift, Rom. 12:3; 1 Cor. 12:11; Eph. 3:7; 4:7. The natural difference among human beings is indeed cleansed by grace but not erased. It is even increased by a difference of spiritual gifts, for the body of Christ consists of many members so that it may be one organism, a creation and work of art of God. Third, the church is not of the world but is nevertheless in the world. It lives and moves in the midst of that world and is connected with it in all kinds of ways. Believers are brought in from the human race, and conversely there is much chaff among the wheat; there are branches on the vine that bear no fruit and are cut off. Therefore, when Christ took the place of his own, he had to assume the flesh and blood in which all human beings share. Through his incarnation he honored the whole human race; according to the flesh he is the brother of all human beings. Even his work has value for all, including those who never believe in him. For although Christ did not acquire natural life in the proper sense through his suffering and death, the human race was nevertheless spared because Christ would come to save it. Christ is not the head of all human beings, not the prophet, priest, and king of all, for he is Head of the church and was anointed king over Zion. But all human beings do owe much to Christ. The light shines in the darkness and enlightens every human being coming into the world. The world was made through him and continues to exist through him, although it did not know him. Even as Christ he grants many benefits to unbelievers: calling through the gospel, admonition to repentance, historical faith, an honorable life, all kinds of gifts and powers, offices and ministries in the midst of the church, such as even the apostolic office to a Judas. _Sans Jésus-Christ le monde ne subsisterait pas, car il faudrait, ou qu’il fut détruit ou qu’il fut comme un enfer_ (Pascal). Even while hanging on the cross, he still prays for forgiveness for that dreadful sin of which the Jews are making themselves guilty at that very moment. Cf. Voetius, _Disp._ II 275, 276. Fourth, the work extends to nonrational creatures. One cannot say with Origen that Christ suffered something for them and merited something for them. But if Christ was made sin and bore the sin of the world, then he also abolished sin with all its consequences. The liberation of the creature from bondage to corruption, the glorification of creation, and the renewal of heaven and earth are a fruit of the cross of Christ, Rom. 8:19 f.; Voetius, _Disp._ II 264, 265. Fifth, the angels in heaven also derive use and benefit from the work of Christ. There is insufficient ground for the claim that Christ acquired perseverance and glory for them, although many have taught this with an appeal to Job 4:18; 15:15; Eph. 1:10; Col. 1:20; 1 Tim. 5:21; Heb. 12:22, 23; Augustine, _De cons. evang._ 35; Cyril, _De ador._ 9; Gregory, Bernard, Diez, Valentia, Suarez, and also Calvin on Eph. 1:10 and Col. 1:20; Polanus, _Synt. Theol._ VI 27; Zanchius, _Op._ III 159–164; Bucanus, _Inst. theol._ VI, qu. 30; Davenant on Col. 1:20; Walaeus, _Synopsis pur. theol._ XII 33 and _Loci Comm._, _Op._ I 195, and so forth. After all, angels do not need Christ for themselves as Reconciler or Savior. They are essentially distinguished from human beings, who alone were made in God’s image. If Christ had to acquire _gratia_ and _gloria_ for them, this would lead to the thought that the Son of God would nevertheless have had to assume human nature—or, better still, the nature of angels—even if the human being had not fallen; cf. volume II 444, and further Lombard, Thomas, Bonaventure, and Scotus on _Sent._ II, dist. 5; III, dist. 13; Thomas, _S. Th._ I, qu. 62; III, qu. 8, art. 4; Petavius, _De incarn._ XII 10; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ I, p. 305; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 476; Gerhard, _Loc._ XXXI § 42; Gomarus on Col. 1:20; Voetius, _Disp._ II 263; Alting, _Theol. probl. nova_ XII 24; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV, qu. 3; Moor II 353; Vitringa III 26; VI 178. Yet a simple denial that Christ merited anything for the angels does not do justice to Eph. 1:10 and Col. 1:20. For it is stated clearly that God has reconciled all things, _τα παντα_—that is, not human beings or angels alone but all created things in general, the whole creation, the world, the universe, further defined by _εἰτε τα ἐπι της γης εἰτε τα ἐν τοις οὐρανοις_—through Christ, not to one another but to himself, _εἰς αὐτον_, and that in him he again gathers them together for himself and brings them to unity. The doctrine of the restoration of all things finds no support in these texts. It is rejected by the whole of Scripture and has found defenders in the Christian church only now and then. If this is thus excluded, these two texts can be understood only to mean that, in Paul’s conception, the devils and the ungodly will one day be consigned to hell and that then, in the new heaven and the new earth with their inhabitants, the whole creation will be restored. Now this creation, conceived in an organic sense, had as a whole been set in hostility toward God by sin and had been internally shattered and devastated. This does not imply that the good angels personally and individually needed reconciliation, nor that Christ had to suffer and die for the nonrational creatures. But it does presuppose that sin changed and disturbed the relation of all creatures both to God and to one another. And that is indeed the case. Sin made the human world an object of God’s wrath and divided and devastated it within itself. The relation of the angels to God was changed, not only insofar as many fell away but also because the good angels formed only one part of the whole world of spirits that had served God. Augustine, _Enchir._ 61, 62, and others were of the opinion that this breach made in the angelic world was healed by the elect from humanity and that the significance of Christ’s reconciliation for humanity consisted in this. This opinion is not acceptable. Human beings differ generically from angels, and an equation of the number of elect human beings with that of the fallen angels lacks all ground in Scripture. Nevertheless, it is true that the fall of so many angels must have disturbed the entire organism of the angelic world, just as an army is thrown completely into disarray and rendered unfit for battle when many officers and soldiers leave the ranks and defect to the enemy. Thus the angelic world, as God’s army appointed for his service, was also shattered. It lost its head, its organization. And it now receives these back in the Son, and indeed in the Son not only according to his divine nature but also according to his human nature. For not only the relation to God but also that to the human world had been disturbed by sin. And now it is Christ who, as Lord of the angels and Head of the church, places both angels and human beings in the right relation to God and likewise to each other. Through his cross he restores the organism of creation in heaven and on earth and also joins these two together again. The inanimate and nonrational in creation—that is, heaven and earth themselves—are not excluded from this. That is impossible for this reason alone: the relation of the angels to heaven and that of human beings to earth are not mechanical but organic. Heaven and earth themselves sank below their original state with the fall of angels and human beings. The whole creation, _πασα ἡ κτισις_, groans together and travails together in birth pangs. _Die gesammte Creatur führt gleichsam eine grosze Seufzersymphonie aus_ (Philippi). All the members of that creation groan and suffer pain jointly, in connection with one another, Rom. 8:22. Just as in the old covenant the tabernacle and all the implements for the service were sprinkled with blood, Ex. 24:3–8; Heb. 9:21, so too through his cross Christ reconciled all things and acquired a new heaven and a new earth. The whole creation, as it will one day stand perfect, without spot or wrinkle, before God’s face, is the work of Christ, the Lord of lords and the King of kings, Heb. 12:22–28.
