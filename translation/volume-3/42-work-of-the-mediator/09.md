## 9.

This Christ, in his whole person and his entire work, is a revelation of God’s love. The Gnostics, and especially Marcion, made a sharp opposition between the God of wrath, vengeance, and righteousness who revealed himself in the Old Testament, and the God of love and grace who made himself known in Christ in the New Testament, vol. II, p. 200. But such an opposition is unknown to Scripture. YHWH Elohim in the Old Testament is indeed righteous, holy, jealous for his honor, and wrathful against sin, but he is also gracious, compassionate, ready to forgive, and great in lovingkindness, Ex. 20:5, 6; 34:6, 7; Deut. 4:31; Ps. 86:15, and so forth. In the New Testament God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, is the God of all grace and compassion, Luke 6:36; 2 Cor. 1:3; 1 Pet. 5:10. There is no opposition between the Father and Christ. Just as loving, compassionate, and ready to forgive as Christ is, so is the Father. It is the Father’s words that Christ speaks, his works that he performs. The Father is himself the Savior, σωτηρ, Luke 1:47; 1 Tim. 1:1; Titus 3:4, 5, the one who in Christ reconciled the world to himself and did not impute its sins to it, 2 Cor. 5:18, 19. Christ therefore did not first move the Father to love and grace through his work, but the Father’s love precedes and comes to revelation in Christ. Christ is a gift of God’s love, John 3:16; Rom. 5:8; 8:32; 1 John 4:9, 10. Paul indeed says in Rom. 3:25, 26 that God openly, before the eyes of all, set forth Christ Jesus as an atonement through faith in his blood, εἰς ἐνδειξιν της δικαιοσυνης αὐτου. And here one must undoubtedly think of righteousness as a virtue of God, which, because the sins previously committed by Jews and Gentiles under God’s forbearance were passed over without atonement, did not seem to come to revelation and therefore seemed not to exist. It was therefore necessary for God in the present time, in the fullness of time, to set forth Christ as an atonement through his blood, so that he himself might be shown to be righteous and could also justify the one who is of faith in Jesus. But here too God’s righteousness is not conceived as in conflict with his grace and love. For God’s righteousness manifested itself not in his punishing sinners for their transgressions, but in his setting forth in Christ an atonement through his blood, so that now in the forgiveness of sins God nevertheless showed himself righteous and could at the same time justify the believer. For in Scripture God’s righteousness consists first of all in his judging justly, not holding the guilty innocent and the innocent guilty; and then further in his helping and rescuing the poor, the miserable, those who are indeed personally guilty but materially have right on their side, and acknowledging them in their right. In this latter sense righteousness then becomes related to mercy, faithfulness, and truth, but is nevertheless always distinguished from them in that it looks especially to the anomaly existing between the right someone has and the condition in which he lives, vol. II, pp. 194 f. Here in Paul too, God’s righteousness is not identical with his goodness, mercy, faithfulness, and truth, but it is much less still opposed to them. For it is precisely that virtue of God that gave Christ as an atonement so that God could forgive sins by grace. Thus there is no question of a conflict between God’s righteousness and love as evangelical hymn 125:5 represents it. In our sinful condition this may indeed appear so to us, but in God all the virtues are one and in full harmony. On the one hand, therefore, the conception must be rejected that Christ is merely a revelation of God’s punitive righteousness. The God of revelation, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, is no pagan god whose envy and hatred against human beings must be averted through all kinds of sacrifices. Sacrifice in Scripture, including the Old Testament, bears an entirely different character; it presupposes the covenant of grace. Then too there would arise a Gnostic opposition between Father and Son that would elevate the Son as the God of grace far above the Father as the God of vengeance and tear apart Old and New Testaments, creation and re-creation, nature and grace. But on the other hand, Christ is not to be conceived merely as a display of God’s love either—at least not of a love as we frequently conceive it and which differs entirely from the love of God in Scripture. For in that case not only would Christ’s suffering and death, which Scripture undoubtedly presents as a punishment for our sin, remain wholly unexplained, but Paul’s word would also be directly contradicted that God gave Christ as an atonement in order that he himself might be shown righteous and might justify the one who is of faith in Jesus. Scripture rather teaches us that in Christ and his sacrifice all God’s virtues came to glorious revelation. Grace takes the lead, but all the others follow it. It realizes itself in the way of right and righteousness, Isa. 1:27; 5:16. _Hinter dem Zorne ist als letztes Agens die Liebe geborgen, wie hinter Gewitterwolken die Sonne_ (Delitzsch). Grundbegriff der δικ. θεου, erörtert auf Grund von Röm. 3:21–26_, Leipzig, 1888.
