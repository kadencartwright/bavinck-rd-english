## 2.

In this sense sanctification, just as much as justification, is a gift and work of God, attributed in turn to the Father, John 17:17; 1 Thess. 5:23; Heb. 13:20, 21; to the Son as πνευμα ζωοποιουν, 1 Cor. 15:45; Eph. 5:26; Titus 2:14; and especially also, as was shown above, to the Holy Spirit, Titus 3:5; 1 Pet. 1:2. Believers are passive in this; they are sanctified, John 17:19; 1 Cor. 6:11; they have died and been raised with Christ, Rom. 6:4 f.; they are ἡγιασμενοι ἐν Χριστῳ Ιησου, 1 Cor. 1:2; God’s ποιημα, Eph. 2:10; κτισις, 2 Cor. 5:17; Gal. 6:15; ἐργον, Rom. 14:20; τα δε παντα ἐκ του θεου, 2 Cor. 5:18. This sanctification consists first of all in believers being separated from the world and coming to stand in a special relation to God. In the Old Testament holiness denoted that relation of God to his people and of the people to God which was described and regulated in the various laws; volume II 184–191. In the New Testament too, the concept of holiness has retained this relational meaning. There is mention of a holy city, Matt. 4:5; a holy place, 24:15; a holy covenant, Luke 1:72; holy ground, Acts 7:33; Holy Scripture, Rom. 1:2; a holy mountain, 2 Pet. 1:18; holy prophets, Luke 1:70; and a holy sacrifice, Rom. 12:1. Of Christ it is said, although he was without sin, that he sanctified himself, that is, in his death offered himself to God as a holy sacrifice for his own, John 17:19. And so believers are called by the standing epithet ἁγιοι, because through the calling, cf. Rom. 1:7; 1 Cor. 1:2, κλητοι ἁγιοι, they stand in a special relation to God and, in place of ancient Israel, are γενος ἐκλεκτον, βασιλειον ἱερατευμα, ἐθνος ἁγιον, λαος εἰς περιποιησιν, 1 Pet. 2:9. But this relation is not merely external. It was not so even under the Old Testament, for by virtue of that holiness God had bound himself to give Israel his covenant and law, to save it or chastise it, and Israel was obligated to walk in God’s statutes; volume II 189. Now in the New Testament the law has been fulfilled in Christ, and it therefore no longer regulates the relation of holiness that exists between God and his people. Christ has taken the place of the law; in and through him God regulates the relation between himself and his people. Believers are ἡγιασμενοι ἐν Χριστῳ Ιησου, 1 Cor. 1:2; and Christ sanctifies his people through the Holy Spirit, ἐν πνευματι, 1 Cor. 6:11, who as such is now called πνευμα ἁγιον and is the principle of sanctification. This sanctification by no means consists only in this, as many now represent it—for example, Paul Wernle, _Der Christ und die Sünde bei Paulus_, 1897, S. 31, 39, 62—that Christians are separated from the world and consecrated to God in an outward, cultic sense; rather, it has a deep ethical significance. For the Holy Spirit regenerates, cleanses, renews, John 3:3; 1 Cor. 6:11; Titus 3:5. With the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, a καινοτης ζωης begins for believers, Rom. 6:4, which stands in contrast to their former walk in all kinds of sins and unrighteousness, 1 Cor. 6:10; Eph. 2:1. They are now new human beings, 2 Cor. 5:17; Eph. 2:10, 15; 4:24; Gal. 6:15; Col. 3:10, who live unto God and present their members as instruments of righteousness for sanctification, Rom. 6. The relation to God in Christ through the Holy Spirit entails that believers are freed from all guilt and every stain of sin. And therefore sanctification in the New Testament consists fully in believers being conformed to the image of the Son, Rom. 8:29; Gal. 4:19. To that extent sanctification coincides with glorification. Glorification does not begin only after this life but commences immediately with the calling; those whom he called, he justified, and those whom he justified, he glorified at that same moment, Rom. 8:30. And this glorification continues in this life, 2 Cor. 3:18, until it is completed at Christ’s return, 1 Cor. 15:49, 51 f.; Col. 3:4; Phil. 3:21; cf. volume II 191.

From this glorious description of the Christian state in the New Testament, many, both earlier and later, have inferred that Scripture teaches the perfectibility of believers already in this life: the Pelagians, Roman Catholics, Socinians, Remonstrants, Anabaptists, Schwenckfeld, Weigel, Böhme, Poiret, Labadie, Quietists, Pietists, Quakers, Moravians, Wesleyan Methodists, and so forth; cf. M. Vitringa III 385–414; Moor IV 805 sq.; and in modern times especially Moody, Sankey, and others; cf. Jellinghaus, _Das volle, gegenwärtige Heil durch Christum_, 1880, more moderately in later editions, 4th ed., Basel 1898. This assertion has also received support from another quarter. Ritschl was the first to observe that Paul himself, after he was converted, had no consciousness of imperfection and did not reflect at all on imperfection among believers either, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ II 365 f. Others developed this thought and even accused the apostle of an impractical idealism that, under the impression of Jesus’ imminent return, gave no thought to sins among believers; cf. Scholz, _Zur Lehre vom „armen Sünder”_, _Zeits. f. Th. u. K._ 1896, S. 463 f.; Clemen, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 109 f.; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 150; especially Wernle in his work cited above; cf. also Karl, _Beiträge zum Verständniss der soteriol. Erfahrungen u. Speculationen des Ap. Paulus_, Strasbourg 1897. This assertion contains a truth that must not be disputed but fully acknowledged. Scripture can scarcely find enough words to describe the glory of the people of God. In the Old Testament it calls Israel a priestly kingdom, chosen by God, the object of his love, his portion and inheritance, his son and servant, perfect in beauty through the glory of God, Ex. 19:5, 6; Deut. 7:7 f.; 32:6, 8, 9, 18; Isa. 41:8; Ezek. 16:14, and so forth. And believers in the New Testament are the salt of the earth, Matt. 5:13; the light of the world, v. 14; born of God and his children, John 1:12, 13; his chosen race and royal priesthood, 1 Pet. 2:9, 10; partakers of the divine nature, 2 Pet. 1:4; anointed with the Holy Spirit, 1 John 2:20; made kings and priests by Christ, Rev. 1:5; unable to sin, 1 John 3:9; 5:18 f., and so forth. Whoever rejects the doctrine of Scripture concerning sin and grace can see in all this only exaggeration; a radical change such as regeneration and sanctification is then neither necessary nor intelligible. But Scripture judges differently. It gives the church a high place, calls it by the most beautiful names, and ascribes to it a divine holiness and glory. In Roman Catholic dogmatics—cf., for example, Heinrich–Gutberlet, _Dogm. Theol._ VIII 550–643—the virtues and gifts the Holy Spirit grants to the church usually come into their own better than in Protestant dogmatics, which often considers the doctrine of Scripture concerning the Christian state exhausted in justification by faith and the expectation of heavenly blessedness. The glorification of the church, which begins with regeneration, is, however, just as much an object of faith as justification. That the church stands guiltless before God is just as difficult to believe as that, in principle, it has been sanctified and glorified by the Holy Spirit and conformed to the image of the Son. Both equally conflict with appearances; both belong to those things that are not seen and are certain only for faith. Scripture itself knows this too. For despite the glorious description it gives of the state of believers, it nevertheless regards them as sinners and does not conceal their transgressions or their confession of guilt: for example, in Abraham, Gen. 12:11; Isaac, 26:7; Jacob, 27:35; Moses, Num. 20:7–12; Ps. 106:33; David, Ps. 51, and so forth; Solomon, 1 Kings 8:46; Prov. 20:9; Isaiah, ch. 64:6; Daniel, ch. 9:4, and so forth. And Paul too knows that when he wishes to do good, evil lies close at hand. It is true that Paul is always clearly conscious of the great change that has taken place in him. With Christ he has been crucified to the world, and he himself no longer lives; Christ lives in him. He is free from the law, stands righteous before God, is assured of his adoption, boasts in the grace through which he can do all things, presents himself as an example, takes pride in his apostolic labor, and is conscious of his faithful fulfillment of office, for example, Rom. 15:17; 1 Cor. 4:3; 9:15; 15:30, 31; II 1:12; 6:3; 11:10; Phil. 2:16; 1 Thess. 2:10, 19. But nevertheless he confesses that he lives in the flesh, Gal. 2:20; that the flesh always desires against the Spirit, Gal. 5:17; that no good dwells in his flesh, Rom. 7:18; and that he has not yet obtained perfection, Phil. 3:12. Especially Rom. 7:7–26 is of great significance in this respect; cf. already above, p. 106. Although Reformation exegesis of this passage has largely been abandoned in modern times, those who acted thus did not know what they were doing. Wernle exaggerates but nevertheless says not entirely without reason: _in der That bedeutet das Zurückgehen auf die alte (griechische) Tradition von Röm. 7 einen viel schwereren Schlag für unsere Dogmatik, als in der Regel von ihr empfunden wird. Gewöhnlich gesteht man zu, dass Röm. 7 sich nicht auf den Wiedergeborenen bezieht, ohne zu merken, dass durch dies Zugeständniss der Paulinismus für uns unbrauchbar wird_, loc. cit., 108. Yet this is not the strongest ground for maintaining the Reformation interpretation of Rom. 7. That lies in the text itself. The present tense in which Paul speaks can be understood only of the present. _In Wahrheit macht man den Apostel zum Komödianten, wenn man ihm zutraut, er habe so, wie hier geschieht, nur in der Erinnerung an einen längst vergangenen Zustand reden können_, Clemen, loc. cit., 112, who also refers to Van Manen, _Paulus_ II, 1891, p. 71. Yet Clemen sees no way to bring Rom. 7 into agreement with Paul’s other statements and therefore says: _sie entstammt wohl einer besonders trüben Stimmung des Apostels, nicht seinem sonst vorherrschenden Bewusstsein!_ But to this sin and consciousness of sin in the saints of the Old and New Covenants must be added the fact that Scripture everywhere proceeds from the assumption that sin remains in believers to the end of their lives. They continually need both to pray for forgiveness, Matt. 6:12, 13, and to confess their sins, 1 John 1:9. All the admonitions and warnings in Scripture presuppose that believers still have only a small beginning of perfect obedience. They stumble daily in many things, James 3:2; if they say that they have no sin, they deceive themselves, 1 John 1:8. Paul too judges believers no differently. He places them very high, calls them the elect, the called, and the saints, notes with joy the Christian virtues that become manifest in them, and gladly and repeatedly gives them a glorious testimony. In this the apostle certainly does not exaggerate. The change must have been great if he could testify of Gentile believers that they formerly lived in all kinds of dreadful sins but were now washed, sanctified, and justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit of God, 1 Cor. 6:11. Nevertheless, he does not overlook the sins that still cling to believers. The Corinthians are still fleshly, 1 Cor. 3:1–4; the Galatians are disobedient, Gal. 5:7 f.; among the Colossians the good work has indeed begun but is not complete, Col. 1:6; indeed, their life is still hidden with Christ in God, 3:3. In Rom. 6 Paul does not say that believers are sinless, but argues that faith in Christ is incompatible with a life in sin; and therefore he specifically admonishes them to present their members to God as instruments of righteousness, Rom. 6:13. Without repeatedly returning to justification, which took place once for all from and through faith, he insists that believers manifest and prove this new state of theirs in a walk according to the Spirit. And in this connection it further deserves our attention that Scripture, although always presupposing the imperfection of the believer, nevertheless never weakens the demand of the law or adapts it to practice. Advocates of perfectibility can never maintain that demand; they lower the moral law and distinguish between mortal and venial sins or between committing sin and having sin. But Scripture does not do so and maintains the full, inviolable demand of the law: _be_ holy, for I am holy, 1 Pet. 1:16; _be_ perfect, as your Father in heaven, Matt. 5:48; James 1:4. Believers must follow Christ, who committed no sin, 1 Pet. 2:21 f.; Eph. 5:1, and on the day of Christ be ἀνεγκλητοι, εἰλικρινεις, ἀπροσκοποι, ἀμεμπτοι, ἀμωμοι, 1 Cor. 1:8; Phil. 1:10; 2:15; Col. 1:22; 1 Thess. 3:13; 5:23. They are therefore earnestly and unceasingly exhorted to a holy walk. Above, in connection with justification, we saw that the Christian is certain of his salvation only through faith, without and independently of works. But in sanctification, in life, salvation seems once again to be made dependent on whether believers do God’s will and walk blamelessly before him.

Sanctification, which at first was only passive, Phil. 1:5; 1 Thess. 5:23, at once takes on an active meaning, Rom. 12:1; 2 Cor. 7:1; 1 Thess. 4:3; Heb. 12:14. Just as in the preaching of the gospel faith is a gift of God and yet the human being is responsible for his attitude toward God’s calling—for example, Rom. 9:1–29 and 9:30–10:21—so here the possession of all the benefits of the covenant—forgiveness, adoption, life, salvation—is established as certain prior to every work, and yet good works are repeatedly urged with such earnestness as though these benefits still had to be obtained by those works. The kingdom of God is a gift granted by God according to his good pleasure, Matt. 11:26; 16:17; 22:14; 24:22; Luke 10:20; 12:32; 22:29, and yet it is a reward, a treasure in heaven, that must be sought with effort and acquired through labor in the service of God, Matt. 5:12, 20; 6:20; 19:21; 20:1 f., and so forth. Believers are branches on the vine who can do nothing without Christ and yet are admonished to abide in him, in his word, in his love, John 15. They are elect and yet must be diligent to make their calling and election sure, 2 Pet. 1:10. They are sanctified and perfected by the one sacrifice of Christ, Heb. 10:10, 14, and God works in them what is pleasing to him, 13:21; yet they must persevere in faith to the end, 3:6, 14; 4:14; 6:11, 12. They have put on the new human being and must continually put him on, Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10. They have crucified the flesh with its desires and yet must put to death their members that are upon the earth, Gal. 5:24; Col. 3:5. Or, to summarize all these antitheses: on the one hand, it is a fact that Scripture speaks not once but repeatedly of reward, connects reward with the least work performed in Jesus’ name, declares that in the end everyone will be repaid according to his works, and frequently urges good works with a view to that reward, Matt. 5:12; 6:4; 16:27; 25:34; Rom. 2:6; 1 Tim. 4:7, 8; Rev. 22:12, and so forth. Alongside this, it is equally certain that there is no relation of merit and reward between what the believer does and what he will receive, Matt. 20:9; Mark 10:30; Luke 12:37, 43, 44; that when he does everything he was obligated to do, he is still an unprofitable servant, Luke 17:10; that there is no reward except by grace, Rom. 4:3, 4; and that all the benefits of the covenant—sanctification and glorification just as much as justification—were acquired by Christ, lie ready in him, and therefore need not—and cannot—be merited by believers but can only be received in childlike faith. Many have seen a contradiction between this all-encompassing activity of God in grace and the human self-activity nevertheless maintained alongside it, accused Jesus, Paul, and John of inward contradiction, and have themselves sacrificed one to the other. Thus it was taught that grace serves at most to restore in the human being the power of the will toward good and to set him to work earning merit for himself; or, on the other hand, it was asserted that good works are not necessary or are even harmful to salvation. But high above all these one-sided positions stands Holy Scripture, as heaven above earth, as the thoughts of God above the thoughts of human beings. It maintains both, preaches both with equal emphasis, and sees no contradiction or conflict between them. Precisely because God works in believers both the willing and the working, they must work out their own salvation with fear and trembling, Phil. 2:12, 13. They are God’s workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared so that they should walk in them, Eph. 2:10. God and the human being, religion and morality, faith and love, justification and sanctification, prayer and work, do not by nature stand in opposition. They became so through sin, and through sin the human being repeatedly brings conflict between them. But they were originally most closely united; they have been restored to their unity by Christ, who is our peace; they are in principle reconciled in the Christian life. Here dependence coincides with freedom; those born of God become God’s children because they are already his children; for them the law holds: _werde was du bist!_ Good works are therefore on the one hand without any merit. For they are not ours but were prepared for us by God in Christ and wrought in us by his Spirit; through our weakness they are always imperfect and stained with sin; they are wholly disproportionate to the salvation that is granted; _ipsa hominis bona merita sunt Dei munera_, Augustine, _Ench._ 107. And nevertheless they are urgently necessary: because of the command of God, Rom. 6:18; 7:4; 8:12, 13; 1 Thess. 4:3; because of the goal of redemption, Eph. 1:4; as fruits of faith, James 2:14; from gratitude, 1 Cor. 6:20; Col. 1:10; 1 Thess. 2:12; for the glorification of God, John 15:8; as the way to salvation, Heb. 12:14; _bona opera via regni, non causa regnandi_ (Bernard). Cf. later the _locus de vita christiana_.
