## 3.

In the same way as it speaks of sanctification, Scripture speaks of the perseverance of the saints. It exhorts believers to persevere to the end, Matt. 24:13; Rom. 2:7, 8; to abide in Christ, in his word, in his love, John 15:1–10; 1 John 2:6, 24, 27; 3:6, 24; 4:12f.; not to stray but to retain the faith, Col. 1:23; Heb. 2:1; 3:14; 6:11; to be faithful unto death, Rev. 2:10, 26. Sometimes it speaks as if apostasy were possible. Let the one who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall, 1 Cor. 10:12. It warns against arrogance and threatens severe punishment in case of unfaithfulness, Ezek. 18:24; Matt. 13:20, 21; John 15:2; Rom. 11:20, 22; 2 Tim. 2:12; Heb. 4:1; 6:4–8; 10:26–31; 2 Pet. 2:18–22. It even appears to name various examples in which apostasy occurred: David in his adultery, Solomon in his idolatry, Hymenaeus and Alexander, 1 Tim. 1:19, 20; 2 Tim. 2:17, 18; Demas, 2 Tim. 4:10; false prophets and teachers who deny the Lord who bought them, 2 Pet. 2:1; believers who fall away from grace and faith, Gal. 5:4; 1 Tim. 4:1. Resting upon these texts, Pelagians, Roman Catholics, Socinians, Remonstrants, Mennonites, Quakers, Methodists, and so forth, and even the Lutherans, taught the possibility of a total loss of received grace, M. Vitringa III 415 sq. Augustine, by contrast, arrived at the confession of the *perseverantia sanctorum*. But because he considered uncertainty and fear regarding salvation beneficial in believers, he taught that those regenerated by baptism could again lose the grace they had received, yet if they belonged to the number of the *praedestinati* they in any case received it again before death. Believers could therefore lose grace *totaliter*, but the elect could not lose it *finaliter*. In the Catholic and Roman church, earlier and later, many agreed with him. Yet this doctrine was maintained only by the Reformed and connected with the *certitudo fidei*: Zwingli, *Op.* IV 121; Calvin, *Inst.* II 3, 11; 5, 3; III 24, 6, 7; etc.; Polanus, *Synt.* VI 43; *Heid. Cat.*, Qs. 1, 53, 54; *Can. Dordr.*, ch. 5; Trigland, *Antapol.*, chs. 39–41; Gomarus, *Op.* II 280; Chamier, *Panstr. Cath.* III 13, chs. 20–22; Moor IV 387, V 158; M. Vitringa III 415; etc.; and in more recent times Schleiermacher, *Chr. Gl.*, § 111; Schweizer, *Chr. Gl.* II 368, 509; Scholten, *L. H. K.* II 505f.; Van Oosterzee, § 121.

Now in this doctrine of perseverance the question is not whether those who are partakers of true, saving faith, if left to themselves, could not lose it again through their own guilt and sin. Nor is it whether among them all activity, boldness, and comfort of faith does not sometimes in fact cease and faith itself withdraw into hiding under the cares of life and the pleasures of the world. Rather, the question is whether God also preserves, continues, and completes the work of grace he began, or whether he sometimes allows it to be wholly nullified by the power of sin. *Perseverantia* is not an act of the human being but a gift of God. Augustine saw this correctly. Only, he distinguished between two kinds of grace and considered possible a grace of regeneration and faith which was in itself liable to loss and to which, in order to continue to exist, a second grace from outside, that of *perseverantia*, had to be added. The second grace is then a *donum superadditum*, has no connection with the first, and in fact stands outside the Christian life without any influence upon it. Among the Reformed the doctrine of perseverance was entirely different. It was a gift of God; he watches over and ensures that the work of grace has progress and completion, but he does this not apart from believers but through them. In regeneration and faith he gives a grace that also bears in itself a character incapable of loss; he grants a life that is eternal by nature; he confers benefits of calling, justification, and glorification that are inseparably connected with one another. All the exhortations and threats named above which Scripture addresses to believers therefore prove nothing against the doctrine of perseverance. They are precisely the way in which God himself, through believers, confirms his promise and gift; they are the means through which perseverance is realized in life. For perseverance too is no compulsion but as a gift of God works upon the human being in a spiritual manner. God wills precisely in a moral manner, through exhortation and warning, to lead the believer to heavenly salvation and, through the grace of the Holy Spirit, causes him willingly to persevere in faith and love. It is therefore wholly incorrect to infer from the exhortations of Holy Scripture the possibility of the total loss of grace. This conclusion is just as illegitimate as if from Christ’s temptation and struggle one inferred his *posse peccare*. Certainty of the outcome does not make the means superfluous but in God’s ordering is inseparably connected with them. Paul knew certainly that no one would lose his life in the shipwreck; nevertheless he says, “Unless these remain in the ship, you cannot be saved,” Acts 27:22, 31. As concerns the examples Scripture is said to cite of real apostasy, it is impossible to prove that all those persons either possessed the actual grace of regeneration (Hymenaeus, Alexander, Demas, the persons in 1 Tim. 4:1; 2 Pet. 2:1), or really lost it in their fall and then later received it back again (David, Solomon), or really possessed it but never received it again (Heb. 6:4–8; 10:26–31; 2 Pet. 2:18–22). These last texts appear to place the greatest obstacle in the way of the confession of the *perseverantia sanctorum*. Yet this is only appearance. For even those who teach the possibility of apostasy must assume that an entirely special sin is in view here. For according to themselves grace is indeed liable to loss, but after total loss it can also be regained. The opinion of the Montanists and Novatians, who inferred from these passages that apostates might never again be received into the church, has been universally rejected by the Christian churches. When Scripture nevertheless expressly says that it is *impossible* to renew again to repentance those of whom these texts speak, Heb. 6:4; 10:26; 2 Pet. 2:18–20; 1 John 5:16, it is incontrovertible that a sin is intended here which brings with it the judgment of hardening and makes conversion impossible. And even according to the confession of those who accept the possibility of apostasy, there is only one such sin, namely blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, above, p. 101. If this is so, the doctrine of the apostasy of the saints leads to the conclusion that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit can also, or can even only, be committed by the regenerate, Quenstedt, *Theol.* II 157, or the texts named above lose all probative force against the *perseverantia sanctorum*. But to this still more must be added. Those who consider total apostasy possible must distinguish between such sins through which the grace of regeneration is not lost and others through which it is. In other words, they are compelled to take refuge in the Roman doctrine of *peccata mortalia* and *venialia*, unless they would have that grace lost through every sin, even the least. Through this, however, the whole of morality is falsified, the nature of sin disregarded, and a casuistry that entangles and oppresses consciences introduced. Further, from this standpoint there is no assurance of faith, no peaceful labor, no quiet development and growth of the Christian life. Continuity can be broken at any moment. Hollaz, *Ex.* 883, tries to argue that regeneration can be lost and received back again three, four, and more times; cf. Schneckenburger, *Vergl. Darst.* I 233 f. Finally, so little does the doctrine of the apostasy of the saints escape the difficulties it seeks to avoid that it enlarges and multiplies them. For if in this connection it maintains the immutability of God’s *praescientia*, then in the end only those are saved of whom God eternally and certainly knew this, and the human will cannot nullify this certainty of the outcome. Or it must proceed to a denial of *praedestinatio* and *praescientia* in every sense, and then it makes everything wavering and uncertain: the love of the Father, the grace of the Son, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit. God may have revealed his love, Christ may have died for sinners, the Holy Spirit may have planted regeneration and faith in the heart, the believer may be able to say with Paul, “I delight in the law of God according to the inner man”; ultimately, until the hour of death—and why not even beyond the grave?—the human will is the decisive and all-controlling power. Everything will be as it determines.

Scripture, however, teaches entirely otherwise. The Old Testament already clearly declares that the covenant of grace does not depend upon human obedience. It indeed brings with it the obligation to walk in the way of the covenant, but itself rests solely in God’s compassion. When nevertheless the Israelites repeatedly make themselves guilty of unfaithfulness and adultery, the prophets do not infer from this that God changes, that his covenant wavers and his promise fails. On the contrary, God cannot and may not break his covenant. He freely bound himself to Israel in it with a costly oath. His praise, his name, his honor depends upon it. He cannot abandon his people. It is an eternal covenant that knows no wavering. He himself will give his people a new heart and a new spirit, write the law within them, and cause them to walk in his statutes; above, p. 194. And when later Paul stands before that same fact of Israel’s unfaithfulness, his heart filled with sorrow, he does not infer from it that the word of God has failed but continues to believe that God has mercy upon whom he wills, that his gracious gifts and calling are irrevocable, and that not all who are from Israel are Israel, Rom. 9–11. John likewise testifies concerning those who become apostate: They were not of us, otherwise they would have remained with us, 1 John 2:19. Whatever apostasy may therefore take place within Christendom, it may never cause us to doubt the immutability of God, the stability of his counsel, the inviolability of his covenant, or the faithfulness of his promises. One must sooner let every creature go than fail to trust in his word. And that word is one rich promise for the heirs of the kingdom. It is not a few texts that teach perseverance; the whole gospel bears and confirms it. The Father chose them before the foundation of the world, Eph. 1:4; appointed them to eternal life, Acts 13:48; predestined them to conformity to the image of his Son, Rom. 8:29. And this election is immutable, Rom. 9:11; Heb. 6:17, and in its time brings with it calling and justification and glorification, Rom. 8:30. Christ, in whom all God’s promises are yes and amen, 2 Cor. 1:20, died for those given him by the Father, John 17:6, 12, that he might give them eternal life and lose none of them, John 6:40; 17:2. And therefore he grants them eternal life and they shall never perish; no one shall snatch them out of his hand, John 6:39; 10:28. The Holy Spirit who regenerates them abides with them forever, John 14:16, and seals them unto the day of redemption, Eph. 1:13; 4:30. The covenant of grace is firm and confirmed with an oath, Heb. 6:16–18; 13:20; inviolable as a marriage, Eph. 5, vv. 31, 32; as a testament, Heb. 9:17. And by virtue of that covenant God calls his elect, writes the law within them, puts his fear in their hearts, Heb. 8:10; 10:14f.; does not permit them to be tempted beyond their ability, 1 Cor. 10:13; confirms and completes the good work he began in them, 1 Cor. 1:9; Phil. 1:6; and keeps them for the coming of Christ so that they may become partakers of the heavenly inheritance, 1 Thess. 5, v. 23; 2 Thess. 3:3; 1 Pet. 1:4, 5. Through his intercession with the Father, Christ so works that their faith does not fail, Luke 22:32; that they are kept in the world from the evil one, John 17:11, 20; that they are saved completely, Heb. 7:25; that their sins are forgiven, 1 John 2:1; and that they will all be with him and behold his glory, John 17:24. The benefits of Christ of which the Holy Spirit makes them partakers are all irrevocable, Rom. 11:29. The one who is called is glorified, Rom. 8:30; the one adopted as a child is an heir of eternal life, Rom. 8:17; Gal. 4:7; the one who believes already has eternal life here, John 3:16. And because that life itself is eternal, it is also incapable of loss. It cannot die because it cannot sin, 1 John 3:9. Faith is a firm foundation, Heb. 11:1; hope is an anchor, Heb. 6:19, and does not put to shame, Rom. 5:5; and love never fails, 1 Cor. 13:8.

From this perseverance flows rich comfort for believers and abundant blessing for the Christian life. Because God, according to his promise, also completes the good work he began, believers can and may at all times be assured of their eternal salvation. Those who oppose justification from faith alone, or who nevertheless, like the Lutherans, reject the *perseverantia sanctorum*, cannot accept this *certitudo fidei*. Even Augustine did not venture to teach this doctrine and said: *Deus melius esse judicavit, miscere quosdam non perseveraturos certo numero sanctorum suorum, ut quibus non expedit in hujus vitae tentatione securitas*, in Schweizer, *Centraldogmen* I 42. Rome therefore also determined that no one can know with certainty that he has obtained God’s grace unless by special revelation, *Trid.* VI, ch. 9 and canons 13–15, and Roman theologians therefore speak only of a *certitudo moralis, conjecturalis*: Thomas, *S. Theol.* II 1, qu. 112, art. 5; Bellarmine, *de justif.* III, ch. 2 sq. Möhler said that in the vicinity of someone who was always certain of his salvation he would feel *im höchsten Grade unheimlich*, and that he would be unable to rid himself of the thought *dass etwas Diabolisches dabei unterlaufe*, *Symbolik*, p. 197. The Remonstrants and, in later times, the Lutherans also opposed the *certitudo fidei*, at least for the future. But the Reformed confessed election and ascribed to faith the firm assurance of salvation, which could be obtained not from curious investigations into the hidden counsel of God but nevertheless from the nature and fruits of faith through the witness of the Holy Spirit. For faith by its nature conflicts with every doubt. Certainty is not later added to it from outside but lies within it from the beginning and in due time comes forth from it. It is, after all, a gift of God, an operation of the Holy Spirit. In it he bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God, Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:6; causes believers to boast that nothing shall separate them from the love of God in Christ, Rom. 8:38; and assures them of their future salvation, Rom. 8:23; 2 Cor. 1:22; Eph. 1:13, 14; 4:30. And this assurance of faith gives strength and support to the Christian life. For Ritschl has clearly brought this to light: among Roman Catholics justification is an equipping for the moral destiny; among Protestants it is the restoration of the religious relation to God. The latter must precede before there can be any question of a truly Christian life. As long as we still stand before God as Judge, seek life through the law, and are held captive by fear of death, love is not in us—the love that is the fruit of faith, the fulfillment of the law, the bond of perfection, and casts out all fear. But when in justification peace with God, adoption, bold access to the throne of grace, freedom from the law, and independence from the world have been granted to us, then good works flow spontaneously from that faith. They do not serve to acquire eternal life but are the manifestation, seal, and proof of the eternal life that every believer already possesses. Faith, which includes the certainty that with God all things are possible, that he makes the dead alive and calls the things that do not exist as though they did, always enables great things.
