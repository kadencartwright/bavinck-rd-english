# § 46. Sanctification and Perseverance

## 1.

Sanctification is connected with justification and is distinguished from it in nature but not in time. There has always been disagreement and struggle in the Christian church over the relation between the two, just as in all religions the bond between religion and morality is established in different ways and among human beings there is a great difference between religious and ethical natures. Nomism, standing up for the interests of the moral life, makes justification dependent upon sanctification, religion upon morality, and the relation to God upon the relation to one’s neighbor. Conversely, antinomism gives primary attention to the demands of the religious life, places justification in the foreground, and frequently never arrives at sanctification; the relation to God stands entirely apart from the relation to one’s neighbor. In reality, both in doctrine and life, it causes great difficulty to place religion and morality, justification and sanctification, in the right relation to one another; cf. vol. I, 193. The two are distinct. Whoever confuses them undermines the religious life, takes away the comfort of believers, and subjects God to the human being. The distinction between the two lies in this, that in justification the religious relation of the human being to God is restored, and in sanctification his nature is renewed and freed from the impurity of sin. At its deepest it rests upon the fact that God is both righteous and holy. As the Righteous One, he wills that all creatures should stand in that relation to him in which he originally placed them, free from guilt and punishment. As the Holy One, he demands that they all appear before his face pure and unstained by sin. The first human being was therefore created after God’s image in righteousness and holiness and needed neither justification nor sanctification, although he had to obey the law and be justified from its works and receive eternal life (*justificatio legalis*). But sin burdened the human being with guilt and made him impure before God’s face. In order to be completely redeemed from sin, he must therefore be freed from its guilt and cleansed of its stain. And this occurs in justification and sanctification. Both are thus equally necessary and are preached in Scripture with equal emphasis. Justification comes first in logical order, Rom. 8:30; 1 Cor. 1:30, for it is a *justificatio evangelica*, an acquittal on the ground of a δικαιοσυνη θεου granted to us in faith, and not ἐξ ἐργων νομου. It is a juridical act and is completed in one moment. But sanctification is ethical, continues throughout the whole of life, and through the renewing activity of the Holy Spirit gradually makes Christ’s righteousness our personal, ethical possession. Rome’s doctrine of *gratia* or *justitia infusa* is not in itself incorrect; what is wrong is only that it makes infused righteousness the ground of forgiveness and thus builds religion upon the foundation of morality. But believers do truly become partakers of Christ’s righteousness also through *infusio*. Justification and sanctification thus grant the same benefits—or better still, the whole, full Christ—but differ only in the manner in which they grant him. In justification he is granted to us in a juridical sense, in sanctification in an ethical sense. By the former we become the righteousness of God in him; by the latter he himself comes through his Spirit to make his dwelling in us and renews us after his image.

Although justification and sanctification are thus distinct in nature, it is no less important never for a moment to lose sight of the close connection between the two. Whoever separates them undermines the moral life and makes grace subservient to sin. In God righteousness and holiness cannot be separated. He hates sin entirely, not only insofar as it incurs guilt but also insofar as it makes impure. The acts of God in justification and sanctification are inseparably connected: οὑς δε ἐδικαιωσεν, τουτους και ἐδοξασεν, Rom. 8:30; δικαιωσις brings ζωη, 5:18; whoever is justified by God and adopted as his child immediately shares in his favor and immediately begins to live. Further, Christ not only bore sin and fulfilled the law for his own; he could do this only because he had already entered into a covenant relation with them and thus was their head and mediator. All his own were comprehended in him, and with and in him they themselves died, were buried, raised, and seated in heaven, Rom. 6:2–11; 2 Cor. 5:15; Gal. 2:20; Eph. 2:5, 6; Col. 2:12; 3:1; etc. Christ is their δικαιοσυνη, but in the same sense also their ἁγιασμος, 1 Cor. 1:30, that is, not their holiness, ἁγιοτης, ἁγιωσυνη, but their sanctification. Christ, namely, through his suffering and death not only brought about the righteousness on the ground of which believers are acquitted by God. He thereby also acquired that holiness by which he can consecrate them to God and cleanse them from every stain of sin, John 17:19. His obedience unto death had redemption in its whole extent as its purpose, ἀπολυτρωσις, not only as release from the juridical dominion of sin, Rom. 3:24; Eph. 1:7; Col. 1:14, but also as liberation from its moral dominion, Rom. 8:23; 1 Cor. 1:30; Eph. 1:14; 4:30. For this purpose Christ grants himself to them not only objectively in justification but also communicates himself subjectively in sanctification and unites himself with them in a spiritual, mystical manner. This *unio mystica* is always considered by the Lutherans from the anthropological side and then naturally first comes into being after justification and regeneration in actual faith, Schneckenburger, *Vergl. Darst.* I 182–225. But the theological treatment of the Reformed led to a different conception. The *unio mystica* already has its beginning in the *pactum salutis*. Incarnation and satisfaction presuppose that Christ is head and mediator of the covenant. The covenant does not first come into being after Christ, or even after the convincing and regenerating activity of the Holy Spirit. Rather, Christ himself stood in the covenant, and every activity of the Spirit as the Spirit of Christ occurs from and in the covenant. For there is no communion in Christ’s benefits except through communion with his person. The imputation and gift of Christ to his own stands first, and our incorporation into Christ in turn precedes the active reception of Christ and his benefits through the act of faith. Genuine sorrow over sin, hungering and thirsting for righteousness, taking refuge in Christ, and so forth are acts and activities which presuppose life and thus the *unio mystica* and flow from it. This union of believers with Christ is, on the one hand, no pantheistic mingling of the two, no *unio substantialis*, as it was conceived by the mysticism of earlier and later times. Yet on the other hand it is also no mere agreement in disposition, will, and intention, as rationalism understood it and Ritschl has now again explained it: *Theol. u. Metaph.*, 1881; *Rechtf. u. Vers.* III² 106, 552 f.; *Gesch. d. Pietismus*, 3 vols., 1880–86, passim; Herrmann, *Der Verkehr des Christen mit Gott*, 1886; Gottschick, “Luthers Lehre v. d. Gem. des Gläubigen mit Christus,” *Zeits. f. Th. u. K.*, Aug. 1898, p. 406. What Scripture tells us of this *unio mystica* goes much deeper than a moral agreement in will and disposition. It expressly declares that Christ dwells and lives in believers, John 14:23; 17:23, 26; Rom. 8:10; 2 Cor. 13:5; Gal. 2:20; Eph. 3:17, and that they are in him, John 15:1–7; Rom. 8:1; 1 Cor. 1:30; 2 Cor. 5:17; Eph. 1:10f. They are united as branch and vine, John 15; head and members, Rom. 12:4; 1 Cor. 12:12; Eph. 1:23; 4:15; man and woman, 1 Cor. 6:16, 17; Eph. 5:32; cornerstone and building, 1 Cor. 3:11, 16; 6:19; Eph. 2:21; 1 Pet. 2:4, 5. Cf. on the *unio mystica*: Calvin, *Inst.* III 11, 5; Boquinus, Zanchius, Olevianus, Eglin in Heppe, *Dogm. d. d. Pr.* II 372; Martyr, *L. C.*, 259; Polanus, *Synt.* VI, ch. 35; Amesius, *Med. Theol.* 1, ch. 26; Voetius, *Disp.* II 459; Mastricht VI, ch. 5; Witsius, *Misc. S.* II 788; M. Vitringa III 78; Comrie, *Catech.* on Qs. 20–23; Kuyper, *Het werk v. d. H. G.* II 163; Pfleiderer, *Paulinismus*² 214 f.; Krebs, *Ueber die unio mystica*, Marburg, 1871; Weiss, “Das Wesen des pers. Christenstandes,” *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1881, pp. 377–417; Deismann, *Die neutest. Formel ἐν Χρ. I.*, Marburg, 1892. This *unio mystica*, however, is not immediate but comes into being through the Holy Spirit. And in him too the connection between justification and sanctification stands firm. For the Spirit whom Jesus promised to his disciples and poured out in the church is not only a Spirit of υἱοθεσια who assures believers of their adoption, but also the Spirit of renewal and sanctification. This Spirit equipped Christ himself for his work and led him from his conception to his ascension. Through his humiliation Christ was exalted to the Father’s right hand, glorified as a life-giving Spirit, acquirer and distributor of the Spirit, who is now his Spirit, the Spirit of Christ. Through this Spirit he also forms and equips his church. The very first gift believers receive is already communicated to them through the Spirit, who takes everything from Christ, John 16:14. It is he who regenerates them, John 3:5, 6, 8; Titus 3:5; grants life, Rom. 8:10; incorporates them into communion with Christ, 1 Cor. 6:15, 17, 19; brings to faith, 1 Cor. 2:9f.; 12:3; washes, sanctifies, justifies, 1 Cor. 6:11; 12:13; Titus 3:5; leads, Rom. 8:14; pours God’s love into their hearts, Rom. 5:5; prays within them, Rom. 8:26; communicates to them all kinds of virtues, Gal. 5:22; Eph. 5:9, and gifts, Rom. 12:6; 1 Cor. 12:4, especially love, 1 Cor. 13; causes them to live according to a new law, the law of the Spirit, Rom. 8:2, 4; 1 Cor. 7:9; Gal. 5:6; 6:2; renews them in understanding and will, in soul and body, Rom. 6:19; 1 Cor. 2:10; 2 Cor. 5:17; 1 Thess. 5:23. In a word, the Holy Spirit dwells in them, and they live and walk in the Holy Spirit, Rom. 8:1, 4, 9–11; 1 Cor. 6:19; Gal. 4:6; etc. Cf. vol. II, 231, 249; and further Pfleiderer, *Der Paulinismus*² 225 f.; Holtzmann, *Neut. Theol.* II 143 f.
