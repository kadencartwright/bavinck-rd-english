# § 37. The Essence of Sin

## 1.

The question concerning the essence of sin is not identical with that concerning its beginning, for only in development does what lies concealed in a principle become fully manifest. Yet sin itself is already active in the first sin and can therefore already be known to some degree in it. There is disagreement, however, about the character and nature of the first sin of which angels and human beings became guilty. The opinion that the sin of the angels began with lust was already refuted earlier, vol. II, p. 436. In view of the nature of the temptation in Gen. 3:5 and Matt. 4:3, 6, 9 and the admonition in 1 Tim. 3:6 not to become puffed up and so fall into the same condemnation as the devil, it is much more probable that the first sin of the angels consisted in pride. But too little has been revealed about their fall for us to speak here with absolute certainty. Others therefore thought of falsehood, John 8:44; envy, Wis. 2:24; or another sin, Heppe, _Dogm. der ev. ref. K._ 157. According to Roman Catholics, the first sin in the human being also consisted in pride, Bellarmine, _De amiss. grat. et statu pecc._ III, c. 4, who appeals to Sir. 10:13, 14; Tob. 4:13; Rom. 5:19; Augustine, _De Gen. ad litt._ XI 30; _Enchir._ 45; _De civ._ XIV 13; Thomas, _S. Th._ II 2, qu. 163; etc. These proofs are not strong. Sirach and Tobit speak only of pride in general; Paul calls the first sin precisely disobedience; and when church fathers and scholastics call the first sin pride, they are opposing chiefly the view that it consisted in sensual lust. Protestants, however, usually had the sin in Eve begin already with the doubt and unbelief that were then followed by pride and covetousness, Luther on Gen. 3; Gerhard, _Loci_ IX, c. 2; Calvin, _Inst._ II 1, 4; Zanchius, _Op._ IV 30; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XIV 9 sq.; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ III 2; M. Vitringa II 267. Tertullian, _Adv. Jud._ 2, and Augustine, _Enchir._ 45, rightly observed, however, that the first sin already included many sins within it and was in principle a transgression of all the commandments. For it was disobedience to God, doubt, unbelief, self-exaltation, pride, murder, theft, covetousness, and so forth; and correspondingly various thoughts, affections, desires, and movements were also aroused in the human being; understanding and will, soul and body participated in it. It was a conscious and free act, ἁμαρτια, παραβασις, παραπτωμα, παρακοη in the proper sense, Rom. 5:12 f. Although tempted, the first human beings were nevertheless not brought to the fall as innocent children who knew no better. They transgressed God’s command consciously and freely; they knew and willed what they did. Excuses have no place here. The circumstances under which the first sin was committed by angels and human beings do not mitigate but increase the guilt. It was committed against God’s express and clear command; by a human being created in God’s image; in a matter of very slight importance that required scarcely any self-denial; and perhaps a short time after the command was received. It became the source of all unrighteous deeds and abominations, of all disasters and calamities, of all sickness and death that have since been committed and suffered in the world. _Hinc illae lacrimae!_ Adam’s sin can be no trifle. It must have been a fundamental reversal of every relation, a revolution in which the creature detached itself from and set itself over against God, a rebellion, a fall in the most proper sense, which was decisive for the whole world and led it in a direction and upon a way away from God, toward godlessness and destruction, _ineffabiliter grande peccatum_, Augustine, _Op. imp. c. Jul._ I 165. So seriously was the first sin understood in the Christian church and theology, Augustine, _De civ._ XIV 11–15; XXI 12; _Enchir._ 26, 27, 45; Thomas, _S. Th._ II 2, qu. 163, art. 3; Council of Trent V 1; Bellarmine, _De am. gr. et st. pecc._ III, c. 8–10; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 594; Belgic Confession, art. 14; Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 7, 9; Mastricht, _Theol._ IV 1, 15; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ III 2, 10; etc.
