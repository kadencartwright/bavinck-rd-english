## 6.

Besides the distinction between diabolical and human sins, there is also great diversity among the latter themselves.
The Stoics, Novatian, Seb. Franck, Deurhof, and others wrongly denied this, M. Vitringa II 377.
It is true that sin and virtue are each indivisible in principle: whoever possesses one virtue possesses them all, and whoever lacks one lacks them all; there is no gradual transition between good and evil; a person either accords with God’s law or does not.
And God’s law is an organism which, when transgressed in one of its commandments, is violated in its entirety, for God, who gave the commandment that was transgressed, is the author of all the other commandments, James 2:10, cf. Vinet, _L’unité de la loi_, in his _Nouvelles études évangéliques_. Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 730. 731.
But this does not mean that all sins are equal.
The various names used for sin already point to this.
In the offering of Cain and Abel in Gen. 4, it becomes evident that the disposition is of greater value than the gift.
The law given to Israel does indeed contain all kinds of ceremonial commandments, but throughout the Old Testament ethical conduct nevertheless stands far above the cultic and liturgical; faith is counted as righteousness, Gen. 15:6; obedience is better than sacrifice, 1 Sam. 15:22; Amos 2:6; 5:14, 21f.; Hos. 4:1f.; 12:7; Mic. 6:6, 8; Isa. 1:11f.; 5:8f.; Jer. 7:3; 22:3; Ezek. 16:49; 18:5f.; Zech. 7:5f.; Mal. 3:5, etc., cf. Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 70.
When Pharisaism arises after the exile and reverses this relation, Jesus and the apostles return once again to the law and the prophets, Matt. 5–7; 19:18f.; Mark 7:21f.; Rom. 1:29f.; 1 Cor. 5:10f.; 6:9f.; 2 Cor. 12:20f.; Gal. 5:19f.; etc.
Moreover, the law itself distinguishes between sins בִּשְׁגָגָה, committed without malicious intent, from ignorance or weakness, which do not break the covenant and can be atoned for within the covenant, and sins בְיָד רָמָה, committed knowingly and deliberately, which place the offender outside the covenant and render him worthy of death, Lev. 4; 5; 22:14; Num. 15:22f.; 35:11f.; Josh. 20:3, 9.
Scripture never abandons the objective standpoint, which seeks the standard of sin solely in the law of God.
Nevertheless, the guilt of the transgression is lesser or greater according as the commandment was violated with a lesser or greater degree of consciousness and will.
On the one hand, Stade, _Gesch. des Volkes Israel_ I 512 f., wrongly infers from Gen. 12:17; 20:3; 26:10; Num. 22:34; 1 Sam. 14:24f., 36f., that ancient Israel did not know the distinction between conscious and unconscious transgressions and that a person could therefore become guilty wholly without his knowledge and will; for all these passages regard the ignorance as self-incurred (_selbstverschuldete_) or speak of no guilt.
But on the other hand, neither is it true _dass Sünde im Allgemeinen nur ist, sofern auch ein Bewusstsein derselben ist_, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ 68, 2.
Sin does presuppose some knowledge of the law; a human being without the least moral awareness would be unaccountable but would also cease to be human, Rom. 2:14, 15.
And sin is ordinarily accompanied by some sense of guilt, but the standard of sin is not the consciousness of guilt but the law of God.
There are sins hidden not only from others but also from ourselves, Job 11:4f.; Ps. 19:13; 90:8, or acknowledged and confessed as guilt only later, Ps. 25:7; 51:7.
Ignorance itself is also frequently sin, and the consciousness of guilt grows weaker the longer sin is served, Amos 2:11; Hos. 4:6; Mic. 3:1; 6:8; Prov. 24:12; Eccl. 4:17.
The subjective consciousness of guilt therefore cannot determine the character of sin.
Thus, although ignorance can never render sin itself good, it nevertheless often serves as an excuse when it is not willful.
Paul says that he was formerly a blasphemer, persecutor, and oppressor, but adds that mercy was shown to him because he acted in ignorance, 1 Tim. 1:13.
And so Holy Scripture repeatedly speaks of the sins of the Jews and Gentiles as committed in ignorance, Luke 23:34; Acts 3:17–19; 13:27; 17:30; Eph. 4:18; Heb. 5:2; 1 Pet. 1:14; 2:25.
These sins are indeed not thereby stripped of their guilty character, as Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._² II 38, 241–246; III 350–354, seems to think, for Rom. 1–3; 5:12f.; Eph. 4:17–19; Col. 3:5–7; 1 Cor. 15:9; 1 Tim. 1:13, 15; etc., teach otherwise.
Yet these sins committed in ignorance are thereby distinguished from sins arising from hardening; ignorance offers a plea for forgiveness.
And just as sins differ in degree and measure according as they are committed from ignorance and weakness or from intent and malice, so they are also distinguished according to the object against which they are directed: sins against the first table are graver than those against the second, Matt. 22:37, 38; according to the subject who commits them: as a person is endowed more richly with gifts, the guilt of his transgression increases, Matt. 11:21; Luke 12:47, 48; John 9:41; 15:22, 24; according to the circumstances under which they are committed: one who steals from poverty sins less grievously than one who does so from greed, Prov. 6:30; Isa. 26:10; according to the degree to which a person yields to sin: one who commits adultery in thought and word is guilty, but aggravates his judgment if he consummates the sin in deed, Matt. 5:28.
There is also a development in sin; there is a law of sin.
A particular sin comes about gradually through _suggestio, delectatio, consensus, operatio_; _in suggestione peccati semen est, in delectatione fit nutrimentum, in consensu perfectio_, Gregory M., cf. Lombardus, _Sent._ II 24, 8. Bonav., _Brevil._ III 8.
And so sin also develops gradually in a person, in a household and family, in a society and nation, and also in the whole of humanity.
Sin is admittedly not so rich in itself that it can assume so many forms, for it is no independent principle and is metaphysically nothing but _privatio boni_.
But just as in its origin, so too in its development, it exists only in and through the good.
It allies itself with the boundless riches of creation, destroys all that exists, fights against God and his holy law with the entire world as its instrument, and thereby assumes all those different forms and shapes which together give it the character of a well-governed kingdom, of an organism animated by one principle, of a κοσμος, standing under the direction of the ἀρχων του κοσμου, the θεος του αἰωνος.

Since sin is in its essence _privatio_, neither a _principium dividendi_ nor a _divisio_ can be derived from sin itself.
_Privatio accipit speciem a forma, cui opponitur._
Earlier dogmatics and ethics therefore did speak about the character of the first sin but otherwise made no effort to discover a so-called principle of sin and to reduce to it all transgressions of the moral law.
Only in more recent times has an attempt been made to establish such a principle, seeking it by turns in sensuousness, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 66, and others; or in selfishness, Müller, _Sünde_ I⁵ 178. Tholuck, _Sünde_⁸ 18. 98. Vilmar, _Moral_ I 136. Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ III² 3. Lange, art. “Selbstsucht” in Herzog, and further Thomasius, Kahnis, Frank, Zöckler, etc.; or also in both, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 461. Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 480.
In fact, human sins generally also display the character of sensuousness or selfishness, of fleshly desire or spiritual pride, of weakness or malice; sometimes sin seems to consist in the dominion of matter over spirit, sometimes also in an abuse of freedom, in revolt against God’s ordinances.
Yet neither Rothe succeeded in explaining selfishness from sensuousness nor J. Müller, with his preexistence theory, in explaining sensuousness from selfishness, cf. Dorner, _Gl._ II 90–98.
And that is also easy to understand.
Metaphysically and abstractly, sin cannot be defined otherwise or more precisely than as _privatio boni_; then it has no principle of its own, no real existence; it exists only in the good.
The forms it assumes are derived from the good in which it dwells and which it corrupts.
It will therefore differ in form according to the creatures in which it resides.
Although always _privatio boni_, it bears a distinctive character among angels and human beings, and even, in turn, in every individual among them.
And because the human being is by nature neither solely a sensuous nor solely a spiritual being but always both together, every sin in him will therefore also display this dual character.
Not a single human sin is exclusively sensuousness or exclusively selfishness.
As in Adam’s first sin, so in every sin different aspects can be observed, even though one is usually more conspicuous than the other.
Every sin in the human being is _aversio a Deo_, disobedience, revolt, anarchy, anomie, and at the same time, because he is never sufficient unto himself, _conversio ad creaturam_, idolatry, pride, selfishness, sensuousness, and so forth, Bonaventura, _Sent._ II dist. 42 art. 3 qu. 2.
And because the creatures to which a human being can turn are so many, sin in him can also assume so many different forms.
There are as many kinds of sins as there are different commandments, duties, virtues, and moral goods.
Thomas classified sins according to the objects toward which they are directed, _S. Theol._ II 1 qu. 72 art. 1; Scotus according to the virtues to which they are opposed, _Sent._ II dist. 37 qu. 1, 9, cf. Liguori, _Theol. mor._ de pecc. II. n. 32.
Alongside these there were still many other classifications, such as that into seven capital sins, _superbia, avaritia, luxuria, via, gula, invidia, acedia_ (_vox memorialis: saligia_); according to the _norma_, into sins against the various commandments of the law or into sins against God, one’s neighbor, and oneself; according to the instrument by which they occur, into sins of thought, word, and deed, or into sins of the spirit and the flesh, or, in accordance with 1 John 2:16, into _peccata sentiendi, sciendi et dominandi_, or into sins of weakness, ignorance, and malice; according to form, into sins of omission and commission, or into sins _per se_ and _per accidens_; according to the _adjuncta_, into hidden and public, reigning and nonreigning, silent and crying sins, and so forth, cf. Lombardus, _Sent._ II dist. 42. Thomas, _S. Theol._ I qu. 72. Gerhard, _Loc._ X c. 5 sq. Ursinus, _Tract. Theol._ 202. Moor III 313. Mastricht, _Theol._ IV 3, 10. Heidegger, _Corpus Theol._ X 61. Vilmar, _Theol. Moral_ I 221. Zöckler, _Das Lehrstück v. d. sieben Hauptsünden_, München, 1893, etc.

Leaving further elaboration to ethics, we discuss here only the Roman Catholic distinction of sins into _peccata mortalia_ and _venialia_.
This distinction has its origin in the practice of penance, cf. Pijper, _Gesch. der boete en biecht in de Chr. Kerk_ I 1891, p. 306f., and is already found in substance in Tertullian, _de pudic._ 2. 3. 19. _adv. Marc._ IV 9, and in Augustine, who spoke of _peccata levia, brevia, minuta, minima, quotidiana_, which still remain in believers, _Enchir._ 44. 71. _de civ._ XXI 27. _de nat. et gr._ 39. _de spir. et litt._ 36.
Elaborated by scholasticism—Lombardus and others in their commentaries on _Sent._ II dist. 42; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1 qu. 88. 89—it was established by the church, Trid. VI c. 11 can. 27. XIV 5. _Cat. Rom._ II 5 qu. 40, and since then has been zealously defended by all its theologians against every attack, Bellarminus, _de amiss. gr._ I c. 3 sq. Becanus, _Theol. Schol._ II 1619 p. 117. Liguori, _Theol. Mor._, de pecc. n. 51 sq. Busenbaum, _Theol. mor._, de pecc. qu. 31 sq. Antoine, _Theol. Mor._, de pecc. c. 2, etc.
According to this distinction, there are sins which cause the loss of received grace and are worthy of death, and others—such as an idle word, an excessively loud laugh, an involuntarily arising desire, irritation, anger, a very small theft, and so forth—which do not cause grace to be lost, are not so much _contra_ as _praeter legem_, and are pardonable by nature.
The distinction is based on the fact that Scripture speaks of different sins and punishments, Matt. 5:22; 7:3; 23:23; Luke 6:41; 1 Cor. 3:12–15; sometimes connects death with sins, Rom. 1:32; 6:23; 1 Cor. 6:9; Gal. 5:21; 1 John 3:14; and yet often continues to acknowledge believers as such even though they stumble in many ways, Prov. 24:16; Matt. 1:19; Luke 1:6; James 3:2; and furthermore on the consideration that there are curable and incurable diseases and that there are minor offenses which do not dissolve friendship.
The Reformers rejected this distinction as contrary to God’s Word.
They did not deny that there were degrees in sins, and they also continued to retain the terms _peccata mortalia_ and _venialia_, but they attached a different meaning to them.
The Lutherans had to adopt the distinction to a certain extent, since believers could commit sins in which grace was preserved and others in which it was lost, Luther in Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 472 f. Melanchthon, _Loci Comm._, de peccato. Gerhard, _Loc._ X c. 20, cf. also Bellarminus, _de amiss. gr._ I c. 4.
But the Reformed went further and wanted nothing at all to do with the entire distinction.
If they sometimes still used the terms, they understood by them that all sins except blasphemy against the Holy Spirit can be forgiven through God’s mercy and in fact are also forgiven to believers, but that all of them in themselves are worthy of death, Calvin, _Inst._ II 8, 58. III 2, 11. 4, 28. _Antid. conc. Trid._ VI 12. Ursinus, _Tract. theol._ 209. Gomarus, _Theses theol._ disp. 13. Moor III 308–312. Turretinus, _Theol. El._ IX 4. Mastricht IV 3, 22. Pictet, _Christ. Godg._ VI 11. Heppe, _Dogm._ 257.
The passages of Scripture to which Roman Catholics appeal in support of their distinction are accordingly all devoid of any probative force.
Only Matt. 5:22 offers some semblance of a basis, but the intent of Jesus’ word there is entirely different from distinguishing light sins from grave ones.
Over against the ancients, namely, who said that only the sinful deed, the actual murder, rendered one guilty and liable to punishment before the local court, Jesus says that not merely the deed but already the first stirring of unlawful anger, even if it has not yet expressed itself in a word, renders one guilty and liable to punishment before that court; that when this anger expresses itself in a slight, angry word, the sin is already so great that it must be dealt with by the Sanhedrin; and that when the anger expresses itself in a word of abuse, it is at once, without any form of trial, worthy of hellfire.
Thus, far from speaking here of venial sins, Jesus, precisely the reverse, regards the slightest sin as punishable in the highest degree, as punishable as the ancients regarded the sinful deed, that is, murder.
Jesus equates the rising of unlawful anger with murder; he says that even the lightest sin is already a very grave sin, deserving as great a punishment as murder did according to the ancients.
Jesus says nothing about what punishment this stirring of anger deserves hereafter; but when that anger is accompanied by a word of abuse, then this sin is so great that at that very moment it is worthy of the punishment of hell; no court is any longer needed to determine a punishment.
Properly understood, this text is therefore an argument against rather than for the distinction between _peccata mortalia_ and _venialia_.
And the whole of Scripture likewise stands opposed to this classification.
The law is an organic whole, James 2:10; whoever transgresses one commandment is in principle guilty of all; it must be fulfilled in its entirety, Matt. 5:17–19; it demands us wholly, with the whole heart and mind, with soul and body, Matt. 22:37; for it nothing is indifferent or slight; cursed is the one who does not continue in all that is written in the book of the law, to do it, Deut. 27:26; Gal. 3:10; even the smallest and slightest transgressions of the law, such as a rising anger, an impure desire, an unnecessary affirmation, an idle word, Matt. 5:22, 28, 37; 12:36; Eph. 5:4, are sin, equal in principle to sinful deeds, and therefore, as sin, also ἀνομια, enmity against God.
Considered according to principle, there are no small and slight sins.
_Nullum peccatum contemnendum ut parvum, cum revera nullum sit parvum, quando Paulus de omni peccato generatim pronuntiaverit, stimulum mortis esse peccatum_, Basil in Gerhard, loc. cit.
When a sin, for example an idle word, is set by itself and detached from its entire connection with the person, the circumstances, and so forth, the assertion that it deserves eternal death seems excessively severe.
But it was precisely this abstract, atomistic conception which the Reformers rejected in principle as contrary both to Scripture and to reality.
Sin is no _quantum_ that, isolated from the doer, can be counted on the fingers or weighed in the balance.
The Roman Catholic distinction has in fact also led to all kinds of evil practices.
Not only do the theologians disagree over whether venial sin does or does not offend God, whether it should or should not be confessed, and whether genuine contrition is necessary for its remedy or the performance of one meritorious work or another is sufficient.
But all also acknowledge that the distinction is very difficult and almost impossible to maintain both in theory and in practice.
One must therefore resort to all kinds of subtle reasonings, which in the process cause the entire character of sin to be lost.
Where reasonings fail, one counts up the opinions of the _doctores_ and contents oneself with a lesser or greater degree of probability.
Thus one arrives at an atomistic, casuistic, mechanical, materialistic estimation of sins and satisfactions, and continually keeps souls in fear that they may perhaps have committed a mortal sin, or else brings them to frivolity and indifference, since sins are usually of a very light nature and very easy to make satisfaction for.
