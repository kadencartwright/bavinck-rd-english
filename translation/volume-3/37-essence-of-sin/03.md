## 3.

By this teaching of Scripture the conception of sin in Christian theology was determined. On the one hand, therefore, it could not accept the opinion that sought the essence of sin in some substance and consequently traced it back to a principle of wrath in God (Böhme), or to an evil power alongside God (Mani), or to some kind of matter, such as the ὑλη, the σαρξ (Plato, the Jews, Flacius, etc.). And on the other hand, the theory also had to be rejected that sin consisted in a not-yet-being, that it belonged to the necessary antitheses in life, and that it was naturally inherent in the finite, self-developing human being who strives for perfection (Spinoza, Hegel, etc.), cf. above p. 45f. Over against this, Christian theology maintained from the beginning that sin was not a substance. There has never been any difference or controversy about this. Petavius cites numerous church fathers who all teach the same thing in this respect. Nor was any doubt or hesitation possible here. If sin were a substance, there would be a being that did not have God as its author, or God would also be its cause. Sin therefore had to be conceived and defined as οὐτε ὀν οὐτε ἐν τοις ουσιν, as an ἐλλειψις, στερησις, ἀναχωρησις του ἀγαθου, as ἀσθενεια, ἀσυμμετρια, just as blindness is a deprivation of sight, Athan. c. Gent. 3 sq. Greg. Nyss., Catech. c. 5. Dionysius, de div. nom. c. 4. Damasc., de fide orthod. II c. 30. In the West, Augustine especially brought to light this privative character of sin and maintained it against the Manichaeans, cf. Nirschl, Ursprung und Wesen des Bösen nach der Lehre des H. Aug. Regensburg 1854. All being is good in itself. _Omnis natura, in quantum natura est, bona est._ Evil can therefore exist only in the good, _non potest esse ullum malum nisi in aliquo bono, quia non potest esse nisi in aliqua natura_. It is even _nulla natura_, but _amissio, privatio, corruptio boni, vitium, defectus naturae_; _bonum minui, malum est_. de civ. XI 17. 22. Enchir. 11–13. c. Jul. I c. 3. etc. For this reason it also has no _causa efficiens_, but only a _deficiens_, de civ. XII 7. 9. And likewise, by the scholastics and by Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed theologians, the concept of sin in the metaphysical sense was reduced to that of _privatio_, cf. Lombardus, Sent. II dist. 34 and 35 and the commentaries of Thomas, Bonaventura, etc.; further, Thomas, S. Th. I qu. 48. 49. c. Gent. III 7 sq. Bonav., Brevil. III 1. Bellarminus, de amiss. gr. et stat. pecc. I c. 1. II c. 18. V c. 1. Becanus, Theol. schol. II 1 tract. 1 cap. 5 qu. 1. Melanchthon, Loc. de peccato. Gerhard, Loc. X c. 1. Quenstedt, Theol. II p. 50. Hollaz, Ex. theol. 501. Buddeus, Inst. theol. p. 546. Zanchius, Op. IV 6 sq. Polanus, Synt. Theol. p. 335. Ursinus, Tract. Theol. 1584 p. 199. Hoornbeek, Theol. practica IV c. 1. Turretinus, Theol. El. loc. IX, etc.

On the other hand, it is clear that sin is not adequately defined by the concept of _privatio_. After all, it is no mere lack, no sheer nonbeing, but an active and corrupting principle, a disintegrating, destructive power. Scripture usually speaks of it in a very positive sense as transgression, perversity, disobedience, lawlessness, and so forth, and attributes to it the activities of bearing witness, ruling, moving, devising, warring, and so forth. Various theologians have therefore also rejected the distinction between _materia_ and _forma_ in sin. They appealed, for example, to the fact that blasphemy, idolatry, hatred against God, and so forth, were sinful as acts and could never assume a good _forma_, and they therefore preferred to define sin as an _entitas quaedam realis ac positiva_, as a _reale quid_, Cajetanus in Becanus, loc. cit. Theol. Wirceb. VII 15. Vitringa Sr., Observ. Sacr. VI c. 15. 16. M. Vitringa, II 288–290; and further also Arminius, Op. 1629 p. 730. Limborch, Theol. Chr. V 4. 2. Strauss, Gl. II 360 f. Müller, Sünde I 396–409. Shedd, Dogm. Theol. I 371. For a proper understanding, however, the following should be observed here. 1º. When most Christian theologians conceived sin as _privatio_, their primary aim was thereby to oppose Manichaeism. To that extent their view is also entirely correct and should be accepted without reservation. Sin is no substance, either spiritual or material, for then it would have God as its cause, or God would not be the Creator of all things. 2º. The essence of sin itself also forbids thinking of it as a substance. For sin is not a physical but an ethical phenomenon. It is a state and an act of the will and has its cause in this will; it was not given with creation but arose after creation through disobedience. It therefore cannot be a _materia_ that existed eternally or was created by God in time, but has reality only as a deformation of being; to that extent it can even be called an οὐκ ὀν, a _nihilum_. 3º. This is by no means intended to say that sin is a _nihil negativum_. Rather, the pantheistic conception of sin as a pure negation, as a not-yet-being, as a necessary moment in the development of a finite and limited being, as a delusion of thought, has at all times been opposed as decisively as possible by Christian theology. Sin was not a _mera negatio_ but a _privatio_. The distinction between the two consists in this: negation denotes only absence (_carere_), whereas privation denotes lack (_egere_); that a stone does not see is a negation, that a human being does not see is a privation, since sight belongs to the qualities of a human being. Sin is a deprivation of that moral perfection which the human being ought to possess. 4º. The definition of sin as _privatio_ therefore does not in the least exclude its also being, considered from another side, an _actio_. It is no οὐσια, _substantia_, _res_, but in its deprivation of the good it is indeed an ἐνεργεια, just as the limping of a lame person is not non-walking but walking wrongly. Augustine, who repeatedly describes sin as a _privatio_, therefore calls it a _transgressio legis_, de cons. Ev. II c. 4; _voluntas retinendi vel consequendi quod justitia vetat_, de duab. an. c. Man. I. c. 11. Retract. I 11; a _deficere_ that includes a _tendere_, _deficere autem non jam nihil est sed ad nihilum tendere_, c. Secund. Man. c. 11; _inclinatio ab eo quod magis est ad id, quod minus est_, ib. c. 12, cf. de lib. arb. I 16. II 19; and gives this definition of sin: _peccatum est factum vel dictum vel concupitum aliquid contra aeternam legem; lex vero aeterna est ratio divina vel voluntas Dei, ordinem naturalem conservari jubens, perturbari vetans_, c. Faust. Man. XXII 27. This definition was later adopted by all, Lombardus, Sent. II dist. 135. Thomas, S. Theol. II 1 qu. 71 art. 6; sin is no _mera_ or _pura privatio sed actus debito ordine privatus_, ib. qu. 72 art. 1 ad 2. qu. 75 art. 1 ad 1, a _privatio cum positiva qualitute et actione_, an _actuosa privatio_, Zanchius, Op. IV p. 1 sq. Polanus, Synt. Theol. VI 3. Heidegger, Corp. Theol. X 8. Bucanus, Inst. Theol. XV 7. Synopsis pur. theol. XVI 4–9, etc.
