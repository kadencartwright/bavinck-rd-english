## 7.

Holy Scripture mentions only one sin that is unforgivable in this life and the life to come, namely, blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. It is not spoken of in the Old Testament, although in the law no atoning sacrifice was instituted for sins committed בְיָד רָמָה, because they nullified the law itself, Heb. 10:28. Jesus is the first to speak of it, Matt. 12:31; Mark 3:28; Luke 12:10. When on one occasion he completely healed a demoniac who was also blind and mute, the crowds were so amazed by this miracle that they acknowledged Jesus as the Son of David, as the Christ. But the Pharisees were thereby driven to a summit of hatred that made them say not only that he cast out devils by the devil but that he himself was also possessed by the devil, Mark 3:22, βεελζεβουλ ἐχει. This accusation was prompted solely by hatred; it arose from pure, conscious, deliberate enmity. Jesus also demonstrates this, Matt. 12:25–30. A kingdom divided against itself cannot stand; Satan does not cast himself out, but the casting out of Satan is proof that the kingdom of God has come upon them; Jesus casts out the devil through the Spirit of God. The opposition between Jesus and the Pharisees is therefore stretched here to its utmost. They say that Jesus is possessed, performs his miracles through the devil, and establishes the kingdom of the devil. And Jesus declares that he is the Christ, that through the Spirit of God he casts out the devil, and that in this way he brings the kingdom of God to them. In this context and on this occasion Jesus speaks of blasphemy against the Holy Spirit as the unforgivable sin. Whether one thinks that the Pharisees committed this sin at that moment or believes this must be denied, among other reasons because the Holy Spirit had not yet been poured out, John 7:39, the context always makes clear that the sin against the Holy Spirit must consist in a conscious, willful, intentional blasphemy against the clearly recognized revelation of God’s grace in Christ through the Holy Spirit, which is nevertheless attributed to the devil out of hatred and enmity. Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit therefore consists neither in simple unbelief, nor in resisting and grieving the Holy Spirit in general, nor in denying the personality or deity of the Holy Spirit, nor merely in sinning against better knowledge and to the end. Neither is it a sin against the law alone, but specifically also against the gospel, and indeed against the gospel in its clearest revelation. Much therefore precedes it: objectively, a revelation of God’s grace in Christ, the nearness of his kingdom, a powerful operation of the Holy Spirit; and subjectively, an illumination and conviction of the understanding so vivid and powerful that one cannot deny God’s truth but must acknowledge it as divine. The sin itself then consists not in doubting or simply denying that truth but in a denial that goes against the conviction of the understanding, against the illumination of conscience, against the voice of the heart; in a fully conscious, willful, intentional attribution to the influence and operation of Satan of what is clearly recognized as God’s work—that is, in a decided blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, in willfully declaring that the Holy Spirit is the spirit from the abyss, that truth is falsehood, that Christ is Satan himself. Its root is therefore the fully conscious, intentional hatred of God and of what is acknowledged as divine; its essence is the sinful in its highest manifestation, the completed, consummated revolution, the placing of God in Satan’s place and Satan in God’s place; its character is no longer human but demonic. Even though the devils do not commit this sin in this definite form, because God’s grace has not appeared to them, Christ did not assume their nature, the Holy Spirit was not poured out among them, and the kingdom of God did not come to them, demonic sin nevertheless bears the same character displayed by blasphemy against the Holy Spirit among human beings. It is therefore also unforgivable. God’s grace is not too small and powerless for it, but in the kingdom of sin too there are laws and ordinances laid within it by God and maintained by him. And in the case of this sin that law consists in its excluding all repentance, searing the conscience, wholly hardening and making the sinner obdurate, and in this way rendering his sin unforgivable. Apart from the Gospels, this sin is nowhere spoken of in Scripture in direct words. But this blasphemy against the Holy Spirit can be committed in different circumstances. Thus Heb. 6:4–8; 10:25–29; cf. 12:15–17, says that those who were once enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, and became partakers of the Holy Spirit, and then relapse into Judaism, tread underfoot, crucify, and put to shame the Son of God, regard the blood of the New Covenant as unclean, and insult the Spirit of grace, cannot be brought again to repentance. Likewise 1 John 5:16 testifies that there is a sin that, necessarily by virtue of its nature, leads to death without repentance and for which John does not say—that is, does not command—that one should pray. Prayer, if not forbidden, is nevertheless fruitless. In connection with his whole letter, John probably thinks here of the decided and willful denial of Christ as the incarnate Son of God. In both these passages, therefore, we are dealing with sins that completely harden the human being and are thus themselves unforgivable.
