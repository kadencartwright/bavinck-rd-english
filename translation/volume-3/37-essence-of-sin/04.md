## 4.

On the ground of Holy Scripture and in agreement with the confession of the Christian faith, the essence of sin can therefore be more precisely described and explained in the following manner.
1º. Since sin is not a physical or metaphysical but an ethical antithesis of the good, it has no existence of its own, independent and separate from the being of things.
Anyone who regards sin as a substance may indeed seem deeply impressed by its power and significance, but in fact weakens it, transfers it from the ethical to the physical domain, and turns the conflict between good and evil into a struggle between light and darkness, spirit and matter, a good and an evil God—a struggle that never ends and makes every deliverance from sin impossible.
It is therefore of the highest importance always to regard sin as an ethical phenomenon.
The punishments and consequences of sin certainly extend into the physical domain as well, but sin itself is and remains ethical in nature.
It can therefore have neither a principle of its own nor an independent existence; it arose after the good and exists only through and in dependence upon it.
Evil is indeed dependent upon the good, but not conversely.
Τῳ μεν γαρ ἐσθλῳ ἐγχωρει κακῳ γενεσθαι, Plato, _Protag._, p. 344.
Το βελτιον και το τιμιωτερον προτερον εἰναι τῃ φυσει δοκει, Aristotle, _Categ._, c. 9.
_Bonum (verum) index sui et mali (falsi)._
The good became the cause of evil by free choice and remains its substrate.
Fallen angels and human beings are and remain good as creatures and exist from moment to moment only through and in and unto God.
And just as in its origin and its being, so sin also remains dependent upon the good in its working and fighting.
It can accomplish something only with and through the powers and gifts bestowed by God.
Satan is therefore rightly called the _simia Dei_: when God establishes a church, Satan builds a chapel; over against a true prophet he raises up a false one; over against Christ he sets the antichrist.
Even a band of robbers can exist only if it respects justice within its own circle.
A liar adorns himself with the appearance of truth.
A sinner pursues evil _sub ratione boni_.
Satan appears as an angel of light.
Sin is always condemned, in its operation and appearance and despite itself, to borrow from virtue.
It lies under the unbreakable necessity that, while striving to destroy all good, it also works toward its own death.
It is a parasite of the good.
2º. Although sin thus strives by its nature toward nonbeing, it nevertheless has no power over being itself.
It cannot create, nor can it destroy.
Therefore neither the essence of the angels nor that of human beings nor that of nature has been changed by sin.
They are essentially the same creatures before and after the fall, with the same substance, the same faculties, and the same powers.
Before and after the fall the human being has soul and body, understanding and will, affections and passions.
What has changed is not the substance, the _materia_, but the _forma_ in which it manifests itself, the direction in which it operates.
With the same power of love with which the human being originally loved God, he now loves the creature.
The same understanding with which he formerly contemplated the things above now causes him, with admirable acuteness and profundity, to honor falsehood as truth.
With the same freedom with which he formerly served God, he now serves the world.
Substantially, nothing has been taken away from the human being or introduced into him through sin.
He is the same human being, but now walking not toward God but away from God, toward destruction.
_Peccatum non est essentia aliqua sed defectus et corruptela, qua scilicet corrumpitur modus, species et ordo_, Bonaventure, _Brevil._ III 1.
3º. The loss of the image of God and the breach of the covenant of works are likewise not in conflict with this conception of sin.
For the image of God, although no _donum superadditum_ but naturally proper to the human being, was no _substantia_ but an _accidens_; that is, the human being as created was so constituted that his nature of itself, without supernatural grace though not without God’s good providence, brought with it and manifested that knowledge, holiness, and righteousness which were the principal content of the image of God.
When the human being fell, however, he lost nothing substantial, not even a faculty or a power; but because sin corrupted the _forma_ of his whole nature, of all his faculties and powers, these now all operate in such a way that they produce no longer the knowledge of God and righteousness but precisely their opposites.
Thus through the fall the human being did not merely lose an unreal addition to his nature, a _donum superadditum_, while his nature otherwise remained intact; neither did he become a devil, incapable of re-creation and never again able to display the features of the image of God; rather, while remaining essentially and substantially the same—that is, human—and retaining all his human components, faculties, and powers, the _forma_, nature and character, disposition and direction of them all has been so changed that now, instead of God’s will, they fulfill the law of the flesh.
The image has been changed into a caricature.
And likewise the _foedus operum_ has been broken insofar as no flesh can any longer be justified by works of the law, Rom. 3:20; Gal. 3:2; but it is so little destroyed and abolished that the law of that _foedus operum_ still obligates every human being to absolute obedience, that it was taken up and perfectly fulfilled by Christ in the covenant of grace, and that it now remains for believers a rule of gratitude.
4º. Apart from the good substrate by which sin is borne and to which it clings, it can therefore never be described otherwise than as _privatio boni_.
One must note carefully, however, that sin is then being spoken of _abstractive_ and _metaphysice_.
And described in this way, it has no being and is no substance but a _nihil_, nothing positive but only something privative; anyone who wished to conceive it otherwise would thereby make evil independent and eternal in the Manichaean sense and set a _summum malum_ over against the _summum bonum_.
The objection mentioned above against defining sin as _privatio_ therefore actually rests on misunderstanding.
_Abstractive_ and _metaphysice_, sin is a _privatio_ and from the Christian standpoint cannot and may not be conceived otherwise.
But _concretive_ it occurs only as the perverse _forma_ of a particular condition or act and makes that condition or act itself sinful, just as a disease, without being a substance, nevertheless makes the body sick.
_In concreto_, therefore, sin is always in and attached to something that is substantially good.
It may be difficult in particular cases of sin to distinguish between _materia_ and _forma_, and much more difficult still to separate them, just as at a given moment the heat cannot be separated from the stove.
Yet no more is the stove therefore the heat itself than the being or act to which sin attaches itself is to be identified with sin.
Even in blasphemy, the power needed to utter it and the language it uses are good in themselves; what makes this and all things perverse and sinful is the _deformitas_, the _aberratio a lege divina_.
5º. For God’s law alone is the standard of sin.
What constitutes sin is determined in the final instance not by the church (Rome) or the state (Hobbes), not by the independent moral law (Grotius) or the autonomous self (Kant), not by humanity (Comte) or the social instincts (Darwin), but solely and exclusively by the law of God.
The concept of sin clearly expresses this and is therefore avoided by all who know no higher standard for moral evil than a human one.
God is also the only one who has absolute authority over us and can bind and obligate us in the conscience.
Now he gave many laws for the distinct creatures: laws for sun and moon, heaven and earth, plant and animal, human being and angel; and, as concerns the human being, again distinct laws for his bodily, spiritual, intellectual, aesthetic life, and so forth, as well as laws proper to his moral life.
More precisely, it is this moral law that is the standard of all sin.
The transgression of all other laws—logical, aesthetic, social, political, ecclesiastical, and so forth—is sin only when and insofar as it directly or indirectly includes a transgression of the moral law, of God’s command.
This moral law, which was implanted in the human being at his creation, continues to operate in his conscience after the fall, was proclaimed by God at Sinai, and remains a rule of life also for believers, is the source of our knowledge of sin, Rom. 3:20; 4:14; 5:20; 7:7.
The Christian faith is certainly necessary to learn to know sin rightly, and the nature and magnitude of sin are indeed revealed to us in the gospel by way of contrast, Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 112, 5; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 384 f.; Kaftan, _Wesen der chr. Relig._ 250; but all this does not alter the fact that not the gospel but the law is the _Erkenntnissgrund_ of sin.
In that moral law God comes to us not only as Father with fatherly admonitions and chastisements but, as the categorical imperative testifies in everyone, also as Lawgiver and Judge with commands and punishments.
Although it does not compel like the laws of logic and is not unbreakable like the laws of nature, the moral law surpasses all others in majesty; it addresses itself to the will, breathes in freedom, desires fulfillment from love; and yet it turns to all human beings without distinction, comes to them in all circumstances, extends not only over their words and deeds but also over their condition, knows no concession or indulgence, speaks inexorably and categorically with sovereign authority, and avenges every transgression of it with severe punishment.
It is a _decretum Numinis_, a revelation of God’s will, an expression of his being.
6º. From this character of the moral law it follows that sin can dwell only in a rational creature.
Irrational nature can suffer under the consequences of sin, but sin occurs only in a being endowed with understanding and will.
More precisely, the will is the proper subject, το δεικτικον, of sin.
The moral law is precisely the law for the will of the creature; the moral good is of such a nature that it can be realized only by the will.
What occurs absolutely and decidedly apart from every influence of the will cannot be sin.
In this sense Augustine rightly said, _adeo peccatum voluntarium est malum ut nullo modo sit peccatum, si non sit voluntarium_, _De vera relig._ 14.
But this statement is liable to misunderstanding.
And when the Pelagians used it to prove that sin can never consist in anything other than an act of the will, Augustine later explained it in such a way that sins of ignorance, concupiscence, and original sin were not excluded by it, _Retract._ I 13; and elsewhere he expressly says that the law also forbids the _motus concupiscentiae involuntarios_, _De spir. et lit._, c. 36; _De nupt._ I 17, 23; _C. Jul._ IV, c. 2; VI, c. 8; _De civ._ XIV 10.
In agreement with this, many scholastics likewise taught that sin indeed had its final cause in the will and thus in that sense always had its seat in the will, yet not _in voluntate sicut in subjecto sed sicut in causa_, Thomas, _Sent._ II, dist. 24, qu. 3, art. 2, ad 2; _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 74, art. 2; II 2, qu. 10, art. 2.
Sin can therefore also have its seat in the _sensualitas_, even if it is then only a _peccatum veniale_, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 24, 8; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 74, art. 3; Bonaventure, _Brevil._ III, c. 8; _Sent._ II, dist. 24, pars 2, art. 2, qu. 2.
But various reasons gradually brought about a modification of this doctrine.
_Concupiscentia_ was an unclear concept; it could be understood just as readily _in bonam_ as _in malam partem_, since many natural desires that arise in us of themselves, such as the hungry person’s desire for food, are nevertheless no sin; Augustine therefore sometimes spoke of _concupiscentia_ in such a way that it was no sin and could do no harm, provided only that it was not indulged in conflict with the law, _C. Jul._ VI 5; _De Gen. c. Manich._ II 14; etc.
Further, scholasticism gradually began to distinguish among _motus primo-primi, secundo-primi_, and _plane deliberati_, that is, among thoughts and desires that arise in us before all consent of the will, wholly involuntarily, and are no sin at all; those against which the will indeed resists but by which it is overpowered and which are venial sins; and those to which the will consciously and fully consents and which are mortal sins.
To this was then added that original sin was conceived ever more weakly and regarded as wholly annulled by baptism; what remained, _concupiscentia_, was itself no sin but could only become an occasion for sinning.
Thus Rome also established that the guilt and stain of original sin are wholly removed by baptism, that _concupiscentia_ indeed remains but does not harm those who do not consent to it, and that it can be called sin only _quia ex peccato est et ad peccatum inclinat_, Trent V 5; and further _Cat. Rom._ II 2, 7; Becanus, _Theol. schol._ II 1519, pp. 145–150; Sylvius, _Comm. in totam primam sec. S. Thomae_, 4th ed., II, p. 336 sq.; Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ I 1; V 520; Daelman, _Theol._ II 1759, p. 174 sq.; Dens, _Theol._ I 1828, p. 314 sq.; Kleutgen, _Theol. d. Vorz._ II 644.
The Reformation opposed this and maintained that even unclean thoughts and desires that arise within us before and without our will are sin; by this it did not mean that all desiring in the psychological and philosophical sense was sin, but it did mean that _concupiscentia_ in its scriptural and theological sense made us guilty before God.
And in this it was undoubtedly right.
For sin certainly began with a conscious and free act of the will.
But that first sinful act did not pass us by without a trace; it corrupted the whole of human nature and left behind a condition that conflicts in every respect with the law of God.
Thus, although sin arose through the will, it now in fact exists outside the will and also has its seat in all the other faculties and powers of the human being—in soul and body, in the lower and higher faculties of knowledge and desire, Gen. 6:3; 8:21; Ex. 20:17; Ps. 19:13; 51:7; Jer. 17:9; Matt. 5:28; Mark 7:21; Rom. 7:7, 15–17; 8:7; Gal. 5:7; etc.
_Sine voluntate non potest esse peccatum, quia sine voluntate non potest existere ut sit; sine autem voluntate potest esse, quia sine voluntate potest manere quod existit_, Augustine, in Dorner, _Augustinus_ 129.
Lutheran and Reformed theologians therefore usually opposed the proposition that every _peccatum_ was _voluntarium_.
By this, however, they by no means intended that there could also be sin that occurred wholly and absolutely apart from the faculty of will.
The essential point is only to form a correct conception of the nature and operation of the will.
The will is by no means the whole faculty of desire but only a particular power and activity of it; cf. my _Beginselen der Psychologie_, 1897, p. 166 f.
The will in this narrower sense precedes only actual sins, as James 1:15 speaks of them, but by no means sins of state and involuntary sins.
If the _voluntarium_ in this sense were a necessary element of sin, not only would all unclean thoughts and desires cease to be sin, but under the slogan _tout comprendre serait tout pardonner_ almost all actual sins would also be excusable.
In order to maintain the innocence of _concupiscentia_, Bellarmine therefore already arrived at the declaration _non omne quod repugnat legi peccatum est_: the involuntary movements do indeed conflict with the law but are nevertheless no sins, _De amiss. gr. et st. pecc._ V 10.
But although the _voluntarium_ in this narrow sense does not always help constitute the concept of sin, sins of state and involuntary sins therefore do not occur entirely apart from the will; there is not only a _voluntas antecedens_ but also a _voluntas concomitans, consequens, approbans_; the will later approves in stronger or weaker degree the sinfulness of our nature and of our thoughts and takes pleasure in it.
And even when the will, enlightened by reason, later resists it, or the regenerate person can testify with Paul that he does not will the evil he does, this certainly diminishes the degree of the sin but does not determine the nature of sin.
For sin has its standard in God’s law alone; Paul most certainly calls what he does not will but nevertheless actually does sin and agrees that the law is good.
Even then, however, a sin committed without being willed does not occur wholly apart from the will.
For Paul can certainly say, “It is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells in me,” and thus draw a contrast between his regenerate self and his unregenerate flesh; but Augustine already rightly explained these words as follows: _etsi concupiscentiae non consentio, etsi post concupiscentias meas non eo, tamen adhuc concupisco et utique etiam in ipsa parte ego sum. Non enim ego alius in mente et alius in carne. Sed quid igitur ipse ego? Quia ego in mente, ego in carne. Non enim duae naturae contrariae sed ex utroque unus homo, quia unus deus a quo factus est homo_, _De verbis apost._, serm. 5.
For it is not one being that commits the sin in the flesh and another that nevertheless does not will it.
Rather, both times it is the same human being who, on the one hand, in _concupiscentia_ impurely pursues what is forbidden and yet, on the other hand, in the depth of his will turns away from it and struggles against it.
And therefore, because the human being, even the regenerate person as long as he is in the flesh, always still desires what is forbidden in weaker or stronger degree, although with his will in the narrower sense he opposes it, it may be said that all sin is nevertheless voluntary in its deepest ground.
There is no one and nothing that compels the sinner to serve sin.
Sin has its seat not outside him but within him and has directed his thinking and desiring along its course.
It is his sin insofar as he, through his various faculties and powers, has made it his own.

7º. Thus, finally, sin proves to be an incomprehensible riddle.
We do not know whence it is or what it is.
It exists and has no right to exist.
It exists, and no one explains its origin.
It itself entered the world without a motive and has nevertheless become the motive for all the thinking and acting of human beings.
Considered abstractly, it is nothing but a privation and yet _in concreto_ a power that controls everyone and everything.
It has no independent principle of its own and yet is a principle that devastates the whole creation.
It lives from the good and combats it to the point of destruction.
It is nothing, has nothing, and can do nothing without the beings and powers God has created, and yet organizes them all into revolt against him.
With what belongs to God, it combats all that belongs to God.
It is the will of the weak, finite creature in resistance against the Creator; dependence in enmity against the Independent One and itself pursuing independence; non-subsistent becoming in conflict with the eternally Existing One; the greatest contradiction, tolerated by God in his creation and used by him in the way of right and righteousness as an instrument for his glory.
