## 2.

The many names Holy Scripture uses for sin indicate its dreadful character and manifold development. חַטָּאת names sin as an act that misses its goal and consists in straying from the right way; עָוֶל or עָוֹן designates it as bending, distortion, perversity, as deviation from the right direction; פֶּשַׁע makes it known as transgression of established boundaries, as breach of the covenant relation to God, as apostasy and rebellion; שְׁגָגָה as a wrong deed committed unintentionally, by mistake; רֶשַׁע as godless, lawless, guilty action and conduct. Further, it is characterized by אָשָׁם as guilt, by עָוֶל as vanity, by שָׁוְא as falsehood, by נְבָלָה as folly, by רַע as an evil, _malum_, and so forth. The principal Greek words are ἁμαρτια, ἁμαρτημα, ἀδικια, ἀπειθεια, ἀποστασια, παραβασις, παρακοη, παραπτωμα, ὀφειλημα, ἀνομια, παρανομια. They speak for themselves and describe sin as deviation, injustice, disobedience, transgression, apostasy, lawlessness, guilt; and in addition the sinful power in the human being is designated by σαρξ, ψυχικος, and παλαιος ἀνθρωπος, and in the world by κοσμος. The derivation of the Latin _peccatum_ is uncertain. The Dutch word _zonde_, which is probably related to the Latin _sons_, that is, _nocens_, Müller, _Sünde_ I 114 f., has become a thoroughly religious concept in Christian usage. It designates a transgression not of a human but of a divine law; places the human being in relation not to his fellow human beings, society, and state, but to God, the heavenly Judge; and for that reason is also disliked in many circles and is then preferably replaced by _moral evil_ and similar terms. By far the greater number of these names make sin known as a deviation from and transgression of the law. Scripture always understands sin as ἀνομια, 1 John 3:4; its standard is the law of God. This law also had a different form in different periods. Adam stood in an entirely exceptional case; no one after him can sin in the likeness of his transgression, Rom. 5:14. From Adam to Moses there was no positive law proclaimed by God. The objection can therefore be made that where there is no law, there can also be no transgression, sin, and death, Rom. 5:13; 4:15. In Rom. 5:12 f. Paul gives no other answer than that through Adam’s one transgression sin entered the world as a power, ruled over everyone, and thus death passed to all human beings. Adam’s transgression constituted all as sinners and subjected all to death. This influence of Adam’s transgression does not exclude but includes the fact that all human beings are themselves also personal sinners. For precisely because through Adam’s one transgression sin began to reign in the human world and all consequently were themselves also personal sinners, death also passed to all. Paul says no more than this in Rom. 5:12 f. In that context it was sufficient. But from elsewhere this answer can be elucidated and supplemented. If sin and death existed from Adam to Moses, Rom. 5:13, 14, then a law must also have existed—not a positive law audibly proclaimed by God as in paradise and at Sinai, but nevertheless a law that also bound human beings personally and rendered them guilty. Paul says this clearly too in Rom. 2:12–16. The Gentiles do not have the Mosaic law, but they nevertheless sin and perish ἀνομως because they are a law to themselves and their own conscience accuses them. There is a revelation of God in nature with both religious and ethical content, sufficient to remove every excuse, Rom. 2:18 f.; 1 Cor. 1:21. While God allowed the Gentiles to walk in their own ways, however, he made his laws and ordinances clearly known to Israel. And this law is now for Israel the standard of all moral action. In recent times this has indeed been disputed, Stade, _Gesch. des Volkes Israel_ I, 1887, p. 507 f.; Clemen, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 21 f. It is thought that חטא originally meant only to be or to be placed in the wrong over against someone more powerful, Ex. 5:16; 1 Kings 1:21; 2 Kings 18:14; that afterward it signified conduct contrary to the customary morality of the people, Gen. 19:7 f.; 34:7; Josh. 7:15; Judg. 19:23; 20:6; 2 Sam. 13:12; 21:1–14; and that only gradually did it designate an ethical transgression of God’s law. For through the prophets YHWH first acquired an ethical character. Whereas his wrath had formerly often been kindled without any guilt on the human side, 2 Sam. 16:10, and one could therefore become a sinner without knowing or willing it, Num. 22:34; 1 Sam. 14:43 f., YHWH could now be angry only over sin; from then on sin truly consisted in _aberratio a lege divina_. This opinion is contradicted by the facts. Even if חטא in the first three passages mentioned had the broader meaning of suffering wrong in a juridical sense, without ethical guilt being involved, this would still prove nothing for the proposition that this was the oldest and original concept of sin in Israel. The word properly means _to miss_, Judg. 20:16, and alongside the ethical sense could therefore also have had the broader meaning _to suffer wrong, to be put in the wrong_. But even this is not the case in Ex. 5:16; 1 Kings 1:21; 2 Kings 18:14. In Ex. 5:16 the Israelites simply say to Pharaoh: We receive no straw and nevertheless must make bricks, and when we do not make them, we are nevertheless beaten and receive the blame, וְחָטָאת עַמֶּךָ. In 1 Kings 1:21 Bathsheba says to David: If you do not decide and Adonijah becomes king, then we, Bathsheba and Solomon, will soon be the guilty ones whom Adonijah kills because we do not acknowledge him. And in fact we are nevertheless in the right on the ground of your oath, verse 17. In 2 Kings 18:14 Hezekiah makes a confession of guilt that is forced from him in distress and that he himself later does not acknowledge. It is furthermore a fact that customary morality everywhere applies as a standard of moral action, but this in no way conflicts with God’s law nevertheless being the ultimate _norma_ of good and evil. Josh. 7:15 teaches how the two, transgressing the covenant and committing a folly in Israel, can coincide. Customary morality is in itself no more at odds with God’s law than conscience, which is also a subordinate, subjective _norma_ of moral life. It always is and remains, even in the most Christian society, a _norma normata_; and in Israel too customary morality, for example that of hospitality, was not destroyed by the law but was acknowledged and sanctified by it. Only where it comes into conflict must it yield to God’s law. No one claims that the customs in Israel always in fact agreed with God’s law or were purified by it, any more than the conscience of Christians is already fully conformed to God’s law. Many deeds of the saints in the Old and New Testaments—of Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Rachel, David, Peter, and others—must therefore also be decisively condemned and not excused with the rabbis because they were believers. The essence of sin is ultimately determined not by what is or is not customary in Israel or in the church but by the law of God. And this is the standpoint Holy Scripture consistently adopts. Sin may be great or small; it is always sin solely because it goes against God and his law, Gen. 13:13; 20:6; 39:9; Ex. 10:16; 32:33; 1 Sam. 7:6; 14:33; 2 Sam. 12:13; Ps. 51:6; Isa. 42:24; Jer. 14:7, 20; etc. It always comes down to the Lord’s will and word and law, his ordinances and statutes, his commandments and appointments. To know and serve God is the all-embracing calling of Israel, and by this it is tested by the prophets. And it is likewise in the New Testament. Jesus sets none other than God himself as the example, Matt. 5:48, and judges everything according to his law, Matt. 19:17–19; Mark 10:17–19; Luke 18:18–20. For him this is the content of the law and the prophets, and he fully maintains it without taking anything away from it, Matt. 5:17–19, 23 f.; 6:16 f.; 21:12 f.; 23:3, 23; 24:20, and precisely from this standpoint judges human ordinances, Matt. 5:20 f.; 15:2 f.; Mark 2:23 f.; 7:8, 13; etc. In Matt. 7:12, therefore, he does not establish a new ethical principle but gives nothing other than a practical interpretation of the commandment to love one’s neighbor. The Mosaic law indeed reached its goal and end in Christ, Rom. 10:4; Gal. 3:24, and the believer is free from it and stands in grace, Rom. 6:14; 7:4; 10:4; Gal. 2:19; 3:15 f.; 5:18, but this freedom does not abolish the law but confirms it, Rom. 3:31; its right is fulfilled precisely in the one who walks according to the Spirit, Rom. 8:4. For that Spirit renews us and teaches us to examine and know and do what God’s will is, Rom. 12:2; Eph. 5:10; Phil. 1:10. That will can be known from the Old Testament, Rom. 13:8–10; 15:4; 1 Cor. 1:31; 10:11; 14:34; 2 Cor. 9:9; 10:17; Gal. 5:14; it is revealed in Christ, 1 Cor. 11:1; 2 Cor. 3:18; 8:9; 10:1; Phil. 2:5; 1 Thess. 1:6; 4:2; 1 Thess. 5:21; and also finds an echo in one’s own conscience, 1 Cor. 8:7; 10:25; 2 Cor. 1:12. It is written in the hearts of believers, Heb. 8:10; 10:16. Everywhere in Scripture the essence of sin is ἀνομια, 1 John 3:4, deviation from the will of God revealed to Adam or Moses or Christ or to the church through the Holy Spirit. Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 20–42; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴ 659 f.; Smend, _Altt. Religionsgesch._ 106 f., 119 f., 192 f.; etc.
