## 4.

Another punishment of sin is pollution.
As with the first sin, so with sins in general the outward transgression is the manifestation and proof of inward sin.
Sin consists not only in the deed but also in the thought, desire, inclination, and so forth.
At each of these stages sin is both guilt and pollution at the same time.
Adam did not first become guilty when he ate, but he already incurred guilt when he desired the fruit of the tree.
Guilt and pollution are always the two sides of sin; they go inseparably together; where one is, the other is also.
Sin is guilt because it conflicts with God’s righteousness; it is pollution because it is opposed to his holiness.
Guilt binds us to punishment; pollution defiles us.
Through guilt the objective relation to God is disturbed, and through pollution the subjective communion with God.
Sin is at the same time a rupture of the covenant of works and a destruction of the image of God.
The former entails that God is no longer the human being’s covenant partner; he cannot turn in favor and love toward the guilty, and the sinner no longer stands in that covenant, can no longer love and keep the law, and thus can no longer acquire life by this way.
No flesh can any longer be justified by the works of the law, Ps. 143:2; Rom. 3:20; 2 Cor. 3:6 f.; Gal. 3:2, 10.
Even though the law of the covenant of works, besides obligating us to repair the evil committed by suffering punishment, also continues to obligate us to complete obedience to its commands, Matt. 5:48; 22:37; although God himself keeps his promise and continues to connect life with keeping the law, Lev. 18:5; Matt. 19:17; Luke 10:28; indeed, even though the covenant of works is indissoluble in the sense that in the covenant of grace God lays upon Christ the demand for satisfaction and absolute obedience to the law, it is nevertheless broken in this respect: the human being is incapable of entering into life by this way.
Paradise is closed to him; the tree of life is denied him.
Cf. Moor III 105–108; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ II c. 15; _Ex. v. h. ontw. v. Tol._ X 478–480; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ I c. 9; Mastricht, _Theol._ III 12, 22.
And at the same time the image of God was destroyed by sin.
Rome understands by this that the supernatural gifts were lost while the natural gifts remained unimpaired.
The Lutherans originally taught that because the image of God consisted solely in the moral qualities, the human being had lost it completely and was now like a stick and a block.
But the Reformed maintained that the image of God was indeed lost in the narrower sense and entirely violated and corrupted in the broader sense, yet was not destroyed; Belgic Confession, art. 14.
The image of God is not an external, mechanical addition to the human being but is organically connected with his essence; it is the health of the human being.
The human being who transgresses God’s law does not cease to be human; he retains his body, his soul, his faculties, his powers, his understanding, will, and so forth, but they all stand in the service of sin and operate in a wrong direction.
As deprivation and disruption of the image of God, the pollution of sin belongs by nature to all human beings except Christ; qualitatively there is no distinction.
But there is nevertheless a quantitative difference.
All are indeed turned away from God and walk by nature on the path that leads to destruction.
But not all have advanced equally far on that wrong path and are not equally far removed in the quantitative sense from the kingdom of heaven.
There is an endless variation and transition between the beginning and the highest development of the sinful life.
Human beings, households, lineages, families, classes, estates, and peoples also diverge widely in sin.
There are those who are not far from the kingdom of God; there are also those who drink in sin like water, are given over to unrighteousness, hardened and obdurate, and impervious to every good impression.
And this quantitative difference among human beings does not first arise through their personal deeds and individual actions but is already given with conception and birth.
Original sin, which comes to us from Adam, is indeed the general presupposition and source of all individual, actual sins, but the sinful acts of individuals in turn react upon this innate moral corruption, strengthen it, and develop it in a definite direction.
Just as a sinful deed, repeatedly performed, cultivates in us a sinful inclination, for example, toward drink, sensual pleasure, or lust, so too sinful mores and customs in household, family, lineage, and people can strengthen the innate corruption and develop it in a definite direction.
And that distinctive modification of innate corruption is also frequently transmitted from parents to children, from one generation to another.
The human being is not an isolated individual.
_Das Individuum ist eine Fiktion so gut wie das Atom_; Natorp, _Archiv f. syst. Philos._ III 1, S. 81.
The human being is born from the community and from the outset lives within a definite circle, condition, and age.
Household, family, society, people, climate, manner of life, culture, century, and so forth—all influence the individual human being and modify his innate moral corruption.
Sin is therefore indeed always the same in essence, but in different persons, families, estates, and peoples and in different conditions and ages it repeatedly manifests itself in another manner and another form.
Every human being also bears a physiognomy of his own as a sinner.
Sins in the East bear a different character from those in the West, in the torrid zone a different character from those in a cold climate; in the countryside a different character from those in the cities, in a state of culture a different character from those in a state of barbarism, in the nineteenth century a different character from those in the eighteenth, and so forth.
There are household, lineage, family, national, and social sins.
Statistics has proved that in definite times, conditions, and circles there also reigns a chilling regularity of crimes, for example, homicide, suicide, illegitimate births, and so forth; A. von Oettingen, _Die Moralstatistik in ihrer Bedeutung für eine Socialethik_, 3te Aufl., Erlangen, 1882.
In every sphere we all stand under the influence of wrong customs, sinful examples, the spirit of the age, and public opinion.
Besides original sin properly so called, there is also a _Gesammtschuld_ and _Gesammtthat der Sünde_.
Just as human beings are connected with one another, so too are their sinful inclinations and deeds.
Penetrating the endless riches of all that has been created, sin too forms a kingdom that, animated by one principle of life, organizes itself in many forms and manifestations.
