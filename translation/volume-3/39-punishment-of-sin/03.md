## 3.

The punishments that God has placed upon sin in this life are guilt, pollution, suffering, death, and the dominion of Satan. Guilt is the first and gravest punishment. The Dutch word _schuld_, which is related to _zullen_ (“shall” or “ought”), at first indicates only that someone is the agent of something, just like αἰτια, _causa_. Usually it includes the thought that someone is the cause of something that ought not to be or to happen (“it is his fault”). In this sense guilt presupposes that we were obligated to do or to omit something. We are obligated to keep the whole law, Luke 17:10; Gal. 5:3. And if we have not done so, we stand guilty. Because we are the cause of the transgression of the law, we stand accused (αἰτιασθαι, _accusare_, _reus_); the deed is imputed to us; we must answer for it and are obligated to satisfy the law; we are liable to punishment. Guilt is the obligation resting upon someone on account of transgression of the law to satisfy the law through proportionate penal suffering. Immediately after his transgression it binds the sinner to the law, to its demand for satisfaction and punishment. The human being imagines that through transgression he becomes free from the law, but precisely the opposite occurs: in another way he becomes much more firmly bound to its demand. God, who cannot cease to be God even though he gives the human being freedom to set himself against him, never lets the human being go, and the human being is never released from God. At the same moment that he places himself outside the law—that is, outside love—the law strikes him with its curse and binds him to its punishment. Guilt is _obligatio ad poenam justam sustinendam, subjectio peccatoris ad poenam_, Polanus, _Synt._, p. 338; Moor III 135; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IX, qu. 3; Mastricht, _Theol._ IV 2, 7; Müller, _Sünde_ I 264; Vilmar, _Moral_ I 195; article “Schuld” in Herzog² and Wuttke, _Ethik_ II 97; Scholten, _Vrije wil_, pp. 217 f.; Hoekstra, _Vrijheid_, pp. 303 f. Roman Catholic theology distinguishes between _reatus culpae_ and _poenae_, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 42; Thomas, I 2, qu. 87, art. 6; Trent VI c. 14, can. 30; XIV, _De poenit._, c. 8 and can. 12–15; Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ V 19; _Theol. Wirceb._ VII 27. But this distinction clearly betrays its intention to justify satisfactory punishments for believers here on earth and in purgatory, and it stands in direct conflict with the nature of guilt and punishment. It is indeed true that the sins of believers—that is, of those who have received full forgiveness—_in themselves_ always remain sins and worthy of punishment. Against the Antinomians, who denied this and therefore considered prayer for forgiveness unnecessary for believers, the Reformed always maintained this and made the distinction between _reatus potentialis_ and _reatus actualis_, Moor III 135. But when the guilt of sins is taken away, all satisfaction and punishment thereby also lapse of themselves, for guilt is nothing other than obligation to punishment. God is then no longer a Judge but a Father. He indeed chastises the son whom he loves, 2 Sam. 12:13, 14, but does not punish him, and demands no satisfaction from the one for whom the whole of righteousness has been brought about by Christ. Thus Augustine too says of the baptized person: _omni peccato caret, non omni malo, quod planius ita dicitur, omni reatu omnium malorum caret, non omnibus malis_, _C. Jul._ VI c. 16; cf. Alting, _Theol. el. nova_ XVII 5; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IX, qu. 3; Moor III 136; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 414.

That sin truly brings guilt is established by God’s testimony both in Scripture and in the conscience. In Scripture, sin, guilt, and punishment are concepts so closely connected that the words for sin, such as עָוֹן, חַטָּאת, imperceptibly acquire the meaning of guilt, Gen. 4:13; Ex. 34:7; Lev. 24:15; Num. 9:13, and so forth. The proper word that designates sin as guilt is אָשָׁם, Gen. 26:10; Lev. 4:13; 5:2; Num. 5:7, and so forth, and ὀφειλημα, Matt. 6:12; cf. 5:26; Luke 7:41, 42; 13:4; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴ 684 f. God by no means holds the guilty innocent and pronounces the curse upon everyone who does not continue in the book of the law, Deut. 27:26; Gal. 3:10. Curse—עָלָה, מְאֵרָה, קְלָלָה, καταρα, ἀναθεμα, _maledictum_—is the opposite of blessing—בְּרָכָה, εὐλογια, _benedictio_, Deut. 11:26; 30:19. Just as God’s blessing assigns to someone every kind of well-being and life, so the divine curse is the surrender of someone to ruin, destruction, death, judgment, and Satan. Human beings can only wish blessing and curse upon others, but God’s blessing and cursing is always exhibitive: it sends what it wishes. At first God’s blessing rested upon creation, Gen. 1:22, 28; 2:3, but that blessing was changed into a curse, Gen. 3:17. Later the blessing of God was indeed once more pronounced over the earth and humanity, but it flows from God’s grace. The pagans therefore do not know the concept of divine blessing. All the more do they know of the divine curse. Classical antiquity is ruled by fear of the vengeance of the Erinyes (Moirai, Furies), the goddesses of the curse. In all pagan religions fear (δεισιδαιμονια, _religio_) far outweighs trust in the gods. Religion increasingly passes over into superstition. Everywhere people consider themselves surrounded and ruled by ruin-bringing gods whom they seek in vain to propitiate through sacrifices and torments. A curse of God truly rests upon humanity and the world. It is impossible to explain life and history from the love of God alone. A principle of God’s wrath operates throughout creation that can be denied only by the superficial. There is not fellowship but separation between God and the human being. The covenant is broken; God has a controversy with his creature. All stand guilty and liable to punishment before his face, παντων ἐνοχοι, Matt. 5:21, 22; Mark 3:29; James 2:10. The whole world is ὑποδικος τῳ θεῳ, Rom. 3:19; it stands under God’s judgment and has nothing to answer. See the article “Segen” in Herzog² and ἀναθεμα in Cremer. Subjectively, this is confirmed by God’s testimony in the conscience of every human being. Guilt and consciousness of guilt are not the same. Whoever wishes to ascend from consciousness of guilt to guilt itself cuts off his way to understanding guilt in its proper meaning and gravity. Ignorance can excuse sin to a certain degree, Luke 23:34; Acts 17:30, just as conscious and intentional transgression aggravates sin, Luke 12:47; John 15:22; 9:41. But there are also sins hidden from ourselves and others, Ps. 19:13; and sins of ignorance too are sins, Acts 17:27–29; Rom. 1:19–21, 28; 1 Tim. 1:13–15. Nevertheless objective guilt is reflected more weakly or strongly in the consciousness of the human being. Immediately after the fall, the eyes of Adam and Eve were opened and they perceived that they were naked. Implicit in this is that they also know and acknowledge that they have done evil. Shame is fear of disgrace, an unpleasant, painful feeling concerning something wrong or improper. And with that shame comes fear of God and the desire to hide oneself from him. That is, conscience has awakened in the human being. Before the fall there was, strictly speaking, no conscience in the human being. There was no chasm between what he was and what he knew he ought to be. Being and self-consciousness coincided. But through the fall separation arises. Through God’s grace, the human being still retains the awareness that he ought to be otherwise, that in every part he ought to agree with God’s law. But reality testifies otherwise; he is not what he ought to be. And this testimony is conscience. Conscience is therefore not the consciousness of God’s fellowship with the human being, as Schenkel understood it, article “Gewissen” in Herzog¹ and _Die christl. Dogm. vom Standpunkt des Gewissens aus_, 1858. It is rather the opposite. It is precisely a proof that fellowship with God has been disrupted, that there is a distance and chasm between God and the human being, between his law and our condition. This comes out clearly when conscience acts as accuser, but even where it acquits in a given case—that is, in fact remains silent—that separation from God lies at its foundation, Rom. 2:14, 15. Conscience is the subjective proof of the human being’s fall, the witness of his guilt before the face of God. God does not merely accuse the human being. In conscience the human being condemns himself and takes God’s side, and the side of God’s verdict, against himself. The more finely and scrupulously conscience judges, the more it justifies God’s thought concerning the human being in Scripture. The best and noblest of our race have confirmed God’s truthfulness and pronounced guilty over their own heads. Cf. concerning conscience my _Beginselen der Psychologie_, 1897, pp. 111, 203.
