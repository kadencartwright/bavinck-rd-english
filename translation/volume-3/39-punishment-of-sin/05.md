## 5.

Besides guilt and pollution, according to Holy Scripture suffering is also a punishment for sin.
Through sin the human being lost not only true knowledge, righteousness, and holiness but also dominion and glory.
This is already evident immediately after the fall and is further confirmed throughout Scripture.
God places enmity between the human being and the serpent and thereby in principle deprives him of the dominion originally granted him over the animal world, Gen. 1:26; 2:19.
Further, God pronounces over the woman the punishment of painful pregnancy and of a desire for the man that nevertheless continually torments her.
The man himself receives his share of suffering in the curse pronounced upon the ground, which obligates him to painful labor for his allotted bread.
With this the history of suffering is opened for humanity and for the whole earth.
And all the suffering that strikes human beings here on earth—a short life, a sudden violent death, famine, plague, war, defeat, childlessness, painful losses, deprivation of possessions, impoverishment, crop failure, death of livestock, and so forth—has its cause, though not always in personal sins, for there is also a sparing of the wicked, Gen. 18:26 f., and a punishment for the testing of the righteous, Job 1; Luke 13:21; John 9:1; 11:4; 2 Cor. 12:7, nevertheless in sin in general; without sin there would also be no suffering, Lev. 26:14 f.; Deut. 28:15 f.; Ezek. 4:21; Hos. 2:8 f.; Rev. 18:8; 21:4.
Even the nonrational creation was subjected by God, because of human sin, to ματαιοτης and φθορα, and now groans together as though in labor pains, awaiting the revelation of the glory of God’s children in hope that it too will then be freed from the bondage of corruption, Rom. 8:19–22.
Scripture is not pessimistic in the ordinary sense of the word, but it knows and acknowledges suffering and gives it voice in the most moving laments, Gen. 47:9; Job 3; 6; 7; 9; 14, etc.; Pss. 22; 38; 39; 69; 73; 74; 79; 89; 90, etc.; Ecclesiastes; Lamentations; Matt. 6:34; Rom. 7:24; 8:19 f.; 1 Cor. 15:19, etc.
And such laments continually arise from the whole of humanity.
Teachers of religion, moralists, philosophers, poets, artists, the richly gifted and the deprived—all speak in the same spirit.
The lighthearted and superficial pass over it, but all who are serious in mind have always sensed something of the mysterious connection between life and suffering.
Greek antiquity is no exception to this; its fundamental mood was not so cheerful as was formerly thought but rather bitter and sad.
To live in sorrow is the lot the gods have ordained for mortals; only they themselves are free from cares (Homer).
Apart from God, no one is happy (Euripides).
No mortal remains free from misfortune; no one escapes his fate; the human being is transitory as a shadow, vain as a dream, his happiness is appearance; the best is not to have been born, and if one is born, to die as soon as possible (Sophocles).
Death is perhaps the greatest good (Socrates).
A sudden death is the greatest happiness, a short life the greatest benefit, and the longing for death the deepest wish (Pliny); cf. Pfanner, _Theol. gent._ c. 17, _de morte_; Nägelsbach, _Homer. Theol._, 310 f.; Paulsen, _Ethik_ I 80 f.; Weiss, _Apol. d. Christ._ I³ 475–501; II³ 464–517.
The optimism of the previous century already turned into pessimism in Voltaire’s _Candide_, 1759, and Hegel’s rationalism made way for the philosophy of Schelling, which elevated not reason but the will into the principle of the world; vol. II 204.
Reason may be able to explain what things are and how they are; _that_ they are can be derived only from a will; and this existence of things is what is properly positive, the irrational _Rest_ that ultimately remains at the bottom of all that exists.
_Nach der ewigen That der Selbstoffenbarung ist nämlich in der Welt, wie wir sie jetzt erblicken, alles Regel, Ordnung und Form; aber immer liegt noch im Grunde das Regellose, als könnte es einmal wieder durchbrechen, und nirgends scheint es, als wären Ordnung und Form das Ursprüngliche, sondern als wäre ein anfänglich Regelloses zur Ordnung gebracht worden. Dieses is an den Dingen die unergreifliche Basis der Realität, der nie aufgehende Rest, das, was zich mit der grössten Anstrengung nicht in Verstand auflösen lässt, sondern ewig im Grunde bleibt..... Ohne diess vorausgehende Dunkel giebt es keine Realität der Creatur; Finsternis ist ihr notwendiges Erbtheil..... Alle Geburt ist Geburt aus Dunkel ans Licht_; _Werke_ I 7, S. 359 f.
For this reason Schelling also had an entirely different view of life and the world from Hegel; for the latter everything was rational, but Schelling saw the irrational, the lawless, the darkness, the chaotic lying at the foundation of everything and acknowledged the path of suffering in the world-process.
_Es ist vergebliches Bemühen, aus friedlicher Ineinsbildung verschiedener Kräfte die Mannichfaltigkeit in der Natur zu erklären. Alles was wird, kann nur im Unmuth werden, und wie Angst die Grundempfindung jedes lebenden Geschöpfs, so ist alles was lebt, nur im heftigen Streit empfangen und geboren. Wer möchte glauben, dass die Natur so vielerlei wunderliche Produkte in dieser schrecklichen äussern Verwirrung und chaotischen innern Mischung..... anders als im heftigsten Widerwillen der Kräfte habe erschaffen können? Sind nicht die meisten Produkte der unorganischen Natur offenbar Kinder der Angst, des Schreckens, ja der Verzweiflung?_ _Werke_ I 8, S. 322; cf. 328, 335; II 1, S. 582.
There is even in God _ein Quell der Traurigkeit, die aber nie zur Wirklichkeit kommt, sondern nur zur ewigen Freude der Ueberwindung dient. Daher der Schleier der Schwermuth, der über die ganze Natur ausgebreitet ist, die tiefe unzerstörliche Melancholie alles Lebens; Freude muss Leid haben, Leid in Freude erklärt werden_; I 7, S. 399.
The present world in which we live can be understood only as an _aussergöttliche_ world.
The human being is its cause through his fall, II 3, S. 352 f.; _diese aussergöttliche Welt ist die Welt des göttlichen Unwillens, alle Menschen, Juden und Heiden, sind τεκνα φυσει ὀργης, von Natur Kinder des göttlichen Unwillens_; ib. 372, etc.; cf. von Hartmann, _Ges. Stud. u. Aufs._, 1876, S. 683 f.
All these thoughts recur in systematic connection among the philosophers Schopenhauer and von Hartmann, in poetic form in Rückert, Lenau, Byron, Shelley, George Sand, Alfred de Musset, and Leopardi, and above all also in more recent _fin-de-siècle_ literature; Buddhism, which sees in the will to live, in being itself, the cause of all suffering, is honored as the highest wisdom.
Life is suffering; it swings back and forth between pain and boredom; it is not worth the trouble of living.
The world with its hospitals, infirmaries, surgical torments, prisons, torture chambers, slave pens, battlefields, courts of law, dwellings of misery, and so forth provides fitting material for a description of hell and is itself a hell in which one human being is a devil to another.
If it were a little worse, it could not continue to exist because of its misery.
Everything that exists is therefore worthy of perishing; Schopenhauer, _Die Welt u.s.w._ I⁶ 366 f.; II 657 f.; _Parerga_ II⁵ 303 f.; von Hartmann, _Philos. d. Unb._ II 273–390, etc.; and further literature on pessimism in Ueberweg-Heinze, _Gesch. d. Philos._⁸, 1897, S. 183 f., 481.
There have been many views concerning the origin and purpose of suffering.
Philosophy is almost always intent on casting the blame for it, as for sin, directly or indirectly upon God.
Suffering is then derived from an independent evil principle (Parsiism, Manichaeism), from an originally evil being (Daub), from a dark nature in God (Böhme, Schelling), from the blind, alogical will to be (Buddhism, Schopenhauer, von Hartmann), from God’s self-objectification and _Entäusserung_ (Hegel), from matter (Plato, Aristotle, Philo), from natural necessity (Weisse, Rothe), from the finitude of the creature (Leibniz), from the developmental state of the world (Ulrici), from the sinful consciousness of the human being, which conceives the imperfections of the world that are necessary in themselves as _Uebel_ (Schleiermacher, Lipsius, Ritschl), and so forth.
That suffering, however, is not to be attributed to God in the sense that it was given with creation itself stands established on the ground of the teaching of Holy Scripture, the testimony of our conscience, and the essence of religion, which connects holiness and blessedness most closely.
Nevertheless, an existence and development of the world and humanity without suffering seems to be an impossible thought.
We can form no conception of a history without sin and misery; as soon as we think these away, no event remains in the empty frame of time and space.
Pain and death seem to belong necessarily and by nature to the physical organism; _impassibilitas_ is a property that cannot be conceived in a sensuous creature.
This already applies to human beings, and much more still to plants and animals; modern natural science has shown us everywhere in organic and inorganic nature a _struggle for life_ in which all creatures stand hostile over against one another and are intent upon one another’s death.

And yet, however natural pain and suffering may seem, the following can rightly be brought against the preceding conception.
1º If we could remove from the world and humanity all suffering that without any doubt was caused directly or indirectly by sin, then by far the greater part and the heaviest suffering would disappear at once, and the problem of suffering would be reduced to very small dimensions.
No one can deny that the closest possible connection exists between sin and misery; in everyone’s judgment many sins drag all kinds of dreadful consequences after them, not only spiritual consequences such as fear, regret, shame, disgrace, remorse, and so forth, but also material consequences such as disease, misery, pain, poverty, brutalization, and so forth.
And this is the case not only with carnal sins such as sensual pleasure, drunkenness, lust, and so forth, but also with sins that bear a more spiritual character, such as idolatry, superstition, unbelief, falsehood, greed, vanity, pride, hatred, envy, wrath, and so forth.
They all affect the body to a greater or lesser degree and wreak devastation in it.
A stream of spiritual and physical misery in individual persons, households, families, lineages, peoples, in state, church, society, science, and art, takes its origin in sin.
Take sin away, and by common consent almost no suffering remains.
_Die Welt ist vollkommen überall, wo der Mensch nicht hinkommt mit seiner Qual_ (Schiller).
_Die Hauptquelle der ernstlichsten Uebel, die den Menschen treffen, ist der Mensch selbst, homo homini lupus_; Schopenhauer, _Die Welt_ II⁶ 663.
2º Yet however much truth there may be in Schiller’s word, it is not entirely true.
Even if we think away the suffering that in everyone’s judgment has its cause directly or indirectly in sin, the world would still not become perfect at once.
There would still remain all those disasters that come upon the human being from without and that in everyone’s consciousness do not stand in connection with sin, such as earthquake, storm, thunderstorm, flood, famine, plague, railway accident, and so forth.
Here too, according to the testimony of Scripture, there is indeed a connection with sin, but it is nevertheless different from that in the suffering mentioned above.
Pharisees and Muʿtazilites sought the explanation of this suffering also in personal sins, but Jesus judged otherwise, Luke 13:4; John 9:1.
The disharmony and hostility of nature have their cause not in personal sins but in the sin of humanity.
Because of human sin God struck the earth with the curse and subjected the whole creation to vanity and corruption.
The fallen human being no longer belongs in a paradise; corresponding to his condition is the earth, which stands between heaven and hell.
With knowledge and righteousness the human being also lost dominion and glory; the powers and elements of nature frequently stand hostile over against him; he can now subject them to himself only through painful, strenuous labor.
As a whole, humanity is worthy of no better place than this earth, and for its development, education, and salvation no better place is possible.
We can almost never indicate the purpose for which God sent particular disasters, although they are certainly never absolutely speechless to the one whom they strike.
But it is all the more presumptuous, with Schopenhauer and von Hartmann, to take the scales in hand, weigh against each other the pleasure and pain of the whole world, and then form the judgment that pain far surpasses joy.
For every creature and every human being, even the most accomplished pessimist, in fact prefers painful being to painless nonbeing and strives to persevere in existence.
And humanity as a whole has found in the struggle with nature a stimulus for its energy, material for its labor, and a spur to its development.
By God’s grace the curse upon the earth has been turned into a blessing for humanity.
3º That the whole of nature shares in the fall of the human being is established not only on the ground of Scripture but also follows as a matter of course from the central place the human being occupies in creation and, with a view to present-day natural science, is more than probable.
At times very strange arguments have been advanced concerning the state of the world before the fall and its change through and after the fall, vol. II 548, 549, but the Reformed generally observed a wise sobriety here, ib. 559, 560, and followed the golden rule that the _forma_ of things was indeed changed by sin but the _materia_ remained the same.
For sin is not a substance and can neither increase nor diminish the substance of things, whose author is God alone.
Just as the human being remained essentially human after the fall, so it is with the whole of nature.
No new _species_ were added in the plant or animal kingdom; thorns and thistles were not newly created by a word of God as were grass, plants, and trees on the third day, Gen. 1:11; and creeping and wild animals also already existed before the fall, Gen. 1:24.
But just as in the human being the same faculties and powers, corrupted by sin, began to operate in another direction, so it is also with the whole creation after it was struck by God with the curse.
Left to itself, emancipated from the human being’s dominion and care, burdened with God’s curse, nature gradually became wild and degenerate and produced thorns and thistles, all kinds of vermin and beasts of prey.
The possibility of such degeneration is placed beyond doubt by modern natural science.
Paleontology provides only very few fossils of beasts of prey; the great animals of the earliest age—rhinoceros, mammoth, mastodon—were all herbivorous.
The case of the so-called saurians, which are regarded as lizards, is uncertain; but even whales live in part on plants.
Insects are all herbivorous; there are only some that in an undeveloped state, such as caterpillars, larvae, and ichneumon wasps, feed upon parts of animal organisms, but beetles, ants, bees, butterflies, and others all live on vegetable food.
Among birds there are indeed many that consume animal food, but they can nevertheless also live on plants.
To the beasts of prey belong the three genera of _felis_ (lion, tiger, cat, panther, leopard, and others), _canis_ (dog, wolf, fox), and _ursus_ (bear, and others).
But of all these animals it is at least doubtful whether eating meat belongs to their nature.
In his work, also in this respect very instructive for theologians, _Het varieeren der huisdieren en cultuurplanten_, translated by Dr. H. Hartogh Heys van Zouteveen, Arnhem-Nĳmegen, Cohen, Darwin proved that animals can become accustomed to changed food and cites various examples of this, II 344 f.
And likewise, through becoming wild branches can change into thorns, and through cultivation thorns into branches, II 361.
Nature has now become in many respects an antithesis of the human being, 2 Kings 17:25; Job 5:22, 23; Hos. 2:20; Isa. 11:6 f.; 7:23; 65:20 f.; Ezek. 14:15, 21; Rev. 21; Sir. 39:32 f.; plants and animals have become living images of human sinful inclinations and passions; fear and worship of animals demonstrate the abnormal relation of the human being to the world around him.
But the wondrous power over animals still occurring now and then among human beings, the many changes that according to paleontology have taken place in the plant and animal kingdoms, domestication, and the gradual variability of plants and animals furnish proofs in abundance that a state such as Isaiah portrays for us in chapters 11 and 65 is by no means among the impossibilities.
There is nothing absurd in the thought that, just as with the human being, so also in nature an important change took place as a consequence of God’s judgment.
In any case, Scripture is far more rational than what is sometimes marketed under the name of science; according to Lindenschmitt, human beings were formerly beasts of prey who had to protect themselves from one another by pile dwellings built in the water, while the beasts of prey at that time were highly innocent creatures; in Weiss, _Apol._ II³ 497.
4º Finally, one must not forget that, theologically speaking, creation itself was in a certain sense infralapsarian; Delitzsch, _Genesis_, 1887, S. 67.
The fall was no surprise and no disappointment to God.
He foresaw it, included it in his counsel, and already took it into account at creation.
Creation therefore took place in such a manner that the whole world, in case the human being as its head fell, could become as it is now.
The state of the human being and of the whole earth before the fall was provisional and could not remain as it was.
It was of such a nature that it could be raised to higher glory but also, in the event of the human being’s transgression, could be subjected to vanity and corruption.
Through sin and God’s curse, everywhere the lawless, the chaotic, the demonic emerged from beneath and behind the harmony, confusing and terrifying us.
A _Schleier der Schwermuth_ is spread over the whole creation.
_Πασα ἡ κτισις συνστεναζει και συνωδινει._
