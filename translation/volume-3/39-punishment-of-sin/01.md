# § 39. The Punishment of Sin

## 1.

The punishment of sin cannot yet be treated fully in this section.
God indeed threatened sin beforehand with the whole punishment it deserved, but after sin was committed this punishment was not executed; neither does it come upon anyone completely in this life, nor is it yet fully applied at death; only after the judgment on the last day does it strike the guilty in its entire weight.
In Gen. 2:7 God had expressly said: _on the day_ you eat of it, you will surely die.
This explicit threat, however, was not carried out.
An element intervened that moderated and postponed this punishment.
Adam and Eve continued to live for many years after the fall; Eve even became the mother of the living; a human race proceeded from them that is borne and nourished by the earth.
The beginnings of history continued after the fall, though in a greatly modified form.
All this is due not to God’s justice but, as will become clearer later, to his grace.
This grace enters into operation immediately after the fall.
It assumes the direction of history, not at the expense of but in connection with God’s justice.
All the consequences and punishments that enter after sin therefore immediately bear a double character.
They are not merely consequences and punishments instituted by God’s justice, but all of them without distinction, viewed from another perspective, are also means of grace, proofs of God’s patience and compassion.
Against this it cannot be objected that God would then have spoken untruthfully in Gen. 2:7.
For there only the true, full punishment that sin deserves is announced; sin breaks communion with God, is spiritual death, and deserves death.
That this punishment would be moderated, postponed, even remitted could by the nature of the case not be disclosed before the transgression.
God therefore mentions in Gen. 2:7 only the one great punishment of sin, that is, death.
With death everything at once comes to an end: life, joy, development, labor, and also the possibility of repentance and forgiveness, of restoration of communion with God.
Sin deserves nothing other than the whole, full death.
All the other punishments that in fact entered and were pronounced after the fall—such as shame, fear, hiding from God, the curse upon the serpent, upon the earth, and so forth—are indeed punishments, but at the same time they presuppose that God does not execute his threat immediately and fully, that he still has another plan for humanity and the world, and that he therefore allows them to continue to exist in his patience and grace.
Yet from a definite perspective they are very certainly also punishments and to that extent belong in this discussion.
God’s grace expresses itself in them, but so does his justice.
For according to the general thought of Scripture, punishment has the purpose of restoring God’s right, which has been violated by sin.
In Israel its purpose was to uphold the laws instituted by God and to remove evil from the midst of the people, Deut. 13:5; 17:7, 12; 22:21 f.; 24:7, and further to prevent similar transgressions and cause Israel to walk in the Lord’s statutes with fear, Deut. 13:11; 17:13; 19:20; 21:21.
The purpose of punishment was therefore twofold; it related to the past and the future and had to redress transgressions committed and prevent future ones.
The standard of punishment was not arbitrariness or revenge, Lev. 19:18; Prov. 24:29, but the nature of the crime, Ex. 21:23–25; Lev. 24:19, 20; Deut. 19:21, although the _jus talionis_ was not always applied strictly and literally, both qualitatively and quantitatively, for example, Ex. 21:30 f.
Pharisaism applied this rule, which holds only for the civil government, also in private life, contrary to the Old Testament itself, Lev. 19:18; Deut. 32:35; Prov. 24:29.
Jesus opposes this, Matt. 5:38; he forbids every desire for revenge, whether one seeks it privately or by public means, and enjoins forbearance and mercy.
Retribution, however, is throughout Scripture the objective standard of punishment, not only for the earthly civil government but also for God himself.
He not only threatens punishment upon sin, Gen. 2:7; Deut. 27:15 f.; Ps. 2:9 f.; 5:5; 11:5, 6; 50:21; 94:10; Amos 6:8; Isa. 10:13–23; Jer. 25:12; Matt. 21:42; 23:13; 24:15; Rom. 2:3; John 8:24; Rom. 6:21, 23; Gal. 6:8; 2 Pet. 2:12; 3:7, etc., but he also determines the measure of punishment according to the nature of the crime and repays each according to his work, Ex. 20:5–7; Deut. 7:9, 10; 32:35; Ps. 62:13; Prov. 24:12; Isa. 35:4; Jer. 51:56; Matt. 16:27; Rom. 2:1–13; Heb. 10:30; Rev. 22:12.
