## 6.

This suffering is consummated in that other punishment for sin which is called death. Many are of the opinion that, except in a few passages, Scripture does not regard death as a consequence and punishment of sin. In Gen. 2:17, it is indeed sudden death, entering immediately, that is threatened as punishment for sin, and such death is also always regarded as a calamity and a punishment. But death in itself is rather natural and is given of itself with the material organism of the human being, Gen. 3:19; 18:27; Job 4:19; Pss. 89:48 f.; 90:3; 103:14 f.; 146:4; Eccles. 3:20; 12:7. So the Pelagians, Socinians, and rationalists already judged in the past, and so many theologians still judge today, such as Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 59, addendum; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 414; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 308 f.; Smend, _Altt. Rel._ 504; Marti, _Gesch. d. israel. Rel._ 193; Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 233 f.; Matthes, _Theol. Tijdschr._, 1890, pp. 239–254, and so forth. There is some truth in this conception. In Gen. 2:17 it is indeed not death in general but specifically death entering immediately after the transgression that is threatened against sin. Gen. 3:19 therefore does not narrate the full execution of the threatened punishment but modifies and postpones it. Consequently, the Old Testament regards precisely a sudden death occurring in the prime of life as a punishment for sin, Gen. 6:3; Num. 16:29; 27:3; Ps. 90:7–10, just as capital punishment too is so understood. This view is connected with the economy of the covenant at that time and with the pedagogy of the people of Israel. In the days of the Old Testament God joined a long and happy life to the keeping of his commandments and placed all kinds of punishments on this side of the grave upon transgression. Thus the eye of the righteous was directed especially to the dispensation of one’s lot in this life and limited within the circle of earthly existence; only rarely did it penetrate to the other side of the grave. The distinction between the righteous and the wicked therefore lay not first of all in death as such, for it was the same for all, but in the different dispensation of the lot preceding death. And when that dispensation too frequently differed so little, the distinction between the righteous and the wicked was sought in the different purpose that suffering had for the former and the latter, Deut. 8:2 f.; Hos. 2:5 f.; Isa. 1:25 f.; Jer. 5:3; 9:7; 31:18; Lam. 3:27 f.; Ps. 119:67, 71, 75; Prov. 3:11 f.; Job 1, and so forth. The righteousness of God that visits sins also becomes again for devout Israel a principle of deliverance and salvation, vol. II, pp. 197 f. But from this it by no means follows that death itself was considered natural and necessary. For the view that death is a consequence of the human being’s material organism by no means excludes death’s being the punishment of sin. Only because the human being is dust and taken from the earth can the punishment of sin consist for him in death. Paul likewise teaches that Adam is earthy, from the earth, and that nevertheless death entered the world through sin. And all Christians speak in the same way: the human being is dust, flesh, perishable, and yet his death is a consequence of sin. Further, there is no people that has felt the dreadfulness and unnaturalness of death more deeply than Israel. The human being is dust and must return to dust, but this is not natural. Sin has gradually weakened the life-force of human beings. Enoch and Elijah escaped death. Death conflicts with the human being’s inward nature, Job 14:1–12. Righteousness and life are intimately connected, Lev. 18:5; Deut. 4:1; 30:15; Jer. 21:8; Hab. 2:4; Ezek. 33:16; Ps. 36:10; Prov. 3:2, 18; 4:4, 13, 22; 8:35, and so forth. In the transience of life a judgment of God is revealed, Ps. 90:7–12; cf. Krabbe, _Die Lehre v. d. Sünde u. v. Tode_, 1836; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._ 690 f.; Oehler, _Theol. d. A. T._ § 77. In apocryphal and Jewish literature, Sir. 25:26; 39:29; 40:9; Enoch 69:11; Wis. 1:12 f.; 2:24; 4 Ezra 3:7; _Apoc. Bar._ 23:4; Weber, _System_ 238 f., and in the New Testament, John 8:44; Rom. 1:32; 5:12; 6:23; 1 Cor. 15:22, 55, 56; Heb. 2:14; 1 Pet. 4:6; James 1:15; 5:20; Rev. 20:14; 21:4, and so forth, it is then clearly stated that death is the wages of sin. Least of all, therefore, do those who always exalt the New Testament at the expense of the Old Testament have reason to deny the connection between sin and death. Yet this frequently happens on the ground of historical testimony and statements of natural science. There are many who appear to have overcome all fear of death and die very calmly. Others even put an end to life through suicide. Rousseau claimed that all natural human beings die in peace and without fear. Lessing thought that the ancients regarded death as a brother of sleep and saw nothing terrifying in it. Romanticism frequently rhapsodized sentimentally about death. Yet, although a few in Stoic apathy have come so far that they face death calmly as their lot, fear of death is innate in every living thing. Fundamentally, we do not believe that we must die. Death plays so great a role in human life that philosophy can rightly be called a μελετη θανατου, Schopenhauer, _Die Welt_ I 324 f.; II 528 f. Death has always been the final and greatest enemy for the human being. In the end all recognize in it an unnatural power and flee it as long as possible. Natural science has indeed frequently called death natural and necessary. Lauvergne said, for example, _la mort de l’homme est une conséquence logique et naturelle de son être. Tout prend fin, dura lex sed lex_, in Delitzsch, _Apol._ 132; cf. also H. Wagner, _De dood toegelicht van het standpunt der natuurwet_, Utrecht, 1856. But in speaking this way, science has claimed more than it could justify. Death is a mystery in the fullest sense. According to natural science, matter and force are immortal, and according to Weismann and others, single-celled protozoa are immortal too. Why then is the physical organism composed of such matter and forces and cells mortal? Moreover, according to science, that organism is renewed every seven years in a human being. It is nourished and strengthened from day to day. Why can this process not continue, and why does it already cease after a few decades? Let no one speak of old age and the decline of powers, for these are names that do designate the phenomena but do not explain them and themselves stand in need of explanation. _Weshalb die Zellen sich abnutzen und dahinsiechen, weshalb sie im Alter Veränderungen unterliegen, von denen sie in der Jugend bewahrt bleiben, das ist uns bis jetzt noch verborgen_, H. de Varigny, _Wie stirbt man? Was ist der Tod?_, trans. S. Wiarda, Minden, n.d., p. 52. Many plants and animals, further, exceed the human life span, sometimes by hundreds of years. Why is the life-force in the human being exhausted so quickly, and why does he attain at most seventy or eighty years if he is very strong? In addition, death through the decline of powers almost never occurs, whether among human beings, plants, or animals. Death nearly always enters as a consequence of a disease, a calamity, an accident, and so forth. Even when, on an exceptional occasion, a person supposedly dies from the decline of powers, death nevertheless still bears a pathological character and is caused by some disturbance of particular cells in the body. What, then, is the reason that death removes nearly all human beings before their time and frequently even in the strength of their years, in the bloom of youth, in the first hours of their existence? Science does not know the cause that makes death a necessity, de Varigny, 18. That the human being dies, Professor Pruys van der Hoeven therefore says in his _Studie der Christ. Anthropologie_, is a riddle that can be explained only by the degeneration of his nature, in Delitzsch, _Apol._ 126. _Le mystère de la mort reste aussi intact que celui de la vie_, Delage, _La structure du protoplasme_, and so forth, 1895, pp. 354, 771; and cf. further Sabatier, _Le problème de la mort_, 2nd ed., 1896; Bourdeau, _Le problème de la mort, ses solutions imaginaires et la science positive_, 2nd ed., Paris, 1896; Newman Smyth, _The Place of Death in Evolution_, London: Unwin, 1897.
