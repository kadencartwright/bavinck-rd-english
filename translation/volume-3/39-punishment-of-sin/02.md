## 2.

In accordance with this, Christian theology spoke of a _justitia judicialis_, distinguished into _justitia remunerativa_ and _vindicativa_, vol. II, pp. 199 f., and derived from it the right and essence of punishment. In fact, there is no other final and deepest principle from which punishment can be deduced than the righteousness of God. Every punishment presupposes that the one who pronounces and imposes the punishment is invested with authority over the one who transgressed the law. This authority, this right, cannot have its origin in the human being—neither in his physical nature, for this creates only the right of the strongest, nor in his ethical nature, for all human beings are sinners, and the right to punish never rests in someone’s standing morally higher than the guilty person. All so-called relative theories devised apart from divine righteousness to maintain the right to punish—such as the theories of self-defense, prevention, deterrence, and rehabilitation—are insufficient to vindicate the right to punish and also conflict with the essence of punishment. They are perhaps moments within punishment but cannot be its principle. Punishment is always first of all _poena vindicativa_ and only afterward _poena medicinalis_ and _exemplaris_. Utility creates no right. All the theories named, which build the right to punish upon the interest of the state, society, or offenders, therefore all fall back into the theory of the right of the strongest; right is exchanged for might. If there is no moral and legal order above and independent of the human being, and if its maintenance has not been entrusted to anyone by a higher authority, punishment may be useful to some persons, to a contingent majority, but no right to it exists. Atheism is the undermining and destruction of every right and morality: _ni Dieu ni maître_. There is a right to punish only if a legal order exists that was established by God and must be maintained in his name by the civil authority, even at the cost of the lives of its violators, because it is more precious than any good and far exceeds every creature in value. On first hearing, it indeed seems strange that the legal order, and behind it the moral order, possesses such incomparable value and demands the human being’s property and life for its maintenance and restoration. But this moral world order is no idea of the human being, no state of affairs produced by him. Neither is it an autonomous, self-subsistent power of which no one can say what it is. Rather, it is the revelation and operation of God’s righteousness in this world. It rests in the perfect, holy will of him who sustains and governs all things; whoever attacks it attacks God himself. It therefore stands high above the human being and is worth more than all creatures together. When conflict arises, the human being perishes in it: _fiat justitia, pereat mundus_; _wenn die Gerechtigkeit untergeht, so hat es keinen Werth mehr, dass Menschen auf Erden leben_ (Kant). Punishment was instituted to maintain it. Punishment intends the restoration of right, the maintenance of God’s righteousness. If it does not serve that end, it becomes coercion and superior force. Cf. Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 36; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2, qu. 87; Stöckl, _Lehrb. d. Philos._ III 53, 97; Polanus, _Synt._, p. 340; Hoornbeek, _Theol. pract._ I 412; Moor III 329; Buddeus, _Inst. theol. mor._ I 612–629; Stahl, _Philos. d. Rechts_⁵ II 1, p. 160 f.; 2, p. 681 f.; Ulrici, _Gott u. d. Mensch_ II 391 f.; Dorner, _Gl._ I 286; II 324; Kohler, _Das Wesen der Strafe_, Würzburg, 1888; H. Seuffert, _Was will, was wirkt, was soll die staatliche Strafe?_, Bonn, 1897; Caro, _Problèmes de morale sociale_, 1887, p. 193; Dale, _The Atonement_, 18th ed., 1896, p. 373; Harris, _God the Creator and Lord of All_, Edinburgh, 1897, II 442; F. C. Domela Nieuwenhuis, _Het wezen der straf_ I, Utrecht, 1867; H. L. Lindaal Jacobs, _Beschouwingen over straf en straffen_, Amsterdam, 1884; Kuyper, _Ons Program_, 1879, p. 738.

Punishment always consists in a certain suffering, in deprivation of some good—whether property or freedom, body or life. It is a _malum passionis, quod infligitur ob malum actionis_. Why the legal order specifically demands suffering from the one who violates it, and how it is thereby restored and satisfied, is difficult to say. This is certainly not arbitrariness and chance. Behind that legal order stands the living, true, holy God, who by no means holds the guilty innocent; and in him punishment rests not upon a _dominium absolutum_ in the sense of Duns Scotus, cf. vol. II, pp. 207, 216, but upon the demand of his righteousness. If he did not punish sin, he would grant evil the same rights as good and would deny himself. In order that God remain God, the punishment of sin is necessary. Thus, as soon as the moral or legal order is attacked, it rises up and calls for restoration. It avenges itself in punishment, both inward and outward, presses down the transgressor, and thereby displays its incomparable majesty. God cannot tolerate that the sinner, instead of standing under his law and obeying it, should exalt himself above it and in principle make himself equal to God. And therefore in punishment he maintains his divine sovereignty. Through suffering he presses the sinner down to the place where he belongs and, when the sinner will not accept it voluntarily, through punishment brings him to the compelled acknowledgment that he is the inferior, that he is not God but a creature. Whoever will not listen must feel. Punishment is powerful proof that righteousness alone has the right to exist, that God alone is good and great. In part punishment flows from sin itself. By its nature sin brings separation from God, and therefore darkness, ignorance, error, falsehood, fear, unrest, consciousness of guilt, remorse, misery, and slavery; the service of sin is so unspeakably harsh. But in part punishment is also added to the transgression from without by the competent authority. So it happens in the family, the school, society, and the state, and so it is also with the punishments that God causes to follow sin. Many have denied the latter and in this domain have wished to know only of natural punishments, but in no case of positive punishments: Spinoza, _Eth._ V, prop. 42; many rationalists in the previous century, cf. Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._ 391 and _Dogm._ I 527; Strauss, _Gl._ I 603; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 108 f., 569 f.; _Vrije Wil_ 236; cf. also Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 84; Biedermann, _Dogm._ II 575 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ § 393; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ I² 397; III² 326 f.; Kaftan, _Dogm._ § 36, and so forth. On this position there is really no reward and no punishment. Virtue bears its reward and vice its punishment within itself. A heaven or hell exists nowhere except in the human being’s own mind. _Die Weltgeschichte ist das Weltgericht._ At most there exists a subjective necessity for the guilty human being to conceive his suffering as punishment. Now the poet’s saying is true: _Das Leben ist der Güter grösstes nicht, Der Uebel grösstes ist die Schuld._ _Puniri non est malum, sed fieri poena dignum. Culpam quam poenam plus de ratione mali habere certum est_, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 48, art. 6. Guilt makes suffering punishment. When guilt has been removed from it, the suffering can remain the same and nevertheless change entirely in character. Death as a fact is the same for believers and unbelievers, but for the latter it is a punishment, for the former a passage into eternal life. Therefore one may not infer personal sin from the suffering that befalls someone, Luke 13:4; John 9:1. For in God’s hand, according to his grace and wisdom, suffering serves not only for punishment but also for testing, chastisement, and education. He is so mighty that he can make all things work together for good for those who love him, Rom. 8:28. But with all this, the objective connection established by God between sin and suffering is not broken. Scripture furnishes proofs of it on almost every page. History is indeed not _the_, but it is nevertheless _a_, judgment of the world. It brings facts to our knowledge that compel even the greatest unbeliever to confess: this is the finger of God. Suffering may frequently not have its cause in a personal sin, yet it does have it in the sin of family, people, or humanity. Each person’s own conscience testifies that there exists and must exist a connection between holiness and blessedness, between virtue and happiness. Kant was so convinced of this that from the disharmony existing between the two here on earth he concluded to a hereafter. The devout person does not serve God for the reward, but neither is he indifferent to it, as the advocates of an _amour désintéressé_ claim to be. Paul says that believers would be the most miserable of all human beings if they hoped in Christ only in this life. After the fall God established such a connection between _ethos_ and _physis_, between the moral and natural orders, between fallen humanity and the ruined earth, that together they serve the honor of his name and the coming of his kingdom. The positive punishments that God frequently causes to follow sin already in this life are therefore no arbitrariness but, even though we do not comprehend it, take their ordered place in the history of human sin and guilt. Whoever denies them or ascribes to them merely a subjective existence runs the risk of seeing in sin itself little more than imagination and delusion. History becomes for him a discord that is never resolved into a higher harmony. Cf. Stapfer, _Wederl. Godg._ IV 448 f.; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I 525 f.; Van Voorst, _Over de Goddelijke straffen_, Hague Society, 1798; Van de Wijnpersse, _Over de straffende gerechtigheid_, ibid., 1798; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ I 287 f.; II 224 f.
