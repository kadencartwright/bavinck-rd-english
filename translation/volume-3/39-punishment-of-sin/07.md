## 7.

The final punishment of sin to be discussed here consists in the fact that the world, in the ethical sense, has come under the power of Satan and his angels. Since Satan deceived the human being and brought him to the fall, John 8:44; 2 Cor. 11:3; 1 Tim. 2:14; Rev. 12:9, 14, 15; 20:2, 10, the world is in his power and lies in the evil one, 1 John 5:19. He is the ruler of the world and the god of this age, John 12:31; 16:11; 2 Cor. 4:4. Although the devils after their fall were cast into hell to be kept until the judgment, 2 Pet. 2:4; Jude 6, that judgment has nevertheless not yet struck them, Matt. 8:29; Luke 8:31; James 2:19. They still come into the assembly of the angels, Job 1; Luke 10:18; Rev. 12:7; reside in the air, Eph. 2:2; 6:12; roam about, dwell and work upon this earth, and possess great power here. The pagan world especially is the field of their activity, Acts 16:16; 26:18; Eph. 2:2; 6:12; Col. 1:13; 1 Cor. 10:20; 8:5; Rev. 9:20. But when Christ came to earth, they also took up the struggle against him within the borders of God’s people. Satan ruled and worked in the Jews who were hostile to Christ, John 8:44 f.; tempted Jesus, Matt. 4:1–11; sent unclean spirits; entered Judas, Luke 22:3; John 6:70; 13:2, 27, and so forth. It was then his hour, Luke 22:53; John 14:30. But Christ was the stronger, Luke 11:22; fought against him throughout his life, Luke 4:13; conquered him and cast him out, Luke 10:18; John 14:30; 12:31; 16:11; Col. 2:15; Heb. 2:14; 1 John 3:8; and in principle withdraws the domain of the church from his dominion, Acts 26:18; Col. 1:13; 1 John 2:13; 4:4; Rev. 12:11. Yet he still always works upon the church from without. He roams over the earth, Job 1; tempts believers and opposes them, Luke 22:31; 1 Cor. 12:7; 1 Thess. 2:18; seeks to deceive them and bring them to a fall, 1 Cor. 7:5; 2 Cor. 2:10; 11:3, 13–15; 1 Pet. 5:8; 1 Thess. 3:5; Rev. 12:10, so that the church is called continually to struggle against him, Matt. 6:13; Eph. 6:12 f.; Rom. 16:20; 1 Pet. 5:9; James 4:7; Rev. 12:11. Once, at the end of the days, he rises up one more time in all his power, Matt. 24; Mark 13; Luke 21; 2 Thess. 2:1–12; Rev. 12 f. But then he too will be conquered by Christ and cast with all his angels into the lake of fire, 2 Thess. 2:8; 1 Cor. 15:24; Rev. 20:10; cf. literature above, p. 91.

Belief in evil spirits occurs among all peoples and in all religions. Sometimes fear of evil spirits has almost entirely displaced trust in the good, Roskoff, _Gesch. des Teufels_, 1869, I 20. And in nearly all pagan religions physical evil is ascribed to one or more spiritual beings who are equal in nature and rank to the good gods, ibid., pp. 24–175. Scripture, however, teaches otherwise, and in its early period Christianity in all kinds of ways restrained and opposed Jewish and pagan superstition. Church and state made all kinds of provisions against sorcery, divination, and so forth, and yet until the thirteenth century imposed on those guilty of them nothing other than disciplinary punishments, ibid. I 287, 293. Even when recourse was later had to capital punishment, the real intention was nevertheless the eradication of pagan superstition. It is therefore not permissible simply to hold the Christian religion and church responsible for all the superstition that prevailed even among Christians, especially from the 13th through the 18th century. All kinds of causes contributed to it: the miserable conditions in state and society, the dreadful visitations of famine and plague, the gross ignorance of the people, defective knowledge of nature, the magical and kabbalistic tendency among practitioners of natural science, and so forth, ibid. II 314 f. Nevertheless, the church is not guiltless in this matter. In theory and practice it frequently adopted pagan superstition. This already appears among the church fathers. Just as every human being has a guardian angel, so he also has his demon. Outside Christianity, the devil possesses almost unlimited dominion. Deliverance in Christ is in the first place a liberation from the devil. All physical evil in the world—sickness, crop failure, famine, plague, death—is ascribed to Satan. He is the invisible cause of all idolatry, magic, astrology, heresy, and unbelief; superstition rests upon a demonic reality. When the largely superficial conversions of the peoples of Europe allowed all kinds of pagan teachings and practices to continue beneath Christian varnish, the belief was added in the Middle Ages that the devil could appear in all kinds of forms—tomcat, cat, mouse, goat, pig, werewolf; could create all kinds of vermin; could commit fornication as _incubus_ or _succubus_; could entice human beings into a covenant sealed with blood, bewitch them, enter into them, ride with them through the air, change them into animals; and could also wreak every kind of harm in nature. Rome has always taken the demonic reality of this superstition under its protection, not only in the Middle Ages (bull of Innocent VIII, 1484; _Malleus maleficarum_, 1487), but also after the Reformation down to the present day. In Roman Catholic theology—for example, that of Suárez, Vásquez, Lessius, Liguori, Görres, and others—belief in the power of the devil occupies an extraordinarily broad place; cf. Count Paul von Hoensbroech, _Religion oder Aberglaube_, Berlin: Walther, 1897, p. 56 f. And how much superstition continues in practice among Roman Catholic Christians has recently come sadly to light in the notorious history of Léo Taxil; cf. H. Gerber, _Leo Taxil’s Palladismusroman_, 2 vols., Berlin, 1897. For Rome, all that exists falls apart into a lower, unconsecrated domain and a higher, consecrated domain. In the first Satan rules with almost unlimited power; the second must be protected from his influence and operation through the sign of the cross, holy water, exorcisms, and so forth, De Harbe, _Verklaring der Kath. Geloofs- en zedeleer_, ed. and rev. B. Dankelman, Utrecht, IV, 1888, pp. 598 f. The Roman Catholic Christian sees himself threatened everywhere by the devil and must take all kinds of measures to drive him away, Kolde, _Die kirchl. Bruderschaften_, Erlangen, 1895, p. 47. It is true that at first Protestantism left this Roman Catholic, and fundamentally pagan, superstition almost entirely untouched. Belief in witches and witch trials were defended as strongly by Protestants as by Roman Catholics, and were later opposed by Roman Catholics (Spina, Molitor, Loos, Tanner, von Spee) just as by Protestants (Weyer, 1563; Godelmann, 1562; Reginald Scot, 1584; van Dale, 1685; Bekker, 1691; Thomasius, 1701). Following Luther, the Lutherans ascribed very great power to the devil, derived all evil from him, and maintained exorcism, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 351 f.; Müller, _Symb. Bücher_ 477, 483, 771; cf. Roskoff, op. cit., II 364 f.; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ III 341. Yet the Reformation, especially in its Calvinist branch, certainly did introduce an important modification into belief concerning the devil. Returning to Scripture and remaining with its data, confessing the absolute sovereignty of God, it could not regard Satan and his angels, however powerful, otherwise than as creatures who without God’s will can neither stir nor move. The Reformation was not rationalistic. It maintained that there were evil spirits that could affect human beings, especially their imagination, and could even reduce them to their instruments. But this power of Satan over humanity was always subject to God’s providence. It was first of all ethical in nature and had its origin in sin. Moreover, it was confined within narrow limits. Not Satan but God is the Creator of light and darkness, of good and evil; sickness and death are sent to us by him, Isa. 45:7. Heb. 2:14 says only that Satan has the power of death because through sin he brought death into the world to rule and is therefore a murderer of human beings from the beginning, John 8:44. Our life and the end of our life are not in Satan’s hand but God’s. Luke 13:11, 16 and 2 Cor. 12:7 give no right to ascribe every sickness and ailment to Satan. Further, Satan cannot create or bring forth anything from nothing. Neither as _incubus_ nor as _succubus_ can he beget children. He cannot change human beings into animals, put them to death, or recall them to life. He cannot work immediately upon understanding and will. He cannot change the substance and quality of things, and so forth, Voetius, _Disp._ I 906 sq.; and further Polanus, _Synt._ V c. 12; Zanchi, _Op._ III 167–216; _Synopsis pur. theol._ XII 36 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ VII, qu. 5; Mastricht, _Theol._ III c. 8; Brahe, _Aanm. over de vijf Walch. art._, 1758, pp. 195 f.; Moor II 328; M. Vitringa II 117 sq.

Only through such a faith, which closely adheres to Holy Scripture, can superstition be overcome—a superstition that has struck such deep roots in the human heart and, despite every so-called intellectual development, continually rises again. Rationalism first opposed the operation of evil spirits upon human beings and afterward their existence. The result has been that faith in Scripture was indeed surrendered, but superstition was not eradicated. On the contrary, today it is making its entrance precisely in circles of unbelief under all kinds of forms—magnetism, hypnotism, telepathy, spiritism, astrology, and so forth. Occultism, which has flourished throughout all ages and among all peoples, cf. Kiesewetter, _Gesch. d. Occultismus_, 3 vols., Leipzig, 1891; A. Lehmann, _Aberglaube und Zauberei_, Stuttgart, 1898, counts its adherents by the millions even in the final decades of this century and is glorified even in art and science as the highest wisdom. Over against this new-fashioned superstition too, the foolishness of God is wiser than human beings. For not only can nothing reasonable be brought against the existence of fallen spirits, but the possibility that they can deceive human beings cannot be disputed on any ground. Just as human beings exercise influence upon others by word and deed, example and conduct, so there is nothing absurd in the thought that a seductive power also proceeds from the fallen spirit world upon the imagination, understanding, and will of human beings. It is indeed objected that the human being can then always cast his guilt upon the devil. But this is also the case when someone is deceived by a fellow human being. Further, temptation always takes place in an ethical way and does not remove one’s own guilt. And in circles where the existence of fallen angels is believed, the sense of guilt is ordinarily no weaker than where their existence is denied. Belief in the devil simultaneously maintains the terrible seriousness of sin and the human being’s capacity for deliverance: _entweder ein Teufel ausserhalb der Menschheit oder Tausende von Teufeln in Menschengestalt_, Weiss, _Apol. d. Christ._ II³ 519. Sometimes there is manifested among human beings such a savage, conscious, intentional hatred against God and all that is divine, ibid., pp. 574–587. Children of God—and among them not the weakest and least but the most advanced, those who stand at the front of the struggle, who live most closely in fellowship with God—frequently complain of such dreadful temptations and assaults. They speak of such godless thoughts and inclinations suddenly rising in their hearts that it is a privilege to be permitted to believe in the existence of devils. There are βαθη του σατανα that are sometimes uncovered for a moment in Scripture, in the history of humanity, in the struggle of the church of Christ, and in the experience of believers. And one should bear in mind that sinful power forms a kingdom and proceeds systematically in its opposition to God and his kingdom. Whoever could survey it entirely would without doubt discover a plan of attack and defense in the history of its struggle. In the sinful life of the individual human being, but much more still in that of families, generations, peoples, and humanity throughout all the ages, there is a conscious, methodical opposition to God and all that belongs to God. And the leadership of this struggle rests with the one who in Scripture is called the ruler of this world and the god of this age. So he already acted immediately in the temptation and fall of the first human being. In paganism he organized a power that stands hostile to every true religion, morality, and civilization. When Christ appeared on earth, he concentrated his power against him, not only by personally attacking and relentlessly pursuing him but also by surrounding him on every side with demonic powers and thus trying to obstruct and destroy his work. The δαιμονιζομενοι in the New Testament were not ordinary sick persons, although symptoms of illness, such as deafness, muteness, epilepsy, insanity, and so forth, do occur among them. For they are repeatedly and clearly distinguished from ordinary sick persons, Matt. 4:24; 8:16; 10:1; Mark 1:32; 3:15; Luke 13:32. What is peculiar in the possessed is that another subject speaks from them than they themselves, that this subject recognizes Jesus as the Son of God, stands wholly hostile toward him, and leaves the sufferer only at his command, Mark 1:34; Matt. 8:29, 31; Mark 1:26; 3:11; Luke 4:34, 41; 5:41; 8:2, 30; Acts 16:17; 19:15. Now this _obsessio_, however terrible, is so far from impossible that in hypnotism, in which one person is subjected to the thought and will of another, we see an analogous phenomenon occur; that even today such cases of possession occur; and that whoever considers it impossible would also have to deny every operation of the soul upon the body and of God upon the human being and the world. Satan imitates everything. God reveals himself through theophany (incarnation), prophecy, and miracle. Their demonic caricature is _obsessio_, mantic arts, and magic, to which Scripture therefore frequently ascribes reality, Gen. 41:8; Ex. 7:12, 22; 8:7, 18, 19; Num. 22; Deut. 33:4; Josh. 24:10; 1 Sam. 6:2, 7; 28; 2 Chron. 33:6; Isa. 47:9–12; Jer. 39:13; Nah. 3:4; Dan. 1:20; 2:10; Acts 8:9; 13:6–10; 16:16, and so forth; which it most emphatically condemns and forbids, Ex. 22:18; Lev. 20:27; Deut. 18:10; Jer. 27:9; 2 Chron. 33:6; Mic. 5:11; Gal. 5:20; but which toward the end of the days will once more be manifested by Satan in all their seductive power, 1 Thess. 2:18; 2 Thess. 2:8–11; Rev. 9:1–11; 13:13–15; 19:20.
