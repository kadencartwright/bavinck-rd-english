# § 44. Calling and Regeneration

## 1.

Through Word and Spirit God brings creation, and also re-creation, into being. By speaking, he calls all things forth from nothing: Gen. 1; Ps. 33:6; John 1:3; Heb. 1:3; 11:3. By the word of his omnipotence he raises the fallen world again. He calls Adam, Gen. 3:9; Abram, Gen. 12:1; Isa. 51:2; Israel, Isa. 41:9; 42:6; 43:1; 45:4; 49:1; Hos. 11:1; Jer. 31:3; Ezek. 16:6; and through his servants invites to conversion and life, Deut. 30; 2 Kings 17:13; Isa. 1:16f.; Jer. 3; Ezek. 18; 33; etc.; Rom. 8:28, 29; 2 Cor. 5:20; 1 Thess. 2:12; 5:24; 2 Thess. 2:14; 1 Pet. 2:9; 5:10; etc. Since this calling of God comes to human beings in and through the Son and Christ is the acquirer of salvation, it is also specially ascribed to him. Just as the Father created all things through him and he nevertheless is himself also the creator of all things, so he is himself also the one who calls, Matt. 11:28; Mark 1:15; 2:17; Luke 5:32; 19:10; who sends laborers into his vineyard, Matt. 20:1–7; invites to the wedding feast, Matt. 22:2; gathers the children as a hen her chicks, Matt. 23:37; and appoints apostles and teachers, Matt. 10; 28:19; Luke 10; Eph. 4:11, whose sound has gone out over the whole earth, Rom. 10:18. Although the calling thus proceeds essentially from God or from Christ, he nevertheless makes use in it of human beings, not only in the narrower sense of prophets and apostles, pastors and teachers, but also in general of parents and relatives, instructors and friends. Speech even comes to us from all the works of God’s hands, from the course of history, from the providential leadings and experiences of our life. Everything speaks to the devout of God. Although the calling in the narrower sense occurs through the word of the gospel, it nevertheless may not be separated from the calling that comes to us through nature and history. The covenant of grace is borne by the general covenant of nature. Christ, who is the mediator of the covenant, is the same who as Logos created all things, who as light shines in the darkness and illumines every person coming into the world. God leaves himself without witness to no one, but does good from heaven and fills even the hearts of the Gentiles with food and gladness, Ps. 19:2–4; Matt. 5:45; John 1:5, 9, 10; Rom. 1:19–21; 2:14, 15; Acts 14:16, 17; 17:27. First of all, therefore, a *vocatio realis* must be distinguished, which comes to the human being not so much in clear words as in things (*res*), through nature, history, surroundings, providential leadings, and experiences. It has as its means not the gospel but the law, and through the law in family, society, and state, in religion and morality, in heart and conscience, calls the human being to obedience and submission; cf. *Synopsis pur. theol.* 30, 2, 3; Mastricht, *Theol.* VI 2, 15; Witsius, *Oec. foed.* III 5, 7–15; Marck, *Theol.* 17, 10; Moor III 386, 387. This calling is indeed insufficient for salvation because it knows nothing of Christ and his grace and therefore can lead no one to the Father, John 14:6; Acts 4:12; Rom. 1:16. With it the world in its folly and darkness nevertheless did not know God, John 1:5, 10; Rom. 1:21f.; 1 Cor. 1:21; Eph. 2:12. Yet it is a rich involvement of God with his creature, a witness of the Logos, an operation of the Spirit of God, which is of great significance for humanity. To it is due the fact that humanity, despite sin, has been able to continue to exist, has organized itself into families, societies, and states, that some sense of religion and morality has remained in it, and that it has not sunk into bestiality. All things hold together in Christ, who upholds all things by the word of his power, Col. 1:16; Heb. 1:3. In particular, it also serves to prepare the way, both in the life of peoples and in that of individual persons, for the higher and better calling through the gospel. As Logos, Christ prepares his own work of grace by all kinds of means and ways. Therefore he himself appeared in the fullness of time. When the world through its wisdom did not know God, it pleased God through the folly of preaching to save those who believe, 1 Cor. 1:21. The gospel does not come to all peoples at once but pursues its course through the world along the way of history. It also comes to individual persons at the moment prepared and appointed by God himself in his providence.

However important this *vocatio realis* may be, the *vocatio verbalis* stands higher, coming to human beings not only through the revealed law but specifically also through the gospel. This calling does not nullify the one through nature and history but takes it up into itself and confirms it; only, it far surpasses it. For it is a calling that proceeds not from the Logos but specifically from Christ; that uses as its proper means not so much the law as the gospel; that invites not to obedience to God’s law but to faith in God’s grace; and that is also always accompanied by a certain operation and witness of that Spirit whom Christ poured out as his Spirit in the church, John 16:8–11; Matt. 12:31; Acts 5:3; 7:51; Heb. 6:4. This calling is not universal in the sense of the old Lutherans, who, appealing to Matt. 28:19; John 3:16; Rom. 10:18; Col. 1:23; 1 Tim. 2:4, claimed that in the time of Adam, Noah, and Christ the gospel had in fact been known to all peoples and had again been lost through their own fault: *Form. Conc.* in Müller, 709; Gerhard, *Loc.* VII, ch. 7; Quenstedt, *Theol.* III 465–476; cf. also the Remonstrants and others in M. Vitringa III 167. Yet it may and must be brought to all human beings without distinction. Scripture expressly commands this, Matt. 28:19, and says in addition that many who did not come were nevertheless called, Matt. 22:14; Luke 14:16–18, but rejected the gospel, John 3:36; Acts 13:46; 2 Thess. 1:8, and therefore stand guilty precisely of the dreadful sin of unbelief, Matt. 10:15; 11:22, 24; John 3:36; 16:8, 9; 2 Thess. 1:8; 1 John 5:10. The universalists, however, object against the Reformed that from their standpoint the latter cannot accept such a general calling through the gospel. For according to them Christ died not for all but only for the elect, and the preaching therefore cannot say, “Christ has made satisfaction for you, your sins have been atoned for; only believe,” but for the unconverted can contain only the demand of the law. If we maintain the general offer of grace, it is nevertheless not seriously meant on God’s part and is, moreover, useless and vain; cf., e.g., Arminius, *Op.*, 661 sq.; *Conf.* and *Apol. Conf. Rem.*, ch. 7; Episcopius, *Antidotum*, ch. 9, *Op.* II 2, p. 38; Limborch, *Theol. Chr.* IV 3, 12–18. These objections are undoubtedly of great weight and have elicited various answers from the Reformed. Some came to preach only the law to sinners and to offer the gospel only to those who had already learned to know themselves and felt a need for redemption. Others maintained the general offer of grace and justified it on the ground that Christ’s sacrifice was sufficient for all, or that Christ had also acquired many and varied blessings for those who would not believe in him, or that the gospel was offered to them only on condition of faith and conversion. Still others approached universalism and taught that according to a first, general decree of God Christ had made satisfaction for all, or that for all he had acquired the legal possibility of being saved and brought all into a “salvable state,” or even that the acquisition of salvation was universal and its application particular; cf. above, p. 395f. However much it might appear that the confession of election and particular satisfaction demanded something else, the Reformed nevertheless as a rule maintained the general offer of grace.
