## 3.

Scripture and experience testify, however, that all these operations of the _vocatio externa_ do not always and in everyone lead to sincere faith and salvation. The question thus naturally arises as to the deepest and final cause of this different outcome. In the Christian church three answers were given to this in the main. Some said that this different outcome was due to the human will, whether its power to accept or reject the gospel had come by nature, by the grace of the Logos, by grace in baptism, or by grace in the calling. From this standpoint there is no distinction between _vocatio externa_ and _interna_, between _vocatio sufficiens_ and _efficax_. Inwardly and essentially the calling is always and in everyone the same; it is called _efficax_ only according to the outcome, when someone heeds it. After all that was said earlier about Pelagianism, especially in volume II 355 f., this answer no longer needs an extensive refutation. It is clear that it offers no solution. In practice one may indeed stop at the proximate cause and specifically ascribe unbelief to the human will. One then also speaks truthfully, Deut. 30:19; Josh. 24:15; Isa. 65:12; Matt. 22:2; 23:37; John 7:17; Rom. 9:32, and so forth; the sinful will of the human being is the cause of his unbelief. But already in practice all the devout, at all times and in every school, ascribe their faith and salvation to God’s grace alone, volume II 351. There is nothing that distinguishes them except that grace alone, 1 Cor. 4:7. And therefore this distinction cannot in the final instance lie in the human will. If one nevertheless stops there as the final cause, then at once all the psychological, ethical, historical, and theological objections raised at all times against Pelagianism arise. An incalculable arbitrariness is introduced, sin is weakened, the decision concerning the outcome of world history is placed in human hands, the government of all things is taken away from God, and his grace is nullified. Even if one ascribes the power to choose for or against the gospel to restoration by grace, the matter is not thereby improved. One then introduces a grace that consists solely and exclusively in the restoration of free choice, of which Scripture makes no mention, which properly already presupposes regeneration and yet must first bring it about after a good choice of the will; cf. Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ II 325. From this standpoint one also finds oneself at a loss regarding all those millions of people who have never heard the gospel or who died as little children and therefore were never placed in a position to accept or reject Christ. Human free will therefore cannot be the final cause of faith and unbelief. Another answer to the question posed above was therefore given by Bellarmine. He rejected the doctrine of both Pelagius and Augustine, sought to walk a middle way, and said that the _efficacia_ of the calling depended on whether it came to someone at a suitable time, when his will was disposed to follow it (_congruitas_), _de grat. et lib. arb._ I 12; IV 11; and after him likewise the Congruists, and in the Reformed church Pajon, Kleman, and also Shedd, _Syst. Theol._ II 511–528, who calls salvation _in the highest degree probable_ for everyone who seriously and diligently makes use of the means of grace. But this answer too is unsatisfactory. The _congruitas_ does contain an important truth, one that is overlooked by Methodism and comes into its own in the Reformed doctrine of _gratia praeparans_. But it is wholly insufficient to explain the _efficacia_ of the calling. For in itself it is nothing other than a _suasio moralis_, which by its nature is powerless to create that spiritual life which according to Scripture arises in the human being through regeneration. Further, it presupposes that at one moment the human being is not, and at another is, suited to accept grace, and thus locates sin in circumstances and weakens it in the human being. Furthermore, it places the decision in the human will and thereby calls forth again all the objections mentioned above and raised by Bellarmine himself against Pelagianism. And finally, between calling and conversion it establishes only a connection of _congruitas_, which, being moral in nature, can always be broken by the will and therefore cannot guarantee the _efficacia_ of the calling. Therefore the Augustinians, Thomists, and Reformed sought the cause by which the calling bore fruit in one and not in another in the nature of the calling itself. The Augustinians said that when the calling is effectual a _delectatio victrix_ was added, which granted not only the _posse_ but also the _velle_. The Thomists spoke of a _physica praedeterminatio_ or _actio Dei physica_, which caused the _posse agere_ granted by the _vocatio sufficiens_ to pass over into an _agere_. But the Reformed objected to these terms, especially to the description of God’s act in conversion as physical, and preferred to speak of _vocatio externa_ and _interna_. This distinction already occurs in Augustine, _de praed. sanct._ c. 8; was adopted from him by Calvin, on Rom. 10:16; _Acta Syn. Trid. c. antidoto_ sess. 6, C. R. 35, 480; _Inst._ III 24, 8, and so forth; and then became established in Reformed theology. At first this twofold calling was also designated in other ways, namely, _vocatio materialis_ and _formalis_, _signi_ and _beneplaciti_, _communis_ and _singularis_, _universalis_ and _specialis_, and so forth, Polanus, _Synt._ VI c. 32; but the terms external and internal calling gained the upper hand and gradually displaced the others.

Although this distinction does not occur in Scripture in so many words, it is nevertheless grounded in it. This follows, 1º, from the very fact that all human beings are alike by nature: worthy of condemnation before God, Rom. 3:9–19; 5:12; 9:21; 11:32; dead in sins and trespasses, Eph. 2:2, 3; darkened in understanding, 1 Cor. 2:14; Eph. 4:18; 5:8; unable to see the kingdom of God, John 3:3; slaves of sin, John 8:34; Rom. 6:20; enemies of God, Rom. 8:7; Col. 1:21; unable to submit to the law, Rom. 8:7; unable by themselves to think or do anything good, John 15:5; 2 Cor. 3:5; and, although the gospel is for the human being, nevertheless hostile toward it and despising it as a stumbling block or foolishness, 1 Cor. 1:23; 2:14. The distinction that becomes perceptible after the calling therefore cannot be explained from the human being. God and his grace alone make the distinction, 1 Cor. 4:7. 2º The preaching of the Word by itself is not sufficient, Isa. 6:9, 10; 53:1; Matt. 13:13 f.; Mark 4:12; John 12:38–40, and so forth. Already in the Old Testament, therefore, the Holy Spirit was promised, who would teach all and grant a new heart, Isa. 32:15; Jer. 31:33; 32:39; Ezek. 11:19; 36:26; Joel 2:28; and for this purpose he was poured out on the day of Pentecost: to testify of Christ with and through the apostles, John 15:26, 27; to convict the world of sin, righteousness, and judgment, John 16:8–11; to regenerate, John 3:5 f.; 6:63; 16:13; and to lead to the confession of Jesus as Lord, 1 Cor. 12:3. Therefore, 3º, the work of redemption, both subjectively and objectively, is ascribed entirely to God, not in the general sense in which he brings forth all things through his providence, but specifically also in that narrower sense in which he works regeneration and conversion through special divine power. It is not of the one who wills nor of the one who runs, but of God who shows mercy, Rom. 9:16; the calling is the realization of election, Rom. 8:28; 11:29. It is God who renews the heart and writes his law in it, Ps. 51:12; Jer. 31:33; Ezek. 36:26; who gives enlightened eyes of the understanding, Ps. 119:18; Eph. 1:18; Col. 1:9–11, and opens the heart, Acts 16:14; who causes his Son to be known as the Christ, Matt. 11:25; 16:17; Gal. 1:16, and leads people to him with spiritual power, John 6:44; Col. 1:12, 13; who causes the gospel to be preached not only in words but also in power and in the Holy Spirit, 1 Cor. 2:4; 1 Thess. 1:5, 6, and himself gives the growth, 1 Cor. 3:6–9; who, in one word, works in us both willing and working according to his good pleasure, Phil. 2:13, and for this purpose employs a power like the working of his mighty strength when he raised Christ from the dead and seated him at his right hand, Eph. 1:18–20. 4º The act itself by which God brings about this change in the human being is frequently called regeneration, John 1:13; 3:3 f.; Titus 3:5, and so forth, and its fruit is designated as a new heart, Jer. 31:33; καινη κτισις, 2 Cor. 5:17; θεου ποιημα, κτισθεντες ἐν Χριστῳ Ιησου, Eph. 2:10; το ἐργον του θεου, Rom. 14:20; his οἰκοδομη, 1 Cor. 3:9; Eph. 2:21, and so forth. That is, what is brought about in the human being by grace is far too rich and great to be explained by a _suasio moralis_ of the word of preaching. Finally, 5º, Scripture itself speaks of the calling in two senses. It repeatedly speaks of a calling and invitation that is not followed, Isa. 65:12; Matt. 22:3, 14; 23:37; Mark 16:15, 16, and so forth, and then it can say that God has done everything on his part, Isa. 5:4, and that human beings through their unwillingness did not believe and resisted God’s counsel, the Holy Spirit, and the calling, Matt. 11:20 f.; 23:37; Luke 7:30; Acts 7:51. But it also knows a calling that has God as its author, is the realization of election, and is always effectual, specifically so in Paul, Rom. 4:17; 8:30; 9:11, 24; 1 Cor. 1:9; 7:15 f.; Gal. 1:6, 15; 5:8; Eph. 4:1, 4; 1 Thess. 2:12; 2 Tim. 1:9; cf. also 1 Pet. 1:15; 2:9; 5:10; 2 Pet. 1:3. Believers can therefore be designated simply as κλητοι, Rom. 1:7; 1 Cor. 1:2, 24; κλητοι Χριστου, or κλητοι ἐν κυριῳ, 1 Cor. 7:22—that is, those called by God, who belong to Christ and live in his fellowship. Alongside this, Paul does indeed also know of the gospel being preached to those who reject it, but to them the gospel is foolishness, 1 Cor. 1:18, 23; an aroma from death to death, 2 Cor. 2:15, 16; they do not understand it, 1 Cor. 2:14. It proves itself to be a power of God, 1 Cor. 1:18, 24, to those who are called by God according to his purpose, Rom. 8:28; 9:11; 11:28; Eph. 1:4, 5.
