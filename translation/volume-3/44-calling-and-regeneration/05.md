## 5.

Against this inward calling and regeneration, however, the objection is always raised from the Pelagian side that it introduces a physical compulsion into the spiritual realm, conflicts with the nature of a rational being, makes the human being wholly passive, and undermines moral freedom and responsibility. Pelagianism therefore always seeks to maintain the resistibility of the calling and to make regeneration, conversion, sanctification, perseverance, and so forth depend upon a decision of the will. Only the person who first voluntarily fulfills one condition or another—believes, converts, is disposed to keep God’s commandments, and so forth—is regenerated and justified. It thereby immediately entangles itself in numerous insoluble difficulties. If the human being is by nature capable of fulfilling those conditions, he is so good that regeneration in the sense of Scripture is not needed at all; some moral education and improvement is then more than sufficient. If the human being must first receive through *gratia praeveniens* in baptism or calling the power either to accept or not to accept the gospel, then here too a *gratia irresistibilis* precedes believing, for the *gratia praeparans* is granted to all without their willing or knowing. Regeneration then in fact already takes place before the human decision, for *operari sequitur esse*: the act follows upon the capacity; the will that enables one to accept the gospel is, according to the Gospel of John, already a renewed and reborn will. Only, it is then impossible to see how after all this a free choice of the will is still possible. For through the good power granted to it apart from its own action, the will has already been determined toward the good, and determined toward the good in precisely the same measure as it received power for a good choice. The more one has the will weakened by sin and the more power one has granted to it in *gratia praeveniens*, the more and in that same measure its indifferent freedom also ceases. In addition, it is puzzling why such a free choice of the will is still necessary. If God must already beforehand and irresistibly renew the human being to such an extent that he can choose for the gospel, what purpose does maintaining the indifferent freedom of the will still serve other than only to frustrate God’s grace once again, to make his covenant of grace just as wavering and uncertain as the covenant of works before the fall, and to represent Christ as even more powerless and less loving than Adam? For he has accomplished and acquired everything, but when he wishes to apply it, his power and his love are thwarted by the human will, even though equipped with new powers! Merely to save a semblance of human freedom, God is robbed of his sovereignty, the covenant of grace of its stability, and Christ of his royal power.

And if only something were gained by it; in reality everything is lost by it. Not only is indifferent freedom of the will among adults saved only in appearance, but among children the whole doctrine proves inadequate and cruelty itself. For it is one of two things. The *gratia* granted to children is sufficient for salvation and, if they die young, opens the gate of heaven to them—and then they are saved without their cooperation and without themselves having chosen and decided. Or it is not sufficient—but then all the little children who died young, before they could choose, are also lost; and of the children who grow up, thousands upon thousands again fall away through free choice of the will. Pelagianism in its various forms appears merciful, but it is nothing other than the mercy of the Pharisee who is unconcerned about the tax collectors. In order to save freedom of the will for a few thousand adults, and even then only in appearance, it gives over proportionately millions of little children to damnation. In the end it remains a mystery what objection Pelagianism can have to God’s glorifying his efficacious grace in sinners. If it raised the question why God grants that grace only to many and not to all, it would find a sympathetic ear with everyone. Who has not felt that question arise within, and who has not been moved by it to the depths of the soul? But that question returns from every standpoint and is answered by Pelagius no more than by Augustine; all without distinction must rest in the good pleasure of God. The confessors of God’s sovereignty are in no respect in a less favorable position here than the defenders of free will. For, as was demonstrated above, according to the Reformed the *gratia externa* grants to all who live under the gospel at least as much grace as, according to the Pelagians, is conferred upon them in the so-called *gratia sufficiens* and deemed by them sufficient for a free choice for or against the gospel. The doctrine of inward calling takes away from outward calling not a single blessing and benefit that, according to Pelagianism or semi-Pelagianism—according to Roman Catholics, Lutherans, or Remonstrants—is granted with it by God. From the Reformed standpoint, all who are outwardly called remain objectively in the same condition in which they find themselves according to other confessions. The Reformed claim is only that all this rich grace to and in the human being, if it is not specifically the grace of regeneration, is insufficient to bring the human being to a free, decided acceptance of the gospel. According to the clear doctrine of the Gospel of John, nothing less than regeneration is necessary in order to believe in Christ—an operation of God’s power such as in the raising of Christ, Eph. 1:19, 20. All lesser grace, however rich and glorious, is insufficient. A grace that, without regenerating, nevertheless restores the will so far that it can choose for the gospel is nowhere taught in Scripture and is also a psychological absurdity. Even if this confession of theirs were incorrect (which it is not), it introduces no change whatsoever for the worse into the condition of those who, according to the confessions of all Christians, are finally lost on account of their unbelief. But the adherents of the Reformed religion have in any case this advantage over the advocates of free choice of the will: that God’s counsel will stand, that his covenant of grace does not waver, that Christ is truly and perfectly Savior, that the good will one day infallibly triumph over evil. What serious objection can be raised against this? If without our knowledge we can become partakers of damnation in Adam—a fact no one can deny—why could we not much rather, without our knowledge, be received by God into grace in Christ? There is, after all, no question of compulsion in this grace. To speak strongly for a moment: if this grace did not by its nature exclude compulsion and God actually used compulsion, who at the end would still have the right or even the desire to complain if in this way he had been snatched from eternal destruction and transferred into eternal life? Who would agree with a man who complained that, without respect for his freedom of will, he had been rescued from mortal danger? But it is not so; in inward calling and regeneration there is no compulsion with God, βια οὐ προσεστι τῳ θεῳ, *Ep. ad Diogn.* 7. No devout person, of whatever confession, has spoken of compulsion in the work of grace, even if he was snatched like a brand from the fire. Rather, it would be his desire that God break sin within him with more power and make him partaker of salvation without the long road of struggle. But God does not act thus in grace; all compulsion conflicts with its nature.

For grace is not a physical power. The Thomists spoke of a *physica praedeterminatio*, and Roman Catholics increasingly conceived the image of God as a substantial addition to human nature. But the Reformed did not speak so. Not only did they hold the image of God to be essentially proper to the human being, but they even refused to describe God’s grace in the *vocatio interna* as physical. It was indeed not merely ethical or moral, but neither was it of a physical nature, and was therefore best defined as *supernaturalis* and *divina*. For in no respect does it introduce any new substance into the existing creation, as the Manichaeans and Anabaptists teach, M. Vitringa III 224 sq. It does not do so objectively, insofar as the person and work of Christ may be designated by grace, for Christ, though conceived by the Holy Spirit, assumed his human nature from Mary and did not bring it with him from heaven. But neither does it do so in a subjective sense. Grace never creates; it re-creates. The regeneration it effects means neither in its first nor in its second nor in its third sense that from outside some substance is added to, or introduced into, the human being or the world. They are the same people who were once darkness, Eph. 5:8, dead in sins and trespasses, Eph. 2:1, robbers, greedy persons, and so forth, 1 Cor. 6:11, and who are now washed, sanctified, and justified. The continuity of the self is maintained. And likewise it is the same heaven and the same earth that will be renewed at the end of the days. Christ is not a new, second Creator but the Re-creator, the Reformer of all things. Because sin is not a substance but has affected and ruined the *forma* of things, re-creation cannot consist in the creation of some substance but must consist in a restoration of the *forma*, in reformation. By this alone every thought of physical compulsion in grace is excluded. But there is more. Not only does grace introduce no new substance into creation, it also takes away nothing essential from it, nothing that is essentially proper to it (although indeed “by nature”). Sin does not belong to the essence of creation. And grace takes nothing from us except sin alone. It does not suppress nature but restores it: *non tollit sed restituit et perficit voluntatem*. It removes the darkness from our understanding, the weakness and impotence from our will. It gives back to us what according to our essence, according to the idea, we ought to possess but lost through sin. It re-creates us after the image of the one who created us. It is true that, just as on the one hand it does not consist in implanting a new substance, so on the other hand it is not exhausted by an external, moral improvement of disposition and actions. It is not merely a *reformatio vitae*, as the Socinians described it, M. Vitringa III 225, cf. 227. For that, Scripture speaks of regeneration in far too strong terms. Already the Old Testament designates it as a circumcision of the heart, Deut. 30:6; a creating of a clean heart and renewing of a steadfast spirit, Ps. 51:12; a taking away of the stony heart and giving of a heart of flesh, Jer. 31:33f.; Ezek. 11:19; 36:25. And the New Testament uses the expressions birth from above, John 3:3; from God, 1:13; from the Spirit, 3:5, 6, 8; regeneration, Titus 3:5; and says that those who received this benefit thereby are a new creation, 2 Cor. 5:17; God’s workmanship, Rom. 14:20; Eph. 2:10; new human beings, Eph. 2:15; Col. 3:10; Eph. 4:24; have become one plant with Christ in his death and resurrection, Rom. 6:5; live and walk in Christ or in the Holy Spirit; and so forth. The benefit of regeneration is therefore far too great to be brought about by a *suasio moralis* of the word of preaching; it rather presupposes a divine, almighty power, Eph. 1:19, 20. And it is also too rich to consist only in a change of the acts or expressions of the human faculties. For although sin began with an act, it penetrated into human nature itself. It is not *substantia*, but neither is it *actus* alone. It is an inward corruption of the whole human being—not only of his thoughts and words and deeds but also of his understanding and will, and again not only of these but also of his heart, from which all iniquities proceed, of the inmost part, the core, the root of his being, of the human self itself. And therefore regeneration is such a wholly supernatural, most powerful, hidden, and inexpressible operation of God, by which he penetrates into the inmost parts of the human being, opens the closed heart, softens the hard, circumcises the uncircumcised, infuses new qualities into the will, and makes it living from dead, good from evil, willing from unwilling, and obedient from rebellious, *Can. Dordr.* III 11, 12. Thus they are indeed new qualities which regeneration implants in the human being, but they are nevertheless no others than those that belong to his essence—habits, dispositions, inclinations that were originally comprehended in the image of God and agreed with God’s law, and that free fallen, sinful human nature from its darkness and slavery, from its death and misery. Although, then, the grace that works regeneration is rightly called *irresistibilis*, or better still *insuperabilis*, because it conquers all resistance of the sinful human being; although it is a *gratia operans* that works in the human being without any cooperation of his will; nevertheless it is and remains a *potentissima simul et suavissima operatio*, which does not work in human beings as in sticks and blocks, does not remove the will and its properties or compel it by violence, but makes it spiritually alive, heals, improves, and bends it in a manner that is gracious and at the same time powerful.
