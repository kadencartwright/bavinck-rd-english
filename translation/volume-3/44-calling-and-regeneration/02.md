## 2.

And this is entirely right. For 1º Scripture leaves no doubt that the gospel may and must be preached to all creatures. Whether we can reconcile this with the particular outcome is another question. But Christ’s command is the end of all contradiction. The rule for our conduct is solely the revealed will of God. The outcome of that preaching is fixed and certain, not only according to those who confess predestination but also from the standpoint of those who acknowledge only _praescientia_. God cannot be proved mistaken; for him the result of world history can be no disappointment. And, with reverence be it said, it is not our task but God’s to bring this outcome into agreement with the general offer of salvation. We know only this: that precisely according to God’s decree this outcome is bound to and obtained through all those means and ways prescribed to us. Among these belongs the preaching of the gospel to all creatures. In this we have nothing to do with the decree of election and reprobation. The gospel is proclaimed to human beings not as elect and reprobate but as sinners who all need redemption. Administered by human beings who do not know God’s hidden counsel, the gospel cannot be other than universal in its offer. Just as a net cast into the sea catches good and bad fish, as the sun shines upon both weeds and wheat, and as the sower’s seed falls not only into good soil but also onto rocky and arid places, so the gospel in its administration comes to all human beings without distinction. 2º The preaching of that gospel does not say to each human being, head for head: Christ died in your place; all your sins are atoned for and forgiven. For although universalists think that they can and may say this to every human being without any further qualification, upon some reflection it appears that this is by no means the case even from the standpoint of universalists. For according to them Christ acquired only the possibility of forgiveness and salvation. That forgiveness and salvation become actual only when the human being believes and continues to believe. Thus they too can preach as the content of the gospel only: believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will receive the forgiveness of sins and eternal life. The Reformed now say the same thing. They too offer the gospel in this way to all human beings and can, may, and must do so. The forgiveness of sins and eternal salvation are there, but they become our portion only in the way of faith. Yet here there is still an important difference between universalists and the Reformed, one entirely to the advantage of the latter. For among the former Christ acquired only the possibility of salvation. Whether it actually becomes anyone’s portion depends upon the person himself. Faith is a condition, a work, that first makes possible salvation actual and always leaves it uncertain even to death. But among the Reformed Christ acquired the whole, full, actual salvation. Faith is therefore no work, no condition, no intellectual assent to the proposition “Christ died for you,” but a resting upon Christ himself, a trusting in his sacrifice alone, a living faith, much simpler than it can be among universalists and one that brings salvation much more certainly than they can ever promise from their standpoint. The fault lies solely with the human being, who is always inclined to reverse the order established by God. He wants to be certain of the outcome before he uses the means, and precisely in order to be released from using them. But God wills that we enter upon the way of faith and then infallibly assures us in Christ of complete salvation. 3º Therefore this offer of salvation is also seriously meant and sincere on God’s part. For in that offer he does not say what he will do, whether he will grant faith or not. He has reserved that to himself and has not revealed it to us. He declares only what he wills us to do: that we should humble ourselves and seek our salvation in Christ alone. If it is objected that God nevertheless offers salvation to those to whom he has resolved not to grant faith and salvation, then this is an objection that remains equally forceful on the standpoint of the opponents. For then God also offers salvation to those of whom he certainly, firmly, and infallibly knows that they will not believe. After all, not only according to the Reformed but according to all confessors of Christ the outcome of world history is eternally and immutably fixed; volume II 352. The only difference is that the Reformed have dared to say: that outcome is in agreement with God’s will and intention. What is and occurs, though we do not comprehend it, God must have been able to will while maintaining all his virtues and perfections; otherwise God would no longer be God. History cannot and may not be a party over against God. Further, therefore, 4º this preaching of the gospel is not vain or useless either. If out of ignorance or impotence God actually intended the salvation of all through the universal offer, then it would indeed become useless and vain. For how few are those in whom this purpose is attained! Then it itself includes an antinomy that for its resolution entices one into ever further departure from Scripture. For if God’s will and intention and Christ’s satisfaction are absolutely universal, then the offer of salvation must also be universal without any restriction. And since it plainly is not, one then comes either to contradict history to its face with the old Lutherans and to claim that the gospel was brought to all peoples by the apostles, or with many more recent theologians to assume a preaching of the gospel even on the other side of the grave; cf., for example, W. Schmidt, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1887, pp. 1–44; or, worse still, with rationalism and mysticism to believe that the law of nature or the inner light is sufficient for salvation. The further one extends the calling in this way, contrary to history, the weaker, more powerless, and more vain it becomes. What one apparently gains in quantity and extent is lost in quality and intensity. The conflict between God’s intention and the outcome becomes increasingly greater. 5º Although through the calling salvation thus becomes the portion of only a few, as everyone must acknowledge, the calling nevertheless retains its great value and significance even for those who reject it. For all without distinction it is the proof of God’s infinite love and seals the word that he has no pleasure in the death of the sinner but in the sinner’s turning and living. It proclaims to all that Christ’s sacrifice is sufficient for the atonement of all sins; that no one is lost because it is not rich and powerful enough; that no right of the law, no power of sin, no dominion of Satan stands in the way of its application; for the gracious gift is not like the trespass. It is even frequently the source of all kinds of blessings for those who harden themselves in their unbelief. Enlightenment of the understanding, the heavenly gift, fellowship of the Holy Spirit, enjoyment of the Word of God, and powers of the coming age have sometimes been the portion even of those who later fall away and despise Christ, Heb. 6:4–6. And not only this, but 6º the external calling through law and gospel also attains the purpose God intends by it. What God does is never vain and useless. His Word does not return empty; it does all that pleases him and prospers in everything for which he sends it. But this purpose is not only, and not in the first place, the eternal salvation of human beings but the honor of his own name. In the calling through law and gospel God maintains his right over his creature. The sinner thinks that through sin he becomes free from God and released from his service. But this is not so. God’s right over the human being, even the one who has fallen deepest, is inalienable and inviolable. By renouncing God’s service the human being can become deeply wretched, but he remains a creature and therefore dependent. Through sin he becomes not less but much more dependent, for he ceases to be a son and becomes a servant, a slave, a powerless instrument that God uses according to his will. God never lets the human being go and never relinquishes his rights over him, over his service, over his complete dedication with understanding and will and all his powers. And therefore he calls him through nature and history, through heart and conscience, through blessings and judgments, through law and gospel. Calling in the broadest sense is the proclamation of God’s right over his fallen creature. 7º As such it maintains in the human being and in humanity all those religious and moral sensibilities—dependence, reverence, awe, duty, responsibility, and so forth, without which the human race could not exist. Religion, morality, law, art, science, family, society, and state all have their root and foundation in that calling which proceeds from God to all human beings. Take it away, and a war of all against all arises; one human being becomes a wolf to another. The calling through law and gospel restrains sin, diminishes guilt, and checks the human being’s corruption and misery. It is a _gratia reprimens_. It is proof that God is God and indifferent to nothing, that not only the _Jenseits_ but also the _Diesseits_ has value for him. However inclined the human being may be to hide behind his inability, or with Pelagius and Kant to infer his ability from his duty, even in this he acknowledges that God’s right and our duty remain undiminished and that he himself is without excuse. But finally, 8º the calling is not only a _gratia reprimens_ but also a _gratia praeparans_. Christ came into the world for a _κρισις_, for a fall but also for a rising, Mark 4:12; Luke 2:34; 8:10; John 9:39; 15:22; 2 Cor. 2:16; 1 Pet. 2:7, 8. And the calling through law and gospel also intends by all it grants and effects to prepare the coming of Christ in humanity and in the individual human being. In the Remonstrant sense, _Conf._ and _Apol._ XI 4, such a _gratia praeparans_ was decisively denied by the Reformed; Canons of Dort I, rejection 4; Trigland, _Antapol._ ch. 25 sq.; Mastricht, _Theol._ VI 3, 19, 28; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ III 6, 9. The spiritual life implanted in regeneration is essentially different from the natural and moral life that precedes it. It comes about not through human activity or evolution but through a creative act of God. Some therefore preferred to call the activities preceding regeneration _actus antecedanei_ rather than _actus praeparatorii_. Nevertheless, one can speak of _gratia praeparans_ in a good sense. Over against all Methodist tendencies that fail to acknowledge natural life, it is even of exceptional value. For the confession of preparatory grace does not entail that the human being, by doing _quod in se est_, by diligently attending church, listening earnestly to God’s Word, acknowledging his sin, longing for redemption, and so forth, can according to a _meritum de congruo_ merit the grace of regeneration or make himself receptive and susceptible to it. Rather, it entails that God is the Creator, Preserver, and Ruler of all things and that even far in advance, through the generations, he orders the lives of those whom he will in his time endow with faith. The human being did not arise on the sixth day by evolution from lower creatures but was created by God’s hand; nevertheless, his creation may be said to have been prepared by God’s preceding acts. Christ himself came from above, but his coming was prepared for centuries. Nature and grace are distinct and may not be confused or mingled, but God establishes a connection between the two. Creation, redemption, and sanctification are economically ascribed to Father, Son, and Spirit, but these three are the one true God, and together they accomplish the whole work of redemption. No one can come to Christ unless the Father draws him, and no one receives the Holy Spirit except the one to whom the Son sends him. And therefore there is a _gratia praeparans_. God himself prepares his work of grace in hearts in many ways. He awakens in Zacchaeus the desire to see Jesus, Luke 19:3; works contrition among the crowd who hear Peter, Acts 2:37; causes a Paul to fall to the ground, Acts 9:4; brings the jailer into distress, Acts 16:27; and in this way guides the lives of all his children even before and up to the hour of their regeneration. Even though on their side they do not yet partake of reconciliation and justification, even though they do not yet have regeneration and faith, they are nevertheless already objects of his eternal love, and by his grace he himself leads them onward to that Spirit who alone can regenerate and comfort. According to God’s ordering, therefore, everything stands in connection with their later incorporation into and calling in the church. Conception and birth, family and lineage, people and country, upbringing and education, development of understanding and heart, preservation from dreadful sins and especially from blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, or also abandonment to all kinds of wickedness and unrighteousness, calamities and judgments, blessings and benefits, preaching of law and gospel, contrition and fear of judgment, awakening of the conscience and need for deliverance—it is all a _gratia praeparans_ for regeneration from the Holy Spirit and for the place the believer will later occupy in the church. The way to heaven is indeed one, but God’s leadings are many, both before and on that way, and the grace of the Holy Spirit is rich and free. Jeremiah, John the Baptist, and Timothy are brought in differently than Manasseh or Paul, and each fulfills a distinct task in God’s service. Pietism and Methodism fail to acknowledge those leadings, restrict God’s grace, and wish to convert and form everyone according to one type. But Reformed theology reveres God’s sovereign freedom and admires the riches of his grace.
