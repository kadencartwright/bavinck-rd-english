## 4.

This calling, understood in Paul’s sense, thereby naturally comes to stand in the closest connection with what is elsewhere called regeneration. This is already evident from the fact that Paul, who always understands calling as effectual, almost never speaks of regeneration. He uses this word only once, when in Titus 3:5 he says that God saved us not on the basis of our works but according to his mercy, _δια λουτρου παλιγγενεσιας και ἀνακαινωσεως πνευματος ἁγιου_, that is, by means of the washing of regeneration and renewal effected by the Holy Spirit. Regeneration is connected here with renewal because it is its commencement and principle. Together they are ascribed to the Holy Spirit, and both are conceived as a washing in which the Holy Spirit has immersed believers and from which he has caused them to arise as new human beings. According to Rom. 6, this occurs in baptism as sign and seal of the covenant of grace. For when the elect are called, through faith they immediately receive justification and adoption as children in the juridical sense, Rom. 3:22, 24; 4:5; 5:1; Gal. 3:26; 4:5, and so forth, but at the same time also communion with Christ, Rom. 6:3 f.; glorification according to his image, Rom. 8:29, 30; 1 Cor. 4:15; 2 Cor. 3:18; Gal. 4:19; and thus, since Christ himself is the life-giving Spirit, 1 Cor. 15:45; 2 Cor. 3:17, the Spirit of Christ as the principle of a new life, Gal. 3:2; 4:6, so that believers are now spiritual, new human beings, 1 Cor. 2:15; 2 Cor. 5:17; Gal. 6:15; Eph. 2:15; 4:24; Col. 3:10, walking in the Holy Spirit, being led by him, and being his temples, Rom. 8:14; 1 Cor. 6:19; Gal. 5:25, and so forth. The word regeneration may therefore occur only once in Paul, but materially it nevertheless underlies the new life that the called person receives in communion with Christ. A related representation occurs in Peter, who in I Pet. 1:23 exhorts believers to love one another because they are _ἀναγεγεννημενοι οὐκ ἐκ σπορας φθαρτης ἀλλα ἀφθαρτου, δια λογου ζωντος θεου και μενοντος_; that is, they were born again by God, cf. 1:3, not from perishable seed as in the first birth but from imperishable seed—namely, the Word of God as implanted in the human being by God’s calling, becoming a _λογος ἐμφυτος_, James 1:21, and the principle of a new life—and this by means of the living and abiding Word of God proclaimed to them; cf. v. 25. James likewise says, 1:18, that God of his own will gave birth to us, brought us forth, _ἀπεκυησεν_, through the Word of truth, so that we Christians should be the firstfruits of God’s creatures, that is, the true Israel, God’s special possession, the rightful heirs of the promise. Everywhere here regeneration is conceived as the commencement, principle, and ground of sanctification, inwardly effected by God or his Spirit, and taking place by means of the calling through the Word. But John considers regeneration from another perspective. What is born of flesh is flesh, 3:6, and stands hostile over against God. Those who have been born only in the natural way, 1:13, are of the world, 8:23; 15:19, and belong to the world, 14:17, 19, 22, and so forth; are from below, 8:23; are of the devil, 8:44; do not comprehend the light of the Logos, 1:5; do not receive him, 1:11; love darkness rather than light, 3:19, 20; do not hear, 8:47; do not know God, 8:19; 15:21; do not see the kingdom of God, 3:3; walk in darkness, 12:35; hate the light, 3:20; and are servants of sin, 8:34. Neither _can_ they see the kingdom of God, 3:3; believe, 5:44; 12:39; hear the Word of God, 8:43; come to Christ, 6:44; or receive the Holy Spirit, 14:17. And therefore regeneration is necessary. It is a _γεννηθηναι ἀνωθεν_, that is, from above, 3:3; cf. 3:31; 8:23; 19:11, 23; _ἐκ θεου_, John 1:13; I John 2:29; 3:9, and so forth; from water and Spirit, 3:5, that is, from the Spirit, 3:6, 8, whose cleansing activity has its image in water; cf. Ezek. 36:25–27; Matt. 3:11; mysterious and wonderful, so that no one knows its origin and nature, 3:8. Regeneration in John is therefore not connected with the Word or with calling but rather precedes it. For Christ as Logos was already at work before his incarnation, 1:1–13. He shone as light in the world, but the world did not know him, 1:5, 9, 10. He came to his own, to Israel, and his own did not receive him, 1:11. Yet even then his coming was not entirely fruitless, for as many as received him already received the authority to become children of God. And these were those who had been born of God, 1:12, 13; cf. 1 John 5:1. Before human beings come to Christ and believe in him, they are already of God, 8:47; of the truth, 18:37. They are given by the Father to the Son, 6:37, 39; 17:2, 9. He draws them to Christ, 6:44, and everyone who thus comes to Christ he does not cast out or lose but preserves to eternal life, 6:39; 10:28; 17:12. Christ comes to bring in those who, as given to him by the Father, already are his sheep, 10:27; to cause them to hear and follow his voice and to gather them into one flock, 10:16; 11:52; to grant to those who already in a certain sense are children of God, 11:52, the _ἐξουσια_, the right and authority to become so, to reveal themselves as such, as those born of God, as _τεκνα του θεου_, and especially to show this in brotherly love, that is, in love toward those who have likewise been born of God, 1 John 5:1. From all this it appears that John conceives regeneration not in the first place as an ethical but as a metaphysical act of God, effected immediately by his Spirit in a wonderful manner so that those thus regenerated might precisely believe in Christ and reveal themselves as children of God in brotherly love.

This doctrine of John is wrongly reduced by some to a Gnostic dualism; Scholten, _Het Ev. naar Joh._, 1864, p. 89 f.; Holtzmann, _Neut. Th._ II 468 f. For it is no dualism existing by nature, since all things were originally created through the Logos, 1:3. The world as such is the object of God’s love, 3:16. God gave his Son not to condemn the world but to save it, 3:17; 12:47. By nature, however, all human beings belong to the world, which hates the light because its works are evil, 3:19, 20. Thus it depends upon faith whether someone receives eternal life, 3:15, 16, 36. That faith is an _ἐργον_, 6:29. It is a coming, 5:40; 6:35, 37, 44; 7:37; a receiving, 1:11, 12; 3:11 f.; 5:43; a thirsting and drinking, a hungering and eating, 4:13–15; 6:35, 50 f.; 7:37. It does not bypass understanding and will but has its root in them, 7:17. Unbelief is therefore also ascribed to the unwillingness of the human being, 5:40; 8:44. The human being remains responsible for it, 3:19; 9:41; 12:43; 15:22, 24. And even though believers can no longer be lost, 10:28, 29, they are nevertheless admonished to remain in Christ and in his Word, since otherwise they cannot bear fruit, 15:4–10. Although John therefore traces the opposition between faith and unbelief back to an act of God by which he gives to one what he withholds from another, he by no means intends thereby to nullify the human being’s own activity and responsibility. Rather, he lets both stand alongside each other; Holtzmann II 494 f. Despite every difference in presentation, there is therefore agreement in substance between Paul and John. The difference is that Paul considers regeneration as the ethical beginning of a new, holy life and has it come about through God’s effectual calling, while John understands it from its metaphysical side and explains from it the fact that many hear and receive Jesus’ Word, go to him through faith, and receive eternal life. In a corresponding way, in the first period of the Reformation regeneration was understood in a broader sense as the whole renewal of the human being, while in a later period it was restricted to the infusion of the principle of the new life that preceded the act of faith. If we now add to this the word of Jesus in Matt. 19:28, it appears that Scripture speaks of regeneration in a threefold sense: as the principle of the new life planted in the human being by the Spirit of God before faith; as the moral renewal of the human being through the Word and Spirit of Christ; and finally, as the restoration of the whole world in its original perfection. Thus regeneration encompasses the whole work of re-creation from its very first beginning in the human being to its completion in the new heaven and the new earth. This entire re-creation has its ground in Christ, particularly in his resurrection, 1 Pet. 1:3; its principle in the Word and Spirit of Christ; and its author in Christ himself, who is the reformer of creation.

From this the connection between regeneration and calling also becomes clear. 1º There is no regeneration without calling. Just as in the beginning God created all things by speaking and still upholds all things by the Word of his power, so Christ too brings re-creation about by the power of his Word. 2º The content of this calling is not a word of God in general but specifically the Word of Christ. Through his suffering and death he acquired the right to re-create all things. He realized it in principle in his resurrection. When Christ as Mediator speaks, everything obeys his Word and life comes forth from death. He gives life to whomever he wills, John 5:21, 25, 28 f. The content of calling is therefore the gospel, the glad tidings of Christ. Through it alone all things are renewed. 3º This Word of Christ, leaving aside for the moment whether it is brought outwardly and audibly by human beings or not, must in any event become a _λογος ἐμφυτος_, James 1:21. Only when it is implanted in the human being, in creatures, does the new life come forth from it as _ἐκ σπορας ἀφθαρτου_, 1 Pet. 1:23. 4º Only the Holy Spirit can proclaim the gospel, the Word of Christ, in such a way that it is not merely brought to creatures but implanted in them. Just as creation took place through Word and Spirit, so Christ too accomplishes re-creation through his Word and his Spirit. He himself became the life-giving Spirit, 1 Cor. 15:45; 2 Cor. 3:17, and re-creates all things by speaking through the Spirit, Rom. 8:9 f. 5º This regeneration by no means presupposes conscious life and active willing. As the restoration of creation in Matt. 19:28, it even has the nonrational creature as its object. According to several examples in Scripture, it occurs in children before they have reached years of discretion. Indeed, infant baptism too is built upon the presupposition that children can be received into grace in Christ without their knowledge. According to John’s Gospel, it precedes coming to and believing in Christ. In all these cases there is regeneration solely through inward calling, without outward calling. 6º Even when it temporally coincides with or takes place after outward calling, _δια λογου ζωντος θεου και μενοντος_, 1 Pet. 1:23, it is nevertheless itself always immediate, since it proceeds not from the preached Word but _ἐκ πνευματος_, John 3:5, 6, 8; _ἐκ σπορας ἀφθαρτου_, 1 Pet. 1:23. The Holy Spirit indeed works with and through the preached Word, but his operation is not enclosed within it. Through the inward calling he himself makes it a _λογος ἐμφυτος_ and causes the new life to come forth from it. 7º Since, however, the new life never arises or can arise except from the implanted Word and the Spirit of Christ, as soon as it awakens to consciousness it is bound to the objective, external Word of Christ, which has the same gospel as its content as that inward Word. The rule of doctrine and life is Holy Scripture alone. 8º Inward calling and regeneration are related as seed and plant, 1 Pet. 1:23; as a speaking by the Father and a hearing and learning from him, John 6:45; as drawing and following, 6:44; as giving and receiving, 6:65; as offer and (passive) acceptance of salvation; Amesius, _Med. Theol._ I 26, 7 f.; Voetius, _Disp._ II 452, 463 sq.; Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ 21, 61. Thus, while a speaking of God, a _λογος ἐμφυτος_, always precedes regeneration, neither Scripture nor experience offers us sufficient data to determine whether regeneration in adults as a rule takes place after and through outward calling or precedes it; Voetius, _Disp._ II 461. From the Gospel and epistles of John it can be inferred that it logically precedes outward calling, but not that it also temporally precedes it. And Paul, Peter, and James always keep outward and inward calling in the closest connection.
