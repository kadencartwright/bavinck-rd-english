## 7.

The preparation of salvation in Israel was of an essentially different character.
Nevertheless, the connection between the _foedus gratiae_ and the _foedus naturae_, between Israel and the nations, may not be lost from view.
Israel was taken from among the nations; it was no different and no better than other nations, even surpassed them in obstinacy and rebelliousness, and was chosen solely by grace, Deut. 7:7; 9:13; 32:5 f.; Jer. 5:23; Ezek. 16:3 f.; Amos 9:7; Matt. 11:21 f.
Neither does the distinctive character of Israel’s religion consist in the occurrence within it of all kinds of elements not found in other religions; on the contrary, there is nothing in Israel for which an analogy cannot also be found elsewhere; circumcision, Sabbath, festivals, sacrifice, prayer, priesthood, temple, altar, ceremonies, morals, customs, political and social laws, and so forth also occur among other nations, and conversely institutions that arose only after the fall, such as polygamy, slavery, and so forth, are also found in Israel.
Even theophany, prophecy, and miracle have their analogy and caricature in paganism; cf. Schelling, _Werke_ II 4, S. 119–151.
It is not only the right but also the duty of Old Testament scholarship to bring all this to light.
But because of the kinship and connection, the essential distinction must not be overlooked.
And this lies in grace, the _gratia specialis_, which was unknown to the pagans.
All the religions of the pagans are self-willed and legalistic.
They are all aftereffects and corruptions of the broken covenant of works.
Here the human being always attempts to bring about his own salvation through purification, asceticism, penance, sacrifice, keeping the law, ceremony, and so forth.
But all this is different in Israel.
1º First, YHWH is from the beginning also Elohim for Israel, the Creator of heaven and earth.
Even the passages regarded by modern criticism as the oldest clearly express this faith, Gen. 2:4; Ex. 20:11.
Never was the relation between God and the world conceived in Israel otherwise than as that of Creator and creature; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴, 565.
With this one dogma all paganism is banished in principle; it is the foundation of true, pure religion.
2º This Creator of heaven and earth is also the one who preserves and governs it and who in particular entered voluntarily and graciously into a special relation with Israel.
Israel was taken from among the nations; Abraham was descended from Shem, in whose families the knowledge and service of God were preserved the longest and most purely.
The covenant with Abraham was prepared in history from the days of Adam onward.
Israel’s religion is built upon the broad foundation of humanity’s original religion.
Yet the covenant with Abraham is a new and higher revelation that once again proceeds wholly and solely from God.
He takes the initiative in this covenant.
He establishes it; he chooses Abraham.
Through the wondrous birth of Isaac he shows himself to be both Israel’s Creator and Re-creator.
In Israel’s religion it is not the human being who seeks God but God who seeks the human being.
3º This covenant with the fathers remains even when it later assumes a different form with Israel at Sinai; it is also the foundation and core of the Sinaitic covenant, Ex. 2:24; Deut. 7:8.
The promise was not nullified by the law that came later, Gal. 3:17.
The covenant with Israel was essentially no other than that with Abraham.
Just as God first voluntarily and graciously, apart from any merit of Abraham, gives himself to Abraham as a shield and reward, as a God for him and his seed, and now on that ground also calls Abraham to an upright walk before his face, so too it is God who chooses the people of Israel, saves them from Egypt, gives himself to that people, and now on that ground also obligates Israel as a people to be holy and to be his people.
The covenant at Sinai is and remains in essence a covenant of grace.
“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage,” Ex. 20:2; that is the preamble and foundation of the law, that is the essence of the covenant of grace.
The Lord is Israel’s God before and apart from any worthiness in Israel, and he remains so eternally.
It is an eternal covenant that cannot be destroyed even by any sins and iniquities on Israel’s side, Deut. 4:31; 32:26 f.; Judg. 2:1; Pss. 89:1–5; 105:8; 111:5; Isa. 54:10; Rom. 11:1, 2; 2 Cor. 1:20.
4º The benefits granted by God to Israel in his covenant are the same as those granted to Abraham, but more fully worked out and specified.
Already Gen. 3:15 contains in germ the whole covenant and all the benefits of grace.
God breaks the covenant made by the human being with Satan, places enmity between the two, brings the human being over to his side, and promises him victory over the hostile power.
The one great promise to Abraham is: “I will be your God, and you and your seed will be my people,” Gen. 17:8.
And this is also the principal content of God’s covenant with Israel.
God is Israel’s God and Israel is his people, Ex. 19:6; 29:46, etc.; and therefore Israel receives all kinds of blessings, not only temporal blessings, such as the land of Canaan, fruitfulness in marriage, a long life, prosperity and welfare, and victory over enemies, but also spiritual and eternal blessings, such as God’s dwelling among them, Ex. 29:45; Lev. 26:12; the forgiveness of sins, Ex. 20:6; 34:7; Num. 14:18; Deut. 4:31; Pss. 32; 103, etc.; sonship, Ex. 4:22; 19:5, 6; 20:2; Deut. 14:1; Isa. 63:16; Amos 3:1, 2, etc.; sanctification, Ex. 19:6; Lev. 11:44; 19:2, etc.
5º All these benefits, however, are not set forth as clearly and plainly in the Old Testament as in the New Testament.
They would then not have been understood and comprehended in their spiritual nature.
The natural comes first, afterward the spiritual.
All spiritual and eternal benefits were therefore clothed in sensuous forms under Israel.
The forgiveness of sins is bound to animal sacrifices.
God’s dwelling among Israel is symbolized in the temple on Zion.
Israel’s sonship has in the first place a theocratic meaning, and the expression “people of God” has not only a religious but also a national meaning.
Sanctification in the ethical sense is symbolized in Levitical, ceremonial purity.
For Israelite consciousness, eternal life conceals itself in the forms of a long life on earth.
It would be foolish to think that for this reason those benefits of forgiveness and sanctification, regeneration and eternal life, did not also exist objectively in the days of the Old Testament.
They were most certainly granted even then through Christ, who is eternally the same.
But consciousness and enjoyment of those benefits in the Old Testament were far from as rich as in the days of the New Testament.
And in order that over the course of time the consciousness of the pious might gradually be opened to the riches of God’s benefits, the covenant of grace assumed such a distinctive, symbolic form under Israel.
Religion under Israel connects with the religious forms of sacrifice, altar, temple, priesthood, ceremonies, and so forth occurring among all nations; the spiritual and eternal clothes itself in the garment of the natural and temporal; God himself, who is Elohim, Creator of heaven and earth, descends as YHWH, as God of the covenant, to the creature, enters into history, and assumes human language, affections, and forms, in order thus to communicate himself with all his spiritual blessings to the human being and to prepare his incarnation, his enduring and eternal dwelling in humanity.
We would not even have had words with which to name the spiritual if that spiritual had not first revealed itself in the form of the natural.
As sensuous creatures, we can never express the spiritual except analogically.
If, therefore, the eternal had not been brought within our reach in the temporal, if God had not become human, then his thoughts could not have been communicated to us in our language in Holy Scripture.
God would then have remained eternally unknowable to us, and we would always have had to remain silent concerning him.
6º Just as Abraham, when God binds himself to him, is obligated to walk before his face, so Israel as a people is also admonished by God’s covenant to a new obedience.
The whole law, which the covenant of grace at Sinai takes into its service, intends to cause Israel to walk in the way of the covenant.
It is merely an explication of the one word to Abraham, “Walk before my face and be upright,” and is therefore no more an overthrow of the covenant of grace and an establishment of the covenant of works than was this word spoken to Abraham.
The law of Moses is therefore not opposed to grace but serviceable to it and is repeatedly understood and praised in this way by Israel’s pious.
But considered apart and detached from the covenant of grace, it was indeed a letter that killed, a ministry of condemnation.
Now in the days of the Old Testament the covenant of grace took the law, among other things, into its service also for this purpose: that it might awaken the consciousness of sin, increase the need for salvation, and strengthen the expectation of a still richer revelation of God’s grace.
Paul views the Old Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace especially from this side.
And then he says that Israel, placed as a minor child under the care of the law, had to be led toward Christ, Rom. 10:4; Gal. 2:23 f.; 4:1 f., and that in connection with this sin would be increased, the worthlessness of works for justification and the necessity of faith would be recognized, Rom. 4:15; 5:20; 7:7 f.; 8:3; Gal. 3:19.
Thus on the one hand the law stood in the service of the covenant of grace; it was not a disguised covenant of works and did not intend that the human being should obtain justification through his own works.
But on the other hand, it nevertheless intended to prepare, in the fullness of time, a higher, better dispensation of that same covenant of grace in whose service it stood.
The impossibility of keeping the Sinaitic covenant and satisfying the demands of the law made another, better dispensation of the covenant of grace necessary.
The eternal covenant of grace is impelled by the imperfection of the temporal form it assumed under Israel toward a higher revelation.
Sin increased in order that grace might abound all the more.
Christ could not become human immediately after the fall, and grace could not immediately reveal itself in all its riches.
Preparation and education were necessary.
_Non decuit a principio humani generis ante peccatum Deum incarnari, cum non detur medicina nisi infirmis; nec statim post peccatum, ut homo per peccatum humiliatus recognosceret se liberatore indigere: sed in plenitudine temporis quod ab aeterno disposuit_; Thomas, _S. Th._ III qu. 1 art. 5.
The necessity of this education and preparation lies not objectively in God, as though he were mutable; not in Christ, as though he were not the same yesterday, today, and forever; not in the spiritual benefits, as though they did not exist and could not formerly have been communicated by God.
Rather, it lies subjectively in the condition of the human race, which had to be saved precisely as a race and therefore had gradually to be prepared and educated for salvation in Christ; Calvin, _Inst._ II 11, 13, 14.
Christ is therefore truly the turning point of the ages, the cross the center of world history.
First everything is led toward the cross; afterward everything is derived from the cross.
7º When the fullness of time has come and Christ has completed his work on earth, the covenant of grace passes over into a higher dispensation.
The believers in Israel knew that the Sinaitic dispensation was only temporary and therefore looked longingly toward the day of the New Covenant.
And Jesus and the apostles, who read the Old Testament in this way, saw in it the same covenant of grace with the same benefits that now came fully to light.
The Old and New Testaments are in essence one covenant, Luke 1:68–79; Acts 2:39; 3:25; they have one gospel, Rom. 1:2; Gal. 3:8; Heb. 4:2, 6; 2 Tim. 3:15; one Mediator, namely, Christ, who also existed in the days of the Old Testament, John 1:1, 14; 8:58; Rom. 8:3; 2 Cor. 8:9; Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:6, etc., exercised his mediatorial office, John 8:56; 1 Cor. 10:4; 1 Pet. 1:11; 3:19; Heb. 13:8, and is the only Mediator for all human beings and in all times, John 14:6; Acts 4:12; 1 Tim. 2:5; one faith as the way to salvation, Matt. 13:17; Acts 10:43; 15:11; Rom. 4:11; Gal. 3:6, 7; Heb. 11; the same promises and benefits of communion with God, 2 Cor. 6:16; Rev. 21:3, forgiveness and justification, Acts 10:43; Rom. 4:22, eternal life, Matt. 22:32; Gal. 3:18; Heb. 9:15; 11:10, etc.
The way on which believers walked in the Old and New Testaments was the same, but the light by which they walked differed; Calvin on Gal. 3:23.
Therefore, along with unity there is also distinction.
As different dispensations of the same covenant of grace, the Old and New Testaments stand over against each other as promise and fulfillment, Acts 13:32; Rom. 1:2; as shadow and body, Col. 2:17; as the letter that kills and the Spirit that makes alive, 2 Cor. 3:6 f.; as bondage and freedom, Rom. 8:15; Gal. 4:1 f., 22 f.; Col. 2:20; Heb. 12:18 f.; as particular and universal, John 4:21; Acts 10:35; 14:16; Gal. 4:4, 5; 6:15; Eph. 2:14; 3:6.
8º What is new in the New Testament is therefore the disappearance of the forms—nonarbitrary, but nevertheless temporary, sensuous, and national—under which the one and same grace was revealed under the old dispensation.
In a certain sense the new dispensation already begins when, with the birth of John the Baptist and of Jesus, the Old Testament promises begin to be fulfilled.
Yet the old dispensation remained in force until the death of Christ.
Jesus himself was an Israelite, fulfilled all righteousness, and still addressed himself only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.
But at his death the veil is torn, Matt. 27:51; the testator dies, Heb. 9:15–17; the New Testament is founded in his blood, Matt. 26:28; the handwriting of the law that was against us is erased, Col. 2:14; the dividing wall of separation is broken down, Eph. 2:14, etc.
In fact the old dispensation may continue to have an effect for a long time, but by right it has been abolished.
Or better still, nothing has been abolished, but the fruit is ripe and breaks through the husk; the church, which was carried as an infant in Israel’s womb, is born into an independent life of its own and receives in the Holy Spirit its own immanent principle of life; the sun of righteousness has risen to the zenith of heaven and shines over all nations; law and prophets are fulfilled and in Christ as their end and goal have reached their destination.
The law was given through Moses, but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ, John 1:14; he is the truth, John 14:6, the body, Col. 2:17, in whom all promises and shadows are realized.
In him everything is fulfilled.
He is the true prophet, priest, and king; the genuine servant of the Lord, the true ἱλαστηριον, Rom. 3:25; the true sacrifice, Eph. 5:2; the true circumcision, Col. 2:11; the true Passover, 1 Cor. 5:7; and therefore his church is the true seed of Abraham, the true Israel, the true people of God, Matt. 1:21; Luke 1:17; Rom. 9:25, 26; 2 Cor. 6:16–18; Gal. 3:29; Titus 2:14; Heb. 8:8–10; 1 Pet. 2:9; Rev. 21:3; the true temple of God, 1 Cor. 3:16; 2 Cor. 6:16; Eph. 2:22; 2 Thess. 2:4; Heb. 8:2, 9; the true Zion and Jerusalem, Gal. 4:26; Heb. 12:22; and its spiritual sacrifice is the true religion, John 4:24; Rom. 12:1; Phil. 3:3; 4:18, etc.
Nothing of the Old is lost in the New Testament; rather, everything is fulfilled, brought to term, reaches its maturity, and now brings the eternal kernel forth from its temporal covering.
It is not the case that under Israel there were a real temple and sacrifice and priesthood and so forth, and that all these have now disappeared.
No, rather the reverse: under Israel there was only the shadow of all this, but now there is the body itself.
The things that are seen are temporal, but the things that are not seen are eternal.
