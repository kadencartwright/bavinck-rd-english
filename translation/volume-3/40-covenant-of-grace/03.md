## 3.

From Scripture this covenant idea passed of itself into Christian theology. It occurs repeatedly among the church fathers, especially in their commentaries. But the doctrine of the covenant acquired dogmatic significance because the Christian religion had to be understood in its connection with, and at the same time its distinction from, the Israelite religion. In this matter two tendencies stood opposed to each other from the days of the apostles onward. Judaism, which appeared in nearly all the churches against Paul’s person and teaching, demanded that the Mosaic law, particularly circumcision, be kept also by Christians from among the Gentiles, Acts 15; Rom. 16:17 f.; 1 Cor. 7:18; Gal. 5:4; 6:13; Phil. 1:15 f.; 3:2 f.; Col. 2:16, 21; Titus 1:10, 14; 2:9; 1 Tim. 1:7 f. After the breach between Jews and Christians was completed, Jerusalem destroyed, the Christian congregation fled to Pella, a Gentile-Christian congregation established in Jerusalem in 135, and so forth, it increasingly became a sect (Nazarenes, Ebionites) that became Jewish in its doctrine of God and denied the deity of Christ. On the other side stood Gnosticism, which likewise already had its origins in the time of the apostles (Simon Magus, Cerinthus), but spread especially in the 2nd and 3rd centuries from East to West and in the person of Marcion directed its fierce attacks against the Old Testament. Proceeding from an eternal dualism between God and matter, it conceived the transition between the two as mediated by all kinds of aeons and ascribed the creation of the world to a lower god, the demiurge or God of the Jews. He was not the highest, true God, but a god of lower rank, the God of law, righteousness, and vengeance. The Old Testament stood far lower than the New Testament, for the God of the Old Testament is jealous, vengeful, creator of evil. He makes obstinate and hardens, commands all kinds of sins such as robbing the Egyptians and killing the Canaanites. He gave laws that were not good and entered into covenant with men such as Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, David, and others, who made themselves guilty of all kinds of sins of deception, falsehood, and so forth. It is this God of the Jews, the demiurge, who even had Christ crucified but was himself consigned to hell by Christ. In Christ an entirely different God has revealed himself, the God of grace and love. The whole legal standpoint has therefore passed away. The Christian is free from the law and from the entire Old Testament. The only one who immediately understood this correctly is Paul; he is the only true apostle.

Over against both these parties, the Christian church and theology saw themselves called to a twofold task. Against Gnosticism they had to maintain the unity, and against Judaism the distinction, of the two testaments; cf. especially Irenaeus, _Adv. haer._ IV; Tertullian, _Adv. Marc._ II and III. To this end they employed Paul’s doctrine concerning the παλαια and καινη διαθηκη, 2 Cor. 3, Latin _testamentum_ or _instrumentum_, which was then applied first to the economies and afterward to the Scriptures. Both are one in origin and content. God or the Logos is the author of both, and in both one faith, one covenant, one way of salvation is presented. There is a distinction only in form, and this had to be so. For God is indeed one, but human beings differ and must therefore also be educated differently. God reveals his grace successively, ever more richly and fully. In the days of the Old Testament there was servitude, but now freedom; then image, now truth; then twilight, now light; then grace for one people, now for all; then the Messiah promised, now come, and so forth. This distinction, however, does not detract from the essential unity, for the law was given specially to Israel and given only because of its obstinacy, Amos 5:17; Jer. 7:21; Ezek. 20:19; it pointed typologically forward to Christ, educated Israel for Christ, and in Christ reached its goal and end. And therefore: _N. T. in Vetere latet, V. T. in Novo patet. Vetus Novum praeoccupat et Vetus interpretatum est Novum, Vetus T. est occultatio Novi, et Novum est revelatio Veteris._ Ἡ διαφορα οὐκ ἐστιν κατα την οὐσιαν ἀλλα κατα την των χρονων ἐυαλλαγην. The Old Testament even acquired great power over the church in the hierarchy, priesthood, and sacrificial idea, in the legal conception of the gospel, in worship, painting, and so forth—a church that in many respects wanted to be an Old Testament theocracy; cf. the article “διαθηκη” in Suicerus; Diestel, _Gesch. des A. T. in der Chr. Kirche_, Jena, 1869; Harnack, _D. G._ I² 531 f. Nevertheless, the church in the new dispensation was thereby still placed high above the old. In scholastic and Roman Catholic theology, the distinction between the two testaments was indicated as follows: that the _promissa_ in the Old Testament were _temporalia_ and now are _coelestia_; that the _praecepta_ then were _exterius regulantia_, but now are _pleniora_, _non solum manum sed et mentem regulantia_; that the _sacramenta_ then were merely _figuralia_, now _gratiam conferentia_, Lombard, _Sent._ III, dist. 40, and other commentators; Thomas, _S. Th._ III, qu. 60, art. 6 ad 3; qu. 61, art. 4 ad 2. In connection with this, the believers of the Old Testament also did not enter heaven at once but went to the _limbus patrum_, from which they were first transferred to heaven by Christ, Thomas, _Suppl. S. Theol._, qu. 69, art. 4–6; Bellarmine, _De Christo_ IV 10, 11; _Catech. Rom._ I 3, 4; I 6, 6.
