## 9.

In this way the doctrine of the covenant maintains God’s sovereignty throughout the work of salvation in a wonderfully beautiful manner. It far surpasses the covenant of works, as far as Christ surpasses Adam. Much more clearly than in creation, God’s triune being comes to revelation in re-creation. It is the Father who purposes and wills redemption; it is the Son who guarantees and actually acquires it; it is the Spirit who effects and applies it. And throughout that whole work of salvation, from beginning to end, nothing enters from the human being. It is God’s work wholly and solely; it is pure grace and unmerited salvation. But for that very reason it is all the more important to observe that this doctrine of the covenant, in spite of—or rather, precisely because—it so purely and fully maintains God’s sovereignty in the work of salvation, at the same time does justice in so beautiful a manner to the rational and moral nature of the human being. This was already set forth at length concerning the covenant of works, vol. II, p. 553. But in the covenant of grace it comes out still more strikingly. In this respect the covenant is greatly distinguished from election. The two are indeed not so distinguished that election is particular and the covenant of grace universal, that the former denies free will while the latter teaches or presupposes it, that the latter takes back what the former confesses. But they do differ in this way: in election the human being appears as entirely passive, while in the covenant of grace he also appears as active. Election says simply who are elect and will infallibly obtain salvation. The covenant of grace describes the way by which these elect will reach their destination. It is the channel in which the stream of election moves onward toward eternity. In the covenant of grace Christ indeed acts as head and representative of his own, but he does not efface and destroy them. He guarantees for them, but in such a way that they themselves too, taught and enabled by his Spirit, consciously and voluntarily consent to the covenant. The covenant of grace was indeed made with Christ, but over and through him it also extends to his own and takes them wholly into itself, body and soul. The _pactum salutis_ expands into a _foedus gratiae_. The head of the covenant of grace is at the same time its mediator. And therefore, immediately at its promulgation, it also appears with the demand of faith and repentance, Mark 1:15. In the early period, the Reformed spoke freely of conditions of the covenant: Calvin, _Inst._ IV 15, 17; on Gen. 15:6; 17:4; Matt. 3:7, 9; Gomarus, _De foedere_; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ VIII 5; Trigland, _Antapologia_ c. 18; preface of the States Translation to the New Testament; Voetius, _Disp._ V 272, 273, and so forth. But when the nature of the covenant of grace was considered more deeply and had to be defended against Roman Catholics, Lutherans, and Remonstrants, many felt objections to this language: Olevianus, _Wezen des genadeverbonds_ I 13, 14; Junius, _Disp. Theol._ XXV 12, 13, 19; Cocceius, _De foedere_ § 87; idem, _Summa Theol._ 41, 5, 12, 13; Cloppenburg, _De foedere_ § 29; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ III 1, 8–16; Franken, _Kern_ c. 23; Brakel, _Red. Godsd._ XVI 17; Comrie, _Heid. Catech._ I 352, and so forth; cf. M. Vitringa IV 224; and in England especially also the Antinomians, Tobias Crisp and others, against whom Richard Baxter, Daniel Williams, and others took action.

Properly speaking, there are in the _foedus gratiae_—that is, in the gospel, which is the proclamation of the covenant of grace—no demands and no conditions. For God gives what he demands. Christ has accomplished everything and, although he did not accomplish regeneration, faith, and repentance in our place, he nevertheless acquired them for us; and the Holy Spirit applies them. Yet in its administration through Christ, the covenant of grace assumes this demanding, conditional form in order to acknowledge the human being in his rational and moral nature; even as fallen, still to treat him as created in God’s image; also on this highest plane, where eternal salvation and eternal ruin are at issue, to hold him responsible and without excuse; to cause him consciously and freely to enter this covenant and to break with sin. The covenant of grace is therefore indeed unilateral: it proceeds from God. He designed and established it. He maintains and realizes it. It is a work of the triune God and completely finished among the three persons themselves. But it is destined to become bilateral, to be consciously and voluntarily accepted and kept by the human being in God’s power. This is the will of God that comes so clearly and beautifully to light in the covenant: that the work of grace should be clearly reflected in human consciousness and should arouse the human will to powerful energy. The covenant of grace does not kill the human being and does not treat him as a stick and a block. Rather, it wholly embraces the human being, with all his faculties and powers, in soul and body, for time and eternity. It comprehends him entirely; does not destroy his power but removes his impotence; does not kill his will but frees it from sin; does not stupefy his consciousness but delivers it from darkness. It re-creates the whole human being and then causes him, renewed by grace, freely and independently, with his whole soul and spirit and body, to love God and dedicate himself to him. The covenant of grace declares that God’s honor and praise are obtained not at the human being’s expense but for his benefit, and celebrate their triumphs in the re-creation of the whole human being, in his enlightened consciousness and restored freedom.

At the same time another thought is thereby given that comes to light in the covenant of grace, in distinction from election. In election, the elect appear as so many persons known to God by name. They have indeed been chosen in Christ and form an organism with him as their Head. Nevertheless, this does not come clearly and strongly to the foreground in election. It is entirely otherwise in the covenant of grace. Here Christ appears as Adam’s substitute, as the second Head of the human race. Here Christ with his church stands in connection with humanity under Adam. Election looks especially to individuals and, considered in itself, left open the possibility that the elect, each by himself, individualistically, by leaps, were taken out of the human race, regenerated, and transferred to heaven. But the covenant of grace says that election is realized in an entirely different manner. It expresses the profound, beautiful truth that Adam is replaced by Christ; that humanity, which fell in the first, is restored in the second; that not a few detached individuals are saved, but that in the elect under Christ the organism of humanity and of the world itself is rescued; that not only the persons of the elect but also, so to speak, the structure of the organism that they form in Christ is derived from the original creation in Adam. Therefore the covenant of grace does not leap from individual to individual either, but continues organically and historically. It traverses a history and has different dispensations. It accommodates itself to the times and circumstances determined by the Father as Creator and Sustainer. It is never made with only one individual person, but always at the same time in that person with his seed. It is a covenant from generation to generation. It never embraces only the person of the believer in the abstract, but that person concretely, as he exists and lives historically—thus not him alone but also everything belonging to him; not only him in his person, but also him as father or mother, as parent or child, with all that is his, with his family, with his money and property, with his influence and power, with his office and calling, with his understanding and heart, with his science and art, with his life in society and state. The covenant of grace is the organization of the new humanity under Christ as its Head, an organization that joins the ordering of creation, reaches back into it, and qualitatively and intensively embraces that entire creation within itself. It therefore follows of itself that the covenant of grace, temporally, in this earthly administration and dispensation, also embraces those who remain inwardly unbelieving and do not partake of the spiritual benefits. With this in view, the Reformed distinguished between an inward and an outward covenant, marginal note on 1 Cor. 7:14; 1 Pet. 2:9; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ III 1, 5; Mastricht V 1, 28; or between covenant and covenant administration, Olevianus, _Wezen des Genadeverbonds_ I 2; Alting, _Theol. catech._, p. 33; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XII 6, 5; or between an absolute and a conditional covenant, Maresius, _Theol. syst._ VIII 7; Koelman, _Historie der Labadisten_, p. 566. Some—such as Blake in Dr. Vos, _De Verbondsleer_, pp. 12, 45; Stoddard at Northampton in Edwards, _Works_ I 34; J. Schuts, _Het verbond der genade verduisterd_, 1713, and _Verhandeling over het H. Avondm._, 1722; M. Swarte, 1740; R. Schutte, _Tweetal verhandelingen_, 1785; and others—even went so far as to assume two covenants, one with the elect and true believers, the other with the outward members of the church who did not sincerely believe, and thereby sought to justify the membership of the latter and their access to the Lord’s Supper. But others, such as Edwards, _Works_ 185–295; Koelman, _Historie der Labadisten_, pp. 95 f.; Appelius, _De Herv. Leer_, 1769; Vitringa, _Obs. Sacr._ II c. 6; Brakel, _Red. Godsd._ c. 16; Moor V 470; also Rev. de Herder, cf. _Heraut_ 937; and others rightly opposed this. The covenant of grace is one, and its outward and inward sides, although never coinciding here on earth, cannot and may not be detached from each other and placed alongside each other. There certainly are evil branches on the vine; there is chaff among the wheat; in a great house there are golden and earthen vessels, Matt. 3:12; 13:29; John 15:2; 2 Tim. 2:20. But we lack the right and power to make a separation between them. God himself will do that on the day of harvest. So long as, according to the judgment of love, they walk in the way of the covenant, they are to be regarded and treated as covenant partners. Although not _de foedere_, they are nevertheless _in foedere_ and will one day be judged as such. Here on earth they are connected with the elect in all kinds of ways; and because they are members of Adamic humanity, the elect as an organism can be gathered into one under Christ as their Head in no other way than by the way of the covenant.
