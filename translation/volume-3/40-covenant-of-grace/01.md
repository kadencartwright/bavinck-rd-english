# Chapter VII. On Christ

# § 40. The Covenant of Grace

## 1.

Sin and misery are acknowledged to a certain extent by all human beings.
And from this acknowledgment a weaker or stronger longing for deliverance is born in everyone; the need for deliverance is also universal.
Indeed, all human beings are conscious to a greater or lesser degree that deliverance must come from above.
Παντες δε θεων χατεους’ ἀνθρωποι.
But the pagans did not know the grace that alone can deliver the human being from sin and misery; they had only conjectures and wishes.
_Humana ad deos transferebat Homerus; divina mallem ad nos_ (Cicero).
Only in Holy Scripture does the grace of God confront us in all its riches and glory.
It begins immediately after the fall.
The punishment threatened in Gen. 2:17 was clearly not fully carried out.
Adam and Eve did not die on the day of their transgression.
Had the just punishment of sin been executed immediately and fully, the whole human race would have been annihilated in the first human pair, the earth devastated, and the cosmos returned to chaos, indeed to nothingness.
But then Satan would also have triumphed and God would have lost.
Therefore, immediately after the fall another principle enters into operation, one that restrains, combats, and conquers sin.
Not only is the punishment not fully applied, but even the punishment that is applied is at the same time still a blessing.
Everything that occurs immediately after the fall is at once a revelation and proof of God’s wrath and of God’s grace.
The consciousness of guilt that awakens in Adam and Eve, the conscience that rises accusingly, the shame over their nakedness, the fear and hiding from God, Gen. 3:7 f., are all evidence that they have transgressed God’s law and are worthy of his wrath and punishment; but they also show that the human being has not become obdurate or hardened, that he has not become a devil but has remained human.
The angels, when they fall, fall all at once into hell itself, Jude 6; they are immediately hardened in evil and irredeemable.
But the human being was so created that even when he falls he can still be raised again; he remains capable of deliverance.
God holds back the full operation of the principle and power of sin in the human being.
More than this, God does not withdraw after the fall and even then does not let go of the human being for a single moment.
When the sense of guilt, shame, and fear awaken in the human being immediately after the transgression, all this is already an operation of God’s Spirit in the human being, a revelation of his wrath but also of his grace, a revelation that is the foundation of all religious and ethical life still present in the human being after the fall.
But God also reveals himself in another way.
When the human being hides from him, he seeks him out and calls him.
It is true that God comes to him from afar, and the human being in fear wishes to hide and to make the distance between himself and God still greater.
There is no longer communion between God and the human being but estrangement and separation; the covenant has been broken.
And yet God comes to the human being and seeks him out; he no longer leaves him to his own folly and anxiety, which cause him to seek salvation by hiding from God.
But he himself, of his own accord, calls the human being back to himself.
And in doing so he does not take the human being by surprise and terrify him.
He comes, as it were, from afar; he comes to him not in storm or tempest but in the רוּחַ הַיּוֹם, that is, in the evening breeze, amid the solemn rustling of the trees in the evening.
And from this they recognized the voice of God and noticed that he was approaching.
That approach was grace.
God gives the human being time to come to himself and consider what he will answer.
This revelation of God’s grace comes out still more clearly in the examination and the punishment, Gen. 3:9–13.
The Lord calls Adam in particular, for he is the head and the responsible person.
God does not come at once with his sentence but institutes an inquiry, begins to question, and gives the human being every opportunity to answer for himself.
When the human being makes use of this opportunity, he does not deny all guilt, but neither does he fall humbly and penitently before God; instead, he seeks to excuse himself. God does not then blaze forth in wrath but allows that excuse to stand to a certain extent.
And then, although in the examination he began with Adam, in pronouncing the punishment he addresses first the serpent, then Eve, and afterward Adam.

And in that punishment mercy triumphs over judgment.
The punishment, Gen. 3:14, 15, applies first of all to the serpent as an ordinary animal.
God humbles the serpent and puts enmity between it and the human being; from now on, instead of the earlier subjection of the animals to the human being, a struggle will prevail between them, in which the human being will indeed conquer but will nevertheless have much to suffer from the animals, especially from the serpent.
But further, through the serpent this punishment reaches back to the evil power whose instrument it was.
With that power the human being had made a covenant and for its sake had broken the covenant with God; God graciously nullifies this human covenant, puts enmity between the serpent’s seed and the woman’s seed, brings the woman’s seed—that is, humanity—back to his side, and thus declares that a human race will still proceed from Eve and that, although this human race will suffer much in the struggle against the evil power, it will nevertheless triumph in the end.
From now on, the path of the human race will pass through suffering to glory, through struggle to victory, through the cross to the crown, through the state of humiliation to that of exaltation.
This is the fundamental law that God here promulgates for entrance into the kingdom of heaven.
The punishment of the woman, Gen. 3:16, falls upon her both as mother and as wife; precisely as woman she will have much sorrow to bear.
The highest thing she desires, namely, to be a mother, she will be able to obtain only by the way of manifold suffering—desire for the man, subjection to him, and giving birth in pain.
Yet in this way she will nevertheless obtain her highest wish and reach her destiny; she will be a mother of the living, although through her transgression and exaltation above the man she had deserved death, and she will be saved in bearing children, 1 Tim. 2:15.
The punishment of the man, Gen. 3:17–19, consists in this: the ground is cursed on his account, he will eat bread in the sweat of his face, and after a life of labor and toil he will return to dust.
In connection with this he is then expelled from paradise and sent out into the wide world, Gen. 3:22–24.
But this punishment too is a blessing.
Labor ennobles; it guards the human being against moral and physical ruin, spurs his energy, and heightens his activity.
Even though the human being is banished from paradise, he is nevertheless not consigned to hell; he is sent into the world so that he may subject and rule it through toilsome and strenuous labor.
Herein lie the beginning and principle of all culture; the dominion of the human being is not wholly lost but is extended through labor, Ps. 8; even in the fallen human being, the image of God according to which he was created is still recognizable.
Indeed, even temporal death is not only a punishment but also a benefit; God instituted death so that sin would not be immortal, Iren., _adv. haer._ III 23. 6.
Thus immediately after the fall a twofold principle enters into operation: wrath and grace, justice and mercy.
Only from this can a world such as we know be explained.
This is a world full of humor, δακρυοεν γελασασα, standing under the sign of the cross and immediately after the fall given to Christ, the man of sorrows, so that he might save and subdue it.

After God had thus pronounced the punishment, Adam and Eve humbly bow their heads; they offer no further objection, lay their hands upon their mouths, and accept the word of God without any protest.
That word does indeed sound unbelievable, and its truth can become apparent only in the distant future, but nevertheless they believe it, blind to the future and seeing in the command.
Relying on God’s promise, Adam names his wife Eve: life, source of life, mother of the living.
Before the fall he saw in her above all the woman given to him as a helper, and therefore called her woman.
But now he beholds in her first of all the mother and calls her Eve.
Death and destruction have indeed been deserved, but God’s blessing nevertheless makes the woman fruitful and causes a humanity to proceed from her that in her greatest son, the Son of Man, will overcome all the evil power of sin.
Adam sees in God’s promise the propagation, existence, development, and salvation of the human race guaranteed and accepts it as such with a genuinely childlike faith.
And this faith was reckoned to him as righteousness.
In principle Genesis 3 contains the entire history of humanity, all the ways of God for rescuing what was lost and overcoming sin.
In substance the whole gospel, the entire covenant of grace, is present here.
Everything that follows is the development of what has now already been planted as seed.
