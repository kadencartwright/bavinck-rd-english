## 8.

This _foedus gratiae_, which through various dispensations has now been fully realized in the New Testament, was from the first moment of its revelation, and still is, surrounded and sustained on every side by the _foedus naturae_ that God established with all creatures.
The _gratia specialis_ is indeed essentially distinct from the _gratia communis_, but it nevertheless stands in the closest connection with it.
For although the covenant with Noah is by way of distinction called the _foedus naturae_, it did not therefore flow from God’s nature, nor was it given with the nature of things; it too rests upon grace, arose from God’s longsuffering, and bestows all natural benefits and blessings from God’s common goodness; it is a covenant of grace in a broader sense.
Furthermore, it is the Father who, not apart from the Son but specifically through the Logos and the Spirit, works all powers and gifts in nature and unregenerate humanity, John 1:4, 5, 9, 10; Col. 1:17; Ps. 104:30; 139:7.
And this Logos and this Spirit, who dwell and work in all creatures and human beings, are the same who, as Christ and as the Spirit of Christ, are respectively the acquirer and applier of all the benefits in the covenant of grace.
Father, Son, and Spirit thus prepare the _foedus gratiae_ in the _foedus naturae_ and, as it were, repeatedly reach back from the _foedus gratiae_ into the _foedus naturae_.

The essence of the covenant of grace therefore consists in this, that it flows from God’s special grace and has nothing but grace—unmerited blessings that have been forfeited—as its content.
In this respect it is essentially distinct from the _foedus operum_ established before the fall but broken by the fall.
To be sure, God was not obligated to establish the covenant of works either; it was condescending goodness, and thus also grace in a general sense, that caused him to grant this covenant to the human being.
He also established it and determined all its parts; it is his ordinance and institution.
But in that covenant of works God nevertheless came to the human being with the demand of obedience and promised to grant him, only by that path and after that obedience had been completed, the blessedness of heaven, eternal life, and the enjoyment of beholding him.
The covenant of works therefore took account of the human being’s free will, rested in part in the human being, and was for that reason wavering and unstable.
In fact, it was also broken, not by God but by the human being.
God holds to the rule that whoever keeps the law will receive eternal life.
He says this in his law, testifies to it in everyone’s conscience, and makes this word good in Christ.
But the human being broke the covenant of works; on his side he can now no longer acquire life by keeping the law; no flesh can be justified by works of the law.
Therefore, in distinction from and in opposition to the covenant of works, God established another, better covenant, not a legal but an evangelical covenant.
But he established this covenant not with one who was merely human but with the man Christ Jesus, who was his own, only-begotten, dearly beloved Son.
And in him, who partakes of the divine nature and the divine attributes, it lies immovably firm.
It can no longer be broken; it is an eternal covenant.
It rests not in any work of the human being but solely in God’s good pleasure, in the work of the Mediator, and in the Holy Spirit, who remains eternally.
It depends on no condition of the human being, bestows no gift on account of any merit, and waits for no fulfillment of the law on the human being’s side.
It is from grace, through grace, and unto grace.
God himself is the only and eternal one, the faithful and true one, in whom it rests and who establishes, maintains, executes, and completes it; the covenant of grace is the preeminent divine work, his work alone, his work entirely.
All boasting is here excluded for the human being, but all glory belongs to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

Yet the full content of the covenant of grace has not thereby been brought to light.
To that end, the covenant of grace must be set forth not only in its distinction from the covenant of works but also in its kinship with it.
After the breaking of the covenant of works, God did not immediately devise an altogether different covenant that had nothing to do with the former and bore an entirely different character.
That cannot be the case, because God is immutable; because the demand placed upon the human being in the covenant of works is no whim and no arbitrariness; because the image of God, the law, and religion can be only one in their essence; and because grace cannot or may not nullify nature, nor faith the law.
Nor is it so.
The covenant of grace is not, as Cocceius taught, the successive abolition of the covenant of works; it is rather its fulfillment and restoration.
_Gratia reparat et perficit naturam._
God holds to the demand that eternal life can be obtained only by the way of obedience; and when the human being transgresses his law, another demand is added, namely, that the transgression must be expiated through punishment.
After the fall God therefore has a twofold demand upon the human being: the demand of punishment for the evil committed and that of perfect obedience to his law, _satisfactio_ and _obedientia_.
In a letter to Uytenbogaerdt, Arminius thought that before the fall the human being was obligated to obedience but now, after the fall, only to punishment, because the contract had been broken and the party was therefore released from its obligation.
But obedience to God’s law was indeed cast into a particular form in the covenant of works and employed for a particular purpose, namely, obtaining eternal life; yet it is in itself grounded in the human being’s nature and is therefore an obligation from which the human being can never be released.
Otherwise, by one transgression he could also free himself henceforth from keeping all God’s commandments, and thus from all sins and punishments.
Scripture therefore not only pronounces God’s judgment upon the sinner, Deut. 27:26; Gal. 3:10; Rom. 6:23; Heb. 10:27, but also maintains for the human being after the fall the demand of perfect obedience as the way to eternal life, Lev. 18:5; Matt. 19:17; Luke 10:28.

The difference between the covenant of works and the covenant of grace therefore consists in this, that in the latter God presents not a single but a twofold demand, and that he presents this twofold demand not to humanity in Adam but to humanity in Christ.
The _foedus operum_ and the _foedus gratiae_ differ chiefly in this, that Adam is exchanged for and replaced by Christ.
Paul says in Gal. 3:16–18 that the covenant with Abraham was not nullified by the law that came later but actually had reference to and essentially rested in Christ, who fulfilled every promise and bestowed the inheritance.
He goes still farther back in Rom. 5:12–21 and 1 Cor. 15:22, 45–49: from Adam sin and death flow to humanity; from Christ righteousness and life flow to it.
Christ is the second and last Adam, who restores and takes over what the first ruined and left undone, the Mediator of the covenant of grace, the head of the new humanity.
Reformed theology did justice to this thought of Scripture in its covenant doctrine better than any other theology.
The course of development was briefly this: first, the doctrine of the covenant of grace arose to maintain the essential unity of the Old and New Testaments; accordingly, the relation of God and the human being before the fall was also represented as a covenant, specifically as a covenant of works; reflection on the agreement and difference between the covenant of works and the covenant of grace led to the insight that the covenant of grace, insofar as it was established with Christ, was essentially a covenant of works; therefore, within the covenant of grace a distinction again had to be made between the covenant as it was established with Christ from eternity (_pactum salutis_, counsel of peace) and the covenant as, in execution of that counsel of peace, it is established in time with the elect or believers; and finally this distinction was again annulled, the covenant of grace and the counsel of peace were conceived as essentially one, and the covenant of grace itself was relocated to eternity, since it was established there with Christ and in him with all his own.
The last point, the identification of the counsel of peace and the covenant of grace, first developed in England in Rollock, Preston, Blake, and the Westminster Larger Catechism; cf. Vos, _De verbondsleer in de Geref. theol._, 1891, p. 27 f.; further, Th. Boston, _Eene beschouwing van het verbond der genade_, trans. from the English by Al. Comrie, 1741.
It was then taken over from the English by Comrie; by Brahe, _Verklaring van Ps. 89_, preface, pp. V–XXXVI; and by others; cf. also Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 360.
Many, however, continued to harbor objections to this identification, such as Turretin, _Theol. El._ XII 2, 12; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ II 2, 1; _Misc. Sacra_ II 820–824; R. Schutte, _Tweetal verhandelingen over Gods testament en verbond_, 1785, p. 143 f.; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 358.

There is indeed also a distinction between the _pactum salutis_ and the _foedus gratiae_: in the former Christ is surety and head, in the latter Mediator; the former remains limited to Christ and demands of him the bearing of punishment and the fulfillment of the law in the place of the elect, while the latter extends over and through Christ to human beings and demands from them faith and repentance, which Christ did not fulfill and could not have fulfilled in our place; the former concerns the acquisition of salvation, is eternal, and knows no history, while the latter treats of the application of salvation, begins in time, and has distinct dispensations.
Yet with this distinction the connection and unity may not be overlooked.
There are in Scripture only two covenants, two ways for the human being to heaven, namely, the covenant of works and the covenant of grace.
The covenant of works is the way to heaven for the unfallen human being; the covenant of grace is the way for the fallen human being.
The covenant of works was made with humanity in Adam, the covenant of grace with humanity in Christ; he and he alone is the head who substitutes for and represents humanity.
Thus when Scripture says that the covenant of grace was established with Adam, Noah, Abraham, Israel, and so forth, this may not be understood as though they were the proper parties and heads in this covenant.
No, Christ was then and is now, in the Old and New Testaments, the head and the party in the covenant of grace, and through his administration the covenant came to the patriarchs and to Israel.
He who existed from eternity and had pledged himself as surety also immediately after the fall actually appeared as prophet, priest, and king, as the second Adam, as head and representative of fallen humanity.
In the covenants with Adam, Noah, Abraham, David, and so forth, he is the Mediator, the surety who guarantees the realization of the covenant, who realizes it through his Spirit in hearts, who administers it to sinners, who bestows its benefits, and who receives his own into the covenant.
The whole covenant is entrusted to him from beginning to end; in him alone it lies firm; as the Father ordained the kingdom for him, so he ordains it for those given to him; he distributes as an inheritance the benefits acquired by him.
The covenant is as firm as a testament; it is a _foedus testamentarium_ and a _testamentum foederale_.
It is relatively indifferent and touches no principle whether one places the duality or the unity of the _pactum salutis_ and the _foedus gratiae_ in the foreground, provided only that it remains established that in the _pactum salutis_ Christ can never for a single moment be conceived apart from his own, nor in the _foedus gratiae_ can believers for a single moment be considered outside Christ.
In both it is the _Christus mysticus_—Zanchius, _Op._ II 400 sq.; Mastricht, _Theol._ V 1, 4—Christ as the second Adam, who appears as the acting party.
Adam and Abraham and David, and so forth, may be types, but the antitype is Christ.
And since Adam was already a type of Christ before the fall, the covenant of grace was prepared not first through Noah and Abraham, and not first through the covenant of grace with Adam, but already in and through the covenant of works.
God, who knows and determines all things and also included the breaking of the covenant of works in his counsel, had already reckoned with Christ and his covenant of grace at the creation of Adam and the institution of the covenant of works, vol. II, 546.
