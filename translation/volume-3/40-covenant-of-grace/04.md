## 4.

The Reformation saw all kinds of tendencies opposite and alongside itself, over against which it had to define its relation on this point too. Judaizing and Gnostic ideas had propagated themselves in the Middle Ages and now continued to exert their influence. Anabaptism was radical. It desired not only religious-ethical but also social and political reformation, and with an appeal to Scripture demanded the abolition of interest, polygamy, community of goods, and so forth. The same opposition between nature and grace from which at first it desired the introduction of all kinds of Old and New Testament ordinances gradually caused it to subordinate the whole of Scripture to the inward word. Scripture was a dead letter. The Old Testament especially lacked all evangelical content. Israel, like a herd of swine, was fed only with temporal goods. The law had lost all value and force for us, M. Vitringa IV 238; Diestel 307 f. Through its rationalistic principle, Socinianism reached the same result. The Old Testament stands much lower than the New. It teaches polygamy, restricts love of neighbor, does not prescribe many virtues at all, and sets far too severe punishments upon minor sins. It knows only servile fear and earthly promise; it knows nothing of full righteousness and forgiveness. It has therefore been completely abolished, and Christ appeared as _novus legislator_, Fock, _Der Socin._ 325; Diestel 535. Arminianism did not speak so boldly, but nevertheless conceived the Old Testament as a covenant that promised only temporal goods, Episcopius, _Disp._ II, disp. 18–20; IV, disp. 12–14; Limborch, _Theol. Chr._ III c. 9; M. Vitringa IV 242. Even Luther frequently opposed Old Testament and New as law and gospel, but later became more cautious and taught that there were also rich evangelical promises in the Old Testament, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ I 84 f.; II 258. Nevertheless, this conception continued to operate in Lutheran theology. It indeed assumes that in the Old and New Testaments there is only one Messiah, one faith, one way of salvation, but it nevertheless refuses to say that there is only one testament. For it, the word _testament_ designates that legal covenant established with Israel at Sinai; and in this sense it differs essentially from, stands opposed to, and is abolished by the New Testament. Many promises were indeed given to Israel in the Old Testament that still apply to us, but the Old Testament as God’s legal covenant with Israel was no _foedus gratiae_. The promises of the gospel given to Adam, Abraham, and others may indeed be gathered under the name _foedus gratiae_, but then even this _foedus gratiae_ in the Old Testament differs from the New Testament not merely in _accidentia_ but also in _substantia_, just as the promise differs from the fulfillment, the shadow from the body, Gerhard, _Loci_ XIV 119 sq.; Quenstedt, _Theol._ IV, pp. 255–280; Hollaz, _Examen_ 1043–1053; Heppe, _Dogm. d. d. Pr._ II 258.

The doctrine of the covenant was developed most richly in Reformed theology. Federal theology did not originate with Cocceius, as Ypey thought, _Beknopte letterk. Gesch. der syst. Godg._ II, pp. 70 f.; neither is it a peculiarity of that German Reformed theology that supposedly had its father in Melanchthon, as Heppe represented it in his _Geschichte_ and in his _Dogmatik des deutschen Protestantismus_. The incorrectness of these views is now acknowledged by all, Sepp, _Godg. Ond._ II 220; Schweizer, _Gl. der ref. K._ I 103; Gass, _Gesch. d. prot. Dogm._ II 265; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst. des luth. u. ref. Lehrbegriffs_ II 146; Diestel, “Studien zur Foederaltheol.,” in _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._ X 209; Van den Bergh, _Calvijn over het Genadeverbond_, pp. 7 f.; Heppe, _Gesch. d. Pietismus u. d. Mystik in der ref. Kirche_, 1879, and so forth. All of them go back to Olevianus, Calvin, or Bullinger. The covenant idea, however, had already been adopted by Zwingli, who in his _Elenchus contra Anabaptistas_, 1527, _Opera_ III 412 sq., 418 sq., 421 sq.; V 45, maintained the essential unity of Old and New Testaments. From Zwingli it was then adopted in Swiss theology: Bullinger, _De testamento seu foedere Dei unico et aeterno_, 1534; _Comp. relig. Christ._, 1556; _Decades_, 1549 f.; Calvin, _Inst._ II c. 10, 11; cf. Van den Bergh, _Calvijn over het genadeverbond_, and the review of this dissertation by Dr. van Dijk, _Studien_ VII, 1880, part 1; in the German Reformed theology of Olevianus, Ursinus, Sohnius, Eglin, Boquinus, Hyperius, and others; in the English theology of Rollock, Howie, Cartwright, Preston, Thomas Blake, Perkins, Ames, John Ball, James Ussher, the Westminster Confession c. 7, Francis Roberts, Thomas Boston, and others; cf. Vos, _De verbondsleer in de Geref. Theol._, 1891; in the Netherlands by Snecanus, Junius, Gomarus, Trelcatius father and son, Nerdenus, Ravensperger, and others. Thus, long before Cloppenburg and Cocceius, the doctrine of the covenants was at home in Reformed theology. But these two made the covenant idea the point of departure and governing principle of the whole of dogmatics: Cloppenburg, _Disp. de foedere Dei_, _Op._ I 487–570; Cocceius, _Summa doctrinae de foedere et test. explicata_, 1648; and likewise Burman, _Synopsis_, 1671; Braun, _Doctrina foederum_, 1688; Witsius, _De oec. foed._, 1677. The controversy against Cocceius was not directly about the doctrine of the covenant at all, which was acknowledged by all, but about the Sabbath with Essenius and Hoornbeek, 1655–1659; about the condition of the church in the twofold economy with Maresius, 1662; about the παρεσις in the Old Testament with Voetius, 1665. What was opposed in Cocceius was not his concept of the _foedus_ but his biblical theology and historical method. As Descartes in philosophy, so Cocceius in theology opposed scholasticism and traditionalism, insisted upon the study of Scripture, and wanted a _summa theologiae ex Scripturis repetita_, as the title of his dogmatics read. To attain that, he did not proceed from God and his eternal counsel but took his standpoint in history and in God’s covenant with human beings. His dogmatics therefore became a history of salvation, a biblical theology in historical form, of which Scripture was not the _principium_ and _norma_ but the object and content. He traced that covenant—that is, true religion—historically from its earliest beginnings down to the present time, and everywhere indicated the development and progress of that covenant, of true religion. There was therefore not merely a difference, as the Reformed said, in the clarity of revelation—that is, in clearness of insight and distinctness of consciousness. No, there was a difference in the objective benefits themselves under the different dispensations of the covenant of grace. Salvation was objectively less in the Old Testament than in the New Testament. In the Old Testament the Sabbath consisted in _cessatio operis_; Israel did not yet possess _vera et permanentia bona_ but was a people of hope, longing for a long earthly life and still held by fear of death. It had no full ἀφεσις but only παρεσις. Justification was incomplete. Animal sacrifice could bring no atonement. The comfort of believers was still weak, their conscience not yet at rest. Circumcision of the heart was indeed promised but first became a good of the New Testament. The law was administered by angels. In a word, everything was indeed present in the Old Testament, but only in type and shadow; the reality of the thing itself was absent or at least much less. Not only subjectively but also objectively, not only in _accidentia_ but also in _substantia_, the Old Testament was another testament than the new. Indeed, Cocceius even undermined the whole doctrine of the covenant when he conceived the covenant of grace in an exclusively negative manner, as a gradual abolition of the covenant of works that was completed historically and successively. In the end nothing remained of the covenant. It was merely a temporary, human, continually changing form of religion. Cf. Gass, _Gesch. der prot. Dogm._ II 266 f.; article “Coccejus” by Ebrard in Herzog²; Heppe, _Gesch. des Pietismus_ 217 f.; Diestel, _Jahrb. f. d. Th._, 1865; Van der Flier, _De Johanne Coccejo_, 1859; also vol. I, p. 121 and Dr. Geesink, _De Ethiek in de Geref. theol._, 1897, pp. 49 f. In this Cocceius undoubtedly abandoned the point of departure and line of Reformed theology. This was immediately felt by many and therefore also opposed to a greater or lesser extent. But it was more an attack upon subordinate points than a principial opposition. Cocceianism, soon in alliance with Cartesianism, found ever wider acceptance and led not only to opposition against various dogmas but also contributed to the degradation of the Old Testament. Everywhere the opinion unconsciously penetrated that the Old Testament now possessed only historical value and was no longer of dogmatic significance. From this changed dogmatic conception the historical criticism of the Old Testament was in due time naturally born, beginning with Spinoza and Richard Simon. Rationalism and supernaturalism did not have the slightest understanding of the significance and value of the Old Testament. According to Kant, Judaism was only _ein Inbegriff bloss statutarischer Gesetze, auf welchen eine Staatsverfassung gegründet war_; the _moralischen Zusätze_ were merely an appendix and not essentially its own. Judaism did not want to be a church at all but only a state, and therefore demanded only external observance of the commandments, no inward disposition. Christianity did not arise from Judaism but is its full abolition, _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, p. 150 f. In Schleiermacher, Judaism, because of its particularism, is still related to fetishism, _Chr. Gl._ § 8.4, and stands in relation to Christianity on the same level as paganism, § 12; cf. § 132. Hegel placed Judaism as the _Religion der Erhabenheit_ even below the Greek religion of beauty and the Roman religion of expediency, _Werke_ XII 39 f. Vatke and Bruno Bauer, both pupils of Hegel, sought to confirm this philosophical conception of the Old Testament through historical criticism of the biblical books. The biblical criticism of the last century did not change the worldview; rather, the changed worldview demanded a frequently very negative judgment concerning the great facts of Holy Scripture, Van den Bergh van Eysinga, _Levensbeschouwing_, Zutphen: Thieme, p. 25. In Old Testament criticism today, accordingly, all the views formerly advanced concerning the Old Testament by Gnostics, Anabaptists, Socinians, and rationalists have returned. YHWH is not the one true God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, but a national god of Israel, originally a sun god. The people of Israel were not chosen by God but in ancient times were a savage horde of different tribes surrendered to every kind of polytheism. The narratives of creation, fall, flood, patriarchs, judges, and so forth are myths and sagas, in part borrowed from other peoples. The law stands much lower than the prophets and frequently bears an external, sensual, eudaimonistic character. The saints of the Old Testament, such as Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and especially David, are unworthy of that name and either did not exist at all or were idealized by later generations. The distinction between true and false prophets is entirely subjective. Christianity was prepared at least as much by paganism as by Judaism.
