## 6.

In the execution of the _pactum salutis_ in time, however, a distinction must be made between the _foedus gratiae_ in the broader and narrower senses. It is not right, in discussing the covenant of grace in time, to proceed at once to Israel and to the church of the New Testament. After all, Scripture does not pass immediately from Adam to Abraham either. It does not let humanity go but describes in broad strokes its development down to the time of Abraham. When Abraham and Israel are chosen from humanity, the bond with that humanity is not cut. Israel does not float like a drop of oil upon the nations but remains connected with those nations in all kinds of ways and to the end holds fast an expectation for those nations too. In the fullness of time Jew and Gentile are reconciled in the one man. Humanity gathers around the cross, and the church chosen from that humanity stands in the closest connection with it. Nature and grace, creation and re-creation, must be placed in the mutual relation in which Scripture places them. And then it deserves notice that the first promises of grace proceeding from God’s mouth to Adam and Eve after the fall are entirely universal and concern the whole of humanity. It was pointed out above that every punishment pronounced by God upon sin in Gen. 3 must at the same time be recognized as a revelation of God’s grace. And there that grace extends without any limitation to the whole of humanity. Common and special grace still flow in one channel. In the punishment that God pronounces over the serpent, the woman, and the man after the transgression, mercy speaks still more than wrath. It is punishment and promise at once; it is _eine gnädige fröhliche Strafe_ (Luther). Therefore, in it lie the origin and guarantee of humanity’s continued existence, expansion and development, struggle and triumph. Religion and morality, worship and culture, take their beginning there. In the long period from Adam to Noah, all these develop under the influence of God’s common and special grace. The original powers placed by God in the various creatures at creation are indeed broken but continue to operate for a long time even after the fall. This appears especially in the strength and much greater longevity of human beings before the flood, Gen. 5:5 f., and in the much more powerful operation of the elements of nature, which were first placed under restraint after the flood, Gen. 8:22. This history of Scripture is confirmed by the tradition of the nations, which in its golden, silver, bronze, and iron age speaks of a gradual decline of humanity, and also by geology, according to which another period preceded our own in which a higher temperature prevailed over the whole earth, the seasons were not yet bounded, and fire and water played a much greater role than today. In the transition from the Mesozoic to the Cenozoic period, all kinds of important changes took place: a great expansion of the land through the elevation of extensive portions of the seafloor above the water; the formation of mountains such as the Alps, Pyrenees, Carpathians, Himalayas, Cordilleras, and others; a marked change in climate; the extinction of the great prehistoric animals and plants; and so forth, Pfaff, _Schöpfungsgeschichte_, chapters 11–15; Haeckel, _Nat. Schöpfungsgesch._⁵ 320 f.; Wossidlo, _Leitfaden der Mineralogie und Geologie_, Berlin: Weidmann, 1889, p. 196 f. In connection with all this, the greater strength and longer longevity of human beings in the period from Adam to Noah is by no means improbable. The simpler manner of life, less strenuous labor, smaller number of malignant diseases, and continued effect of the condition before the fall sufficiently explain this fact. Even now, by way of exceptions, ages of 120 to 150 years still occur, and no physical necessity can be conceived why human life-force must be exhausted after 70 or 80 years. Cf. Fürer, “Das Lebensalter des Menschen,” and so forth, _Beweis des Glaubens_, 1868, pp. 97 f., 184 f.; Zöckler, _Die Lehre vom Urstand des Menschen_, 1879, pp. 244–288; Schanz, _Das Alter des Menschengeschlechts_, Freiburg, 1896; Büchner, _’s Menschen Levensduur_, Dutch trans. Schwencke, Amsterdam, 1892. Religion also continues after the fall and acquires a fixed form in sacrifice, Gen. 4:3, and prayer and proclamation, Gen. 4:26. Culture begins with agriculture, animal husbandry, city building, and so forth, Gen. 4:2, 17. Arts and sciences develop, Gen. 4:20 f. But this first period in the history of humanity also soon distinguished itself through the most dreadful ungodliness: _corruptio optimi pessima_. Extraordinary powers and gifts were abused in the service of sin. This period was introduced with fratricide. The Cainites separated themselves from the Sethites, applied themselves to mastery of the earth, Gen. 4:20 f., and sought their strength in the sword, Gen. 4:24. But only when Sethites and Cainites commingled did ungodliness reach its peak. Wickedness was great upon the earth; every formation of the thoughts of the human being’s heart was only evil all day long, Gen. 6:5. It was a period so full of unrighteousness that there has never been another like it and it will recur only in the future of the Son of Man, Matt. 24:37. In the mighty flood this entire generation perished, except for Noah’s family, which then became the origin of a second humanity. The period after the flood differs essentially from the preceding one. From Adam to Noah, nature, the plant and animal world, and humanity bore an entirely different character than after Noah. Powerful and furnished with rich gifts, the world was in a certain sense left to itself; but it soon became evident that if God did not intervene powerfully, the world would perish in its own ungodliness. Therefore another period begins with Noah. The grace manifested immediately after the fall now acts more powerfully in restraining evil. God formally enters into covenant with all his creatures. This covenant with Noah, Gen. 8:21, 22; 9:1–17, does indeed have its origin in God’s grace. It also stands in the closest connection with the _foedus gratiae_ proper because it bears and prepares for it, but it is not identical with it. It is rather a _foedus longanimitatis_, made by God with all human beings and even with all creatures. Through it the curse upon the earth is limited. Nature is put under restraint; its destructive force is checked; the water is constrained in its terrible violence; a regular alternation of seasons is introduced; the whole of irrational nature is bound to fixed ordinances established in God’s covenant; and as sign and pledge the rainbow is placed in the clouds, Gen. 8:21, 22; 9:9–17. A humanity appears that, in comparison with the preceding one, is much gentler in nature, much smaller in strength, much shorter in duration. The blessing of multiplication is expressly pronounced again, Gen. 9:1; fear and dread of the human being are laid upon all animals, v. 2; green plants and flesh are given to the human being as food, v. 3. Human life is guaranteed through the institution of capital punishment for murder, and thereby in principle of civil government, vv. 5, 6. And when humanity later forms the plan at the building of Babel’s tower to continue dwelling together and to establish a world empire, God frustrates that plan, causes humanity to divide into peoples and languages, and in this way too prevents the development and outbreak of ungodliness. Thus God’s grace acts much more powerfully after the flood than before that time. To it are owed the existence and life of humanity; the expansion and development of the nations; the states and societies that have gradually formed; the religion and morality that have not been entirely lost even among the most uncivilized peoples; the arts and sciences that have risen to great heights. Everything good that after the fall still exists in sinful human beings in every domain, the whole of _justitia civilis_, is the fruit of God’s common grace. God indeed allowed the Gentiles to walk in their own ways, Acts 14:16, but did not withdraw himself from them. He did not leave himself without witness among them, determined their dwelling, was not far from each one of them, and revealed himself to them in the works of his hands, Acts 14:16, 17; 17:27, 28; Rom. 1:19; James 1:17. The Logos enlightens every human being coming into the world, John 1:9. The Holy Spirit is the author of all life, power, and virtue even among the Gentiles, Gen. 6:17; 7:15; Pss. 33:6; 104:30; 139:2; Job 32:8; Eccles. 3:19. Through this grace and under the dispensation of this _foedus naturae_, humanity before Christ was guided and prepared for his coming. One can indeed speak in a good sense of God’s education of humankind. The capacity for redemption was maintained; the need for redemption was awakened.
