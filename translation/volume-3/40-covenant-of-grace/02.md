## 2.

Yet in Gen. 3 the word covenant is not yet mentioned; it is first spoken of with Noah, Abraham, and Israel at Sinai. The newer criticism believes that here too it has been antedated and that Israel’s religion originally did not differ from that of other peoples. The relation of YHWH to Israel was like that of Chemosh to Moab. The idea of a covenant existing between YHWH and his people was wholly foreign to ancient Israel. It knew nothing of God, as the God of heaven and earth, having chosen Israel out of all peoples, made it his possession, and placed it in a covenant with himself. All these ideas arose only much later. Even in Amos the covenant relation does not yet occur; Hosea says only that Israel’s unfaithfulness can be compared to a breach of covenant, 6:7. Only Deuteronomy is in truth a covenant book; it was introduced and adopted in the manner of a covenant, 2 Kings 23:3; cf. Jer. 34:8 f.; Ezra 10:3; Neh. 10:1. But when that covenant first arose, it was again understood quite differently from later. At that time it was understood chiefly as Israel’s obligation toward YHWH to be a holy people and to keep his laws. Thus, if Israel did not keep the covenant, it was broken and Israel was laden with the curse, Deut. 27 and 28. But although Israel formally accepted the covenant in Josiah’s reformation, it did not keep it, was punished, and was sent into exile. Then there gradually arose the idea that God’s covenant consisted in a law he had given, that God had obligated himself to remain faithful to Israel and not to abandon it even when it fell away; God could indeed punish Israel but could not destroy the covenant. The Israel that returned from exile clung to this covenant idea and projected it back into the past, into the history of the patriarchs; cf. Wellhausen, _Gesch. Israels_ I 434 f.; Smend, _Altt. Theol._ 116 f.; and especially R. Kraetzschmar, _Die Bundesvorstellung im A. T. in ihrer gesch. Entw._, Marburg: Elwert, 1896. But this result was obtained by means of a violent criticism that flies in the face of the facts and in the end is nothing but arbitrariness. Thus, for example, in Gen. 18:21 the words “so shall the Lord be my God,” which really do suggest a covenant, are said to be certainly a later addition. Ex. 21–23 does indeed bear the name of the covenant book, and Ex. 34 does contain the words of the covenant, but these sections are not from Moses’ time, and although they are pre-Deuteronomic, they nevertheless contain no formal covenant such as is spoken of in the days of Josiah; perhaps they are even post-Deuteronomic. Hos. 8:1 is interpolated. In Gen. 15:18 the word covenant is merely the solemn name for the oath by which God binds himself to give the land of Canaan to Abraham’s seed; there is no question of reciprocal obligations, and so forth. A criticism that reasons in this way attains no certainty except that which it itself wishes to have. First, on the ground of the alleged spuriousness of the writings, it constructs the history of religious conceptions, and later it uses this same reconstructed history to demonstrate the spuriousness of the writings directed against it. Cf. G. Vos, _Recent Criticism of the Early Prophets_, _Presb. and Ref. Rev._, April 1898, pp. 214–238; also Strack, in a review of Kraetzschmar’s book, _Theol. Lit. Blatt_ 1898, no. 5. Further, the chief question in the investigation of the covenant idea in Holy Scripture is by no means whether the word ברית originally meant covenant or ordinance. Some, such as Delitzsch, Gesenius, Dillmann, Schultz, Oehler, Wellhausen, Guthe, Bredenkamp, König, and Cremer, affirm the former, assume covenant stipulation as its second meaning, and thus allow the sense to pass into ordinance, law, because a law acquires legal validity by way of covenant. Others, such as Hofmann, Buhl, Friedrich Delitzsch, Orelli, Strack, Siegfried, Stade, and Nowack, believe that determination, ordinance is the oldest meaning and that this passed into the sense of covenant because a law regulates a reciprocal relation. These explanations do not look plausible; it is also impossible to demonstrate historically in Scripture the transition from the one meaning to the other; now the one, then the other meaning predominates, without older or younger sources being distinguishable in this respect. The concept of ברית must therefore be defined differently. Its derivation sheds little light on this. According to most, it comes from ברה, to cut, and thus points back to the ancient Eastern custom of passing between the pieces of slaughtered animals laid opposite each other at the making of a covenant, to indicate that the same fate as befell these animals might befall the breaker of the covenant; hence כרת ברית, ὁρκια τεμνειν, _foedus ferire_; cf. Gen. 15:8 f.; Jer. 34:19. According to others, it comes from an Assyrian root meaning to bind, Kraetzschmar, 245. However this may be, Gen. 21:22 f.; 26:26 f.; 31:44 f., clearly show that three things belonged to a ברית: an oath or promise containing the agreed stipulations, a curse calling down the divine punishment upon the breaker, and a cultic ceremony symbolically representing the curse. The making of a ברית was therefore always a religious act. The word was indeed first used in the secular sphere for stipulations and treaties between human beings. Long before God established his covenant with Noah and Abraham, covenants had already been established between human beings. And this also had to be so if Noah, Abraham, and Israel were to understand and value religion as a covenant. That is why the word does not yet occur in Gen. 3:15. Only when covenants repeatedly became necessary in sinful, mendacious human society for the defense or acquisition of some good could the significance and value of a covenant be understood and religion be conceived from this point of view. But even though ברית is first used of human covenants, it nevertheless always denotes a religious act. The chief point in ברית is not whether it denotes a covenant or an ordinance. Rather, it signifies in general such a promise, agreement, treaty, covenant, stipulation, disposition, and so forth as is placed under God’s protection by a solemn ceremony and thereby acquires a character of inviolability, Kraetschmar, loc. cit., 29, 30, 39–41. Whether ברית denotes more a bilateral covenant or a unilateral disposition depends neither on the word nor on the historical development of the concept, but simply on the parties involved in it. To the degree that one of the two parties is subordinate and has less say, the ברית involuntarily takes on the character of a disposition imposed by the one party upon the other; ברית then becomes synonymous with חֹק, Ex. 34:10; Isa. 59:21; Jer. 33:20; 31:36; 34:13, and כרת ברית is construed not only with עם and בין but also with ל, Josh. 9:6; Isa. 55:3; 61:8; Jer. 32:40. When a victor makes a ברית with someone vanquished, or a king with his subjects, all the emphasis falls upon the duties the latter have to fulfill in it, Josh. 9; 1 Sam. 11; 2 Sam. 5:3; 1 Kings 20:34; Ezek. 17:13. The meaning of disposition and ordinance becomes still stronger when there is figurative mention of a ברית with the eyes, Job 31:1; with the stones and animals, Job 5:22; 40:28; with death, Isa. 28:15; of God with nature, Gen. 8:22; Jer. 33:20, 25; or with the animals, Gen. 9:12; Hos. 2:17–22; Isa. 11:6, and so forth. But also when God and a human being make a covenant, its monopleuric character naturally comes strongly to the fore again and again; they are not equal parties, but God is the Sovereign who imposes his ordinances upon creatures. When God makes a covenant with Abram in Gen. 15:8 f., it is not properly a _pactio_ but a _sponsio_; God gives his promise, binds himself to its fulfillment, and passes between the pieces of the sacrificial animal. Elsewhere he swears by himself, Gen. 22:16; by his life, Deut. 32:40; by his soul, Amos 6:8; Jer. 51:14, solely to demonstrate to human beings the immutability of his counsel, Heb. 6:17. And this monopleuric character of the covenant had to emerge ever more clearly in history. For although God’s covenant also imposed obligations on those with whom it was made, these were obligations not as conditions for entering into the covenant—for the covenant had been made and rests solely in God’s mercy—but as the way those received into the covenant by grace were henceforth to walk, Gen. 17:1, 2; Ex. 19:5, 6, 8; 24:3, 7; Lev. 26:14 f.; Deut. 5:29; 27:10 f.; 28:1 f.; 30:1 f., and so forth. But although Israel repeatedly accepted God’s covenant anew, Ex. 19:8; 24:3, 7; Deut. 29:10–13; Josh. 24:16; 2 Kings 23:3; 2 Chron. 15:12; 23:16; 29:10; 34:31; Neh. 8, and so forth, it did not walk in the way of the covenant and profaned and broke it at every moment. Thus the question arose whether this covenant of grace was then as precarious as the covenant of works before the fall. And to this revelation gave the answer, ever more powerfully and loudly as apostasy increased: no, this covenant does not waver; human beings may become unfaithful, but God does not forget his promise; the covenant rests solely and entirely secure in his mercy, Lev. 26:40–44; Deut. 4:31; 30:1 f.; 32:36 f.; Judg. 2:1; 2 Kings 13:23; Ps. 81:9, 12; 89:1–5; 105:8–10; 106:45; 111:5; Isa. 1:3; 5:13; 54:10; Jer. 18:5–10; Ezek. 33:10–16; Hos. 6:1–3; 11:7–9; 14:2–9; Joel 2:12–14. God cannot and may not break his covenant; freely, of himself, he has bound himself to it with a solemn oath; his name, his honor, his very being depend on it, Ex. 32; 33; Num. 14:16; Deut. 32:26; 1 Sam. 12:22; Isa. 48:8–11; Jer. 14:7, 20, 21; Ezek. 20:9, 14, 22, 43, 44; Joel 2:17–19, and so forth. And in this shines the glory of the religion we confess as Christians. Earlier, vol. II, 551 f., it was shown why true religion must bear the form of a covenant. In it God descends to the human being and receives him into his fellowship. But when God thus gives himself to the human being, the latter is also bound to give himself wholly to God. The covenant that proceeds from God and receives us admonishes and obligates us to a new obedience. But when we sometimes fall into sins through weakness, we must nevertheless not for that reason despair of God’s grace or remain in our sins, for the covenant of grace is firmly grounded in God’s immutable good pleasure. This inviolability, which is implicit in the word ברית, is probably also the reason why it was rendered in the LXX not by συνθηκη but by διαθηκη. The New Testament adopted this and applied it both to the Old and to the New Testament dispensation of the covenant of grace. It is noteworthy that our Dutch States Translation, like the English translation, renders the word διαθηκη in the first case as covenant, Luke 1:72; Acts 3:25; 7:8; Rom. 9:4; 11:27; Gal. 3:15, 17; 4:24; Eph. 2:12; Heb. 9:4, and in the second as testament, Matt. 26:28; Mark 14:14; Luke 22:20; 1 Cor. 11:25; 2 Cor. 3:6; Heb. 9:15; 12:24; 13:20. Underlying this is the thought that the concept _foedus_ is more fitting for the dispensation of the Old Testament and the concept _testamentum_ for that of the New Testament; cf. Bengel on Matt. 26:28. After the covenant with fleshly Israel was broken, spiritual Israel took its place, gathered according to God’s election from all peoples, receiving the goods of salvation from the Son as by testamentary disposition, standing in a filial relation to God, and awaiting heavenly blessedness as an inheritance, Luke 22:29; Rom. 8:16; Gal. 3:15–17; Heb. 9:15–17; 1 John 3:1, 2; 1 Pet. 1:4. Literature on the doctrine of the covenant in Scripture is found in Kraetzschmar, loc. cit., 1 f.
