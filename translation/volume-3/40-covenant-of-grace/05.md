## 5.

The doctrine of the covenant is of the greatest significance for dogmatics and also for the practice of the Christian life. The Reformed church and theology understood this more than Roman Catholic and Lutheran theology did. On the ground of Holy Scripture, it always conceived the true religion of the Old and New Testaments as a covenant between God and the human being, whether established with the unfallen human being (_foedus operum_), with creation in general at Noah (_foedus naturale_), or with the people of election (_foedus gratiae_). And it did not even stop here, but sought for these covenants in time a firm, eternal foundation in the counsel of God, and once more conceived this counsel, insofar as it intends the salvation of the human race, as a covenant of the three persons within the divine being itself (_pactum salutis_, counsel of peace, covenant of redemption). This last covenant already occurs briefly and substantially in Olevianus, _Wezen des Genadeverbonds_, 2nd article, § 1; Junius, _Theses Theol._ c. 25, th. 21; Arminius, _De sacerdotio Christi_, 1603; Gomarus on Matt. 3:13; Luke 2:21; _Theses Theol._ XIX 1; Ames, _De morte Christi_ I 5; Voetius, _Disp._ II 266; Essenius, _De subjectione Christi ad legem_ X 2. It is then further developed at length by Cloppenburg, _De foedere Dei_ III 4–28, and Cocceius, _Summa de foedere_ c. 5; afterward receives a definite place in the dogmatics of Burman, Braun, Witsius, Vitringa, Turretin, Leydecker, Mastricht, Marck, Moor, and Brakel; then is opposed by Deurhof, _Over natuurkundige en schriftuurlijke samenstelling van de H. Godg._ I 12; Wesselius, in the preface to Pictet’s _Godgeleerdheid_, and others; and gradually disappears entirely from dogmatics once more. The Reformed development of the doctrine of the _pactum salutis_ was not free of scholastic subtlety either. The _locus classicus_, Zech. 6:13, cited for this doctrine proves nothing and says only that the Messiah, who unites kingship and priesthood in himself, will counsel and promote the peace of his people (Keil). From Job 17:3; Isa. 38:14; Ps. 119:122—none of which, however, refer to the Messiah—and Heb. 7:22, which says only that Christ, because he lives eternally, is the guarantee that the new covenant will continue to exist eternally, it was inferred that Christ had become surety in the _pactum salutis_ from eternity. And indeed he was surety not from God toward us, as Crell on Heb. 7:22 and Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ III 21, 7, asserted—for God as the Truthful One needs no surety—but from us toward God, Cocceius, _De foedere_ V § 150–162; Owen on Heb. 7:22; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ II c. 5; Heidegger, _Corp. Theol._ XI 23; Van den Honert, _Het Hoogepr. van Christus_, 1712, p. 403; Boston, _Eene beschouwing van het Verbond der genade_, 2nd ed., 1868, p. 68, and so forth. Further, the distinction between _fidejussor_ and _expromissor_ was borrowed from jurists, and the question was treated whether Christ in the _pactum salutis_ had taken upon himself the sins of the Old Testament elect conditionally or absolutely. The former was maintained by Cocceius, _De foed._ § 150–162; Wittichius, _Theol. pacif._ § 290; Allinga, van Til, d’Outrein, Perizonius, and others; cf. Heppe, _Dogm._ 270. The latter was maintained by Leydecker, _Fax Verit._ V 7; _Vis Verit._ II 1; _Filius Dei sponsor_, 1674; Turretin, _Th. El._ XII 9; Mastricht V 1, 34; Voetius, _Disp._ V 346; Moor IV 569–580; M. Vitringa III 12, and so forth. And finally the disputed point was also discussed whether this _pactum salutis_ bore more the character of a testament, with an appeal to Luke 22:19; John 17:24; Heb. 6:17; 8:6; 9:15; 13:20, as Cocceius, Burman, Heidegger, and Schiere, _Doctrina test. et foed._ I c. 10, asserted, or of a covenant, as Leydecker, _Fax Verit._ V 6; Wesselius, and others maintained. Nevertheless, despite its defective form, this doctrine of the _pactum salutis_ rests upon a scriptural thought. As Mediator, the Son is subordinate to the Father. He calls him his God, Ps. 22:3; John 20:17. He is his servant, Isa. 49 f., to whom a work is assigned to do, Isa. 53:10; John 6:38–40; 10:18; 12:49; 14:31; 17:4, and who receives a reward for accomplished obedience, Matt. 26:42; John 4:34; 15:10; 17:4, 5; 19:30; Ps. 2:8; Isa. 53:10; John 17:4, 11, 17, 24; Eph. 1:20 f.; Phil. 2:9 f. This relation between Father and Son, although coming most clearly to expression during Christ’s walk on earth, did not first begin at the moment of the incarnation, for this incarnation already belongs to the execution of the work assigned to the Son. Rather, it falls in eternity and thus also already existed during the time of the Old Testament. Scripture also teaches this clearly when it ascribes the guidance of Israel to the Angel of YHWH, Ex. 3:2 f.; 13:21; 14:19; 23:20–23; 32:34; 33:2; Num. 20:16; Isa. 63:8, 9, and lets Christ already function officially in the days of the Old Testament, John 8:56; 1 Cor. 10:4, 9; 1 Pet. 1:11; 3:19. After all, there is only one Mediator between God and human beings, John 14:6; Acts 4:12; 1 Tim. 2:5, who is the same yesterday and today and forever, Heb. 13:8, who from eternity was chosen as Mediator, Isa. 42:1; 43:10; Matt. 12:18; Luke 24:26; Acts 2:23; 4:28; 1 Pet. 1:20; Rev. 13:8, and as Logos also existed eternally, John 1:1, 3; 8:58; Rom. 8:3; 2 Cor. 8:9; Gal. 4:4; Phil. 2:6, and so forth. Through all this Scripture gives us a rich and glorious conception of the work of redemption. The _pactum salutis_ enables us to know the relation and life of the three persons in the divine being as a covenant life, as a life of the highest self-consciousness and the highest freedom. Here, within the divine being, the covenant has its full reality. While the covenant between God and the human being, because of the infinite distance between them, always bears more or less the character of a sovereign disposition, διαθηκη, here among the three persons it is a συνθηκη in the fullest sense. Here the highest freedom and the most perfect agreement coincide. The work of salvation is a work of the three persons, to which all contribute and in which each performs his particular task. In the decrees, including that of predestination, the one will of God came to the foreground and the trinitarian character did not yet come out so clearly. But here in the _pactum salutis_ the work of redemption comes out in its full divine beauty. It is the divine work _par excellence_. Just as in the creation of the human being God first deliberately takes counsel with himself, Gen. 1:26, so in re-creation each of the three persons acts still more clearly in his distinct character. Re-creation, like creation, is a work of God alone. From him, through him, and to him are all things. No human being has been his counselor or first given to him, that it should be repaid. It is the triune God alone—Father, Son, and Spirit—who together devise, determine, execute, and bring to completion the whole work of salvation. Further, this _pactum salutis_ also connects God’s eternal work for salvation with what he does toward it in time. The covenant of grace revealed in time does not hang in the air but rests upon an eternal, immutable foundation. It is established in the counsel and covenant of the triune God and is their infallibly following application and execution. Indeed, in the covenant of grace that God establishes with human beings in time, the human being is not the acting and active party. Rather, it is once again the triune God who, after designing the work of re-creation, also brings it about. It is not the case that God first establishes his covenant with Adam and Noah, with Abraham and Israel, and only finally with Christ. Rather, the covenant of grace lies ready from eternity in the _pactum salutis_ of the three persons and is realized by them from the moment after the fall. Christ does not first begin to work with and after his incarnation, and the Holy Spirit does not first commence his labor with his outpouring on the day of Pentecost. Just as creation is a trinitarian work, so too re-creation from its first moment has been a work of the three persons. All grace flowing to creation after the fall comes to it from the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit. Immediately after the fall the Son appeared as Mediator, as the second and last Adam, who takes the place of the first and restores and accomplishes what he corrupted and left undone. And the Holy Spirit immediately appeared as the Comforter, as the applier of the salvation to be acquired by Christ. All change, development, and progress in insight and knowledge therefore falls on the side of the creature. In God there is no change or shadow of turning. The Father is eternally Father, the Son eternally Mediator, and the Holy Spirit eternally Comforter. Therefore the Old Testament too is to be understood as one in essence and substance with the New Testament. For although God communicates his revelation successively and historically and makes it richer and fuller, and humanity thus advances in knowledge, possession, and enjoyment of revelation, God is the same and remains so. The sun gradually illuminates the earth, but it itself is the same in the morning and evening, by day and by night. Although Christ first accomplished his work on earth in the middle of history and the Holy Spirit was first poured out on the day of Pentecost, God could nevertheless also distribute in full during the days of the Old Testament the benefits to be acquired and applied by them. The believers of the Old Testament were saved in no other way than we. There is one faith, one Mediator, one way of salvation, one covenant of grace. Cf. Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ II 135 f.; Dr. Vos, _De Verbondsleer in de Geref. Theol._, Grand Rapids, 1891.
