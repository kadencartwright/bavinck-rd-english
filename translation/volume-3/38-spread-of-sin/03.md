## 3.

Pelagianism was condemned by the Christian church.
From the beginning the church fathers assumed a certain connection between Adam’s sin and that of his race.
Even though this connection was not yet thought through, Adam’s transgression nevertheless brought about a great moral change for himself and for his descendants.
The nature of that moral change, however, was understood in very different ways.
According to Semi-Pelagianism, the consequences of Adam’s fall for himself and his descendants consisted, besides death, chiefly in the weakening of moral power.
There is indeed no original sin in the proper sense that would constitute guilt, but there is nevertheless an _Erbübel_; through Adam’s fall the human being became morally sick; his will was weakened and inclined toward evil; a conflict arose in him between flesh and spirit that makes it impossible for a human being to live without sin; yet he can will the good, and when he does so, grace comes to his aid in accomplishing it, Wiggers, _Aug. u. Pelag._ II 54–82.
The Greek church occupies this standpoint, vol. II, p. 319; and although Augustine had much influence in the West, the church nevertheless strayed more and more toward Semi-Pelagianism and taught at Trent that freedom of the will was indeed weakened but not destroyed and that _concupiscentia_ is not in itself sin, cf. vol. II, p. 322 f.
Entirely in agreement with this is the opinion of the Anabaptists, in Moor III 205; of Zwingli, cf. my _Ethiek van Zwingli_, pp. 17, 18; of the Remonstrants, _Conf._, c. 7, and _Apol. Conf._, ibid.; Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV, sect. 5, c. 2; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ III, c. 2; of the Moravians, Plitt, _Zinzendorfs Theol._ II 213 f.; of the supernaturalists, Reinhard, _Dogm._ §§ 81–84; Krabbe, _Lehre v. d. Sünde u. v. Tode_ 148 f.; and of many more recent theologians, Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 485 f.; H. Schmidt in Herzog² 15, 31; Kaftan, _Wesen der chr. Rel._ 260 f.; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._ 325; van Oosterzee, _Dogm._ II 278 f.; Doedes, _Leer der Zaligheid_, no. 27, and _Ned. Gel._, art. 15; etc.
All agree that Adam’s fall also had consequences for his descendants because they stand in physical connection with him.
But the moral condition that entered the human race as a consequence of Adam’s transgression is not a condition of sin and guilt but of weakness, defect, and sickness.
In itself this original sin cannot condemn the human being and at most entails a _poena damni_ without a _poena sensus_; it is an occasion for sin but not sin itself in the proper sense.
Because the will is weakened, however, it readily heeds the temptation that proceeds from the flesh; and then, when the will consents and yields to concupiscence, original sin becomes personal sin, which renders guilty and deserving of punishment.
In substance this doctrine of original sin agrees entirely with the theory that sin proceeds from sensuousness, Biedermann, _Dogm._ II 573.

This Semi-Pelagian conception of original sin, however, is fundamentally not much better than that of Pelagius and encounters equally serious objections.
1º. It fails to recognize the character and seriousness of sin.
For sin is ἀνομια, _dislegalitas_.
The condition in which human beings are born either accords with God’s law or deviates from it; it is good or evil, sinless or sinful; there is no third possibility.
Even the Semi-Pelagians do not dare to claim that this condition is good and accords in every part with God’s law.
Yet neither do they call it sinful in the proper sense.
And so they create an intermediate condition and speak of original sin as a sickness, defect, or illness that is not sin in the proper sense but can only be an occasion for sin; or they separate sin and guilt and say, as do Rothe and Kaftan, that original sin is indeed sin but not guilt.
2º. Both alternatives are impossible.
Sin and guilt are inseparably connected with each other, Gal. 3:10; James 2:10; 1 John 5:17; if sin is ἀνομια, then it is punishable, and conversely, where guilt and punishment are present, sin must be present.
Original sin, however, is of such a nature that death follows upon it, Rom. 5:14; that it makes one unworthy of fellowship with God and of his heaven (Doedes); that it is unclean in itself; and that it is the occasion and source of many sins; it must therefore itself be sin.
Otherwise God would be unjust, punishing with death—the wages of sin, Rom. 6:23—what is not sin and does not deserve death; the law would lose its absolute validity, for there would be a deviation that deserved no punishment; fellowship with God would be withheld without any question of guilt; between heaven and hell, good and evil, light and darkness, there would arise a condition that was neither, a _poena damni_ without a _poena sensus_; that which produces all manner of sins would itself be no sin; the tree would be good and yet bear evil fruits; the spring would be pure but the water flowing from it unclean.
3º. The claim that this innate sinfulness first becomes sin and guilt when the will consents to it does not improve the theory but makes it still worse.
For one of two things is true: either the will stands, as it were, above and outside that innate inclination, in which case original sin consists of nothing but the innate sensuous nature and the whole character of sin is lost; or the will itself has been affected and weakened to a greater or lesser degree by that original sin, is itself rooted in and arises from the sinful nature, in which case, precisely to the same degree that one allows the will to be weakened, one loses what this theory intended to maintain, namely, that there is no sin without a free decision of the will.
Moreover, even if such a will, standing wholly or partly outside the innate sinful nature, were conceivable, it would not in fact supply what one intends by it.
The first decisions of the will that consent to innate _concupiscentia_ all occur in the earliest years, when the will is still weak and powerless.
No one is conscious that with those first decisions of the will he incurred such guilt that only then did he actually fall and become a child of wrath.
Over against anyone who claims this, every person could excuse himself by saying that he knew no better and could do no other, that for such a weighty decision concerning his eternal weal or woe he had been placed in very unfavorable circumstances; indeed, if original sin is no sin, then all other later sins that flow from it so readily and so necessarily can be no sins either.
Schleiermacher therefore also rejected the conception that original sin cannot be guilt before it breaks out in actual sins, _indem ja der Umstand, dass es noch an Gelegenheit und an äusserer Anreizung gefehlt hat, den geistigen Werth des Menschen nicht erhöhen kann_, _Chr. Gl._ I 382.
4º. The Semi-Pelagian theory not only fails to solve the problem before us but does not even touch it and deliberately closes its eyes to it.
The universality of sin is a fact that the Semi-Pelagians also acknowledge; they reject its explanation from imitation; they assume that an unclean, defective, sick, sinful condition—even if it is supposedly not sinful and punishable—precedes sinful deeds; they acknowledge that this unclean, sick condition leads everyone without distinction to sinful, guilty, punishable deeds, so that the weakened free will in fact amounts to very little.
How, then, is this dreadful phenomenon to be explained?
How can it be reconciled with God’s righteousness that, leaving aside the covenant of grace, he allows all human beings to be born in such a condition, one that in the case of children who die very young entails at any rate death and banishment from his fellowship and in the case of all others eternal destruction?
The Semi-Pelagian theory does not think through this problem at all and contents itself with a superficial and meaningless doctrine of free will.
