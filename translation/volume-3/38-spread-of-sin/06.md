## 6.

The doctrine of original sin is one of the most important but also one of the most difficult subjects in dogmatics. _Peccato originali nihil ad praedicandum notius, nihil ad intelligendum secretius_, Augustine, _de mor. eccl. cath._ I 22. _Chose étonnante, que le mystère le plus éloigné de notre connaissance, qui est celui de la transmission du péché, soit une chose sans laquelle nous ne pouvons avoir aucune connaissance de nous-mêmes. Car il est sans doute, qu’il n’y a rien qui choque plus notre raison que de dire que le péché du premier homme ait rendu coupables ceux qui, étant si éloignés de cette source, semblent incapables d’y participer.... et cependant, sans ce mystère, le plus incompréhensible de tous, nous sommes incompréhensibles à nous-mêmes. Le noeud de notre condition prend ses replis et ses tours dans cet abîme, de sorte que l’homme est plus inconcevable sans ce mystère que ce mystère n’est inconcevable à l’homme_ (Pascal). _Le péché originel explique tout et sans lui on n’explique rien_ (de Maistre), and yet it itself needs explanation more than anything else. From ancient times it was designated in theology as _peccatum originale_, not as though it belonged to the human being from his origin by virtue of creation, but because in all human beings it is the origin and source of all other sins. Much misunderstanding is avoided if this original sin is clearly distinguished into _peccatum originans_ (_imputatum, culpa_) and _peccatum originatum_ (_inhaerens, poena_). Strictly speaking, by original sin, _peccatum haereditarium_, is to be understood only that moral corruption which the human being brings with him immediately at his conception and birth from sinful parents. But this moral corruption, which belongs to all human beings by nature and does not first arise in them later through their own wrongful deeds, must nevertheless have a cause. And according to Holy Scripture, this cause is and for Christian thought can be no other than the first transgression of the first human being, through which sin and death came into the world. Adam’s disobedience is the _peccatum originans_; Scripture says this clearly, Rom. 5:12; 1 Cor. 15:22; and experience confirms it at every moment: all human beings are conceived in sins and born in iniquity. This is conceivable only if that transgression of Adam concerns us all in one way or another. If absolutely no connection existed between Adam and us, it would be impossible for us to be born in sins because he transgressed God’s commandment. Scripture and history thus point us together to an original, common guilt of the human race. The hypothesis of Plato and others, after all, that every soul had already existed for a long time before its entrance into the human body and had already fallen there, was shown above, p. 70, to be wholly untenable for decisive reasons; it rests only on the undeniable truth that every human being is born under guilt. Each person has not brought this guilt upon himself personally, individually, and actually; it rests upon every human being on Adam’s account: δια νης παρακοης του ἐνος ἀνθρωπον ἁμαρτωλοι κατεσταθησαν oἱ πολλοι, Rom. 5:19. Apart from whether we can make it at all intelligible that God immediately constitutes all human beings sinners through and on account of Adam’s disobedience, the fact itself is established on the basis of Scripture and experience. Yet something may still be said, if not to explain this way of God, then at least to remove from it the appearance of arbitrariness. In the first place, humanity is not an aggregate of individuals but an organic unity, one race, one family. The angels all stand independently alongside one another; they were all created at the same time and did not proceed one from another. Among them a judgment of God such as was pronounced in Adam over all human beings would have been impossible; each stood and fell for himself. But it is not so among human beings. God made them all from one blood, Acts 17:26; they are not a heap of souls on a piece of ground but are all related to one another by blood, joined to one another by all kinds of bonds, and therefore in everything determining one another and being determined by one another. The first human being in particular occupies an altogether unique and incomparable place. As _rami in radice, massa in primitiis, membra in capite_, so all human beings were contained in Adam’s loins, and they have all sprung from his loins. He was no private person, no isolated individual alongside others, but was _radix, stirps, principium seminale totius generis humani_, the _caput naturale_ of us all. In a certain sense it can be said that _nos omnes ille unus homo fuimus_, that what he did was done by us all in him; his choice and act of will was that of all his descendants. Undoubtedly, this physical unity of the whole of humanity in Adam is already of great significance for explaining original sin; it is the necessary presupposition, its _praerequisitum_. If Christ was to be able to bear sin for us and make us partakers of his righteousness, he first of all had to assume our human nature. Yet realism by itself is insufficient to explain original sin. For in a certain sense it can indeed be said that all human beings were contained in Adam, but then only in a certain definite sense; it is true _repraesentative_ but not _physice_. Nor does anyone speak that way in the covenant of grace. We can and may indeed say that God appropriates the righteousness of Christ to us as if we had accomplished all the obedience that Christ accomplished for us, Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 60, but that does not mean that we personally and _physice_ are the ones who satisfied God’s justice; Christ made satisfaction for us and in our place. And so it is also with Adam; _virtualiter, potentialiter, seminaliter_ we may have been contained in him, but _personaliter_ and _actualiter_ he transgressed the probationary command, not we. If realism refused to acknowledge this distinction and wished to be consistent to the utmost, it would make all imputation superfluous with both Adam and Christ; in both cases, it would then be every human being himself who personally sinned by the deed and made satisfaction through his suffering and death. Further, if Adam’s transgression in this realistic sense was ours, then the human being is also guilty of all the other sins of Adam, all the sins of Eve, indeed all the sins of his ancestors from whom he was born, for he was contained in these just as much as in Adam when Adam transgressed the probationary command. Nor can it then be understood how Christ, who _physice_, that is, as far as the flesh is concerned, is from the fathers and from Adam and Eve, could be free from original sin; on this position, physical unity necessarily brings moral unity with it. Furthermore, realism faces no slight difficulty in the covenant of grace, for if there is no _foedus operum_, then neither is there a _foedus gratiae_; the one stands and falls with the other. Now if the righteousness of Christ is not acquired and applied by way of the covenant but in a realistic way, then this consists, in Christ’s case, in his having assumed our nature, and in that case satisfaction and salvation are the portion of all human beings, for Christ assumed the nature of them all; or it consists in each person’s first obtaining this physical, realistic unity with Christ through regeneration or faith, and then it cannot be understood how Christ could make satisfaction beforehand for those with whom he first becomes one through faith. Regeneration and faith then run the risk of losing their ethical character, the center of gravity is shifted from Christ to the Christian, and the benefits of the covenant first come into existence after and through faith. Finally, realism does defend an excellent interest, namely, the unity of the human race, but in doing so it loses sight of another interest that is no less weighty, namely, the independence of personality. A human being is certainly a member of the whole, but he also occupies his own place in that whole; he is more than a wave in the ocean, more than a passing form in which universal human nature appears. It was therefore already observed earlier, vol. II, 551 f., that the relations in which human beings stand to one another differ from those found among angels and among animals; for though related to both, the human being also differs from both; he is a creature with a character of his own. Physical unity is therefore not sufficient for him; another unity, an ethical and federal one, must be added. Cf., against Shedd, who is a strong advocate of realism, _Dogm. Theol._ II, p. 6 etc., also Arch. Alex. Hodge, _The Atonement_, Philadelphia: Presbyterian Board of Publication, n.d., p. 99 etc.

As soon as serious reflection began in the Christian church on the connection between Adam’s sin and ours, physical unity was not enough. Shedd does claim that Augustine, the scholastics, and the oldest Reformed theologians were all realists, loc. cit., II 37. But this is incorrect; the doctrine of the covenant had not been worked out, but the thought already occurs among the church fathers and the medieval theologians; cf. vol. II, 549; above, p. 124; and further Schwane, _D. G._ III 393 f.; IV 166 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ II 711; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch. d. Menschheit_, 165, 167; Scheeben, _Dogm._ I 500; Pesch, _Prael._ III 136, and so forth. The single fact that nearly all of them adhered to creationism already says enough, for a creationist cannot be a realist. Federalism therefore does not exclude the truth concealed in realism; on the contrary, it accepts it fully. It proceeds from it but does not stop there; it recognizes a _unitas naturae, cui unitas foederalis est innixa_. In humanity we encounter all kinds of forms of community that rest by no means only, or even chiefly, on physical descent, but on another, higher, moral unity. There are “moral bodies”—household, family, society, people, state, church, and associations and societies of all kinds and for every purpose—which lead a life of their own and are subject to special laws, in particular also to the law Paul formulates when he says: και εἰτε πασχει ἑν μελος, συμπασχει παντα τα μελη, εἰτε δοξαζεται μελος, συγχαιρει παντα τα μελη, I Cor. 12:26. All the members of such a body can be a blessing or a curse to one another, and the more so according as they themselves are more eminent and occupy a more important place in the organism. A father, mother, guardian, caretaker, educator, teacher, employer, guide, prince, king, and so forth have the greatest influence upon those placed under them. Their life and conduct decide the lot of their subordinates, raising them up and bringing them honor or casting them down and dragging them along to ruin. The household of a drunkard is devastated and covered with shame because of the father’s sin. The family of a criminal is associated and condemned with him over a wide circle and for a long time. A congregation languishes under the faithlessness of its minister. A people perishes through the folly of its prince. _Quidquid delirant reges, plectuntur Achivi._ Among human beings there is a solidarity in good and evil, a sharing in blessings and judgments. We stand on the shoulders of our ancestors and inherit what they have amassed in material and spiritual capital; we enter into their labor, rest on their laurels, and enjoy what they often obtained through struggle and suffering. We receive all of this undeservedly, without having asked for it; it all lies ready at our birth and is granted to us by grace. No one objects to this or rebels against this law. But when the same law also begins to prevail in evil and makes us participants in the sin and suffering of others, the mind rises in rebellion and the law is accused of injustice. The son who accepts his father’s inheritance refuses to pay his father’s debt. So too the Israelites complained in the days of Ezekiel. In the Old Testament a law of solidarity applied, Gen. 9:25; Ex. 20:5; Num. 14:33; 16:32; Josh. 7:24, 25; 1 Sam. 15:2, 3; 2 Sam. 12:10; 21:1 f.; 1 Kings 21:21, 23; Isa. 6:5; Jer. 32:18; Lam. 3:40 f.; 5:7; Ezra 9:6; Matt. 23:35; 27:25. But when Israel in its imagined righteousness complains about this, the Lord has the prophet proclaim not what he can justly do but what he will do if Israel repents and does not walk in the way of the fathers. There is a solidarity of sin and suffering, but God permits a person, and often grants him the strength, to break that moral communion and himself become the beginning of a race that walks in the fear of the Lord and enjoys his favor. Far from abolishing solidarity itself, this rather confirms it. Christ has proved the truth of the solidarity of the human race in still another and better way than Adam. If this solidarity could also be broken, not only all compassion but also all love, friendship, intercession, and so forth would cease to exist; humanity would fall apart into lifeless atoms; there would be no mystery, no mysticism, no human life any longer. Yet what Shedd claims is true, _Dogm. Theol._ II 187, that this solidarity of suffering does not yet explain the imputation of Adam’s sin to all his descendants. To suffer because of another’s sin is not the same as to be punished for another’s sin and therefore oneself be regarded as the perpetrator of that sin; there is suffering without personal transgression, Luke 13:1–5; John 9:3. But this solidarity, which we see daily, nevertheless deprives us of the argument for accusing God of injustice when he makes the whole of humanity share in Adam’s punishment. After all, he acts this way at every moment, both in blessings and in judgments. If such a way of acting is compatible with his justice, then this is and must also be the case with Adam’s transgression. But there is in addition a special reason why the aforementioned law of solidarity does not and indeed cannot apply altogether in Adam’s case. The law of solidarity does not explain the covenant of works and the covenant of grace but is built upon them and points back to them. It always prevails within circles narrower than that formed by humanity itself. However great the blessing or curse of parents and guardians, philosophers and artists, founders of religions and reformers, princes and conquerors, and so forth may have been, there were always “circumstances” of place, time, country, people, language, and so forth that set bounds to it; the circle in which their influence prevailed was always enclosed by other and larger circles. Only two human beings have existed whose life and work extended to the boundaries of humanity itself, whose influence and dominion continue to operate to the ends of the earth and into eternity. They are Adam and Christ; the former brought sin and death, the latter righteousness and life into the world. It follows from this entirely exceptional place occupied by Adam and Christ that they alone can be compared with each other and that all other relationships, drawn from circles within humanity, can indeed serve as clarification and are of great value, but nevertheless offer only analogy and no identity. This means that Adam and Christ were both placed under an altogether special ordinance of God, precisely with a view to the special place they occupy in humanity. When a father plunges his household, a prince his people, a philosopher his pupils, or an employer his workers into misery along with himself, we can go back behind their persons and, in the solidarity that prevails within humanity and its various circles, find explanation and satisfaction to a certain extent. But we cannot do so with Adam and Christ. They have humanity not behind but before them; they do not proceed from it but bring it into existence; they are not borne by it but themselves bear it; they are not a product but, each in his own way, the beginning and root of humanity, _caput totius generis humani_. They are not explained by the law of solidarity but explain it only through themselves; they do not presuppose but constitute the organism of humanity. If humanity was truly to remain a unity in both a physical and an ethical sense, as it was destined to be; if there was thus truly to exist in that humanity not only a community of blood, as among the animals, but on that basis also a community of all material, moral, and spiritual goods, then this could be brought about and maintained only by judging all in one. As it fared with them, so it would fare with the whole human race. If Adam fell, humanity fell; if Christ remained standing, humanity was raised up in him. The covenant of works and the covenant of grace are the forms through which the organism of humanity is maintained also in a religious and ethical sense. Because God is concerned not with isolated individuals but with humanity as his image and likeness, it therefore had to fall in one and also to be raised up in one. Such is the ordinance, such the judgment of God. He declares all guilty in one, and therefore humanity is born from Adam unclean and dying; he declares all righteous in one, and therefore that same humanity is reborn from Christ and sanctified unto eternal life. God has shut them all up under obedience so that he might have mercy upon them all. Turretin, _Theol. El._ IX 9; A. A. Hodge, _The Atonement_, p. 78–121; Ch. Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 192; _Princeton Theol. Essays_, 1846, p. 128–194; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ II 704 f.; Thomasius, _Christi Person u. Werk_ I³ 211; E. Bersier, _La solidarité_, Paris 1870; Vercueil, _Etude sur la solidarité dans le Christianisme d’après St. Paul_, Montauban 1894.
