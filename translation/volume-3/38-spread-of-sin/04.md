## 4.

Paul, however, instructed by the contrast with Christ, had answered this problem by saying that through the trespass of one man sin and death entered the world and passed to all human beings. Gradually Christian theology awakened to an awareness of this profound doctrine; cf. Schwane, _D. G._ II² 439 f., 480 f. Irenaeus said: _in primo quidem Adam (Deum) offendimus, non facientes ejus praeceptum, in secundo autem Adam reconciliati sumus, obedientes usque ad mortem facti_, _Adv. haer._ V 16, 3. Tertullian spoke of a _malum animae_ that has come to us _ex originis vitio_ and is to be derived from Adam’s fall: _omnis anima eo usque in Adam censetur donec in Christo recenseatur, tamdiu immunda, quamdiu recenseatur_, _De an._ 40, 41; cf. also Cyprian, _De op. et eleem._ 1; _Ep._ 64, 5. Ambrose speaks most strongly. More than those before him, he emphasizes the culpable character of all sin and the sinful condition in which we are born, and he traces this condition back to Adam’s fall: _fuit Adam et in illo fuimus omnes, periit Adam et in illo omnes perierunt_, Harnack, _D. G._ III 45. Yet it was especially Augustine who took hold of Paul’s thought and developed it further. Again and again, especially in his two works against Julian, he appeals to Rom. 5:12; 1 Cor. 15:22; Eph. 2:3. He also cites Cyprian, Hilary, Ambrose, Gregory of Nazianzus, Chrysostom, and others in defense of his view. Then he also sees in the practice of infant baptism a powerful proof of his doctrine of original sin. And finally he points out that the dreadful misery of the human race can be explained only as a punishment for sin. How can God, who is nevertheless good and just, subject all human beings from their conception onward to sin and death if they are completely innocent? An original guilt must rest upon them all; otherwise the _grave jugum_ that weighs upon all Adam’s children cannot be understood. Whoever surveys the miseries of human life, from the first cries of infants to the final sighs of the dying, must come with Paul to the acknowledgment of an _originale peccatum_. _Quoniam Dei non est iniquum judicium, ideo in miseria generis humani, quae incipit a fletibus parvulorum, agnoscendum est originale peccatum_, _Op. imperf. c. Jul._ III 77, and so _passim_, I 25, 49; II 107; III 44, 202; VI 17, 28, and so forth. Adam’s sin must therefore be understood as an act of his and of all his descendants. Adam was not a private person, not an individual alongside others, but all human beings were comprehended in him. Augustine does not make the manner of this entirely clear. Since, on the question of the soul’s origin, he refrained from choosing between traducianism and creationism, he likewise does not state decisively here whether, in speaking of the human race’s being comprehended in Adam, he thinks only realistically or also federally. On the one hand, he repeatedly says that all were in Adam’s loins, just as the Israelites were in Abraham’s; that Adam could not bring forth anyone better than he himself was; that Adam’s sin is transmitted through propagation and not through imitation; and that Adam’s sin becomes ours through birth in the same manner that Christ’s righteousness and life become ours through regeneration, _De civ._ XIII 3; _Op. imp. c. Jul._ I 48, and so forth. But on the other hand, it is nevertheless significant that he does not accept Tertullian’s traducianism and that he repeatedly states as emphatically as possible that all were in Adam and sinned in him: _omnes ille unus homo fuerunt, omnes fuimus in illo uno_, _De pecc. mer. et rem._ I 10; _De civ._ XIII 14, and so forth. Original sin is distinguished from actual sins in that it was not personally committed by us, yet it is nevertheless sin because in a certain sense it was again our act. It is both another’s sin and our own: _aliena enim (sunt peccata originalia), quia non ea in sua vita quisque commisit; nostra vero, quia fuit Adam et in illo fuimus omnes_, _Op. imp. c. Jul._ III 25; cf. I 48, 57. It is even a _peccatum voluntarium, quia ex primi hominis mala voluntate contractum, factum est quodam modo haereditarium_, _Retract._ I 13; _C. Jul._ III 5; _De nupt. et conc._ II 28, and so forth. The sinful condition in which we are conceived and born is a consequence and punishment of our transgression in Adam, _Op. imp. c. Jul._ I 47; VI 17. God frequently punishes sin with sin, _C. Jul._ V 3; _De nat. et gr._ 22. And in fact this condition consists in _concupiscentia_. Augustine sometimes takes this word in a broad sense and says that inherited sinfulness is seated not only in sexual desire but can be known _in quocunque corporis sensu_, _Op. imp. c. Jul._ IV 28; that _carnalis concupiscentia_ also has its seat in the soul, ibid. V 7; and that original sin is not a substance but an _affectionalis qualitas_, a _vitium_, _languor_, _morbus_, _substantiae accidens_, _De nupt. et conc._ I 24, 25; II 34; _Op. imp. c. Jul._ VI 7; _Conf._ VII 12, and so forth. Yet in speaking of _concupiscentia_ he thinks first of all of sexual desire. The corruption of nature is displayed especially in the autonomous _motus genitalium_, independent of the will, and shame serves as proof of it. Sin is therefore also propagated through the sexual drive and makes the whole of humanity a _massa corrupta_ subject to the _misera necessitas non posse non peccandi_; cf. especially _De nupt. et conc._, _passim_; Harnack, _D. G._ III 190 f.; Wiggers, _Aug. u. Pelag._ I 107 f. This _concupiscentia_ is better called _peccatum originale quam naturale_, since it is not of divine but of human origin, _Op. imp. c. Jul._ V 9. It is sin, _quia peccato facta est appetitque peccare_, ibid. I 71, and makes the human being _originaliter reum_, _C. Jul._ VI 5. Infants who die unbaptized perish on account of it. Its guilt, however, is removed in baptism, while it itself remains as an incentive to the struggle: _reatus ejus regeneratione solutus est, conflictus ejus ad agonem relictus est_, _Op. imp._ I 71, 101; _C. Jul._ VI 5; _De pecc. mer. et rem._ II 4; cf. also Gangauf, _Die metaph. Psych. d. h. Aug._ 420 f. Scholastic and Roman Catholic theology built upon this foundation but nevertheless introduced a not insignificant modification into Augustine’s conception. The thought was retained that Adam’s transgression was the cause of the sin and death of all human beings. Original sin consists first of all in the imputation to all human beings of the transgression that Adam committed, because they were all comprehended in him. It is guilt in the first place, and then punishment. Scripture accordingly expressed this clearly, and the church included it in its confession: Council of Milevis, can. 2; Council of Orange, c. 2; Trent V 2; _Cat. Rom._ I 3, 2. Pighius and Catharinus even went so far as to make original sin consist entirely in this imputation of Adam’s transgression and to regard everything that followed from it—the loss of original righteousness, the corruption of nature, and so forth—only as punishment and not as sin, in Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ V c. 16. But these theologians undoubtedly thereby expressed a thought that flowed from the development of the doctrine of original sin in Roman Catholic theology. For disagreement soon arose over the character of that moral condition which entered Adam and all his descendants after his disobedience. For Augustine, it consisted in _concupiscentia_, whose principal seat and organ was then the sexual drive. Lombard still held to this, _Sent._ II, dist. 30, 31. But gradually the doctrine arose of the _donum superadditum_, which had been bestowed on Adam but was lost through his fall. The first consequence of Adam’s transgression for him and for all human beings was therefore the loss of the _gratia supernaturalis_ (_justitia originalis_). This was the first, negative element in original sin, to which the second, positive element—namely, _concupiscentia_—was then added. Anselm already defined original sin in this way, _De casu diaboli_ 27, and was followed in this by Hales, Bonaventure, Albert, and Thomas. Original sin thus consisted in _privatio originalis justitiae_ and in _quaedam inordinata naturae dispositio_ (_concupiscentia_), Thomas, _S. Th._ II 1, qu. 82, art. 1; and further Schwane, _D. G._ III 393–413. Upon further reflection, however, disagreement had to arise again over this latter element. For the image of God came gradually to be conceived as a _donum supernaturale_, vol. II, p. 518. A human being without that image and yet without sin—a _homo naturalis_—was therefore also conceivable and capable of existence. In such a human being, however, flesh and spirit would naturally stand opposed and wage war against each other. That is, _concupiscentia_, the desire of the flesh against the spirit, belongs to the human being by nature, necessarily, by creation, and therefore cannot in itself be sin. The image of God had indeed been bestowed on Adam as a _remedium_ and _frenum_, but when he lost it the conflict of flesh and spirit naturally arose again. This conflict lay in his nature; it had indeed been suppressed but was now set free again. _Concupiscentia_ is a _morbus seu languor naturae humanae, qui ex conditione materiae oriebatur_, vol. II, p. 527. It cannot in itself be sin and therefore cannot constitute part of original sin. The Roman Catholic doctrine of the _donum superadditum_ takes its revenge here in a troubling way within the doctrine of original sin. It can be shown historically how, for this reason, the center of gravity in the Roman Catholic doctrine of original sin gradually shifted from _concupiscentia_ to _privatio justitiae originalis_, from the positive to the negative. Augustine defined the whole of original sin by _concupiscentia_. The scholastics included with it the loss of original righteousness, yet still maintained original sin in a positive sense as an _inordinata dispositio_, _languor naturae_, _habitus corruptus_, Thomas, _S. Th._ II 1, qu. 82, art. 1. Trent speaks very cautiously. It says that Adam not only lost righteousness but also changed for the worse in body and soul; that after he was defiled, he poured out upon his descendants not only death and the punishments of the body but above all sin; that Adam’s sin belongs to each one not through imitation but through propagation, _inest unicuique proprium_, and can be removed only through Christ’s merit in baptism, sess. 5. But the Synod deliberately refrained from narrower definitions, Möhler, _Symb._ 57. The nature of this sin is not further defined. The words _in deterius_ say little. The _concupiscentia_ that remains in the baptized is itself not sin but only _ex peccato et ad peccatum inclinat_, ibid. 5. Free will has not been lost but weakened, VI c. 5, and it can perform good works even before faith, ibid. 7. Taken together, it is impossible to see wherein original sin could further consist apart from the imputation of Adam’s transgression and the loss of original righteousness. Nothing remains for it. After Trent, therefore, Lombard’s opinion—later still embraced by Henry, Gregory of Rimini, and Driedo—that original sin consists _formaliter_ or _materialiter_ in _concupiscentia_, in a positive _qualitas_, is expressly opposed, Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ V c. 15; Becanus, _Theol. schol._, _De pecc. orig._, qu. 3 and 4; _Theol. Wirceb._ VII 84–94. Appealing to Thomas, Bonaventure, Scotus, and others, original sin is located only in the loss of _justitia originalis_. Bellarmine stated it openly and clearly: _non magis differt status hominis post lapsum Adae a statu ejusdem in puris naturalibus, quam differat spoliatus a nudo, neque deterior est humana natura, si culpam originalem detrahas, neque magis infirmitate et ignorantia laborat, quam esset et laborasset in puris naturalibus condita. Proinde corruptio naturae non ex alicujus doni naturalis carentia neque ex alicujus malae qualitatis accessu sed ex sola doni supernaturalis ob Adae peccatum amissione profluxit_, _De gr. pr. hom._ 5. The condition in which the human being is born after the fall is completely identical with Adam’s condition before the fall without the _donum superadditum_. One can speak of a _corruptio_ or _vulneratio naturae_ only insofar as this condition ought not to exist, since Adam received the _donum superadditum_ and lost it; its loss is guilt. But materially this condition is not wrong; it is _nackte Natürlichkeit_. Original sin is nothing other than _reductio ad statum mere naturalem_; _supernaturalia amissa, naturalia integra_. In baptism this loss is repaired through _gratia infusa_. After death original sin is punished only with _poena damni_. Some indeed still attempt to maintain a _corruptio naturae_, but the supernaturalistic conception of Christianity makes this impossible. There remains only disagreement over whether the imputation of Adam’s sin, which resulted in the loss of the _donum superadditum_ for all his descendants, rests on a _nexus physicus_ or on a _nexus moralis_ (_foederalis_). Some say that all human beings were comprehended in Adam, if not _formaliter_, nevertheless _causaliter_, _materialiter_, _seminaliter_. Others believe that the imputation can be explained only by assuming that Adam was not only our progenitor but also our head and representative. Still others combine both conceptions. Cf. Anselm, _De conceptu originali et de originali peccato_; Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 30–33, and the commentaries of Thomas, Bonaventure, and others; Thomas, _S. Theol._ II 1, qu. 81 sq.; Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr._ V 17–20; Becanus, _Theol. Schol._ II, tr. 2, c. 9, _De pecc. orig._; _Theol. Wirceb._ VII 65 sq.; Vasquez, Suárez, and others in Schwane, _D. G. der neueren Zeit_, 1890, pp. 166–198; Schwetz, _Theol. cath._ II 163–177; Dens, _Theol._ I 356 sq.; Perrone, _Prael. theol._ III, 1839, pp. 189, 216–223; Scheeben, _Dogm._ II 633 f.; Oswald, _Relig. Urgesch. der Menschheit_, 1887, p. 110 f.; Kleutgen, _Theol. der Vorzeit_ II 616–675; Simar, _Dogm._ 351–367; Pesch, _Prael. theol._ III 111–152, and so forth.
