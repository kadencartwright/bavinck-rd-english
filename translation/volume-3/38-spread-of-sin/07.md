## 7.

The consequence of the _peccatum originans_ is the _peccatum originatum_. Because all are reckoned as sinners in Adam, they are also all born from him in a sinful condition. Inherited pollution is a punishment for inherited guilt. Original sin was first viewed from this standpoint by Augustine, which provoked a vigorous protest from the Pelagians, cf. Augustine, _C. Jul._ V 3, and later likewise from the Remonstrants, _Apol. Conf._ VII 4. But Scripture frequently speaks in this sense and sees subsequent sins as punishment for preceding ones, 2 Sam. 12:11, 12; 1 Kings 11:11–31; 22:30; Isa. 6:9, 10; 7:17; 10:5–7; 14:3; Jer. 50:6–8; Rom. 1:24–28; 2 Thess. 2:11, 12, and so forth. Sins too stand under God’s government. The laws and ordinances that govern the life of sin have been established by God and are upheld by him. And among those laws is also this: _Das eben ist der Fluch der bösen That, dass sie fortwährend Böses muss gebähren_. Sin has such a nature that it increasingly stupefies and hardens the sinner, entangles him ever more tightly in its snares, and makes him fall ever more swiftly down a slope into the abyss. It is true that sin, considered in itself, can never be punishment for sin, for the two differ essentially and stand opposed to each other. Sin arises from the will, while a person undergoes punishment against his will; sin is transgression, punishment is the maintenance of the law; God is the author of punishment but not of sin. Yet subsequent sin may be called a punishment for preceding sin because it removes the sinner still farther from God, makes him more miserable, and surrenders him to every kind of desire and passion, anxiety and remorse, Lombard, _Sent._ II, dist. 36; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2, qu. 87, art. 2; qu. 75, art. 4; qu. 84, art. 1–4; Gerhard, _Loc._ VI c. 10 n. 140; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IX 15; Spanheim, _Op._ III 1268–1270; Moor III 332–335; Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 71, 2; Müller, _Sünde_ II 589 f.; Frank, _Chr. Wahrh._ I 489; Christ, _Die sittl. Weltordnung_, p. 59. According to this law, a sinful condition followed the sinful act in Adam and all his descendants. The Pelagians represent the matter as though any given act of will left no consequences whatsoever. The will that did evil at one moment can immediately afterward do good again, if it pleases. For them, the will neither has nor acquires a definite nature, a determinate character. It is and remains neutral, indifferent, without inward inclination, always placed between opposites and, with incalculable arbitrariness, directing itself now to one side and then to the other. But such a conception meets contradiction from every side. A great moral change took place in Adam and Eve when they transgressed God’s command. Shame and fear of God seized them; repose, peace, and innocence were gone; they hid themselves from God among the trees of the garden; they cast the blame upon one another. Cain made himself guilty of fratricide, and soon the Lord saw that the wickedness of the human being was great upon the earth and that every formation of the thoughts of his heart was evil from his youth. With Adam’s transgression a terrible degeneration of the human race begins. Here we face a dreadful reality whose explanation eludes us. How is it possible that a single sinful act had such terrible consequences and brought about a radical reversal in the whole of human nature? In general it may be observed that the relation between an act and its consequences repeatedly appears very disproportionate to us in life. One hour of thoughtlessness can cause years of tears. One small mistake, a single misstep, gives an entirely different direction to the lives of many people. Small, insignificant events have effects that continue for generations. Our happiness or unhappiness often depends upon a so-called accident. Adam’s one transgression brought about a total change in the deliberations, dispositions, and inclinations of his nature. For experience teaches that whatever a person does reacts upon that person to a greater or lesser degree and leaves a trace in his character. Fundamentally, nothing is indifferent and nothing passes us by without a trace. Every act of will, arising from prior inclinations and desires, reacts upon them and strengthens them. Every sin can in this way become a disposition, an inclination, a passion that rules over the human being like a tyrant. A human being is so changeable, so pliable in form; he accommodates himself to every circumstance, adapts to every environment, becomes accustomed to everything, and takes on the shape of whatever surrounds him. Whoever commits sin immediately becomes a servant of sin. A crime, a lie, a theft, a murder never ends with the moment in which it is committed. Adam’s disobedience changed his whole nature. Moreover, his transgression had not only an outward but also an inward side. It is not the case that Adam’s sinful act, consisting in eating the forbidden fruit, suddenly occurred without any preparation and only afterward produced all kinds of moral changes in his nature. Guilt and pollution are not related to each other in Adam in that way. The act of eating was itself already the manifestation of an entire moral change that had taken place within. Strictly speaking, it was not the first sin but the first fully matured sin in the sense of James 1:15. The sinful act was preceded by sinful deliberations of the understanding (doubt, unbelief) and sinful inclinations of the heart (desire, pride), which had their occasion in the serpent’s temptation and were cultivated by the human will. Before, during, and after eating from the forbidden tree, the human being’s relation to God and his law was changed. Not first one and then the other, but simultaneously and in connection with each other, he became both guilty and unclean before the face of his Maker. Guilt and pollution are both simultaneous consequences of the one and same sin, two sides of the same matter. Finally, the change that occurred in Adam did not consist in some sinful principle being implanted in him or in some constituent part of his being, of his soul or body, or of his faculties or powers being taken from him. Rather, it consisted in this: through his doubt and unbelief, through his pride and desire, and finally through the sinful act itself, the human being increasingly detached himself from God and from his law, placed himself outside God’s favor and fellowship, and began to use all his gifts and powers precisely against God and his command. And then, when this happens, when the human being places himself outside fellowship with God and outside God’s law, the sinful condition enters of itself, just as darkness enters when light disappears. _Fieri nequit quin maxime sese amet creatura, quam non absorpserit amor Dei_ (Melanchthon). The human being, withdrawing from the fellowship of God in which he was created, cannot be conceived otherwise than as a sinner, guilty and corrupt before his face.

The same religious and ethical change that took place in Adam during and in his fall is also the portion of all his descendants. They are all born in the same moral condition into which Adam fell through his transgression. This fact is scarcely susceptible of denial. Scripture not only teaches it, but experience and history prove it day by day. If anything is certain, it is this: human beings are conceived and born not as righteous and holy persons but as sinners. This indicates that they share one guilt with Adam, for guilt and pollution always go together in sin. Just as it did not happen with Adam that he first committed the sinful act, thereby incurred guilt, and as a consequence then also became morally unclean and corrupt, so too his descendants do not enter the world, as it were, with a double guilt—first the guilt of Adam’s transgression and then in addition the guilt of their moral corruption. For this moral corruption is not a later, externally added supplement and not an accidental consequence of the first transgression, but an essential element in it and inseparable from it. Just as Adam by his transgression simultaneously and in the same measure made himself guilty and became unclean, so among his descendants standing guilty in him and being born unclean from him are two sides of the same matter, Edwards, _Works_ II 482; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 170. It is incorrect to infer from this, with Edwards, that pollution precedes guilt. Here there is no before and no after. The uncleanness in which all human beings are born is the reverse side of the guilt of that transgression which Adam committed as _caput foederale_. Guilt, pollution, and death stand in the same relation among Adam’s descendants as in Adam himself and in that mutual connection have passed to all.
