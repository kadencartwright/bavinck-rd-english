## 10.

As extensive as original sin is in humanity, so extensive is it also in the individual human being. It rules over the whole human being—over understanding and will, heart and conscience, soul and body, over all faculties and powers. His heart is evil from his youth and a source of all kinds of sins, Gen. 6:5; 8:21; Ps. 51:7; Jer. 17:9; Ezek. 36:26; Mark 7:21. He cannot renew himself, Jer. 13:23; Ezek. 16:6; cannot understand the things of God, 1 Cor. 2:14; cannot submit to God’s law, John 8:34, 36; Rom. 6:17, 20; 8:7; and is dead through sins and trespasses, Eph. 2:1. Regeneration is therefore necessary for entrance into the kingdom of God, John 3:3. The whole of salvation, objectively and subjectively, is a work of God’s grace, John 6:44; 15:5; 1 Cor. 4:7; 15:10; Phil. 2:13, and so forth. Upon these positive statements of Holy Scripture, Augustine and his followers, and later the Reformers, built the doctrine of the human being’s incapacity for good. Since the whole of human nature was corrupted in Adam, nothing truly good can any longer proceed from it, any more than an evil tree can bring forth good fruit. Rather, the human being now lives under the _dura necessitas non posse non peccandi_; his virtues are _vitia potius quam virtutes_; he is by nature inclined to every evil, inclined even to hate God and his neighbor. This teaching is undoubtedly hard, and it is no wonder that in every age it has encountered decided contradiction. The pagans did not know this profound corruption of sin. However often they too expressed its universal spread, they nevertheless continued to believe in the natural goodness of the human being and in the possibility of virtue: _Sunt ingeniis nostris semina innata virtutum, quae si adolescere liceret ipsa nos ad beatam vitam natura perduceret_, Cicero, _Qu. Tusc._ II 1. _Erras, si existimas, nobiscum nasci vitia, supervenerunt, ingesta sunt_, Seneca, _Ep._ 96. This pagan doctrine was renewed in Pelagianism, which denied original sin altogether. Through its mechanical conception of the image of God, the Roman Catholic Church came to the doctrine that the _homo naturalis_, after the loss of the _donum superadditum_, can still do truly good works—though not in a supernatural, nevertheless in a natural sense, Trent VI, can. 5, 7. Socinians, Remonstrants, Anabaptists, Quakers, rationalists, and others fell back to this Roman Catholic position. Coornhert took offense at the 5th and 8th questions of the Heidelberg Catechism. Rousseau preached the natural goodness of the human being. And Allard Pierson testified concerning himself that he was by no means inclined to every evil but indeed to much good, _Gids_, November 1895, p. 259. The natural aversion that involuntarily arises in the heart against the doctrine of the human being’s total moral corruption is undoubtedly joined in its opponents by much misunderstanding. For if this doctrine is clearly brought to light, it is confirmed day by day by everyone’s experience and justified by the testimony of its opponents themselves. 1º. The doctrine of Scripture and church is not that every human being lives in all possible actual sins and is _actu_ guilty of transgressing all the commandments. It speaks only of the deepest inclination, the innermost disposition, the fundamental direction of human nature, and confesses that this nature is directed not toward God but away from him. If the human being is an organic unity, then one of these two must be the case. Many dismiss the matter by saying that the human being is by nature neither, or both at once, Hegel, _Werke_ XII 209 f.; but this betrays a lack of reflection, conflicts with the nature of the good, and was therefore also very seriously opposed by Kant, _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 23–26. Whoever commits one sin is in principle guilty of transgressing all the commandments, and whoever truly possesses one virtue possesses them all in principle. At the root of his being, the human being is either good or evil—there is no third possibility. 2º. Sin, however, is no substance. It indeed dwells in, upon, and alongside the human being, but it is not and cannot be the essence of the human being. Even after the fall the human being remained human. He retained reason, conscience, and will, and can thereby control his lower sensual drives and inclinations and thus compel himself to virtue. Augustine, who called the virtues of the pagans _splendida vitia_, nevertheless fully acknowledged this. Many of their deeds are not only not deserving of blame but rather worthy of our praise and imitation; cf. Wiggers, _Aug. u. Pelag._ I 119–123. The Lutherans spoke of the natural human being as a block and a stick in the spiritual domain, but still attributed to him all kinds of powers for good in the so-called lower hemisphere of civic life, Frank, _Theol. der Conc._ I 144 f. And Calvin and the Reformed, more than all of them, honored the virtues of unbelievers and frequently held them up as an example even to Christians; cf. my address on _Common Grace_, pp. 27 f. The doctrine of the total corruption of human nature by no means entails, therefore, that the sinful inclination lying at the bottom of the heart always erupts in deeds that clearly betray enmity and hatred toward God and neighbor. Various circumstances intervene and prevent the inclination from expressing itself fully. Not only are many sinful deeds restrained by the sword of the civil authority, civic propriety, public opinion, fear of disgrace and punishment, and so forth; but all kinds of factors—such as the natural love that still belongs to every human being, the moral character cultivated through education and struggle, and favorable circumstances of constitution, environment, occupation, and so forth—frequently lead a person to practice beautiful and praiseworthy virtues. Only, the sinful inclination of the heart has thereby been suppressed, not eradicated. It continually rises to the surface in all kinds of evil deliberations, thoughts, and desires. When opportunity is favorable and necessity presses, it frequently breaks through every dam and dike that held it in. And those who show in dreadful words and deeds that they hate God and neighbor bear no nature other than that in which all human beings share. 3º. When it is taught that through sin the human being is incapable of any good, and this incapacity is called natural, no physical necessity or fatalistic compulsion is meant. Through sin the human being has not lost his will or the freedom implanted in him. By virtue of its nature, the will excludes every compulsion and cannot will otherwise than freely. But the human being has indeed lost the will’s free inclination toward the good. He no longer wills to do the good; he now voluntarily, from inclination, does evil. The inclination, the direction of the will, has changed: _semper est in nobis voluntas libera, sed non semper est bona_, Augustine, _De gr. et lib. arb._ 15; cf. also the Reformed in Heppe, _Dogm._ 237, 264. Incapacity for good is in this sense not physical but ethical in nature; it is an incapacity of the will. Some therefore preferred to speak of moral rather than natural incapacity—Amyraut, Testard, Venema in Moor III 231–233—and especially Jonathan Edwards. In his day Edwards had to defend human incapacity against Whitby and Taylor, who denied original sin and considered the human being able to keep God’s law. They objected that if the human being _could_ not keep God’s law, he also did not need to do so and, if he then failed to do so, was not guilty either. To defend against this, Edwards distinguished between natural and moral incapacity and said that the fallen human being had the natural but not the moral power to do good. And he added that only natural incapacity was really incapacity, while moral incapacity could be so called only in an improper sense. For sin is not a physical defect in the nature or powers of the will, but an ethical defect, a lack of affection and love for the good; see his _Freedom of the Will_, _Works_ II 1–190, _passim_. Edwards did indeed say that the human being could not give himself this affection for the good and could not change his will; in this respect he stood entirely on the side of Augustine and Calvin. But by refusing to call this unwillingness toward the good natural incapacity, he fostered much misunderstanding and in fact promoted Pelagianism. The Reformed have therefore spoken both before and since of natural incapacity. This word _natural_, however, can have different meanings. One can have in view by it the original human nature created by God in Adam according to his image, just as Protestants said that the image of God was natural—in that case incapacity for good is not natural. One can also mean by it the physical substance or power of any creature, and in that case too incapacity is not to be called natural, since all substance and power were created by God. Incapacity for good is no physical impossibility, as it is, for example, impossible for a human being to touch the stars with his hand. But when speaking of natural incapacity, one can also think of the properties of fallen human nature and thereby indicate that, in the fallen condition, incapacity for good now belongs to every human being “by nature,” is innate in him from the first moment of his existence, and is not first introduced into him from without by habit, education, or imitation. In this sense the name natural incapacity is entirely correct, and the name moral incapacity is liable to misunderstanding. For that is often called morally impossible which, on the ground of someone’s character, habit, or upbringing, is held to be impossible for him to do. It is morally impossible for a virtuous person suddenly to become a thief, for a mother to hate her child, for a murderer to strangle an innocent child. That which is morally impossible nevertheless certainly does happen under some circumstances. It is not so with incapacity for good. It is indeed ethical in nature and an incapacity of the will, but it belongs to the human being by nature; it is innate in him; it is a property of his willing itself. And precisely because the will, even now in the fallen condition, by virtue of its nature cannot will otherwise than freely, it cannot will otherwise than what it wills, than that to which it is inclined by nature; cf. Thomas, _S. c. Gent._ IV 52; Calvin on Eph. 2:3; Ursinus and other commentaries on the Heidelberg Catechism, questions 5, 8; _Form. Conc._ I 12; _Cons. Helv._ § 21, 22; Turretin, _Theol. El._ X 4, 39; Moor III 232; Hodge, _Syst. Theol._ II 257–272; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ 219–257; III 364–374. 4º. Finally, one must bear in mind that Scripture and church, in teaching the total corruption of the human being, apply the highest standard—namely, the law of God. The doctrine of incapacity for good is a religious confession. According to the standard ordinarily used by human beings in daily life or even in philosophical ethics, it can be fully acknowledged that much good and beauty is done by human beings. With this standard in hand, the follower of Augustine can be even milder and more generous in judging and appreciating human virtues than the most convinced Pelagian. There is, however, still another, higher ideal for the human being; there is a divine law to which he must answer. Virtues and good works are distinct. Good—truly good, good in the eyes of the holy God—is only that which is done from faith, according to God’s law, and to God’s glory. And tested by this standard, who then dares to say that any work is done by human beings that is completely pure and does not need forgiveness and renewal? To divide the human being in two with Rome and in part also with the Lutherans, and to say that in the supernatural and spiritual realm he is capable of no good but in the natural realm can do something completely good, conflicts with the unity of human nature, the unity of the moral law, and the teaching of Scripture that the human being must always be God’s image, must do everything he does to God’s glory, and must love God always and everywhere with all his heart and understanding and strength. If this is so, if the essence of the human being consists in his being God’s image and likeness, then nothing in the human being as he now lives and works can stand before God’s face. Weighed in the balances of God’s sanctuary, all his doing is found too light.

One can differ over this standard and, in order to reach a more favorable conclusion, pull God’s law down from its height and tailor it to human conduct. But given this standard, no other judgment is possible than that of Scripture: there is no one who does good, not even one. And this judgment of Scripture is confirmed by all kinds of testimony. Let those who do not believe Scripture listen to the voices of the greatest members of our race. As soon as one does not stop at words or deeds in oneself or in others but investigates the hidden motives and secret intentions, the sinful nature of all human striving comes to light. _Nos vertus ne sont le plus souvent que des vices déguisés_ (Rochefoucauld). _L’homme n’est que déguisement, que mensonge et hypocrisie, et en soi-même et à l’égard des autres_ (Pascal). _Homo homini lupus_; without the state, human society would be a _bellum omnium contra omnes_ (Hobbes). According to Kant, _der Mensch ist von Natur böse_; there is in him a natural _Hang zum Bösen_, _ein radicales, angebornes Böse_. The dreadful facts made known to us by the history of humanity prove this sufficiently. _Ein jeder Mensch hat seinen Preis, für den er sich weggiebt_. What the apostle says is a universal truth: there is no one who does good, not even one, Kant, _Relig._, ed. Rosenkranz, 35 f. _Diejenigen, welche ein servum arbitrium behaupteten und den Menschen als einen Stock und Klotz charakterisirten.... hatten vollkommen Recht_, Fichte, _Syst. der Sittenlehre_, 1798, p. 265. _Das natürliche Herz, worin der Mensch befangen ist, ist der Feind, der zu bekämpfen ist_, Hegel, _Werke_ XII 270. _Der Mensch hat sich von Ewigkeit in der Eigenheit und Selbstsucht ergriffen und alle, die geboren werden, werden mit dem anhängenden finstern Princip des Bösen geboren. Dieses ursprüngliche Böse im Menschen, das nur derjenige in Abrede ziehen kann, der den Menschen in sich und ausser sich nur oberflächlich kennen gelernt hat, ist in seinem Ursprung eigne That_, Schelling, _Werke_ I 7, p. 388. _Die Haupt- und Grundtriebfeder im Menschen, wie im Thiere ist der Egoismus, d. h. der Drang zum Daseyn und Wohlseyn. Dieser Egoismus ist im Thiere wie im Menschen mit dem innersten Kern und Wesen desselben aufs genaueste verknüpft, ja eigentlich identisch._ This egoism is indeed restrained by all kinds of bonds of propriety, fear, punishment, civil authority, and so forth; but when these are removed, _die unersättliche Habsucht, die niederträchtige Geldgier, die tief versteckte Falschheit, die tückische Bosheit_ leap forth. _Kriminalgeschichten und Beschreibungen anarchischer Zustände muss man lesen, um zu erkennen, was in moralischer Hinsicht der Mensch eigentlich ist. Diese Tausende, die da vor unseren Augen in friedlichen Verkehr sich durcheinander drängen, sind anzusehen als eben so viele Tiger und Wölfe, deren Gebiss man durch einen starken Maulkorb gesichert hat._ Even conscience is for the most part composed of 1/5 fear of human beings, 1/5 _deisidaemonia_, 1/5 prejudice, 1/5 vanity, and 1/5 habit, Schopenhauer, _Die beiden Grundprobleme der Ethik_, 3rd ed., 1881, p. 186 f.; _Welt als Wille u. V._ I⁶ 391 f.; _Parerga u. Paral._ II⁵ 229 f. The adherents of evolution have returned to the doctrine of Mandeville, Helvétius, Diderot, d’Alembert, and others, that egoism is the basis of morality and the norm of all human action. The human being descended from the animals and fundamentally remains an animal, guided by egoistic instincts. Civilization can tame but can never make the human being anything other than what he originally and by disposition is. What we call moral life is an accidental product of circumstances, of the life of human beings in a particular society. Under other circumstances and in another society, good and evil would have an entirely different content, Darwin, _Descent of Man_, chapters 3–5; Büchner, _Kraft u. Stoff_, 16th ed., 478–492. In his _Ethisch idealisme_, Amsterdam, 1875, de Bussy makes a great distinction between the moral person, whose egoistic nature has been restrained in its arbitrariness by the community but not destroyed, whose virtues are frequently _splendida vitia_, pp. 22 f., and the ethical person, in whom a new principle has been planted. It is truly not Scripture alone that judges the human being harshly. Human beings have passed the harshest and strictest judgment upon human beings. And then it is still better to fall into the hand of the Lord than into the hand of human beings, for his mercies are many. For when God condemns us, he at the same time offers his forgiving love in Christ; but when human beings condemn human beings, they frequently thrust them away and abandon them to their contempt. When God condemns us, he has this judgment brought to us by human beings—prophets and apostles and ministers—who do not place themselves high above us as saints but include themselves with everyone in a common confession of guilt. But philosophers and moralists, in despising human beings, usually forget that they themselves are human. When God condemns us, he speaks of sin and guilt, which are indeed great and heavy but nevertheless can be taken away because they do not belong to the essence of the human being. But moralists frequently speak of egoistic, animal inclinations that belong to the human being by virtue of his origin and belong to his essence. They indeed strike him down but do not lift him up. If we are animals from the outset, why then ought we to live as children of God?
