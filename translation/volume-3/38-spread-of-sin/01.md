# § 38. The Spread of Sin

## 1.

Sin, already so dreadful in its essence, acquires a still more serious character through the extent of its dominion.
The absolute universality of sin is also acknowledged outside special revelation.
The various religions, with their priests and altars, their sacrifices and acts of penance, are all built upon the confession of sin; dogma and cult, prayer and song, religion and philosophy sometimes interpret the universal consciousness of sin in a moving way.
_Ἀνθρωποισι γαρ τοις πασι κοινον ἐστι τοὐξαμαρτανειν. Vitiis nemo sine nascitur. Communis hominum labes._
Cf. Pfanner, _Theol. gent._ IX 7; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ II⁴ 16; Lamennais, _Essai sur l’indifférence_ III, pp. 393–408; Hettinger, _Apol. d. Christ._ III⁷ 30, 412.
This universality of sin is expressed still more clearly in Holy Scripture, Gen. 6:5; 8:21; 1 Kings 8:46; 2 Chron. 6:36; Job 4:17–21; 14:4; 25:4–6; Ps. 14:3; 53:2; 130:3; 143:2; Prov. 20:9; Eccles. 7:20; Isa. 53:6; 58:1; 59:4, 12, etc.; cf. Sir. 8:6; 25:26; Wis. 9:6, 13–18; 12:10, 11, etc.
Jesus presupposes it when he calls everyone without distinction to repentance and makes entrance into the kingdom of God dependent upon rebirth from water and Spirit, Matt. 4:17; Mark 1:15; 6:12; Luke 12:47; John 3:3, 5.
He does indeed speak of the healthy and the righteous, Matt. 9:12, 13; Mark 2:17; Luke 5:31, 32, but he does so objectively, according to the opinion of those whom he thus describes, without passing judgment upon the nature of that health and righteousness.
How he himself thinks of that righteousness is sufficiently evident from other passages; he pronounces his woe upon the Pharisees but invites the weary and burdened to his rest.
Likewise, the apostles teach that all have sinned, that the whole world lies in evil and is condemnable before God, and that all need the forgiving love of the Father, redemption through Christ, and renewal by the Holy Spirit, Rom. 3:9–20, 23; 5:12; 11:32; 2 Cor. 5:19; Gal. 3:22; 1 John 1:8; 5:19.
Further, according to Scripture, sin is proper to the human being from youth, from birth, even from conception onward, Gen. 6:5; 8:21; Job 14:4; 13:26; Ps. 25:7; 51:7; 58:4; 103:14; Isa. 43:27; 48:8; 57:3; Ezek. 16:3; Hos. 5:7; John 3:6; Rom. 7:7 f.; Eph. 2:3.
In his confession of guilt David goes back to the deepest ground of his sinfulness, finds it in the sins of his parents, and explains from this that he himself stands unclean before God from conception and birth onward, Ps. 51:7.
In John 3:6 Jesus says that flesh, that is, the human being as a sensuous being, can produce only flesh; that such a human being has no entrance into the kingdom of God and for this needs rebirth from the Spirit; and that the human being as such a sensuous being, as σαρξ, is unclean and corrupt from birth.
And in Eph. 2:3 Paul declares that Jews and Gentiles are τεκνα φυσει ὀργης; he certainly does not set φυσει here over against the actual sins of which he is speaking precisely there, vv. 1–3, but says that they were φυσει children of God’s wrath while now, after having been made alive and saved by grace, they are objects of his love, and thus indicates that their former state, their being dead through sins and trespasses, was a natural state, a φυσις that rested in their very existence; cf. Rom. 2:14; 11:21; 2:15; 4:8.
For the rest, it is true that Holy Scripture presupposes the universality of sin and the corruption of the human being reaching back into conception much more than it describes them extensively and repeatedly.
Human beings themselves are everywhere portrayed as sinners—not only the generation before the flood and all pagans and ungodly people but also the people of Israel; and among that people many sins are also recounted of the pious, of the patriarchs, Moses, Job, David, Solomon, and others; indeed, it is precisely the pious who, even where they are convinced of the justice of their cause, nevertheless fall down before God in humble confession of guilt and plead for mercy and forgiveness, Pss. 6; 25; 32; 38; 51; 130; 143; Isa. 6:5; 53:4–6, etc.
It is the human heart that is corrupt, Gen. 6:5; 8:21; Ps. 14:1; Jer. 17:9; Ezek. 36:26; Matt. 15:19; from it are the issues of life, Prov. 4:23, and from it proceed all unrighteousness and also foolishness, Mark 7:22.
The human being’s understanding is darkened, Job 21:14; Isa. 1:3; Jer. 4:22; John 1:5; Rom. 1:21, 22; 1 Cor. 1:18–28; 2:14; Eph. 4:18; 5:8; his soul is guilty and unclean and needs atonement and conversion, Lev. 17:11; Ps. 19:8; 41:5; Prov. 19:2, 15; Matt. 16:26; 1 Pet. 1:22; his spirit is proud, erring, and defiled and must therefore be broken, enlightened, and cleansed, Ps. 51:19; Prov. 16:18, 32; Eccles. 7:9; Isa. 57:15; 66:2; 1 Cor. 7:34; 2 Cor. 7:1; 1 Thess. 5:23; his conscience is defiled and needs cleansing, Titus 1:15; Heb. 9:9, 14; 10:22; his desire, inclination, and will extend toward what is forbidden and are powerless for good, Jer. 13:23; John 8:34, 36; Rom. 6:17; 8:7; 2 Cor. 3:5; and the body with all its members—eyes, Deut. 29:4; Ps. 18:28; Isa. 35:5; 42:7; 2 Pet. 2:14; 1 John 2:16; ears, Deut. 29:4; Ps. 115:6; 135:17; Isa. 6:10; Jer. 5:21; Zech. 7:11; feet, Ps. 38:17; Prov. 1:16; 4:27; 6:18; Isa. 59:7; Rom. 3:15; mouth and tongue, Job 27:4; Ps. 17:10; 12:4; 15:3; Jer. 9:3, 5; Rom. 3:14; Jas. 3:5–8, etc.—stands in the service of unrighteousness.
In a word, sin is not merely upon and around the human being but is within him and extends over the whole human being and the whole of humanity; Schultz, _Altt. Theol._⁴, 670 f.
Of the passages cited for the opposite view, only Rom. 7:7–26 needs some further discussion.
At all times the Pelagians have appealed to this pericope as proof that the νους or πνευμα in the human being remained free from sin and that sin resides only in the σαρξ; in more recent times this exegesis has been almost universally accepted.
Augustine, however, in his later period and all his followers in both the Roman Catholic and Protestant churches have always opposed this interpretation and understood verses 14–25 as spoken by Paul as regenerate and concerning the present, an interpretation still defended today by Delitzsch, Philippi, Luthardt, Harless, Thomasius, Umbreit, Kohlbrugge, and others; cf. Tholuck in his _Comm. zum Brief an die Römer_⁵, 1856, S. 333–369, and further Meyer, Philippi, and others.
The latter exegesis deserves preference for various reasons.
1º In Rom. 7:7–26 Paul argues that the law from which the believer has been released by having died with Christ is not itself sinful.
And he does this by pointing out that it first brought the believer to knowledge of his sin and his death, 7–13, and that it still now has the assent of his inner human being, even though his flesh resists it, 14–26.
In this context it is necessary to argue that the law still now has the believer’s assent, even though he has been released from it.
For the thesis that the law itself is holy, it is not enough that the unregenerate person approves it; what is necessary is precisely that the regenerate person does so.
And thus from verse 14 onward Paul also speaks in the present tense, not from vividness of presentation, but because precisely as regenerate he loves and approves the law.
Because the law indirectly gave him the painful experience of his sin and death, because in communion with Christ, v. 4; Gal. 2:19, 20, he died through the law to the law and was freed from its curse, Gal. 3:13, therefore and thereby he learned to know it as holy and righteous and good, bears witness to it, and confirms the law through faith, Rom. 3:30.
2º Because in the course of his argument Paul wishes to honor the law in its holy character, he casts all guilt upon the sin that dwells in him and makes a sharp distinction between the center and the periphery of his being.
Inwardly, according to his will, according to his inner human being, he loves God’s law, but in his members another power and another law dwells, namely, sin.
Such a profound distinction is nowhere assumed in Scripture in the unregenerate person.
In the latter there is indeed knowledge of God and of the law and also a doing by nature of the things of the law, Rom. 1:19; 2:14, 15, that is, a struggle between reason and sensuousness, conscience and desire, understanding and heart; but a struggle between flesh and spirit such as Paul portrays here exists only in the regenerate, Gal. 5:17; only they can say that they love God’s law, approve it, and will it with their whole heart.
3º Although as regenerate Paul still calls himself σαρκινος, πεπραμενος ὑπο την ἁμαρτιαν, he does not thereby say that he is ἐν σαρκι, 8:8, 9, or walks κατα σαρκα, 8:1, for he calls himself this only because, on account of the sin dwelling in his members, he cannot do what he wills and is thus held captive by that law of sin in his members.
Although wretched because with the flesh he serves the law of sin, he nevertheless with his νους serves the law of God, v. 26, and it is precisely this latter point that is worked out further in Rom. 8.
In Christ he is righteous and walks according to the Spirit.
4º In addition, if Rom. 7:14 f. were to be understood of the unregenerate person, regeneration itself would be unnecessary, assisting grace would be sufficient, and the whole doctrine of Scripture concerning sin and grace, justification and sanctification, faith and repentance would be overthrown.
Rather, Rom. 7:7–25 is a strong proof of the total corruption of human nature; for if the regenerate person still has so to lament the power of sin dwelling in him, then the unregenerate person, entirely and without knowing it, is a servant of sin, being in the flesh and walking according to the flesh, and the mind of the flesh is enmity against God.

This universality of sin is derived in Gen. 3 from the fall of the first human being.
This is not indeed stated there in so many words; Gen. 3 pays much more attention to the change in circumstances that follows as a consequence of the fall—the woman’s sorrow, the curse upon the ground, expulsion from the garden—than to the moral reversal that took place in Adam and his descendants.
Nevertheless, it relates that immediately after the evil was committed, shame and fear take possession of them, consciousness of guilt awakens, and they flee from God.
The punishments pronounced in Gen. 3:16 f. apply not only to Adam and Eve but undoubtedly also to their descendants, and according to the entire conception of the Old Testament these punishments also presuppose common guilt.
The first event reported after the fall is Cain’s murder of his brother; history itself takes on a different character, even without this being stated more explicitly: it becomes a history of sin, misery, and death.
The human being is now evil by nature, Gen. 6:5; 8:21.
Likewise, the later Old Testament writings do not expressly connect the universality of sin with the fall of the first human being.
They rest content with the fact of the universality of sin or also trace it to the perishable, sensuous nature of the human being, Job 4:17 f.; 14:4; 15:14 f.; 25:4 f.; Ps. 78:38 f.; 103:13 f.; Mark 14:38; John 3:6; Rom. 6:7 f., etc.; cf. above, p. 49 f.
It is Paul who first explains the universality of sin from Adam’s disobedience in 1 Cor. 15:21 f.; Rom. 5:12 f.; cf. also Wis. 1:13, 14; 2:23, 24; Apoc. Baruch 23:4; 54:15; 56:5, 9; 4 Ezra 7:68.
In 1 Cor. 15:21 f. he says that the death of all human beings has its cause in the human being Adam in the same way as the resurrection from the dead has its cause in the human being Christ.
It is clearly implied here that the death of all human beings was not first caused by their personal sins but had already been pronounced over all human beings and passed to all human beings solely and exclusively because of Adam’s disobedience, in the same way that the resurrection was merited not by believers’ personal good works, faith, and so forth, but exclusively by Christ’s obedience.
They die not in and through themselves but in Adam; and they rise not in and through themselves but only in Christ.
How and why that death passed to all is explained in greater detail in Rom. 5:12 f.
Because through Christ’s death we have been reconciled as enemies, we are certain that with him we shall also live eternally; indeed, so certain are we that we already now boast through Christ, who gave us the reconciliation, vv. 10, 11.
The grace and life of Christ therefore (δια τουτο, v. 12) far surpass the sin and death of Adam.
This is the theme of the pericope, vv. 12–21, and it is worked out and proved in the following way.
Through one human being sin entered the world as a powerful and all-dominating principle, and in this manner and by this path death also passed to all human beings, since all sinned.
The words ἐφ’ ᾡ are not to be translated with Origen, the Vulgate, Augustine, and others by _in quo_, for they stand too far from ἀνθρωπος to refer back to it, nor do they mean _in whom_, although this thought is not in itself unscriptural, Heb. 7:10; 1 Cor. 15:22; rather, they are a conjunction, ἐπι τουτῳ ὁτι, _eo quod, quia, quandoquidem_; Calvin, Martyr, and most modern exegetes.
The word ἡμαρτον does not designate a sinful state but an act.
Paul therefore says in verse 12: Adam transgressed; thereby sin and death entered the world and reigned over all.
The causal clause ἐφ’ ᾡ παντες ἡμαρτον does not indicate that Paul seeks the cause of each human being’s death in his personal, actual sins, for in 1 Cor. 15:22 he expressly says that all die in Adam; here in verse 14 he declares that death also reigned over those who did not sin in the likeness of Adam’s transgression—and, we may add, also over children; and in vv. 15 and 17 he states as strongly as possible that through the trespass of one, many died.
Yet on the other hand, one should not silently supply in that causal clause that all sinned _in Adam_.
This is already improbable in itself, but it is rendered impossible by the fact that vv. 13 and 14 deal precisely with the sin that human beings committed not in Adam but themselves personally, even though at that time they did not sin in the likeness of Adam’s transgression.
Rather, Paul means that through Adam’s transgression both sin and death, in connection with each other, came to dominion in humanity; that as a consequence of Adam’s transgression all human beings themselves became sinners personally and all die one by one.
He argues for this proposition in the following verses as follows: the sin that entered the world through Adam’s transgression was also present and also reigned in the time from Adam to Moses, when God had not yet proclaimed his law; human beings sinned then too.
But—Paul objects to himself, as it were—sin is surely not imputed when there is no law; the law of Moses did not yet exist, and thus sin could not be imputed then either.
To this he answers: most certainly it could, for death nevertheless reigned from Adam to Moses, even though human beings then transgressed no positive law explicitly and verbally proclaimed by God and therefore could not sin in the likeness of Adam’s transgression.
They nevertheless did sin, because even then they had a law known to them by nature, Rom. 1:18 f.; 2:12–15.
If death nevertheless reigned then, sin must also have reigned then.
And it reigned then not because each person personally transgressed a positive command as Adam did, and thus each became a sinner for himself and through himself; no, but because through Adam’s transgression sin had entered the world and reigned over all, and death followed in its train.
Adam is therefore precisely a type of Christ.
Sin and death, which come to us from Adam, proceed in the same way as righteousness and life, which Christ acquired.
There is a difference in intensity—the grace is more abundant and the life more powerful—but the _modus quo_ by which both become ours is the same.
The trespass of one was the cause of the guilt, sin, and death of all human beings, and so the obedience of one is the cause of the righteousness, acquittal, and life of all.
In one all were condemned and died; in one they are all justified and saved.
In both cases a judgment of God, a κριμα, precedes; and from that κριμα are derived, on the one hand, our sin and death and, on the other, our righteousness and life.
And thus the thought Paul develops in this pericope comes down to this: 1º concerning Adam’s one transgression, God pronounced a sentence consisting in a declaration of guilt and condemnation to death; 2º that sentence was pronounced in Adam over all human beings because they were included in Adam in one way or another, which Paul does not explain further here but allows us to infer from the course of his argument; all were declared guilty and condemned to death in Adam; 3º by virtue of this antecedent judgment of God, all human beings became sinners personally and all also actually die.
God conceives and regards, judges and condemns all human beings in one, and therefore they all also proceed from him as sinners and are all subjected to death.
