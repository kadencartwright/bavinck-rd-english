## 5.

The Reformation opposed this Roman Catholic weakening of original sin. In itself, the scholastic definition of original sin as _privatio justitiae originalis, homini inesse debitae_ presented no difficulty, provided that it was not understood in a purely negative sense. In Roman Catholic theology, however, this increasingly became the case, and for that very reason the Reformation emphasized that original sin was not only a _privatio_ but at the same time a total corruption of human nature. In the early period this corruption was still frequently designated by the name _concupiscentia_, though it was not then conceived one-sidedly with Augustine and Lombard as sexual desire, but as ἀταξια _omnium appetitionum_, seated in both the higher and the lower faculties of the human being. Calvin says very clearly that those who define original sin as the loss of original righteousness do indeed describe it completely, but do not adequately express its force and activity. For our nature has not merely been deprived of something good; it is also _malorum omnium fertilis et ferax_. If original sin is therefore defined as _concupiscentia_, this is correct, provided that one adds: _quidquid in homine est, ab intellectu ad voluntatem, ab anima ad carnem usque hac concupiscentia inquinatum refertumque esse, aut ut brevius absolvam, totum hominem non aliud ex se ipso esse quam concupiscentiam_, _Inst._ II 1, 8. Further, the Reformers taught that this desire was sin even in its _primoprimi motus_. It does not first become sin when the will has consented to it, but is sin in itself—not only when _formata_, therefore, but already when _informis_. Calvin again explains that on this point he departs from Augustine. Augustine calls _concupiscentia_, once its guilt has been removed in baptism, by the name _infirmitas_; _nos autem illud ipsum pro peccato habemus_, _Inst._ III 3, 10. Finally, this corruption of human nature was so total that the human being is by nature incapable of any spiritual good, inclined to every evil, and worthy of eternal punishment on its account alone. It cannot be denied that at times, in reaction against Rome, especially on the Lutheran side, statements were made too strongly. Even if it was not meant so badly, it was certainly liable to serious misunderstanding when Luther called original sin _peccatum essentiale_ and _essentia hominis_, Köstlin, _Luthers Theol._ II 366 f. Flacius spoke still more strongly of original sin as _substantia hominis_. And the Formula of Concord also said, in Luther’s own words, that the human being’s understanding, heart, and will in spiritual matters were _prorsus corruptus atque mortuus_, capable of no more than _lapis, truncus aut limus_, ed. Müller, 589, 594; cf. also Frank, _Theol. d. Conc._ I 138. However much Roman Catholics therefore tried to accuse the Lutherans of Manichaeism, Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et stat. pecc._ V c. 1 sq.; Becanus, _De pecc. orig._, qu. 2 sq.; Möhler, _Symbolik_, p. 66 f., the Lutherans nevertheless expressly confessed in the _Form. Conc._ that sin was no substance, and all theologians agree with this. Indeed, although the Lutherans generally embraced traducianism and were therefore inclined to have original sin propagated through _carnalis concupiscentia_, Luther in Köstlin II 367; Melanchthon, _Loci C._, _De pecc. orig._, _C. R._ XXI, p. 668 sq.; cf. Frank, _Theol. der Concordienformel_ I 50 f., Melanchthon nevertheless also says there that he has no objection to _natos etiam propter lapsum Adae reos esse_. The _Form. Conc._ declares that original sin is _culpa seu reatus, quo fit, ut omnes propter inobedientiam Adae et Hevae in odio apud Deum et natura filii irae simus_, Müller, _Symb. Bücher_ 576. And later several Lutheran theologians say that Adam is to be regarded not only as _caput physicum_ but also as _caput morale seu foederale generis humani_, and that his transgression is therefore imputed to all, Quenstedt, _Theol._ II 53, 118; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 515; Schmid, _Dogm. der ev. luth. K._ 172 f. From the beginning the Reformed guarded against such strong expressions as the Lutherans sometimes used. Calvin by no means approved the images mentioned above, Schweizer, _Centrald._ I 385, 394. Yet they too taught that original sin consisted negatively in _carentia justitiae originalis_ but positively also in _corruptio naturae_, and that it had its cause in the imputed transgression of Adam, Calvin, _Inst._ II 1; commentary on Ps. 51:7; Rom. 5:12, and so forth; Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 344; Martyr, _Loci C._ 68 sq.; Polanus, _Synt._ 336; Ursinus, _Tract. Theol._ 202; _Explic. Catech._, qu. 7; Zanchi, _Op._ IV 30; Junius, _Theses Theol._ 19, 20; Heppe, _Dogm._ 245 f. On this point there was very broad agreement. Rivet collected a long series of statements from ecclesiastical confessions and theological works that all teach the same thing, _Testimonia de imputatione primi peccati omnibus Adami posteris_, _Op._ III 798–826, largely translated into English in _Princeton Theol. Essays_, 1846, pp. 195–217. Opposition to this doctrine arose, however, from the school of Saumur. In his theses _De statu hominis lapsi ante gratiam_, _Synt. thesium theol. in acad. Salm._, ed. 2, 1665, p. 205, Placaeus taught that Adam’s disobedience was imputed to his descendants only mediately—that is, only insofar as and on the ground that they had already been born unclean from him. Previously it had been taught unanimously: _corrupti nascimur, quia primum peccatum nobis imputatur_. Placaeus reversed this and said: _primum peccatum nobis imputatur, quia corrupti nascimur_, and further explained his view in _Disp. bipartita de imputatione primi peccati Adami_, 1655. The Synod at Charenton condemned his view in 1645. At the Synod’s instruction Rivet collected his aforementioned _testimonia_, and various theologians opposed Placaeus’s doctrine: Heidegger, Turretin, Maresius, Driessen, Leydecker, Marck, Comrie, Holtius, and others. But the time for the Reformation doctrine of original sin seemed to have passed. Placaeus found acceptance everywhere—in France, Switzerland, England, and America; among theologians such as Wyttenbach, Endemann, Stapfer, Whitby, John Taylor, Röell, Vitringa, and others; cf. M. Vitringa II 349—and also among the Lutherans, Walch, _Bibl. theol._ I 85. In America Jonathan Edwards indeed still wrote his famous treatise _Original Sin Defended_, 1757, _Works_ II 305–310, against Whitby; but in it he himself already embraced the mediate imputation of Adam’s sin and brought the New England Theology of Hopkins, Edwards Jr., Dwight, Emmons, and others entirely into Placaeus’s line. Pelagianism made its entrance everywhere. Protestantism, to the same degree that it became unfaithful to its past, fell back into Romanism: the human being was good by nature but succumbed very easily in the struggle against sensuality. Even the more recent theologians who assume an influence of Adam’s sin upon his descendants have thereby returned only in part to the doctrine of the Reformation, Vilmar, _Dogm._ I 270; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ III 40; Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 340–344; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ § 82, 83; Frank, _System d. chr. Wahrh._ I 460, 482; Müller, _Sünde_ II 426–503, and so forth. Yet through all kinds of causes the profound meaning of the doctrine of original sin has again become clear to many. In 1845 Gervinus wrote, _wir sind der Erbsündenangst entnommen, die wie die Gespensterfurcht nur die Furcht einer abergläubischen Religionslehre war_, in Delitzsch, _Christl. Apol._ 119. There will not be many today who still agree with this superficial judgment. The philosophy of Kant, Schelling, Schopenhauer, and others, the doctrine of heredity and solidarity, and historical and sociological studies have offered the dogma of original sin unexpected and important support. When theology had rejected it, philosophy took it up again.
