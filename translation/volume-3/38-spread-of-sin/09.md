## 9.

Original sin is a property of human nature and therefore belongs to all creatures who partake of this nature.
_In Adamo persona corrumpit naturam, in aliis hominibus natura corrumpit personam_; Thomas, _S. Theol._ III qu. 8 art. 5; qu. 69 art. 3.
The Pelagian assertion that there are, or at least can be, human beings without any sin is contradicted by Scripture, experience, and the testimonies of all religions and peoples.
There is only one exception to the rule that every human being is a sinner, namely, Christ; but he was also the only-begotten Son of God, the second Adam, the head of another and better covenant, and conceived in a special manner by the Holy Spirit.
The Roman Catholics, however, also make an exception for Mary, the mother of Jesus.
The three privileges gradually granted to Mary in Roman Catholic theology—freedom from original sin (_immaculata conceptio_), freedom from all actual sin (_perfectio justitiae_), and freedom from death (_assumptio in coelum_)—are simply deductions from the high rank of mediatrix to which the church elevated her on the ground of her _virginitas_ and _deipartus_.
With respect to the immaculate conception, Pius IX established in the bull _Ineffabilis_: _beatissimam virginem Mariam in primo instanti conceptionis suae fuisse singulari omnipotentis Dei gratia et privilegio, intuitu meritorum Christi Jesu Salvatoris humani generis, ab omni originalis culpae labe praeservatam immunem_.
This does not imply that Mary was not included in Adam and did not fall in him, for Mary was preserved from original sin only by a special grace of God and in view of Christ’s merits.
But neither does it state that Mary was first conceived in sin and then immediately sanctified, for it expressly declares that she was preserved from original sin in the very first moment of her conception.
For this dogma, however, there is not the slightest ground in Scripture.
Thomas said frankly: _quod de sanctificatione B. Mariae, quod scilicet fuerit sanctificata in utero, nihil in Scriptura canonica traditur_; _S. Theol._ III qu. 27 art. 1.
The Roman Catholic theologians are therefore in no small embarrassment here, seek all kinds of reasons to explain this “hiddenness of Mary” in Scripture, and force the strangest texts into a semblance of proof.
Thus they appeal to Gen. 3:15; Ps. 45:11 f.; Song 1:8–16; 2:2; 3:6; 4:1 f.; 6:9; Wis. 1:4; Luke 1:28, 41, 48; Rev. 12; and to types such as Noah’s ark, the dove with the olive branch, the burning bush, and so forth; but all these quotations and arguments serve only to conceal their lack of arguments and need no refutation; cf., for example, Spencer Northcote, _Maria in den Evang._, Mainz, 1889; Schaefer, _Die Gottesmutter in der h. Schrift_, Münster, 1867; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 455–472.
Rather, Scripture decisively teaches that all human beings except Christ alone are sinners; no exception is ever made for Mary; although no definite sinful words or deeds of hers are recorded, not even in Mark 3:21; John 2:3, she nevertheless rejoices in God her Savior, Luke 1:47; she is called blessed because of her motherhood of Christ but never because of her sinlessness, Luke 1:28, 48; even in this motherhood itself she is placed below those who are Jesus’ mother and brothers and sisters in the spiritual sense, Matt. 12:46 f.; Mark 3:31 f.; Luke 8:21; and she perseveres with the apostles in prayer and supplication, Acts 1:14.
Neither do the church fathers teach either the immaculate conception or the sinlessness of Mary; Irenaeus, _adv. haer._ III 16, 7; Tertullian, _de carne Chr._ 7; Origen, _hom. in Luc._ 17, and others, speak of actual transgressions in her; and even Roman Catholic theologians cannot deny this.
Dr. von Lehner, _Die Marienverehrung der ersten Jahrh._, Stuttgart, 1881, S. 151, says that this was the prevailing conception at the time; Schwane, _D. G._ I² 382, acknowledges that the tradition from that period furnishes no more stringent proofs than Holy Scripture; and Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 474, 476, concedes that the person of Mary recedes into the background in the first four centuries and remains in relative _Dunkelheit_.
At most, it was believed _propter honorem Domini_ that by special grace Mary had remained free from actual sins; Augustine, _de nat. et gr._ 36; Damascenus, _de fide orthod._ IV 14.
Even when the veneration of Mary increased more and more from the fifth century onward and later the feast of her conception arose, the foremost theologians—as Canus, _Loci_ VII c. 1; Scheeben, _Dogm._ III 541 f., and others also acknowledge—did indeed teach a _sanctificatio B. Virginis post animationem et contractionem peccati in anima_ but opposed a _praeservatio_ that _apriori_ kept Mary free from all original sin; Anselm, _Cur Deus homo_ II 16; Lombardus, Thomas, Bonaventure on _Sent._ III dist. 3; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 2 qu. 81 art. 3; III qu. 27 art. 1, 2; _Comp. Theol._ c. 224, etc.
But Duns Scotus brought about a change in this; he argued that even though Mary was included in Adam, God could nevertheless grant her in the very first instant of her conception the _gratia_ that preserved her from all sin.
And because this was more worthy of God, Christ, and Mary and was not in conflict with the authority of Scripture and the church, he considered it _probabile, quod excellentius est attribuere Mariae_; _Sent._ III dist. 3 qu. 1.
And this also indicates the ground upon which this dogma rests in Rome.
It has no support in Scripture or in the tradition of the ancient church, but like the assumption of Mary it is simply a deduction from the mediatorship gradually ascribed to her.
It is not fitting, not _conveniens_, that Mary was conceived in sin, committed sin, and died.
She must be sinless, and therefore she is, even though neither Scripture nor tradition teaches it.
