## 2.

Upon these data of Scripture the doctrine of original sin was built in the Christian church. From the beginning a certain connection was acknowledged between Adam’s sin and that of his descendants; cf. the many citations in Vossius, _Hist. Pelag._, p. 134 sq.; Horn, _Comm. de sententiis eorum patrum, quorum auctoritas ante Aug. plurimum valuit, de peccato originali_, Göttingen, 1801; Wiggers, _Aug. u. Pelag._ I 403 f.; Münscher–von Coelln, _D. G._ I 343, 353; Schwane, _D. G._ II 439; Harnack, _D. G._ III 38, 151. To this extent the claim that the doctrine of original sin was an invention of Augustine is wholly incorrect, and he himself could testify, _non ego finxi peccatum originale, quod catholica fides credit antiquitus_, _De nupt._ II 12. Yet it is true that in the earliest period, especially in the East, theology placed much more emphasis over against Gnosticism on free will and personal, actual sin than on original sin from Adam. And where it was acknowledged that a _malum animae ex originis vitio antecedit_, Tertullian, _De an._ 41, the essence of that original sin was nevertheless not more closely defined and the manner of its propagation not considered more deeply. Pelagius could therefore appeal to many predecessors with some appearance of right, but he nevertheless went much further than they, taught something essentially different, and denied original sin. According to Pelagius, the image of God consisted only in free personality, not in positive holiness, immortality, and so forth. Adam’s transgression did not deprive him of that image of God and in fact had no harmful consequences at all. There is no original sin. Adam’s transgression harmed his descendants only insofar as it set an evil example which, being followed by others, made sin a power in humanity. The inheritance of sin is a Manichaean error. Sin is not a condition but an act and always bears a personal character; it would conflict with God’s justice to impute another’s sins to us. Propagation in marriage would also be unlawful if the inheritance of sin took place by this means. Moreover, baptized parents can no longer propagate sin, since it has been effaced in baptism. Sin is therefore propagated not through generation but through imitation. Human beings, whose souls are created pure by God, are still born in the same condition in which Adam was before the fall. Sickness, suffering, death, and so forth are not punishments for sin. The human being is still completely free and can know and do the good from himself; he does not need grace. It is possible to abstain from all sins, and some human beings have in fact attained this; cf. Augustine’s anti-Pelagian writings and the literature, vol. II, p. 320. In this denial of original sin Pelagius agreed with the Jews, who believed that souls were created pure by God and were able to oppose the יצר הרע dwelling in the flesh and to live without sin, Weber, _System_ 217 f. Later the Socinians agreed with him, Fock, _Der Socin._ 654 f.; some Anabaptists, Cloppenburg, _Op._ II 151 sq.; many humanists and rationalists, Wegscheider, _Instit. theol._ § 117; and today also Ritschl and his school. According to Ritschl, behind particular, actual sins we may not assume a general concept of sin, for such a concept is wholly unintelligible. A passively inherited condition cannot be conceived as sin. Scripture does not teach it: Ps. 51:7 is only an individual confession, Eph. 2:3 refers to the former actual sins of those who are now Christians, and Rom. 5:12 is too obscure for anything to be derived from it. Original sin would remove all responsibility, make education impossible, and nullify differences of degree in sins. Every human being would then already have risen to the highest degree of sin in original sin, and personal, actual sins would scarcely count alongside it. No, sin is not a unity by virtue of one principle but a collective unity as the result of all individual actions and inclinations. And the subject of sin is not humanity as a race, which is an abstraction and only a memory-image, but humanity as the sum of all individuals. It is not first the sinful condition and then the act, but conversely the self-determination of the will is the ground of all sins. Only the will that continually does evil gradually acquires an inclination, a _habitus_, and this then in turn affects acts of the will. Thus there indeed arises in the human being a _Gesetz der Sünde_, a kingdom of sin, a common sin, but this is something entirely different from original sin and was wrongly so called by Schleiermacher. A sinless development of life therefore cannot be denied _a priori_; the will of children is not directed toward evil, and there is rather a general inclination to good in them, Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 311 f.; _Unterricht in d. chr. Rel._, 1886, p. 25; Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 256.

This Pelagian explanation of the universality of sin, however, encounters too many and too serious objections to find acceptance for long or among many. In the previous century, with its rationalism, individualism, and optimism, there was room for it. Rousseau disclosed the thoughts of many hearts when he glorified uncorrupted nature and sought the cause of all sin and misery in society. But the historical sense that awakened after the Revolution, the insight into the incalculable value of communal life, society, and state, the organic view that has penetrated everywhere, and above all the doctrine of heredity, whose significance also in the spiritual and moral domain has been brought to light more than before, have put an end to the individualistic and atomistic conception of humanity and of the sin reigning within it. If anything is certain, it is that sin is not merely an accidental phenomenon in the lives of individuals but a condition and way of life of the whole human race, a property of the species. Sinful deeds, which do not occur now and then but belong to all human beings in every age and circumstance, point back to a sinful disposition, just as evil fruits presuppose an evil tree and turbid water points back to an unclean source. This alone explains why all children from their youth display a desire for what is forbidden and an inclination to every kind of evil. Pelagians have indeed repeatedly spoken of the innocence of children with an appeal to Jonah 4:11; Ps. 106:38; Matt. 18:3; 19:14; Luke 18:17; John 9:3; 1 Cor. 7:14. And in a relative sense this is also correct. There is a difference in degree among sins; in connection with age and development, sin in children has not yet come to its full manifestation and cannot yet display itself in such dreadful forms as at a later age. But the experience of all parents and teachers also teaches that as knowledge and so forth increase, the sinful inclination also develops in children. And if we ourselves examine our own lives, we find sin as far back as we can remember; and the deeper our consciousness of guilt descends, the more it reaches back to the sins of youth, indeed of birth and conception, Pss. 25:7; 51:7. The passages of Scripture named above therefore offer Pelagians no support. At most they speak of a relative innocence but do not teach the sinlessness of children. Further, the assertion that some human beings have lived or can live without sin is devoid of all probability and every ground. No one understands Xenophon’s testimony concerning Socrates, _Mem._ I 1, 11; IV 8, 11, in an absolute sense. Numerous sinful deeds of the devout in the Old and New Testaments are reported to us. Those most advanced on the way of sanctification have felt their guilt and imperfection all the more deeply in the same measure. And so strong and universal is the conviction of the sinfulness of the whole human race that if someone appeared with the claim to be sinless, we would all immediately ascribe it to lack of self-knowledge, pride, or insanity, Müller, _Sünde_ II 370. This absolute universality of sin cannot be explained by imitation. It precedes every conscious and intentional act of will and is in us all a condition long before it passes into deeds. That Ritschl does not acknowledge this is connected in him too with nominalism. Humanity is not an organism but the sum of all individuals; sin is not a condition but consists only in acts of will; things in general exist—or at least are knowable—only in their operations. Yet Ritschl himself does not remain faithful to his point of departure. For although he denies innate sin and teaches the integrity of human nature, he nevertheless, like Pelagius, assigns such power to evil example and the influence of society that the universality of sin is explained by them and even a common sin arises. Now one of two things must be true: either these external influences operate only accidentally, in which case they explain nothing and the universality of sin remains a riddle; or in fact they cause universal sinfulness, but then they are of a far worse nature than the scriptural doctrine of original sin. Further, Ritschl indeed opposes this doctrine by claiming that it abolishes all degrees in sin, but this rests on misunderstanding. He himself has only two classes of sins, those of ignorance and those of definitive wickedness, which certainly do not encompass the many-sidedness of sinful life. And the attempts to remove this doctrine from Holy Scripture, despite all their acumen, bear too violent a character to be called successful. Cf. Pfleiderer, “Die Ritschl’sche Theologie,” in _Jahrb. f. prot. Theol._, 1889; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 149; Orr, _The Ritschlian Theology_, London, 1897, p. 145.
