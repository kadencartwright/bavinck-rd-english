## 8.

The way in which this _peccatum originatum_ becomes the portion of all human beings is not imitation but generation. A κριμα, a judgment of God, precedes it, and by virtue of that judgment all human beings are born from Adam guilty, unclean, and dying. They do not first become all this at a later age through their actual sins, but are so from their conception and birth onward. Death is proof, for it reigns not only over adults but much more powerfully over little children, even the unborn; and according to Scripture this death is not a natural process but the wages of sin. Now this doctrine of original sin, although formerly condemned as irrational, has recently found favor again in the eyes of so-called science. After all, it proved not to be so foolish and absurd as earlier interpreters of science had portrayed it. What is scientific now is precisely the opposite of what counted as such in the previous century. The doctrine of the human being’s innate goodness and of society as the cause of all evil has had its day. Today the precise opposite is taught: the human being, descended from an animal, remains an animal at heart; in every human being lurks _la bête humaine_, _vitium hominis natura pecoris_; and it is “holy” society and the “divine” state that fortunately keep him in restraint and compel him to virtue. Every vice is innate, every virtue acquired; cf., for example, Brunetière, _La moralité de la doctrine évolutive_, Paris, 1896. It is not newly discovered facts that have brought about this change in outlook. That every kind produces its like, that children take after their parents, that not only physical but also all kinds of spiritual properties are inherited—this is not a discovery of the 19th century but, as the doctrine of traducianism shows, was well known earlier too. And that, conversely, not all properties are inherited, that each individual is nevertheless something different from and more than the sum or product of his parents—that too, as the doctrine of creationism shows, was not unknown to earlier generations. The knowledge of the facts has certainly been expanded and increased, but by no means in such a way that it alone could radically change an entire outlook. This change is chiefly to be ascribed to the fact that roughly the same facts were viewed from another standpoint and with another eye—namely, from the standpoint of monism and with the eye of a dogmatician of evolution. Contrary to every sober scientific sense, a series of far-reaching conclusions has been built upon a few facts and promoted to the public in all kinds of popular writings: the human being—it had become plainly evident—was no independent being whatsoever, but nothing other than a product of existing factors, a plaything of circumstances. To speak of freedom in his case is foolishness; he is a will-less instrument of fate. Criminals are a distinct type, already born as such and incorrigible and unaccountable. Every human being must necessarily be what he is. An army of in part talented writers, such as Zola, Ibsen, Nietzsche, and others, have painted this outlook in novel and drama for the fevered imagination. And whoever does not join this chorus and doubts the scientific character of this outlook is placed under the ban. Yet for the sober observer, everything science has brought to light in this field amounts to the following little: 1º There is not only heredity but also variation, not only unity but also change, not only memory but also imagination. Many properties pass from parents to children, for all kinds produce their like; yet not all properties pass over, for each individual is something new. No child resembles its parents exactly. Nothing new has been said here; only an old, familiar truth never denied by anyone has been repeated. 2º. These two facts are very readily labeled laws, but how those laws rule is still almost entirely unknown. Heredity and variation stand in continual relation to each other; they work together and intersect, and for that reason explanation of the facts is so difficult. And this difficulty is increased still further by the fact that inheritance in all higher beings takes place through the commingling of two individuals (_amphimixis_, as Weismann called it). 3º. This becomes evident as soon as one specializes somewhat more closely. It is certain that species properties are inherited—that is, that parents bring forth children of the same species—but this does not take us much further. That the properties of races and varieties are constantly transmitted is subject to legitimate doubt. Through selection a race, for example of animals or plants, can indeed be improved, but this improvement is limited and temporary. After four or five generations, the height of improvement has been reached. As soon as selection ceases, the descendants return to the old type. The properties are not incorporated into the nature of the creatures; they retain the tendency to revert to the original form. Finally, that individual, acquired properties pass from parents to children has indeed been claimed by Darwin and others, but is opposed as strongly as possible by A. Weismann, professor at Freiburg, and his theory is today finding increasing agreement. 4º. The attempt to combine the phenomena occurring here under a few laws has not only failed but must for the present be regarded as highly premature. The phenomena are far too numerous and complicated—like those of the weather, for example—to permit us already to speak of fixed laws. The law of atavism therefore gives every appearance of having been invented only to lend a semblance of regularity to the many cases in which favorable properties are not inherited and the “law” of heredity therefore does not hold. _Nicht begriffene Thatsachen scheinen schliesslich bekannt, sobald man sie mit einem bekannten Worte bezeichnet_, Dr. Kohlbrugge, _Der Atavismus_, Utrecht, 1897, p. 3, who accordingly regards atavism, as Emery does, as a legend. Lombroso’s hypothesis concerning the criminal type already belongs again to the past. And although statistics may indicate a certain regularity in births, marriages, crimes, and so forth, it is by no means thereby entitled to the conclusion that every person is compelled to his deeds, any more than the fact that the average age of a population is thirty years compels every thirty-year-old to die, Wundt, _Grundzüge der physiol. Psych._ II² 397; A. von Oettingen, _Die Moralstatistik_³, 1882, p. 24 f. Finally, 5º. the various theories explaining heredity and variation have all proved inadequate to the present day. The very large number that have been proposed proves that none is satisfactory. All natural philosophers and biologists have tried their powers upon it without having unveiled this mystery of life. The professor at the Sorbonne, Yves Delage, discussed all the theories at length in his learned work _La structure du protoplasma et les théories sur l’hérédité et les grands problèmes de la biologie générale_, Paris: Reinwald et Cie, 1895, and then comes to the conclusion: _après avoir étudié et discuté les nombreuses théories émises pour résoudre les problèmes de l’hérédité et de l’évolution, nous sommes obligés de reconnaître, qu’aucune ne présente une solution acceptable. Toutes pèchent en quelques points, non pas accessoires mais fondamentaux, et la plupart sont, en outre, appuyées sur des hypothéses gratuites et tout à fait improbables_, p. 743. He himself therefore also does not venture to offer the reader a _théorie complète_: _nos connaissances sont loin d’être assez avancées pour que cela soit possible_, p. 747; cf. also Hugo de Vries, “Eenheid in veranderlijkheid,” _Album der Natuur_, 1898, pp. 65–80; Ribot, _L’hérédité psychol._⁵, Paris: Alcan, 1894; van Bemmelen, _De erfelijkheid van verworven eigenschappen_, 1890; R. Schäfer, _Die Vererbung_, Berlin: Reuther u. Reichard, 1897; Dr. Jonker, “Erfel. en Toerekenb.,” in _Theol. Stud._ of Dr. Daubanton, 1894, pp. 291–322, and so forth. With all this, however, we do not deny the facts of heredity or its extensive dominion. Christian theology has not the slightest interest in detracting from it in any way. On the contrary, it fully acknowledges and honors the laws established by God in this domain. The more fixed laws are discovered in heredity, the greater becomes the glory of him who is the Creator of all ordinances and a God not of confusion but of order. It is also entirely true that we can almost never indicate precisely the boundaries separating personal guilt from common guilt. What Schleiermacher says of original sin is something entirely different from what Scripture and church declare concerning it, but in itself it is nevertheless entirely true of sin in general that it is a _Gesammtthat und Gesammtschuld des menschlichen Geschlechtes_; that is, the sinful condition and sinful deeds of every human being are on the one hand caused by those of preceding generations and on the other hand are themselves again the cause of the sinful conditions and deeds of later generations. Sin is _in Jedem das Werk Aller und in Allen das Werk eines Jeden_, _Chr. Gl._ § 71, 1, 2. But however true all this may be, so long as biology must acknowledge variation alongside heredity, there is no right to take away the human being’s independence and freedom and to represent him as a will-less instrument of evil powers. Such a representation does not rest upon sound science but is a fruit of diseased imagination, and by killing all power of will in the human being it causes incalculable devastation. Further, although the support offered by the science of the day to the church’s doctrine of original sin may to a certain extent be acknowledged with gratitude, the doctrine itself is not made stronger by it, just as it is not weakened if that same science should perhaps be pleased tomorrow once again to exhibit it as foolish and absurd. Original sin is still something different from what is understood today by heredity. For it is not a species property belonging to the essence of the human being, since it entered human nature through transgression of God’s command and can again be removed from it through regeneration and sanctification. On the other hand, neither is it an individual acquired property, for it belongs to all human beings without exception and is so inherent in human nature that even regenerate persons bring forth children who are by nature children of wrath: _justus non generat unde ipse regeneratus sed unde generatus est_ (Augustine). Original sin therefore occupies a peculiar position. The present doctrine of heredity may have divested it of its apparent absurdity, but it does not explain it. In former times this was attempted by traducianism or creationism. But whatever position one adopts concerning the origin of souls, the propagation of original sin always remains equally difficult. Original sin is not a substance seated in the body and capable of being transmitted by generation. It is a moral quality of the human being who lacks the fellowship with God that he ought to possess according to his original nature and once did possess. Adam’s corruption arose of itself and at the same moment when, in doubt and unbelief, in pride and desire, he tore himself loose from God. And in the same way moral corruption arises in his descendants from the first moment of their existence. As God withdrew his fellowship from Adam on account of his transgression, so he also does this from all his children. And just as God, while nevertheless continuing to sustain Adam after his transgression, did not become the positive cause of his corruption by withdrawing his fellowship, neither does he become so with Adam’s descendants, whether one conceives the origin of souls in a traducianist or creationist manner. Every human being is born under guilt and in pollution by virtue of the physical and ethical relation in which he stands to Adam. _Est ergo quisque sui individui peccati originalis et proximum principium et subjectum et auctor_, Voetius, _Disp._ I 1104; cf. Martyr, _L. C._, p. 70; Polanus, _Synt._ VI c. 3; Zanchi, _Op._ IV 50; Voetius, _Disp._ I 1078 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ IX 12; Moor III 289; M. Vitringa II 358; Edwards, _Works_ II 478.
