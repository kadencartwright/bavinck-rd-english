## 6.

Yet the objection raised against *justitia imputata* must be seriously considered. 1º Bellarmine, *De justif.* II, ch. 7, developed it in this way: the righteousness of Christ, if merely imputed to us and thus remaining outside us, cannot be the *forma* in which we are justified before God. God’s judgment is according to truth. He cannot declare someone righteous who is not righteous. As long as Christ’s righteousness is only imputed and remains outside the human being, he is not righteous and cannot be declared righteous. It will be said: but through faith the sinner is clothed with Christ’s righteousness! Yet even if this is so, when someone appears in two forms, a *forma extrinseca* and a *forma inhaerens*, he is named not according to the former but according to the latter. Let an Ethiopian put on a white garment; he nevertheless remains black and is called such, even though he is white according to the *forma extrinseca*. Indeed, more strongly still, Christ too can be considered in two forms: according to the *forma intrinseca* he was holy; according to the *forma extrinseca* he was burdened with our sins. Yet he is named not according to the latter but according to the former. And so in justification *justitia imputata* cannot be our *forma* either; we can be justified only on the ground of a righteousness dwelling within us. This objection of Bellarmine recurs in all opponents of the Reformation’s doctrine; everything they have against the doctrine of *justitia imputata* comes down to this. To begin, then, with the last point: Christ is indeed named and treated in Scripture according to the *forma extrinseca*. He is even said to have been made sin for us and to have become a curse for us. In a legal, juridical sense Christ can be called a sinner, although, to avoid an antinomian misunderstanding, the expression is not to be commended; above, p. 366. And so it is said in Rom. 4:5 and 5:6 that God justifies the ungodly. Stronger expressions than these cannot be used. The opponents of *justitia imputata* must raise their objection not against Luther and Calvin but against Paul. 2º The illustration of the Moor is ill chosen. The two forms in which the human being appears in justification stand in an entirely different relation to one another than do the black skin and the white garment of the Ethiopian. The human being is ungodly in an ethical sense, but on account of the righteousness of Christ he becomes righteous in a juridical sense. Putting on a white garment, however, brings about no legal change whatsoever in the Ethiopian. More apt is the illustration of the child who, adopted by a rich man as an act of grace, may already be called rich as a future heir, even though at present the child has nothing in its possession. God declares the sinner righteous, adopts him as his child, promises him Christ and all his benefits, and therefore he is righteous and will one day be placed in possession of all the treasures of grace. 3º The imputation of Christ’s righteousness, however, is understood entirely wrongly by Bellarmine and his associates. They conceive of it as a fiction that conflicts with reality; according to them, *justitia imputata* is a righteousness that exists only in the imagination, and *justitia infusa* alone is true, essential righteousness. This, however, is an entirely false conception. Justification is as true as sanctification, imputation as real as infusion. This alone is the difference: in justification righteousness is granted to us in a juridical sense; in sanctification it becomes our portion in an ethical sense. Both are equally real and both equally necessary. A judge must first pronounce someone’s right to a certain property before that person can come into possession of it. The former is no fiction, no figment of the imagination that is of no consequence and conflicts with reality. No, first the *imputatio justitiae* is necessary, the recognition of the right, and only thereafter can the *infusio justitiae* follow, the taking possession of that to which one has a right. If all this is already true of the earthly judge, how much more of the heavenly judge? When God justifies the ungodly, that is no fiction, no *imputatio putativa*, but it is so and becomes so. As God pronounces judgment, so it is and so it remains eternally, and so it will one day also be acknowledged by all on the day of judgment. For when God justifies the ungodly, he does so on the ground of a δικαιοσυνη that he himself has brought about in Christ. Through Christ’s sacrifice he acquired, over against every hostile power, the right to acquit the ungodly; and when he pronounces judgment, he will also execute it. The ungodly person is righteous in a legal sense and will therefore certainly also become righteous in an ethical sense. For God is the one who makes the dead alive and calls the things that do not exist as though they did. And justifying faith consists precisely in unshakable trust in that God of miracles, with whom all things are possible. 4º The righteousness on the ground of which the ungodly person is justified is indeed not his own; it is a δικαιοσυνη θεου, standing over against the ἰδια δικαιοσυνη. Yet it is not foreign and outside him in the sense that it has nothing to do with him and stands in not the slightest relation to him. On the contrary, already in the *pactum salutis* Christ placed himself in relation to his own and, as mediator, took their place. In the state of humiliation he died on account of their sins, and he was raised for their justification. There exists a covenant of grace, a *unio mystica* between Christ and his church, long before believers are personally incorporated into it; otherwise Christ could not have made satisfaction for them either. An imputation and gift of Christ and all his benefits takes place on God’s part before the particular persons come to faith. Specifically, this imputation and gift takes place in the *vocatio interna*, and regeneration is the passive acceptance of this gift of grace. God must first give in order that we may be able to receive. The very first grace granted to us already presupposes the imputation of Christ, for he is the only source of grace, the acquirer and distributor of the Spirit, who is his Spirit, the Spirit of Christ. Thus the righteousness that is the ground of justification is a foreign righteousness only in a certain sense. It is the righteousness of the head and therefore also of all the members, of the mediator and therefore also of all the covenant partners.
