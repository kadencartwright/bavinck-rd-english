## 5.

Now God says in his law that the righteous must be justified and the unrighteous condemned, Deut. 25:1, and everyone’s conscience and sense of justice agrees with this. God himself acts according to this rule. He by no means holds the guilty innocent and he does not condemn the innocent, Ex. 20:5f.; 34:7; Num. 14:18. The one who justifies the wicked and the one who condemns the righteous are both an abomination to the Lord, Prov. 17:15; cf. Ex. 23:7; Prov. 24:24; Isa. 5:23. And yet, apparently directly contrary to this and as if contradicting himself, Rom. 1:18; 2:13, Paul says that God justifies the ungodly, 4:5. The human being, namely, has no righteousness in himself on the ground of which he could be acquitted by God. Those of Pelagian inclination, who find the ground of acquittal in faith, that is, in the human being’s good disposition, virtues, and good works, and regard these as perfect because they bear within themselves the guarantee of perfection or are reckoned by God as perfect for Christ’s sake, come into conflict at every point with the doctrine of Scripture and the Christian confession. For Scripture testifies that no flesh can or will be justified from works of the law, Isa. 64:6; Rom. 3:19, 20; 8:7; Eph. 2:2; etc. Works performed after justification from faith cannot enter into consideration for justification, because then the order of salvation would be reversed and justification made dependent upon sanctification, and because even those good works are still always imperfect and stained with sin, not answering to the full demand of the divine law, Matt. 22:37; Gal. 3:10; James 2:10. God as the truthful one cannot regard as perfect what is not; as the righteous and holy one he cannot abandon the demand of the law or be satisfied with half a righteousness, which at bottom is none. Scripture therefore also sets one’s own righteousness and the righteousness of faith or the righteousness of God over against one another, Rom. 10:3; Phil. 3:9. They exclude each other as works and faith, Rom. 3:28; Gal. 2:16, as wages and grace, Rom. 4:4; 11:6. While the law thus condemns and must condemn the human being on account of his sin, it pleased God to reveal another righteousness that can be the ground of his acquittal. This righteousness is called δικαιοσυνη θεου by Paul. Apart from James 1:20 and 2 Pet. 1:1, the expression occurs in Paul in Rom. 1:17; 3:5, 21, 22, 25, 26; 10:3; 2 Cor. 5:21; Phil. 3:9, and has a distinctive sense in him. In the Old Testament God’s righteousness is that virtue by which he judges justly, does not hold the guilty innocent or the innocent guilty, and then especially helps and saves the poor and afflicted—who indeed are personally guilty but substantively have the right on their side—and recognizes them in their right; above, p. 340. But this righteousness of God appeared at the end of the Old Testament economy to have disappeared and been entirely lost. For the whole world was liable to condemnation before God, Rom. 3:19; no one was justified before God from works of the law, 3:20; and the sins committed beforehand had been passed over without punishment in God’s forbearance, 3:25. It was therefore necessary that his righteousness should once again be revealed. God did this not by condemning the whole world but by giving in Christ an ἱλαστηριον, a means or sacrifice of atonement, for sins. Thereby it appeared that God himself was righteous, but thereby it also became possible that, while preserving—indeed, in accordance with—his righteousness, he could justify the one who is of faith in Jesus, 3:25, 26. For—and so Paul arrives at the modification of the concept δικαιοσυνη θεου distinctive to him—in Christ, in the gospel, apart from the law, God revealed a “righteousness of God” standing over against ἰδια δικαιοσυνη. His righteousness as virtue manifested itself most gloriously in this, that in the gospel he granted another righteousness, apart from works of the law, on the ground of which he can justify the one who is of faith in Jesus. This is called the righteousness of God not because it is a human righteousness which is indeed outside him but is received by him in faith and as such really counts “before God” (objective genitive, appealing, e.g., to Rom. 2:13; Gal. 3:11, παρα τῳ θεῳ; 3:20, ἐνωπιον αὐτου)—Luther, Calvin, Kantt., Philippi, Umbreit, Fritzsche. Nor is it so called because it is a human righteousness infused into him and by which he can stand before God—Osiander, Schleiermacher, Rothe, Martensen, Nitzsch, Beck, and others. Rather, it is so called because it is a righteousness that belongs not to human beings but to God, which he possesses and which therefore he alone gives and can give, which is not from the human being but ἐκ θεου, Rom. 10:3, 5; Phil. 3:9. Thus most recent exegetes, whether they conceive the genitive more as a subjective genitive (Haussleiter), possessive (Fricke), or of origin, author, efficient cause (Bengel, Rückert, Van Hengel, Winer, Baur, Hofmann, Godet, Weiss, Pfleiderer, Holtzmann, and others). Cf., besides the literature named earlier, vol. II, 198f.: Rauwenhoff, *Disq. de loco Paulino, qui est de δικαιωσει*, Leiden, 1852; Lipsius, *Die Paulin. Rechtfertigungslehre*, Leipzig, 1853; Ortloph, *Zeits. f. luth. Theol. u. Kirche*, 1860; Schultz, “Die Lehre v. d. Gerecht. aus d. Glauben im A. u. N. B.,” *Jahrb. f. d. Th.*, 1862, pp. 510–572; Cremer, s.v. δικ.; Weiss, *Bibl. Theol.*, § 82; Pfleiderer, *Der Paulin.*² 183; Weiszäcker, *Das apost. Zeitalter*² 143; H. Beck, “Die δικ. θεου bei Paulus,” *Neue Jahrb. f. d. Th.*, 1894, pp. 249–261; Holtzmann, *Neut. Theol.* II 127; Häring, *Δικ. θεου bei Paulus*, Tübingen, 1896 (understands δικ. θεου as an act, God’s justification, as does Kölbing, *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1895, pp. 7–17).

Although this righteousness is a righteousness of God, it is nevertheless communicated to the human being, and Paul establishes the connection between it and the human being in faith. This righteousness of God *exists*; no more than the καταλλαγη, 2 Cor. 5:19, is it effected by the human being, by faith, but it lies objectively in Christ, Rom. 4:25; 1 Cor. 1:30; is revealed in the gospel, 1:17; 3:21; and is a gift, a gift of grace, 3:24; 5:15, 16, 17. Nevertheless—or rather, precisely for that reason, 4:16—it is δια πιστεως Ιησου Χριστου, 3:22; Phil. 3:9; ἐκ πιστεως, 9:30; 10:6; a δικαιοσυνη πιστεως, 4:11, 13; and in the human being’s possession ἐπι τῃ πιστει, Phil. 3:9. It is revealed in the gospel ἐκ πιστεως and εἰς πιστιν, 1:17; εἰς παντας τους πιστευοντας, 3:22; cf. 10:4, 10. God justifies τον ἐκ πιστεως Ιησου, 3:26, ἐκ or δια πιστεως, 3:30; Gal. 3:8. And the human being is justified πιστει, 3:28; ἐκ πιστεως, 5:1; Gal. 3:24; δια πιστεως, Gal. 2:16. Faith is reckoned εἰς δικαιοσυνην, Rom. 4:3, 5, 9, 11, 22, and the righteous person lives ἐκ πιστεως, 1:17; Gal. 3:11. What place, then, according to these expressions, belongs to faith in justification? Apart from those who modernize Paul, conceive faith as a good disposition, and have God accept the will for the deed, only two views are possible. The first is that of Roman Catholics, Remonstrants, mystics, ethical theologians, and many recent Protestant theologians, who say that faith is indeed specifically faith in Christ but nevertheless conceive it as the whole of, or as a part of, that righteousness on the ground of which God acquits the sinner. That faith is indeed imperfect and does not answer to the demand of the law, but God nevertheless regards it as a perfect righteousness and is satisfied with it—whether for Christ’s sake, or because it is nevertheless an obedience to God’s will in the gospel and makes the human being pleasing to God, or because it is perfect in principle and bears within itself the guarantee of future perfection. But for all sorts of reasons this opinion conflicts with Scripture. 1º The righteousness that is the ground of justification is a righteousness of God and not of the human being. It becomes the human being’s portion only through a gift of grace; it thus lies ready before his faith in Christ and needs no supplementation whatever. God, namely, set forth Christ as an ἱλαστηριον, 3:24, and this Christ was delivered up for our sins, 4:25; died for us, 5:6–11; became a curse, Gal. 3:13; was made sin, 2 Cor. 5:21; and was thus also raised for our justification, Rom. 4:25, that is, because we were justified in him. He is our righteousness, 1 Cor. 1:30, and we are justified δια της ἀπολυτρωσεως της ἐν Χριστω Ιησου, 3:24; ἐν τῳ αἱματι αὐτου, 5:9; ἐν Χριστῳ, Gal. 2:16. In Rom. 5:12f. Paul argues that in Christ matters proceed as in Adam. On the ground of one transgression all human beings are condemned and subjected to death, but so also the gracious gift of righteousness in Christ comes to δικαιωμα, that is, to a judgment of acquittal for many, 5:16. Through one δικαιωμα, that is, the judgment of acquittal over Christ in his resurrection, 4:25, it comes for all human beings to δικαιωσις ζωης, that is, the act of justification that brings life, 5:18. Through the obedience of one the many are constituted righteous, δικαιοι κατασταθησονται, 5:19. Alongside the righteousness God granted in Christ and on the ground of which in Christ’s resurrection he justified Christ as mediator of the covenant for all his own, there is no place for a righteousness consisting in faith or love. The latter would nullify the former. 2º Nowhere, therefore, is faith represented as the ground of justification. Righteousness, justification, is ἐκ or δια πιστεως, but never δια πιστιν. Phil. 3:9 does indeed say that Paul possessed την δια Χριστου, την ἐκ θεου δικαιοσυνην ἐπι τῃ πιστει, on the ground of his faith, but the righteousness Paul possessed is clearly described as δια πιστεως, ἐκ θεου. He says only that for himself he possessed that righteousness of God on the foundation of faith. Faith never occurs as righteousness itself or as a part of it. On the contrary, precisely because it is according to grace, it is from faith. Grace and faith do not stand over against one another, but faith and works do, the righteousness of faith and righteousness from works, Rom. 3:20–28; 4:4–6, 13, 14; 9:32; 10:5, 6; Gal. 2:16; 3:11, 12, 23, 25; 5:4, 5; Eph. 2:8, 9. Faith justifies not through its essence or act, because it is itself righteousness, but through its content, because it is faith in Christ, our righteousness. If faith justified on account of itself, the object of that faith, namely Christ, would lose all his value. But the faith that justifies is precisely that which has Christ as object and content. If therefore righteousness were from the law, and faith were a work possessing merit and value and making the human being pleasing to God, then Christ would have died in vain, Gal. 2:21. So little does faith enter into consideration as ground in justification that Paul can say that God justifies the ungodly, Rom. 4:5. Even when his doctrine elicits the accusation that it leads to carelessness and godlessness, he never defends himself by saying that faith is wholly or partly the ground of justification, Rom. 3:5–8; 6:1, but maintains that there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ because Christ died and was raised for them, Rom. 8:33, 34. 3º Since faith is therefore not a work but a renunciation of every work, an unconditional trust in God, who makes the dead alive, Rom. 4:17, who raised Christ, 4:24, who in Christ gave a δικαιοσυνη θεου, 3:22–26, the expression that faith is reckoned for righteousness cannot mean that it is itself accepted by God as a work of righteousness in place of or alongside God’s righteousness in Christ. For the word λογιζεσθαι can indeed mean to hold or reckon someone to be what he is, 1 Cor. 4:1; 2 Cor. 12:6, but it can also have the sense of charging to someone’s account something that he personally does not possess. Thus sins are not imputed to the one who believes, although he does possess them, Rom. 4:8; 2 Cor. 5:19; cf. 2 Tim. 4:16. Thus they were indeed imputed to Christ, although he was without any sin, Isa. 53:4, 5, 6; Matt. 20:28; Rom. 3:25; 8:3; 2 Cor. 5:21; Gal. 3:13; 1 Tim. 2:6. And in the same way the righteousness that the believer does not possess is imputed to him, Rom. 4:5, and therefore that imputation is κατα χαριν, 4:4; it is a λογιζεσθαι δικαιοσυνην χωρις ἐργων, 4:6. The words “faith is reckoned for righteousness” are an abbreviated expression for the fact that in faith God imputes to someone his righteousness revealed in Christ and on that ground acquits him. This is confirmed by that other expression, ὁ δικαιος ἐκ πιστεως ζησεται. Faith is not properly the principle and source of life, for Christ is the life and gives life, Rom. 5:17, 18; 6:4f.; 2 Cor. 4:10, 11; Gal. 2:20; Col. 3:3, 4; 2 Tim. 1:10; cf. John 1:4; 6:33f.; 11:25; 1 John 1:2; 5:11; etc. Whoever believes has life precisely because he receives it from Christ. And so too whoever believes has the δικαιοσυνη θεου that God grants him in Christ. 4º To this is finally added that, if faith itself is the ground of justification, God is satisfied with a lesser righteousness than the one he demands in his law. The gospel then does not establish the law, as Rom. 3:31 says, but destroys it. God abandons his own righteousness and denies himself. Or he reckons faith as something it is not, as a perfect and sufficient righteousness, and detracts from his truthfulness. The accusation raised by the advocates of *justitia infusa* against *justitia imputata*, that God holds someone to be what he is not, returns upon themselves. It is precisely they who have God reckon something for righteousness that is not righteousness. And moreover, they take away the comfort of believers. If our faith—which is often so small and weak and often hides entirely beneath doubt and fear, and which according to the defenders of *justitia infusa* can even be wholly lost—if that faith is the ground of our justification, the Christian life is a life of continual anxiety and uncertainty. Instead of being directed toward Christ, the eye of faith is always turned inward, toward itself. A genuine Christian life in the service of God becomes impossible, for fear of God as Judge must first have been transformed into consciousness of his fatherly love before there can be any question of truly good works.
