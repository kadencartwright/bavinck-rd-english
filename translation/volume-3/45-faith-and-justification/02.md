## 2.

In the Old Testament a technical term for faith is still lacking. The reality is not lacking, for salvation frequently appears in the form of a promise that can be accepted only by faith. Acts and activities of faith are therefore recounted to us on almost every page. But the religious relation of the human being to God is ordinarily expressed by other words, such as fearing, serving, loving, and cleaving to God; trusting, relying, and resting upon him; hoping, waiting, and so forth; above, p. 427. Most closely related to believing is the word ‎‏האמין‏‎ with ‎‏ב‏‎ or ‎‏ל‏‎ from ‎‏אמן‏‎: to make firm, make oneself firm, hold fast to something, rest upon, trust, Gen. 15:6; Ex. 4:31; 14:31; Deut. 9:23; especially Isa. 7:9—Ahaz, who seeks help from Assyria and rests upon it, will not be established if he does not turn away from it and rely upon God alone—and Hab. 2:4—the Chaldeans make their strength their god and their soul is puffed up, but the righteous shall live by his trust in God and his promise. In later Judaism faith consisted not only in accepting the law as God’s gift but also in the opinion that God’s goodness would make up what might be lacking in righteousness according to the law. Over against this, John the Baptist appears with the preaching that such faith and such righteousness have no value, that μετανοια and baptism are necessary for all in order to enter the kingdom of the Messiah. Jesus joins himself to this but adds that this kingdom has come in him, the Son of Man. He brings its good news and says: Repent and believe the gospel. As long as Jesus was on earth and himself preached, faith naturally consisted first of all in this, that one placed unconditional personal trust in Jesus and thereby was convinced that God spoke and performed miracles in and through him, e.g., Matt. 8:10; 9:28; 15:28; 17:20; 21:21, 22; etc. Jesus’ person had its correlate in the words he spoke and the works he did, and conversely these words and deeds were in turn verified by his person. But when Jesus had gone away, this change occurred, that one could no longer meet him personally but was bound to the word of the apostles in order to know him. Faith now acquired, as it were, two sides: 1º accepting as true the apostolic testimony concerning Christ; and 2º personal trust in that Christ as still living in heaven and able to forgive sins, and so forth. Although both sides occur alongside one another in the writings of the apostles, John nevertheless lays the emphasis especially upon the first moment: πιστευειν with the accusative, or with an object clause, or with the dative of the thing, John 2:22; 4:50; 5:47; 6:69; 8:24; 11:42; 13:19; 17:8, 21; 1 John 5:1, 5. Paul, by contrast, emphasizes the second and then speaks of πιστις with the genitive: of Jesus, Rom. 3:22; Gal. 2:16, 20; 3:22; Eph. 3:12; Phil. 3:9; of the truth, 2 Thess. 2:13; of the gospel, Phil. 1:27; προς θεου, 1 Thess. 1:8; εἰς Χριστον, Col. 2:5; Philem. 5; ἐν Χριστῳ, Gal. 3:26; Eph. 1:15; 2 Tim. 3:15. And he speaks of πιστευειν τινι, Rom. 4:3; Gal. 3:6; 2 Tim. 1:12; Titus 3:8; ἐπι τινα, Rom. 4:5, 24; ἐπι τινι, Rom. 9:33; 1 Tim. 1:16; and especially εἰς τινα, Rom. 10:11; Col. 2:5; Phil. 1:29; etc. But this is not an absolute opposition, for John frequently speaks of πιστευειν εἰς τινα, 2:11; 3:16, 18, 36; 4:39; 6:29; etc.; εἰς το ὀνομα, 1:12; 2:23; 1 John 5:13; and also τινι, 3:15; 5:24, 38, 46; 6:30; and τῳ ὀνοματι, 1 John 3:23. And Paul also constructs πιστευειν with τι and ὁτι, Rom. 10:9; 1 Cor. 13:7; cf. 15:14, 17; 1 Thess. 4:14. It is especially incorrect to identify with the distinction named above the later distinction between *Deo credere* and *in Deum credere*, or also that between historical and saving faith. For the construction of πιστευειν with ὁτι—for example, that Jesus is the Christ—frequently does indeed include saving faith, John 6:69; 8:24; 11:27; 17:8; 1 John 5:1; Rom. 10:9. And πιστευειν τινι or εἰς τινα is often no more than a historical faith, John 7:31, 40f.; 8:30f.; 10:42; 11:45, 48; 12:11, 42. In 1 John 5:10 there even stands πιστευειν εἰς την μαρτυριαν. And this is also understandable. Truly believing the testimony God has borne concerning his Son is possible only for, and is done only by, the one who places unconditional trust in the person of Christ. And conversely, whoever trusts in Christ as the Son of God also unconditionally accepts God’s testimony concerning Christ through the mouth of the apostles. Faith in the New Testament thus includes two elements: trust in the person of Christ and acceptance of the apostolic testimony. These two are inseparably connected; in the subjective sense they constitute the essence of Christianity. If Christ were only a historical person who by his teaching and life had left us an example, then historical belief in the transmitted testimony would be sufficient, but then it would also not arrive at true religion, at essential communion with God, and deism would be right. If, conversely, according to the opinion of pantheism, Christ were not the historical but only the ideal Christ, then faith in a testimony would be wholly superfluous and Christ would be nothing other than the being of God within us. But then once again it would not arrive at true communion between God and the human being, since this presupposes the essential distinction between the two. But now Christ is both: a historical person, the Christ of the Scriptures, and at the same time the glorified Lord in heaven, who still lives and reigns as the head of his church. He acquired salvation in the past but himself applies it in the present. And these benefits of the covenant encompass the re-creation of being and of consciousness; they consist in justification and redemption, in light and life, in truth and grace. They change the human being in the world of thought, free him from falsehood, error, and darkness, cause him to know God as the gracious one who forgives sins, place him in the right relation to God and all things, cause him to consider the things that are above, and give him in faith a firm foundation of the things hoped for and a proof of the things not seen. And they also change the human being in his being, free him from the stain of sin, cause him to live in the hidden depths of his heart in fellowship with God through Christ in the Holy Spirit, make him a citizen of heaven, born from above, from God, re-created in conformity to the image of the Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brothers. And these two stand in an indissoluble connection. For the one who descended is the same who also ascended far above all the heavens in order that he might fill all things, Eph. 4:10. The Holy Spirit who regenerates is the same who also bears witness to Christ within us. Scripture leads us up to Christ, who is above, seated at God’s right hand; and Christ, who dwells in our hearts through the Spirit, leads us back to Scripture. With the heart one believes unto righteousness, but with the mouth one confesses unto salvation, Rom. 10:10. Faith according to Scripture excludes both: a faith of the heart that does not confess and a confession that is not rooted in the faith of the heart. It is mystical and noetic at the same time, an unconditional, unwavering trust in Christ as the one who, according to the testimony of Scripture, accomplished everything for me and on that ground is now and eternally my Lord and my God.
