## 4.

This character of faith comes clearly to expression where it appears as *fides justificans*. Justification is not the only fruit of faith, for faith receives Christ and all his benefits. Conversion, sanctification, good works, perseverance, and so forth also become ours only through faith. Nevertheless, justification is one of the most glorious fruits of faith. Some disapproved of the word justification, *justificatio*, because through its composition with “make,” *facere*, it gave occasion for an ethical interpretation. But no more than the expression *Deum glorificare, magnificare*, to magnify God, does the word “justify” as such imply a moral change. If this were so, the word would be incorrect here and ought to be exchanged for a declaration of righteousness. For Scripture establishes beyond all doubt the forensic, juridical meaning of justification. The Hebrew ‎‏הצדיק‏‎ designates that act of the judge by which he declares a person innocent, and stands over against ‎‏הרשיע‏‎, condemn, Deut. 25:1; Job 32:2; 33:32. It is used in this way of God, Ex. 23:7; 1 Kings 8:32; 2 Chron. 6:23; Isa. 50:8. The forgiveness of sins is not yet expressed by this word in the Old Testament. It is indicated by, or is in any case included under, the words redeem, Ps. 39:9; 51:16; not impute, Ps. 32:2; forget, not remember, Isa. 43:25; Jer. 31:34; cast behind the back, Isa. 38:17; blot out or erase, Ps. 51:3, 11; Isa. 43:25; forgive, Ex. 34:9; Ps. 32:1. The Greek word δικαιουν means in general to regard as right and equitable, to judge what is right, and can thus be used both *in malam partem*, to do right to the wicked, that is, punish him, and *in bonam partem*, to do right to the righteous person, to recognize him as such. In the New Testament, under the influence of the Old Testament, it has always acquired a juridical and favorable meaning. Thus it occurs in general in Matt. 11:19, where Wisdom is declared righteous with respect to, ἀπο, her children—naturally not in an ethical but in a juridical sense; likewise Luke 7:29, where the tax collectors justify God; and further Matt. 12:37; Luke 10:29; 16:15; 18:14. In Paul too the forensic meaning is established. In Rom. 3:4 it cannot have an ethical meaning, since God is the subject who is justified in his words. Further, it alternates with λογιζεσθαι εἰς δικαιοσυνην, 4:3, 5, and stands over against κρινειν, ἐγκαλειν, and κατακρινειν, 8:33, 34, just as δικαιωμα over against κατακριμα, 5:16. It means, after judicial investigation of the guilt, to acquit someone, declare him righteous, δικαιον καθισταναι, Rom. 5:19.

Now the word ‎‏הצדיק‏‎, δικαιουν, to justify, can in itself indeed have an ethical meaning. It is used in this way several times by church fathers, Suicerus, s.v. In Luther and Melanchthon and in the older symbols of the Lutheran church, especially the *Apol. Conf. Aug.*, *justificari* is used in a twofold sense: as *justos pronuntiari seu reputari* and *ex injustis justos effici seu regenerari*, *Symb. Bücher*, ed. Müller, pp. 100, 108. From this some have wrongly inferred that the Lutheran Reformation originally conceived justification not in a juridical but in an ethical sense and regarded faith as *ipsa justitia*: Loofs, “Die Bedeutung der Rechtfertigungslehre der Apologie für die Symbolik der luth. Kirche,” *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1884, pp. 613–688; Eichhorn, “Die Rechtfertigungslehre der Apologie,” ib., 1887, pp. 415–490; and Zitzlaff, “Die wahre Bedeutung der Glaubensrechtfertigung,” ib., 1898, p. 522 f. Yet on the other side the claim of the Formula of Concord, in Müller, 528, 613, that *regeneratio* in the *Apol. Conf.* is the same as *justificatio*, is not correct. In the first period, the antithesis between Rome and the Reformation regarding justification was formulated not in the words ethical or juridical but as justification by works (love) or by faith, on account of our own works or on account of the righteousness of Christ received in faith. But this *justificatio* on the ground of Christ’s righteousness through faith alone, which from the beginning was also conceived by Luther and Melanchthon and in the oldest Lutheran symbols as a declaration of righteousness, was not formally separated from but conceived together with a *regeneratio* of the human being. This consisted in his being comforted and raised up by that same faith and made pleasing to God. The *Form. Conc.* also acknowledges this again when it says: *cum enim homo justificatur, id ipsum revera est quaedam regeneratio, quia ex filio irae fit filius Dei et hoc modo e morte in vitam transfertur*, ib., p. 614. Cf. further Heppe, *Dogm. d. d. Prot.* II 274 f.; Köstlin, *Luthers Theol.* II 447; Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* III 557; Nitzsch, *Ev. Dogm.*, 583. The Reformed too sometimes said that the word justification could have a broader sense and had to be understood in this way in Isa. 53:11; Dan. 12:3; 1 Cor. 6:11; Titus 3:7; Rev. 22:11, e.g., *Synopsis pur. theol.* 33, 3, while others maintained the narrower meaning of declaration of righteousness in all those passages as well: Witsius, *Oec. foed.* III 8, 6 sq., 12, 14; Mastricht, *Theol.* VI 6, 19; Moor IV 550; Owen, *De rechtv. uit het geloof*, Amsterdam, 1797, p. 140. The word in itself does in fact permit the whole work of redemption to be understood under it. Just as re-creation can as a whole be called a regeneration, so from beginning to end it is also a justification, a restoration of the *state* and *standing* of the fallen world and humanity before God and with respect to itself. But although this meaning of the word is not impossible, and although there is in itself no objection to thinking that Scripture sometimes uses the word in the sense of sanctification or at least includes the latter under it, exegetically this is nevertheless improbable. Isa. 53:11 says that through his knowledge, that is, *per cognitionem sui* or also *per cognitionem suam*, the servant of the Lord will justify many. The juridical meaning is not only possible here but is made probable by the addition, “and he shall bear their transgressions.” Likewise, in Dan. 12:3 it is said of the leaders and teachers of the people of God that they justify many, that is, through their righteousness, their faithfulness to the law, they are an example for many to imitate so that they too may be reckoned among the righteous. In 1 Cor. 6:11 the juridical meaning of δικαιουν is now almost universally recognized. Paul there reminds the Corinthians that they, formerly ἀδικοι, have been washed, sanctified, justified. In view of the first word, Paul is thinking of baptism, and the three concepts do not indicate that the Corinthians received these benefits successively, temporally one after another, but, as appears from the repeated ἀλλα, they contain a climax. Then, in baptism, they were not only negatively washed from the sins of their former walk and positively sanctified, but by a judicial judgment of God they were also placed in an entirely different standing, the standing of the δικαιοι. And all these benefits became theirs in the name of the Lord Jesus και ἐν τῳ πνευματι του θεου ἡμων. These last words too refer to justification, which has its objective foundation in Christ and realizes itself in believers through the Spirit: Gloël, *Der h. Geist*, 149 f.; Gennrich, *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1898, p. 402 f. Titus 3:7 contains no reason whatever to depart from the ordinary juridical meaning of δικαιουν. In Rev. 22:11 the reading δικαιωθητω deserves preference on account of the parallel forms and means that the one who is righteous should, by acting righteously, be recognized still more as righteous. Thus every compelling proof is lacking that the word δικαιουν is ever used in Scripture in an ethical sense. But even if this were the case once, when the justification of the sinner before God is in view it always has a juridical meaning. Roman Catholics strive in vain to overthrow this exegetical result; cf., e.g., B. Bartmann, *St. Paulus und St. Jacobus über die Rechtfertigung*, Freiburg, 1897, p. 67 f.
