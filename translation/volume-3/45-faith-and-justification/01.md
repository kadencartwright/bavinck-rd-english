# § 45. Faith and Justification

## 1.

The spiritual life implanted in regeneration agrees with natural life in this, that in order to increase and grow it must be nourished and strengthened. There is otherwise a great difference between the two, for the spiritual life has its origin in God not as Creator but as Savior; it was acquired through the resurrection of Christ; it is an eternal life that cannot sin and cannot die. Nevertheless, the regenerate person continually needs to be strengthened with power by the Spirit in the inner man. This strengthening of the spiritual life, no less than its origin, is also from God and the riches of his grace. Even after it has begun, the life of the spiritual person cannot for one moment be conceived apart from God and his fellowship. In the same strict and special sense in which it is from God, it is also through and unto God. He is the one who nourishes and sustains it, who never abandons it, who causes it to pass into acts and activities, who grants not only the ability but also works the willing and the working according to his good pleasure, Phil. 2:13; 2 Cor. 3:5. It is a life in fellowship with Christ. In baptism believers have become one plant with him in his death and also in his resurrection, Rom. 6:5. They are in Christ and Christ lives in them, 2 Cor. 13:5; Gal. 2:20. They can do nothing without him if they do not abide in him as branches in the vine, John 15:4, 5. They can be strong only ἐν κυριῳ και ἐν τῳ κρατει της ἰσχυος αὐτου, Eph. 6:10, through the Spirit of Christ and in his fellowship, Rom. 8:13, 26; 2 Cor. 13:13; Eph. 3:16. But that Spirit works in the regenerate in different directions. This can and must be so because the new person is not immediately perfect in “degrees,” but is perfect in “parts.” In regeneration the whole human being is re-created in principle; the human self itself dies and rises again from and in Christ, Gal. 2:20. It is immediately a καινος ἀνθρωπος, created in Christ, Eph. 4:24; Col. 3:10—small and tender indeed, but nevertheless complete in all its parts. And therefore the Holy Spirit works in different directions in order to cause the new person to grow uniformly and proportionately in all his parts. He works as the Spirit of wisdom, holiness, and glory and adorns believers with all kinds of powers and gifts and virtues, Rom. 15:13; 1 Cor. 12:3f.; Gal. 5:22. In particular, on the side of the understanding he works the virtues of faith, knowledge, wisdom, and so forth. Although implanted in the human being by the Spirit in a mysterious, inscrutable manner, John 3:8, the spiritual life is nevertheless, as soon as it becomes conscious in the human being, bound from the first moment to the Word of God. It was awakened by the Holy Spirit from a λογος ἐμφυτος, from the inward, evangelical calling of Christ. In its growth it remains bound to that word and thus, as soon as it becomes conscious, also dependent upon Holy Scripture, which is the word of Christ. By its nature the spiritual life stands in relation to Scripture as the plant to the soil in which it is rooted and from which it draws its sap. It is related not only to Scripture as externally written in letters but also as continually borne, animated, and spoken in the heart by the Holy Spirit. The *vocatio interna* is necessary not only at the origin but also in the growth of the spiritual life. It does not occur once and end with the creation of life, but always continues. Just as God first created everything through the Word and thereafter sustains all things through that same Word, so the *vocatio interna* is also active in the sustaining and development of the spiritual life. Believers are κλητοι, Rom. 1:6, who share in the heavenly calling, Heb. 3:1, who are continually called by God to his kingdom until they will actually have inherited it, 1 Thess. 2:12; 5:24. Now the act by which the Holy Spirit causes the Word of Christ to be understood in its spiritual sense and content and opens consciousness to the truth still bears in Scripture the special name illumination. Because sin has darkened the understanding, Rom. 1:21; 1 Cor. 1:21; 2:14; Eph. 4:18; 5:8, there is also need of an ἀποκαλυψις του νοος, Rom. 12:2; Eph. 4:23. This is brought about by God, who through ἀποκαλυψις in the human being, ἐν ἐμοι, Gal. 1:16, removes the obstacle that until then prevented a right knowledge of things, Matt. 11:25; 16:17; Gal. 1:16; cf. vol. I, 245. He does this by giving the Holy Spirit, who is a πνευμα σοφιας και ἀποκαλυψεως, Eph. 1:17, leads into the truth, John 16:13, teaches all things, John 14:26; 1 John 2:20, and causes the things of God to be understood, 1 Cor. 2:10–16. Just as in creation by his word of power he caused light to shine out of darkness, so through the Son, Matt. 11:27, and through the Spirit he also causes light to shine in human hearts, 2 Cor. 4:6, and makes the eyes of the heart enlightened, Eph. 1:18. Thereby they know the things granted them by God in the gospel, 1 Cor. 2:12; they have a γνωσις and ἐπιγνωσις, that is, a knowledge that concerns them personally and acts upon them, of the Father, Matt. 11:27; 2 Cor. 4:6; Eph. 1:17, of Christ, Matt. 16:17, of the things of the Spirit of God, 1 Cor. 2:14; etc. And they are children of light, Luke 16:8; Eph. 5:8; 1 Thess. 5:5, citizens of the kingdom of light, 1 Pet. 2:9; Col. 1:12, and walk in the light, Eph. 5:8; 1 John 1:7; 2:9, 10. Through the illumination of the Holy Spirit an entirely new light dawns upon the human being concerning all things—God, Christ, sin, grace, Scripture, church, world, death, judgment, and so forth. In God’s light he now sees light.

This knowledge is now further described in Scripture as a knowledge of faith. It is entirely in accordance with Scripture to say that the knowledge of God in the face of Christ saves, justifies, and grants the forgiveness of sins and eternal life, 1 Kings 8:43; 1 Chron. 28:9; Ps. 89:16; Isa. 1:3; 11:9; 53:11; Jer. 4:22; 31:34; Hos. 2:19; 4:1, 6; Matt. 11:27; Luke 1:77; John 8:32; 10:4, 14; 17:3; Rom. 10:3; 2 Cor. 2:14; Gal. 4:9; Eph. 4:13; Heb. 8:11; 1 John 5:20; 2 Pet. 1:2; 3:18. But because by virtue of its origin, essence, and object it bears an entirely distinctive character, it is called a knowledge of faith. Yet when it is thus defined, it is by no means thereby designated as something foreign to human nature as such and added to it in the sense of a *donum superadditum*. Darkness, error, falsehood, and so forth are contrary to nature, properties of fallen nature, but the light of knowledge belongs to the image of God that was originally and essentially proper to the human being. The knowledge of faith too is not an absolutely supernatural addition to the human being. Every human being believes in the most general sense, always and in every domain, especially also in that of science. There is no knowledge without some faith; the dualism between faith and science is theoretically and practically impossible. Even in dogmatics, besides saving faith, theologians also speak of historical faith, Matt. 7:26; John 12:42, 43; 13:17; Acts 26:27, 28; James 2:19; temporary faith, Matt. 13:21; and miraculous faith, Matt. 9:2; 17:20; Acts 14:9; 1 Cor. 13:2. These are indeed essentially distinct from the first and can also occur in the unregenerate, but they must nevertheless have enough agreement with it to bear the same name of faith. But even faith in the narrower sense, the *fides justificans* or *salvifica*, is not a *donum superadditum* according to the Roman Catholic conception. Faith is indeed a gift of God, Acts 5:31; Eph. 2:8; Phil. 1:29, a fruit of his δυναμις, 1 Cor. 2:4, 5; Eph. 1:19; 1 Thess. 2:13, and specifically granted by the Holy Spirit, 1 Cor. 12:3; 2 Cor. 4:13. Yet it is a gift necessary not in an absolute sense but only contingently, on account of sin. For re-creation never introduces a new substance into world or humanity. In faith too it does not grant the human being such a new faculty, power, or activity as original human nature, created in God’s image, did not possess. On the contrary, over against the Remonstrants—for example Arminius, *Op.*, 160—the Reformed rightly maintained that before the fall Adam possessed in his nature the power to believe in Christ, although naturally he did not know Christ and at that time did not need him as Savior: Gomarus, *Op.* I 93; Moor IV 461; Shedd, *Dogm. Theol.* I 454, II 482f. And over against the Roman Catholics they likewise maintained that Christ as a human being lived on earth by faith, above, p. 294. Since regeneration is in principle a re-creation of the whole human being after the image of the one who created him, the capacity for faith (*fides potentialis, seminalis, habitualis, semen* or *radix fidei*) is naturally given with and in it. Just as little children are rational beings *ante rationem actualem*, so, if they are children of the covenant, they are also *fideles ante fidem actualem*. It is indeed incorrect, with older Lutheran theologians, to make regeneration consist entirely in *donatio fidei*, cf. M. Vitringa III 222, for in regeneration the *semina spei, caritatis*, and so forth are implanted just as much. Nevertheless, as all faculties and powers are restored in it, so too is the capacity for faith. Believing in God, in Christ, and so forth is as natural for the regenerate person as such as it is natural for every human being to believe in the visible world. Indeed, just as every *potentia* passes into *actus* only through a certain influence from outside and a grain of wheat germinates only in the bosom of the earth, so also the capacity for faith implanted through regeneration passes into the act of faith only through the continuing *vocatio interna*. Yet in regeneration God restores the vital relation that originally existed between him and the human being. Re-created after God’s image, the human being is once again akin to God himself and to all that is God’s—to his Christ, to the things of the Spirit, to his Word, to his church, to his heaven, to the things that are above. Crucified to the world and dead to sin, he lives to God. And therefore, being illumined by the Holy Spirit, he also knows God and is blessed in that knowledge, John 17:3.

But this may not be understood as if the regenerate person drew this knowledge of God in Christ from his own heart, from the inward instruction of the Holy Spirit. Mysticism has at all times set Word and Spirit over against one another, despised the letter, exalted the inward word at the expense of the outward, and even appealed for this to Holy Scripture, Isa. 54:13; Jer. 31:34; Matt. 11:25, 27; 16:17; John 6:45; 1 Cor. 2:10; 2 Cor. 3:6; Heb. 8:10; 1 John 2:20, 27. Against this it must be observed: 1º that all knowledge, in the natural and spiritual domains, most certainly presupposes a relation, a kinship between object and subject. To see, an eye is necessary, and object and subject must be illuminated by the same light. To know, understanding is necessary, and it is the same Logos who created the known object and the knowing subject for one another. Thus also in the spiritual domain the Spirit must be added to the Word, the *vocatio interna* to the *vocatio externa*, illumination to revelation, in order to cause us to know God in the face of Christ. 2º Scripture expresses this decidedly and clearly in the passages mentioned above and in others. In God’s light alone do we see light. But it nowhere says that the regenerate person can or must draw the material of this knowledge from himself. In 1 John 2:20–27 the apostle connects the anointing of the Spirit, which believers have received from the Holy One, that is, from Christ, most closely with the truth they have heard, vv. 21–24. If they abide in it, they also abide in the Son and the Father and need no further instruction. Everywhere Scripture directs the believer outside himself, toward God’s revelation in nature, law, and gospel: Deut. 4:1; Isa. 8:20; John 5:39; Rom. 1:20; 15:4; 2 Tim. 3:15; 1 Pet. 1:25; 2 Pet. 1:19; etc. 3º In the natural realm the case is that the human being indeed brings consciousness, understanding, reason with him but must nevertheless obtain all the content of knowledge from outside, vol. I, 161f.; II, 39f. This is much more the case in the spiritual realm. For even though all believers have been taught by the Lord, they nevertheless still live in the flesh and remain prone to error. Thoughts repeatedly rise within them which they must lead captive to the obedience of Christ. Left to themselves, they would immediately lapse into error and falsehood. Therefore an objective revelation is necessary here to serve as a rule of doctrine and life. 4º To this is added that not visible but invisible, spiritual, eternal things are the object of this religious knowledge. What no eye has seen and no ear has heard and what has not arisen in the human heart, God has prepared in the gospel for those who love him. How shall we know these things, firmly and certainly, unless they are set before our eyes in a faithful image, pure and unmixed? Here we walk not by sight; we must therefore behold the glory of the Lord as in a mirror in order to be transformed into the same image. 5º And finally, just as in the natural realm every creature seeks food according to its nature, so the new life in the believer always again draws toward the gospel, the word of Christ, the Scriptures, as toward the ground upon which it rests and the food by which it is strengthened. Scripture becomes not more dispensable but ever more indispensable and glorious to the person who grows in faith. The witness of the Holy Spirit in his heart binds him in the same measure and power to Scripture as to the person of Christ himself, vol. I, 502f. From all this it also now becomes clear why religious knowledge is described in Scripture as a knowledge of faith and why faith is placed so strongly in the foreground in the subjective work of salvation. Strictly speaking, faith or knowledge does not save; God saves in Christ through the Holy Spirit. He saves by granting to the sinner the benefits of the covenant, Christ, himself. But what would that salvation benefit us if we were not conscious of it and had no knowledge of it? Then it would not even exist. Unconscious bliss is indeed the highest for the Buddhist, and many today prefer nonbeing to being. But for the Christian the highest being is to know God and through that knowledge to possess eternal life. Knowledge is therefore not an accidental addition to salvation coming from outside but forms an indispensable element in it. There is no salvation that is not known and enjoyed. What benefit would we have from the forgiveness of sins, from regeneration and complete renewal by the Holy Spirit, from heavenly glory, if we had no consciousness and no knowledge of them? They could not exist; they presuppose and demand consciousness, knowledge, enjoyment, and in these give blessedness. God saves by causing himself to be known and enjoyed in Christ. Since, however, the benefits of the covenant of grace are granted here on earth only in part, since fellowship with God, regeneration, and sanctification are still imperfect, since knowledge is imperfect, has invisible things as its object, and is bound to Scripture, therefore the knowledge of God here on earth is a knowledge of faith. Faith is the only way along which it is obtained, the only form in which it can appear. Indeed, all the benefits—forgiveness, regeneration, sanctification, perseverance, heavenly salvation—exist for us only through faith; only in faith do we enjoy them; we are saved only in hope.
