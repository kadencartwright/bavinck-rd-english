## 8.

From the beginning there was some difference in the churches of the Reformation concerning the parts of justification. Those who, with Piscator, denied the _obedientia activa_ had to restrict justification to the forgiveness of sins and thus sooner or later arrive at the conclusion that believers, after being delivered by Christ from the guilt and punishment of sin, had themselves to fulfill the law in order to acquire eternal life; see above, p. 350 f.; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._² I 271; II 61 f. This opinion was therefore also quite generally rejected. Although the forgiveness of sins could synecdochically, as _pars pro toto_, be taken for the whole of justification, justification nevertheless included more than forgiveness alone. For Christ not only restored us to the state of Adam before the fall but also kept the law for us and acquired eternal life; cf. above, pp. 351 f., 361, 373; and especially Gomarus on Luke 1:77, _Op._ 1664 I 175 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XVI 4; Moor IV 681; Hodge III 125, 127. But although it was quite generally acknowledged that justification encompassed more than the forgiveness of sins, there was still disagreement over what that additional element consisted in. Older theologians frequently named the imputation of Christ’s righteousness as the second part of justification: Luther, Chemnitz, Gerhard, Quenstedt, Polanus, Wollebius, Junius, Trelcatius, Rivetus, Belgic Confession, art. 23, and so forth; cf. M. Vitringa III 311; Schneckenburger, _Vergl. Darst._ II 28; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 61. Although this is not definitely incorrect when Christ’s active obedience is understood here by his imputed righteousness, the parts are nevertheless not purely coordinated and they also make too strong a separation between Christ’s passive and active obedience; cf. Mastricht VI 6, 17. It is therefore better to describe justification by the imputation of Christ’s entire obedience, just as in Paul the word _δικαιουν_ alternates with _λογιζεσθαι εἰς δικαιοσυνην_; cf. Heidelberg Catechism, qu. 60; _Synopsis pur. theol._ 33, 8. And it is still more accurate to let the two parts of justification consist in the forgiveness of sins and the granting of the right to eternal life, since these benefits are built upon the imputation of Christ’s entire obedience; Voetius, _Disp._ V 279 sq.; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XVI, qu. 4. Adoption as children was sometimes also named as the second part; Turretin, ibid., XVI, qu. 6; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III 90; Pfleiderer, _Paulinismus_ 189; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._ II 134. But others, such as Martyr, _L. C._, p. 354, preferred to regard it as a fruit of justification.

As for the forgiveness of sins, this does not consist in the removal of the stain of sin, as Rome claims insofar as it makes justification consist in the _infusio gratiae_ and makes forgiveness dependent upon sanctification. Neither does it consist only in the removal of the _reatus culpae_, that is, in effect, in deliverance from eternal punishment, while the punishment for the _peccata venialia_ committed after the _infusio gratiae_ must be expiated by the human being himself here or hereafter in purgatory, for guilt and punishment are correlative concepts; see above, p. 162; cf. p. 98. Rather, the forgiveness that is a part of justification is nothing less than the complete remission of all guilt and all punishment of sin, and not only of past and present but also of future sins. Out of fear of antinomianism, some objected to this rich and broad conception of forgiveness and therefore restricted it to the remission of the guilt of past and repeatedly confessed sins, appealing also to Matt. 6:12; 1 John 1:9; 2:1, and so forth; Rivetus, _Op._ III 1099; Pictet, _Christ. Godg._ XI 11, 3; Brakel, _Red. Godsd._ 34, 53–62; 56, 6, 62; and further Claude, Vlak, and others. Over against antinomianism they defended an important truth. It is a fact that after receiving forgiveness believers still stumble in many ways, sometimes even fall into gross sins, and continue to experience all kinds of adversities in life as punishment. Rome thinks it may infer from this that believers themselves must still make satisfaction for their _peccata venialia_ and thus detracts from the riches and grace of forgiveness. Antinomianism wishes to honor the latter and therefore thinks that the sins believers commit are charged not to the new but only to the old human being and that believers no longer even have to pray for the forgiveness of sins. Over against this, all the Reformed maintained that forgiveness does remove the _reatus actualis_ but not the _reatus potentialis_ of sin; that is, forgiveness removes the punishment but not the punishability of sin. The latter remains as long as sin remains. Sin, also and especially in believers, brings with it an awareness of guilt, grief, sorrow, estrangement from God, humiliation, and so forth. It takes away peace of conscience, confidence, and assurance of faith. This cannot be otherwise; the nature of sin is such that it necessarily includes an awareness of guilt and punishability. Even when believers, after having received forgiveness long before, later learn to look more deeply into the corruption of their own heart, they feel the need to confess even the sins of their youth and to trace their guilt back to their conception and birth, Ps. 25:6; 51:6, 7. This confession is then no condition of forgiveness, but whoever truly knows his sin naturally confesses it and in contrast feels all the more strongly the need for the comfort of forgiveness. Therefore prayer for forgiveness remains necessary for the believer every day. But he does not then pray in doubt and despair. He does not pray as though he were no longer a child of God and had once again to expect eternal condemnation, but he prays from and in faith, as a child to his Father who is in heaven, and says amen to his prayer. And this praying is not only a need but is also necessary, for justification does not consist merely in a transcendent acquittal of the sinner with God _in foro coeli_, but it is an _actus transiens_ that is brought by the Holy Spirit into the consciousness of the believer and, in this unity, bears the name justification in Scripture. Confession and prayer are therefore the way by which God once again awakens and strengthens this consciousness of forgiveness in the believer. Under sin it lies hidden. Faith as _habitus_ indeed remains, but it can no longer express itself in acts. In order that this faith may revive, that the Spirit of God may once again testify loudly and powerfully with our spirit that we are children of God, renewed humility, confession, and prayer for forgiveness are necessary after sin. If we stood perfectly in faith, we would never doubt the forgiveness of our sins, our childship, or the future inheritance, and we would never understand or experience any calamity in this life as a punishment but only as a chastisement of the Lord. But standing perfectly in faith would be possible only if we were also elevated above sin. Since this is not the case and sin repeatedly brings doubt with it, conversion and confession therefore remain the means by which God once more brings us into his communion and assures us of his favor. From this, however, it may not be inferred with Rivetus and others that God each time forgives only past and confessed sins. For all the sins of the church were transferred to Christ, and he atoned for them all in his blood. In the imputation of Christ to the elect in the _pactum salutis_, incarnation and resurrection, and in external and internal calling, he with all his benefits is granted to them. As soon as they receive this gift of God, they too, from their side, are at once placed into a new relation to God that is immutable and unbreakable. And although the activities of faith may be absent for a time, the gift of faith by which they are incorporated into Christ and receive all his benefits is nevertheless indefectible, John 3, v. 36; Rom. 8:30; Gal. 3:27; Heb. 9:12; 10:12, 14, and so forth. Therefore, after every stumbling and every fall, believers have the confidence to go with trust to the throne of grace and to plead the faithfulness of him whose gracious gift and calling are irrevocable, Rom. 11:29; Heb. 4:16; 1 John 1:9; cf. Calvin, _Inst._ III 11, 11; 20, 19; Voetius, _Disp._ V 282; Alting, _Theol. probl. nova_ XVII 10; Witsius, _Misc. Sacr._ II 806–820; M. Vitringa III 313.

This benefit of the forgiveness of sin is so great that it is incredible to the natural human being. The pagans did not know it and thought that they had to acquire the favor of the gods through all kinds of works. Celsus mocked it and regarded it as foolishness; cf. Witsius, _De theol. gentilium circa justificationem_, _Misc. Sacr._ II 668–721. Even most Christians make it dependent upon faith and good works. Everything also argues against it: sin itself, which demands punishment; conscience, which accuses; the law, which condemns; the world, which knows no mercy; Satan, who is an accuser of the whole human race; God himself, whose righteousness and holiness cannot tolerate sin. The forgiveness of sins is a gift that can be received and enjoyed only by faith. It is not an object of sight but of faith. Truly to confess it requires that same faith which alone also enables one to accept the divine authority of Holy Scripture, the deity of Christ, and his satisfaction and resurrection. The objections brought against all these dogmas are in essence no different from those that also apply against the forgiveness of sins. Appearances are against it. But faith is also the firm ground of things hoped for and the evidence of things not seen. It is faith in a God who works miracles, who makes the dead alive, who calls the things that do not exist as though they did, and who justifies the ungodly. So prominently does this forgiveness of sins stand in Scripture that it is sometimes identified with justification. Yet another benefit is connected with it that is equally rich and glorious and that may indeed not be separated from it but may nevertheless be distinguished from it. It is the granting of the right to eternal life, or adoption as children, named by Paul immediately alongside redemption from the law, Gal. 4:5; cf. Dan. 9:24; Acts 26:18; Rev. 1:5, 6. Already in the Old Testament God is called the Father of his people and Israel his son; volume II 113. But in the New Testament this fatherhood and sonship acquire a much deeper sense. God is now the Father of believers not in a theocratic but in an ethical sense, and they are his children, _τεκνα_, born of him and therefore through faith in Christ obtaining the _ἐξουσια_ to become so, _γενεσθαι_, until one day they will be so perfectly, when they will see God as he is, 1 John 3:2. This ethical childship, which occurs especially in John, does not belong here, however, but must be discussed with regeneration and sanctification. Paul, by contrast, speaks of _υἱοθεσια_ in the juridical sense. Just as believers receive the forgiveness of sins on the ground of Christ’s righteousness, so they are also adopted as children, _υἱοι θεου_ (not _τεκνα_). This _υἱοθεσια_, which thus rests upon a declaration of God, was acquired by Christ, Gal. 4:5, and becomes our portion through faith, 3:26. Whoever is acquitted of the guilt and punishment of sin is at the same time adopted as a son and made an object of God’s fatherly love. Believers are thereby placed in the same standing as Christ, who is the firstborn among many brothers, Rom. 8:29. He was the Son of God by nature, 8:32, and was declared such at his resurrection, 1:3. Believers become _υἱοι θεου_ by adoption. And just as Christ at his resurrection was declared Son of God in power _κατα πνευμα ἁγιωσυνης_, 1:3, and believers were justified _ἐν τῳ πνευματι του θεου ἡμων_, 1 Cor. 6:11, so through the _πνευμα υἱοθεσιας_ they also become sons of God, Rom. 8:14–16, and afterward are also assured of this sonship by that same Spirit, ibid.; Gal. 4:6. As sons they are at the same time _κατ’ ἐπαγγελιαν κληρονομοι_, Gal. 3:29; 4:7; Rom. 8:17. And since this inheritance still lies in the future, _υἱοθεσια_ too in its full realization is still an object of hope, Rom. 8:23. Justification, which has its beginning in eternity, is realized in the resurrection of Christ and the calling of believers, and receives its completion only when in the last judgment God repeats his sentence of acquittal in the hearing of the whole world and every tongue will have to confess that Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father. But although the _“Rechtsfolge der Adoption”_ still awaits, believers have nevertheless already been adopted as children here on earth. They are sealed by the Holy Spirit as guarantee and pledge until the day of their redemption, 2 Cor. 1:22; 5:5; Eph. 1:13, 14; 4:30, and are kept for the heavenly inheritance just as it is kept for them, 1 Pet. 1:4, 5. By that Spirit they are continually led (_ἀγονται_, not _φερονται_ as in 2 Pet. 1:21), Rom. 8:14, assured of the love God has for them, 5:5; cf. v. 8, and of their childship, 8:15, 16; Gal. 4:6, and already now made partakers of peace, Rom. 5:1; Phil. 4:7, 9; 1 Thess. 5:23, and joy, Rom. 14:17; 15:13; 1 Thess. 1:6.
