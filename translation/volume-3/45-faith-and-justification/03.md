## 3.

What thus occurs undivided and in organic unity in Scripture has often been torn apart or only mechanically connected in theology and in the Christian life. Rome conceives faith as _assensus firmus ac certus ad ea omnia, quae Deus credendo proponit_ and rejects the Protestant definition that it is a _fiducia specialis_ that my sins are forgiven. Trust is a fruit of faith in the will and can follow only upon justification, since otherwise the Protestants themselves could not maintain that faith alone justifies; after all, trust also includes hope and love. This faith as assent is therefore insufficient for justification in Rome. Together with other activities it is indeed a preparation for justification, but in itself it is _informis_ and must become a _fides formata_ through love. In Rome faith is not yet at all a personal, religious relation to God; it bears an introductory character and is to a large extent _fides implicita_, an accepting as true of everything _quod credit sacrosancta mater ecclesia_. The personal relation to God first comes into being in love, and it is therefore love that justifies and saves; cf. above, p. 442, and volume I 476. In essence the conception of faith in the Greek church, among Socinians, Remonstrants, Rationalists, and so forth agrees with this. In itself it is nothing but historical assent, properly not a religious but an _erkenntnisstheoretisch_ concept, derived not from Scripture but carried into theology from ordinary, everyday usage. The Reformation, however, did not ask, “What is believing in general?” and then determine the religious concept of faith accordingly. Rather, it returned to Scripture, investigated what meaning and place Scripture assigned to faith, thereby arrived at an entirely different conception of _fides salvifica_, and declared in antithesis to Rome: it is not only a _firmus assensus_ but also a _certa fiducia_; faith is the central, personal, religious relation of the human being to God. But it cannot be denied that this definition of faith brought peculiar difficulties with it and occasioned all kinds of differing views. First, the question soon had to arise as to the relation in which these two elements of faith stand to each other. From the beginning some placed the seat of faith in the understanding, preferred to define it by _cognitio certa_, _certitudo_, and regarded _fiducia_ as a consequence and fruit of faith in the will; others sought the essence of faith in _fiducia_ and seated it in the heart. Later this difference developed into the opposition between orthodoxy and Pietism. Orthodoxy wished to come through the understanding to the heart, through doctrine to life, and frequently became indifferent to life; Pietism walked the opposite way and often regarded doctrine as of little significance. Second, difference soon arose concerning the relation between faith and justification. The Reformation had conceived faith as _cognitio_ or _fiducia certa_ that Christ is _my_ Savior and that _my_ sins are forgiven. But it appeared that this certainty could not precede justification but had to follow it. Thus, from Gomarus onward, a distinction was made between an _actus directus_ and _reflexus_ of faith, between refuge-taking and assured trust, between the essence and well-being of faith. This difference later led to the opposition between nomism and antinomianism. Nomism held that all kinds of conditions and experiences had to precede the certainty of the forgiveness of sins and thus placed the essence of faith in a seeking, hungering, and thirsting for Christ, and so forth; antinomianism regarded all this as superfluous, as a nullification of Christ’s work, and described faith as a firm conviction that sins are forgiven. Third and finally, the relation between faith and work was difficult to describe. According to the Reformation, it was a living faith, _fides sola_ but not _solitaria_, that justified. Standing opposed to all works, faith therefore could not on the one hand be a work in any sense. There must be, says Comrie, _Heid. Cat._, Preface XXVI, Nijkerk: Malga, 1856; cf. p. 412, something in the inward nature of faith by which it is distinguished from all works, whatever they may be called. On the other hand, it was nevertheless a living faith and thus indeed a work and an act, for the _potentia_ or _habitus fidei_ that is implanted in regeneration is _formaliter_ not yet _fides_, any more than an egg is already a chicken; Voetius, _Disp._ II 403, 499. In practice this difference concerning the relation between faith and work led to the opposition between passive and active Christians. The former stop at justification and do not arrive at sanctification; the latter risk failing to recognize justification and making it dependent upon sanctification.

All these objections and dangers, however, which are connected with the Reformation conception of faith, plead not against it but for it. Rome has a very simple and comprehensible definition of faith, but precisely thereby fails to do justice to the richness of this concept in Scripture. For faith is not merely an acceptance of the apostles’ testimony concerning Christ but a bond of the soul to Christ himself, who is above, seated at God’s right hand. It is preeminently το ἐργον του θεου, John 6:29, the be-all and end-all in the Christian life, the means by which we become partakers of Christ and all his benefits, the subjective source of all salvation and blessing. While through Scripture it binds us to the historical Christ, it at the same time lifts us into the invisible world and causes us to live in fellowship with the Lord from heaven. Wherever its seat in the human being may be, it acts upon all his faculties and powers, gives them direction and guidance, governs his understanding and heart, his thinking and doing, his living and acting; Christians are believers, πιστοι. It is mystical and noetic, receptive and spontaneous, passive and active, an antithesis to all works and itself preeminently the work of God, the means to justification and the principle of sanctification, accompanying our whole life and passing over into sight only at death. It is more than natural that theology struggles to give a somewhat correct description of this faith. And even if it should succeed in this, it is nevertheless never able to govern life and prevent all one-sidedness and errors in practice. Yet it is possible to give faith in the order of salvation that place and significance which according to Scripture belong to it. 1º It must be placed in the foreground that all the benefits of salvation were acquired by Christ and are present in him, and that he himself, as the Lord from heaven, is their distributor and applier through his Spirit. Neither faith nor conversion are conditions that in any way acquire salvation; they are only the way in which the benefits of the covenant come into the subjective possession of those for whom they were acquired. 2º To that extent it is entirely correct to say that justification, like the other benefits of the covenant, precedes faith. In the _pactum salutis_ the Son already acted as surety and mediator for his own. According to 2 Cor. 5:19, God reconciled the world to himself in Christ and did not count its sins against it; and Rom. 4:25 says clearly that, just as Christ was delivered up for our sins, so he was also raised δια την δικαιωσιν ἡμων, that is, on account of, for the sake of, our justification, because objectively in him, through his suffering and death in our place, we had been justified. The καταλλαγη is not distinguished from the ἱλασμος in that the latter is objective and the former subjective. The former too is objective; the content of the gospel reads: God _is_ reconciled; accept that reconciliation, believe the gospel; see above, p. 383. Reconciliation, forgiveness, sanctification, and so forth do not come into being through our faith or conversion, but they have been fully acquired by Christ, and he distributes them according to his will. 3º This must be maintained all the more because there is no participation in the benefits of Christ except through communion with his person. The benefits of the covenant are not material goods that can be possessed and enjoyed without and apart from the mediator of that covenant. Rather, they are enclosed in him and never and nowhere exist independently of him. When it is said that Christ acquired them, this indicates that without violation of his righteousness God can grant all those benefits by grace in the fellowship of Christ. 4º In particular, the gift of the Holy Spirit also belongs among those benefits Christ acquired. He himself became Spirit; through his suffering and death he also made the Spirit of the Father and the Son his Spirit, the Spirit of Christ, and therefore distributes him as he wills, while that Spirit himself takes everything from Christ. The gift of the Holy Spirit thus presupposes that God has already communicated and given his Christ, and that Christ has already communicated and given himself. Even the very first benefit of salvation is a benefit of the covenant that presupposes the _unio mystica_. There is not only no resurrection, but also no being crucified and buried and no dying of the old human being except in the fellowship of Christ. 5º This imputation and gift of Christ and his benefits may now have taken place ideally, in the decree, already from eternity; it may have been objectively realized in Christ as head and mediator when he became human, died, and was raised; it may also in substance be the content of the word of the gospel; yet it is first individually applied and distributed in the _vocatio interna_ and passively accepted on the human side in regeneration. 6º This real gift of Christ and his benefits in the _vocatio interna_ is of such great significance that there is no objective imputation of Christ in the decree and in the satisfaction unless it is also individually realized in the _vocatio interna_, even if before birth, and conversely that without regeneration there is no participation in Christ and his benefits. Antinomianism evaporates time; for God, however, what occurs in time has significance for eternity, since it itself has its foundation and principle in eternity. 7º For those who grow up, however, even regeneration is not enough. Since the human being is a rational creature and the covenant of grace does not kill but rather restores and confirms his independence and freedom, regeneration must pass over into acts of faith. It is Christ himself who through the Holy Spirit brings the human being into his fellowship and to the free exercise of faith and conversion. And antinomianism with its pantheistic evaporation of time is so much in error here too that Scripture connects salvation precisely to faith, which can be exercised only here on earth. 8º This faith itself has no conditions that the human being must first fulfill in order to be permitted to believe, for the liberty to believe is abundantly present for everyone in Scripture and the offer of grace. Nor is sorrow over sin a condition, since as _poenitentia_ it may only in a certain sense be called _gratia praeparans_, and as _resipiscentia_ it is precisely a fruit and proof of faith. Nor is faith itself a condition for the other benefits, at least not in the sense that they would in any way be brought about by it, but at most only in the sense that faith is subjectively necessary to receive and enjoy the benefits present in Christ. 9º Since the gift of Christ and his benefits precedes faith and is in no way caused or effected by it, faith can be, and is, nothing other than the subjective, conscious, and active acceptance of the benefits granted. The description of faith in terms of taking refuge, hungering, thirsting, and so forth does not strike its essence, since these are all expressions and activities of faith and thus already presuppose it. The distinction of faith into refuge-taking and assured trust may have some right to exist as a distinction and with a view to the practice in which the believer is repeatedly brought to doubt; as a separation it must be rejected and is very harmful to the growth of spiritual life. 10º Faith as acceptance of the benefits acquired and granted by Christ is not separated temporally from the assurance of faith but immediately coincides with it. Just as knowledge as such naturally brings with it the consciousness of knowing, so faith too by its nature includes immediate certainty. It stands opposed to anxiety, Matt. 6:31; 8:26; 10:31; fear, Mark 4:40; 5:36; doubt, Matt. 14:31; 21:21; Rom. 4:20; James 1:6; disturbance, John 14:1. It is unconditional trust, Matt. 17:20, the ὑποστασις and ἐλεγχος of unseen things, Heb. 11:1. Out of this assurance of faith the devout of the Old and New Covenants speak and boast, Gen. 49:18; Ps. 16:8–10; 23:4–6; 31:2; 56:5, 10; 57:3, and so forth; Rom. 4:18, 21; 8:38; 2 Tim. 4:7, 8; Heb. 11, and so forth. And when Rome rejected this certainty, _Trid._ VI c. 9 and can. 13–15, the Reformation, and Calvin in particular, _Inst._ III 2, 14 f., and so forth, again demonstrated this certainty in faith from Scripture. _Fides numquam se ipsam ignorat_, Sohnius, _Op._ I 976. 11º The error of antinomianism therefore did not consist in its describing faith as certainty, but lay in this: that it erased the distinction between the acquisition and application of salvation, between the work of Christ and that of the Holy Spirit; regarded regeneration, conversion, and so forth as unnecessary and impermissible; understood by faith nothing other than an intellectual acceptance of the proposition “your sins are forgiven”; and finally regarded all forgiveness, like sin itself, as a delusion without objective reality. 12º As the conscious and free acceptance of Christ with all his benefits, faith here on earth can be nothing other than simultaneously _firmus assensus_ and _certa fiducia_. For Christ has been glorified, and we know him and his benefits only from his Word. Yet he does not, like other historical persons, continue to work only through his example, word, and spirit. Rather, just as he is the sole acquirer of the benefits, so he is also their sole possessor and distributor. Through his suffering and death he himself became life-giving Spirit and communicates himself together with his benefits to believers. Through his Spirit he grants himself to them as he is and as, because he has been received into heaven and can no longer be seen, he has caused himself to be described by that same Spirit in his Word. Thereby he re-creates our being and our consciousness, our doing and our thinking, our heart and understanding; he regenerates and justifies us and thus frees us from all sin, both from its stain and from its guilt and all its consequences. Himself prophet and priest and king, he makes us prophets who proclaim God’s thoughts, priests who consecrate ourselves to God, and kings who rule over the world and death. And faith accepts Christ as he gives himself, _Christum evangelio suo vestitum_, Calvin, _Inst._ III 2, 6. 13º Whether one then describes faith by _cognitio_ or by _fiducia_ is not the main issue; the two form no opposition. Calvin calls it, _Inst._ III 2, 7, a _firma certaque divinae erga nos benevolentiae cognitio_, but at the same time says that it is more _cordis quam cerebri, affectus quam intelligentiae_, ibid., § 8, and, at Rom. 10:10, speaks of it as a _firma et efficax fiducia_. Strictly speaking, a trust precedes faith in the logical sense. We believe someone’s testimony because we place trust in his person. Believing as an act of the understanding already rests, after all, upon an inclination of the will; _nemo credit nisi volens_, Voetius, _Disp._ II 499. And a trust also follows it: when something is firmly believed, one relies on it and rests in it; _fides antecedit, fides generat fiduciam_. Calvin says that in Eph. 3:12 the apostle _ex fide deducit fiduciam_, _Inst._ III 2, 15; cf. Piscator, in Heppe, _Dogm._ 387; Voetius, _Disp._ V 288–300; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._ III² 95 f. But however one defines it, faith is in its essence always certainty, excluding all doubt, fear, and despair. All kinds of care and doubt repeatedly arise in the believer, and throughout his life he has to struggle with them; but faith is in itself absolute certainty, Calvin, _Inst._ III 2, 17 f.; Zanchi, _Op._ VIII 712 sq. Assurance does not come to faith later from outside but is present in it from the outset. The different acts of faith—_actus fidei_, such as knowledge, assent, trust, rest, peace, and so forth; Voetius, _Disp._ II 499–512; Witsius, _Oec. foed._ III c. 7; Turretin, _Theol. El._ XIV qu. 8, to be carefully distinguished from _fructus fidei_ or _bona opera_—are not stages that follow one another temporally, but within those acts themselves there are all kinds of degrees: there is little and great, weak and strong faith; there are children and young men, men and fathers in the faith. But by its nature faith remains firm certainty, unconditional trust. Today it is still the same as it was in Jesus’ day, namely, that with God all things are possible, Mark 10:27; 11:23, 24; that he who raised Christ from the dead, Rom. 4:24; 10:9, still gives life to the dead, saves sinners, and calls the things that do not exist as though they did, Rom. 4:17.
