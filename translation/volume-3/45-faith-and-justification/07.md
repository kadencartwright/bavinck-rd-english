## 7.

Yet even with this the Reformation doctrine of justification has not yet been set in full light. It may be true that an imputation and gift of Christ to his own already takes place in the *pactum salutis*, in incarnation and satisfaction, in external and internal calling; nevertheless, this is not yet enough, it is not yet justification proper. It is true that Reformed theology soon began to distinguish between *justificatio activa*, which took place before faith, and *justificatio passiva*, which took place after faith, and began to set the first over against the latter as justification proper over against a coming to consciousness of the justification that had already occurred. Scripture gives some ground for this in Rom. 4:25 and 2 Cor. 5:19, and in more recent theology too the correctness of this Reformed thought has frequently been acknowledged, though for a different reason and with a different intention; cf., e.g., Schelling, *Werke* II 4, p. 217 f.; Dorner, *Chr. Gl.* II 754 f.; Ritschl, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* III² 76 f.; Kaftan, *Dogm.*, 493 f.; etc. So important a truth lies here that its denial inevitably leads to Remonstrant conceptions. God does not wait to acquit the sinner until the latter has, as it were apart from him, received Christ’s righteousness through faith. The imputation and gift of Christ and his benefits precedes faith; it occurred in the decree of the Father, in the satisfaction of Christ, in the *vocatio interna* of the Holy Spirit. But however true all this may be, it is nevertheless remarkable that Scripture never designates this imputation by the name justification but always understands by it that acquittal by God which occurs from and through faith. At the very least this implies that justification from faith occupies a weightier place in the order of salvation than those who place the center of gravity in *justificatio activa* can assign to it. Apart from the fact that this presentation of the doctrine of justification departs from the consistent usage of Holy Scripture, it also gives occasion for misunderstanding, sees itself compelled to exegetical violence, and does not sufficiently do justice to justification from faith as an acquitting act of God. One can indeed speak of eternal justification and intend and understand it in a good sense, but the expression is nevertheless not without objection. The Reformed rejected it almost without exception, including Maccovius, *L. C.*, 676; Voetius, *Disp.* V 281; *Westm. Syn.* XI 4; and even Comrie, Brahe, and others accepted it only in a restricted and well-defined sense: Comrie, *Brief over de regtv.*, Sneek, 1858, p. 91f.; Brahe, *Godg. Stellingen over de leer der rechtv.*, Amsterdam, 1833, p. 26f. For in God not only justification but everything he thinks and does is eternal. In that sense, as an *actus immanens*, creation too is eternal, vol. II, 411; but whoever therefore began to speak of eternal creation would cause confusion and introduce a pantheistic terminology. And so it is also with preservation, government, regeneration, calling, sanctification and glorification, and no less justification. One can also speak in a good sense of a justification in the resurrection of Christ and in the gospel, but there is nevertheless a great difference between these and the justification that occurs from faith and more particularly bears the name justification in Scripture. The former concerns Christ as the surety of his own, but the latter concerns believers themselves, and not only those believers conceived as a whole, as a church, as Ritschl claims, *Rechtf. u. Vers.* III 103 f., but also all of them personally, as is incontrovertibly proved by Rom. 3:26; 4:3, 24; 5:19; 8:1, 30; 10:4, 10; 1 Cor. 6:11; Gal. 2:16; Phil. 3:9; etc. This latter justification is not an accidental element added to the proper and essential justification in God’s decree, in Christ’s resurrection, or in the calling through the gospel. Rather, in justification—which, as it were, begins in eternity and receives its full completion only in the last judgment—it constitutes an indispensable and supremely important moment. The application of salvation by the Holy Spirit may in no respect be made into an acquisition of salvation, for the Holy Spirit takes everything from Christ. But application is nevertheless in its own domain just as necessary and of just as great significance as acquisition. Scripture therefore makes entrance into the kingdom of heaven dependent upon regeneration, faith, and conversion. And acquisition and application stand in so close a connection that the first is inconceivable and cannot exist without the latter, and conversely the latter without the first. Acquisition necessarily brings application with it. Through his suffering and death Christ also acquired that all his benefits, and thus also the forgiveness of sins, would be applied personally and individually to all his own. As Savior, Christ is concerned not only with objective satisfaction but also with the subjective redemption of his own from sin. This does not come about through an objective justification in the decree of God or in Christ’s resurrection but receives its completion only when the human being is freed from sin both in being and consciousness, and thus is regenerated and justified. Scripture ordinarily has this justification in view when it uses this word or also speaks of forgiveness of sins and so forth. Paul in particular does not in justification think of an eternal act in God, for he speaks of it as ἐκ, δια πιστεως. Nor does he indicate by a single word that it consists in God’s silently acquitting the human being from guilt and punishment and granting him the right to eternal life within himself, *in foro coeli*, in the heavenly court, at a given moment—for example, when the human being believes. For only the counsel of God is an *actus immanens*, but all other works of God—creation, preservation, government, redemption, justification, and so forth—belong to the *actus transeuntes*. They are not *ratio ordinis* but *executio ordinis*, above, p. 6. Nor does Paul in justification from faith think of the so-called *justificatio passiva*, at least not if this is temporally separated from *justificatio activa* and conceived as a coming to consciousness of a justification that occurred long before. For he places all the emphasis precisely upon the fact that God justifies from and through faith. Here, as so often, we stand before the mystery of the relation between eternity and time. As Christians we confess that God’s eternal willing, without ceasing to be eternal, can produce effects in time, just as his eternal thinking can also have temporal things as its content, vol. II, 413. In justification from faith, an acquittal pronounced by God centuries ago does not finally come to human consciousness. Rather, God, who is immutable, is himself the acting one when he acquits the sinner through faith. From him, without his ceasing to be the Eternal One, proceeds that activity which is received and enjoyed by the human being as justification from faith. The application, just as much as the acquisition, of salvation is from moment to moment an activity of God—of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. All three persons are involved also in justification from faith. It is the Father who through the Son and in the Spirit justifies the sinner, Rom. 8:33, 34; 1 Cor. 6:11. Paul does not think of making a separation here and placing the justification by the Father in eternity, that by the Son in the resurrection, and that by the Holy Spirit in human consciousness. Rather, just as all three cooperated in the acquisition, so they also cooperate in the application of salvation. In the former guilt and punishment were removed and life acquired; in the latter the human being is also placed by God, on his side and subjectively, into the relation in which he already stood objectively in Christ as his surety and mediator. Just as regeneration in a subjective sense frees from the stain of sin, so justification removes the guilt of sin. Both are equally necessary, equally real, equally grounded in Christ’s sacrifice, but also of the same high significance in the application.

This justification therefore does not occur apart from the human being but takes place from and through faith. In this connection Scripture undoubtedly thinks most often of faith as *actus*. But naturally faith as *habitus* is not thereby excluded. Regenerate little children and adult believers have and retain in this *habitus fidei* God’s acquittal, the witness of the Holy Spirit that they are children of God, even if their spirit does not always bear witness with him. Further, Scripture sets this justification from faith sharply and strictly over against justification from works. This antithesis does not mean, however, that faith is not a work and a principle of good works. It does not compel us to continue searching until in the inward nature of faith we find something that is no work, no act, but mere passivity. The antithesis Scripture, and especially Paul, makes is this: justification does not come about through works of the law, that is, it does not have in such works its ground, its *causa meritoria*. For in Christ God has given another, better δικαιοσυνη than sinful works can offer, and that δικαιοσυνη, which is Christ, is the sole and sufficient ground of our justification. In the antithesis οὐκ ἐξ ἐργων ἀλλ’ εκ πιστεως, the preposition ἐκ thus has a different meaning on each occasion. In the first member it indicates that works of the law cannot be the δικαιοσυνη on the ground of which God can acquit us. In the second member, however, it indicates not that faith itself can indeed be that righteousness, but that faith seeks the righteousness necessary to be justified not in the human being, in his works, but outside him, in Christ. The antithesis thus reads purely as follows: not one’s own righteousness of works, but the righteousness of God in Christ. Although this righteousness was perfectly acquired by Christ and lies ready in him, although in the *vocatio interna* and thus in a logical sense before regeneration and faith it is imputed and granted, nevertheless from the human side it is first received in faith (*habitus* or *actus fidei*) and only then becomes the ground upon which he personally is justified by God in the sense stated above. Faith is therefore no *causa materialis* or *formalis*; it is not even a *conditio* or *instrumentum* (*causa instrumentalis*) of justification. For it does not stand to justification as, for example, the eye to seeing or the ear to hearing. It is no condition upon which and no instrument through which we receive justification, but it is the act of receiving Christ himself—and specifically Christ as he gives himself to us inwardly through the Spirit and outwardly through the Word—and thus the firm, certain consciousness that he is my Lord and I am his possession. Faith is not an instrument with which the human being receives Christ, but much more a means of the Holy Spirit by which he causes the human being to receive Christ and causes his spirit to bear witness with himself that he is a child of God: Calvin, *Inst.* III 11, 5; *Heid. Cat.*, Q. 61; *Ned. Gel.*, art. 22; Witsius, *Misc. S.* II 792, 797 sq.; Trigland, *Antapol.*, p. 515; Mastricht VI 6, 28; Owen, *De rechtv. uit het geloof*, ch. 3; Moor IV 695; M. Vitringa III 295; Jonathan Edwards in Dorner II 752. Therefore faith does not stand opposed in every respect to every work. It stands opposed to works of the law in a twofold sense, namely, in that they can be neither the *causa materialis* nor the *causa instrumentalis* of justification. It also stands opposed to works of faith (*justitia infusa, obedientia, caritas*) as soon as these are regarded even in some measure as the ground of justification, as wholly or partly forming that righteousness on the ground of which God justifies us. For that ground is Christ and Christ alone. Faith itself is not a ground of justification, and therefore neither are the good works that proceed from it. But faith does not stand opposed to works of faith insofar as these, as fruits of faith, are used by the Holy Spirit as means to assure the believer of the sincerity of his faith and thus of his salvation, *Heid. Cat.*, Q. 86. In this sense faith is itself a work, John 6:29, the best work and principle of all good works, the only work through which God can free us here on earth from our guilt and assure us of our righteousness in Christ. The Reformed therefore also said that it is indeed *fides sola, quae justificat, fides tamen, quae justificat non est sola*, Calvin, *C. R.* 7, 477; *Inst.* III 11, 20, and after *justificatio peccatoris* they also spoke of a *justificatio justi*. In this sense Paul and James too do not contradict one another. It is indeed not correct to say that Paul speaks only of *justificatio peccatoris* and James of *justificatio justi*. But both deny that the ground of justification lies in works of the law, and both acknowledge that faith—living faith, faith that includes and produces good works—is the means by which the Holy Spirit assures us of our righteousness in Christ. There is only this difference: Paul conducts his struggle against dead works and James contends against a dead faith. The faith that justifies is the certainty of our righteousness in Christ worked in our heart by the Holy Spirit. Therefore the more living and the more powerful it is—not the more passive—the more it justifies us. Faith cooperates with works and is perfected from works, James 2:22. Vitringa III 317; James Buchanan, *The Doctrine of Justification*, Edinburgh, 1867, p. 491; Usteri, *Stud. u. Krit.*, 1889, 2tes Heft; Schwarz, ib., 1891, 4tes Heft; Böhmer, *Neue kirchl. Zeits.*, 1898, pp. 251–256.
