## 9.

Although sin thus stands under God’s government from its beginning, it nevertheless has its origin not in God but in the will of the rational creature.
But here a new problem immediately arises.
How can sin ever be explained from the will of a being who was created in God’s image in true knowledge, righteousness, and holiness?
The Pelagian conception that the first human being existed in a state of childlike innocence, of moral indifference, has already proved unacceptable to us, vol. II, 517; it does not explain the human fall but changes it into an insignificant accident and makes it incomprehensible that such terrible consequences and such dreadful miseries for the whole human race have proceeded from it.
If a spring can bring forth such a stream of unclean water, it must itself be inwardly corrupt.
It is therefore impermissible to reduce the distance between the _status integritatis_ and the _status corruptionis_ so far that the transition becomes easy and gradual.
The human being was created by God not morally indifferent but positively holy.
Yet the following must be borne in mind.
First, God most certainly willed the _possibility_ of sin.
The _possibilitas peccandi_ is from God.
The thought of sin was first conceived in his consciousness.
God eternally thought sin as his absolute opposite and incorporated it as such, with that nature, into his decree; otherwise it could never have arisen and existed in reality.
Satan and Adam and Eve were not the first to conceive the thought of sin; God himself set it visibly before their eyes, as it were.
Through the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and through the probationary command, God clearly indicated to the human being two paths that he could take.
And before his fall, God even tolerated an evil power penetrating into paradise from outside, using the serpent as its instrument, and beginning to negotiate with Eve about the meaning of the probationary command.
The possibility of sin was therefore without doubt willed by God.
Second, in conformity with that objective possibility, God so created angels and human beings that they could sin and fall.
They did not yet possess the highest good; they were placed not at the end but at the beginning of the way; the gift of _perseverantia_, which is and always remains a gift, which can never be merited in the proper sense and can never belong to the _nature_ of a creature, was still withheld from them.
Otherwise it would also have appeared as though God wished forcibly to prevent sin and feared its power.
Angels and human beings therefore had the _gratia, qua potuerunt stare_, not the _qua vellent perpetuo stare_, Heppe, _Dogm. d. ev. ref. K._ 178, 179.
They did not yet possess the highest, indefectible freedom, that is, the freedom of no longer being able to will to sin.
The image of God in the human being was therefore still limited; it had not unfolded in all its fullness; it still had a boundary in the possibility of sin.
The human being did indeed stand in the good, but the possibility of evil still lay alongside it; he did indeed walk on the good path but could wander aside; he was good, but mutably good, _mutabiliter bonus_.
God alone is the One who is in all his attributes and is therefore immutable.
Every creature, however, _becomes_ and can therefore also degenerate.
If _materia_ and _forma_ are distinct, as is always the case with creatures, then the possibility always remains open that the _materia_ may change its _forma_.
What is formed can be _de_-formed and thus also reformed; what is created can be _mis_-created and thus also _re_-created.
Moral freedom, however strong, is in itself essentially distinct from logical necessity and physical compulsion.
A _creatura naturaliter impeccabilis_ is therefore a contradiction, Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 63, art. 1; _C. Gent._ III 109.
Third, in the question concerning the origin of sin, the power and activity of the imagination must be taken into account.
Formerly, dogmatics gave little consideration to this, even though theologians were aware that temptation in a human being directs itself first and chiefly to the imagination and thereby acts upon desire and will, Voetius, _Disp._ I 943; Burmannus, _Synopsis_ I 46, 54; Edwards, _Works_ III 122.
In mysticism, however, imagination occupied a large place; according to Böhme, Lucifer imagined himself through the _Phantasey_ into the abyss of sin (_hinein imaginiert_); _er neigte sich in die Phantasey, also ergriff sie ihn auch und ergab sich ihm in sein Leben_, in Joh. Claassen, _J. Böhme_ II 95.
And in reality the sinful act always arises in the manner described by Thomas à Kempis: _primo occurrit menti simplex cogitatio, deinde fortis imaginatio, postea delectatio et motus pravus et assensio_, _De imit. Chr._ I 13, 5.
Consciousness receives the thought of sin, imagination adorns it and transforms it into an alluring ideal, desire reaches out toward it, and the will accomplishes it.
Thus, in angel and human being too, imagination was the faculty that made transgression of the commandment appear to be a way to likeness with God; cf. Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ II 422 f.; Frank, _Syst. d. chr. Wahrh._ I 433 f.; Kuyper, _Heraut_ 906.
And finally it should be noted that in 1 Cor. 15:45 f. Paul speaks of the first human being as earthly, from the earth, as having become a living soul through creation, and thus places him over against Christ, the Lord from heaven, who became a life-giving spirit.
This comparison and contrast between Adam and Christ is also of deep significance for the fall of the first human being.
Adam was earthly, from the earth, even before the transgression of God’s commandment; through his creation he became a living soul; the natural is first, afterward the spiritual.
This expresses the fact that the origin and nature of sin differ greatly in angels and human beings.
It is true that we know little of the fall of the angels, but with a view to 1 Tim. 3:6 and 2 Pet. 2:4 it may nevertheless be considered highly probable that pride, the desire to be equal to God in power and dominion, was the beginning and principle of their fall.
The angels were not seduced as human beings were; temptation did not come to them from outside; they fell through themselves.
Jesus says that the devil speaks ἐκ των ἰδιων when he speaks a lie.
Of himself, through his own thinking, he became dissatisfied with his station and his power; he brought forth the lie from himself and set it over against God’s truth as a realm, as a system.
But it is not so with the human being.
He was not a pure spirit; he was not placed so high, although, as one created in God’s image, he stood still nearer to God than the angels; he could not think so loftily or exalt himself so boldly; he was earthly, from the earth, a living soul, organized more finely and delicately but for that reason also more weakly and frailly.
As such a being, created indeed in God’s image but nevertheless earthly and sensual, he offered Satan a suitable opportunity for temptation.
It came to him from outside, accommodated itself, as it were, to his nature, awakened in him the desire of the eyes, the desire of the flesh, and the pride of life, and thus brought him to the fall.
The origin and essence of sin bear an entirely different character in the human being than in the angels; in both it becomes evident that the human being sins not as a devil but as a human being, as a being who is earthly, from the earth, and who through creation became a living soul.
For this reason Scripture, and especially Paul, establishes such a close connection between the sensual nature of the human being and sin.
Underlying this is by no means the opposition between sensuality and reason or the thought that matter is of a lower order and the principle of sin.
This opposition and this thought are not Israelite but Greek in origin.
Scripture knows nothing of such a dualism, but it does know something else, namely, that the human being is by nature a sensual, psychical being.
He was created from the outset as a living soul, earthly, from the earth.
He was therefore already such in the _status integritatis_; and for that reason, despite the knowledge and righteousness he possessed, he was susceptible to seduction and temptation.
Already in the first sin it became evident that the human being was σαρξ; and all subsequent sins have only brought his susceptibility to temptation, weakness, and unreliability ever more clearly to light.
All human sin, even spiritual sin, bears a character that corresponds to his psychical nature and differs from the character of sin in the angels.
Before the fall the human being was the image of God not despite but in his distinctive psychical nature; and from this his sin too receives its stamp.

By all this nothing other and nothing more than the possibility of sin has been demonstrated.
How that possibility became actuality is a mystery and will likely remain so.
We can indicate that the thought of sin existed eternally in God’s understanding, that it was set before the human being in the probationary command, that he therefore had knowledge not only of the good but also of a forbidden evil, and that imagination is the faculty that transforms thoughts into ideals.
But this does not yet explain the transition from possibility to actuality, from the mere representation to the sinful act.
This explanation eludes us not only in the origination of the first sin but repeatedly in all kinds of human deeds and actions.
In psychology and biography we are indeed satisfied with a few data; if we know something of a person’s ancestry, parents, education, and so forth, we think we have explained his personality, life, and deeds.
But this is really rather superficial; every human being is a mystery, and every action has another and deeper root than that of the milieu.
This applies in a much stronger degree to sin.
Here we enter the mysterious domain of moral freedom and stand before a phenomenon that by its very nature escapes explanation in its origin.
For a moral action is never like a conclusion from premises or a physical or chemical result.
It is essentially distinct from both and has a nature of its own; moral life is entirely distinctive, it is always a life of freedom, and freedom is by its very nature a mystery.
But this is still much more the case with a sinful act, and then especially with the first sinful act.
Sin cannot be deduced logically or physically from preceding circumstances, reasonings, or deliberations; above all, it cannot be derived from a holy nature created in God’s image.
Whoever comprehended and explained sin, that is, whoever could demonstrate that it had to follow necessarily from what preceded it, would do injustice to its nature, erase the boundaries between good and evil, and reduce evil to something good.
The sinful act has the sinful will as its cause, but who can indicate the cause of this sinful will?
_Causas defectionum istarum, cum efficientes non sint sed deficientes, velle invenire, tale est, ac si quisquam velit videre tenebras vel andire silentium_, Augustine, _De civ._ XII 7.
Sin began with a lie, John 8:44; it rests upon a false imagining, an untrue representation, an imagination of a good that was not good; in its origination it is therefore folly and absurdity; it has no origin in the proper sense but only a beginning.
Not without reason has Satan therefore been called an _Ironie aller Logik_; cf. Tholuck, _Die Lehre v. d. Sünde_, 8th ed., 1862, S. 15; Philippi, _Kirchl. Gl._ III 256; H. Schmidt, Herzog² 15, 22; Vilmar, _Theol. Moral_ I 37; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ II 156.
This impossibility of explaining the origin of sin must therefore not be understood as an evasion, an _asylum ignorantiae_.
Rather, it must be stated openly and clearly: here we stand at the limits of our knowledge.
Sin _is_ there, but it will never be able to justify its existence; it is illegitimate and irrational.
