## 4.

To the first group belong those who seek the origin of sin in the domination of the human being by matter.
Greek philosophy generally held the opinion that reason had the task of restraining sensual drives and passions.
The Jews assumed in the human being by nature a יצר הרע, which continually gained strength with physical development, reached its climax in sexual desire, and lay at the foundation of all disobedience to God’s commandments, Weber §§ 49, 50.
This thought repeatedly returns in ascetic movements; Roman Catholic theology even acknowledged its relative validity when, in the human being without the rein of the _donum superadditum_, it spoke of a natural conflict between flesh and spirit, of a _morbus_ and _languor naturae humanae_, Bellarmine, _De gratia primi hom._, c. 5.
In more recent philosophy and theology, sin is repeatedly derived in the same way from an original opposition between nature and reason, sensuality and understanding, the lower and higher self, flesh and spirit, egoistic and social inclinations.
On this standpoint, sensuality is indeed still not itself regarded as sin, but it is nevertheless viewed as the occasion and stimulus to sinning.
All sin therefore consists essentially in this: that the spirit serves sensuality and allows it to rule over itself; and all virtue consists in the human being’s ruling over nature by his reason and thus becoming a free, independent personality.
This conception even gladly appeals to the Pauline doctrine of the σαρξ and rejoices in this scriptural support.
Cf. Descartes, Wolff, Fichte, Hegel, _Werke_ XI 190 f.; XII 209 f., in Jodl, _Gesch. der Ethik in der neuern Philos._ I, 1882; II, 1889; and von Hartmann, _Das sittliche Bewustsein_², 1836, S. 265 f.; and further Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 66; Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 459 f.; Biedermann, _Chr. Dogm._ § 763 f.; Pfleiderer, _Grundriss_ § 100 f.; Lipsius, _Dogm._ §§ 468 f., 477 f.; Schultz, _Grundriss d. ev. Dogm._², 1892, S. 61; Scholten, _De vrije wil_ 177; _L. H. K._ II 422 f., 574 f.; and so forth.

But this explanation of sin suffers from inconsistency.
For one of two alternatives must be true: either the sensual nature of the human being is not in itself sin, and sin first arises when human reason and will consent to its demands, in which case this theory falls back into that of Pelagianism; or the sensual nature is sinful in itself, in which case sin is proper to matter as such and the anthropological explanation must advance to the cosmic one.
And this has in fact been done by many.
Plato assumed an eternal ὑλη alongside and over against God.
The world was indeed a work of reason, but from the beginning another, blind power was also at work in it that could not be completely mastered by the δημιουργος.
God could therefore not make the world as good as he wished; he was bound to finitude, to the ὑλη.
The cause of sin, suffering, and death thus lies in the σωματοειδες; the ὑλη resists the inward working and permeation of the idea; the body is a prison for the soul, a source of fear and unrest, of desire and passion, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II 765 f., 855 f.
The ὑλη has the same significance in Neoplatonism and Gnosticism and in many ascetic and theosophical movements; cf. Zeller, ibid., V 125, 171, 236, 297, 386, 547.
And related to this doctrine of Plato are all theories that derive sin from a matter that was indeed created by God but nevertheless stands over against him, Weisse, _Philos. Dogm._ §§ 541 f., 561 f.; Rothe, _Theol. Ethik_ § 55; or from finitude and limitation, _l’imperfection originale des créatures_, Leibniz, _Theodicée_ § 156; or in general from the realization of the world-idea.

This explanation of sin from the nature of creaturely being, however, cannot escape the consequence of returning in one way or another to God and seeking the origin of sin in his nature or work.
In Plato, the ὑλη itself had an eternal and independent existence alongside God.
In Parsism and Manichaeism, two personal divine beings stood eternally over against one another as creators of light and darkness and gave the existing world its two-sided character, Schwane, _D. G._ II 503 f.
Neoplatonism and Gnosticism made creation, fall, redemption, and so forth into moments in an emanation that proceeds from God as the βυθος ἀγνωστος, the absolute _pleroma_, in ever-descending formations, finally gives existence to the material world with its ignorance, darkness, sin, suffering, and death, but then in the process of redemption leads that emanated world, fallen away from God, back to God, Stöckl, _Die spekulative Lehre v. Menschen_ II 52 f.
The theosophy of Böhme and Schelling fed upon these ideas when it sought to explain God’s personality, the Trinity, creation, fall, and redemption from the being of God; cf. vol. II, 304, 393.
The three _Potenzen_ assumed in God so that he may become person and spirit are at the same time the _principia_ of another being, namely, the world.
As person, God has the freedom and power to set in tension outside himself the _Potenzen_ that are in him but that he eternally masters, Schelling, _Werke_ II 3, S. 272 f., 310, 338 f.
In that tension lies the possibility of sin.
In the original creation, whether it was first only ideal or also real, these _Potenzen_ were at rest.
Sin, misery, darkness, death, and so forth were indeed present but only potentially; they slumbered in the _Urgrund_ of creation.
But the human being, in whom these _Potenzen_ were also present, broke that unity and unleashed the evil powers that were potentially present in creation.
A world such as the present one, with so much savagery and misery, can be explained only from a fall; this is the _Urthatsache der Geschichte_; cf. Joh. Claassen, _Jakob Böhme_ II 185 f.; Schelling, _Werke_ I 7, S. 336-416; I 8, S. 331 f.; II 3, S. 344 f., 358 f.
Hegel went still further and regarded the Idea of the Absolute’s realizing itself in the world as its other-being as a falling away.
Although nature in Hegel was a product of reason, he nevertheless could not deny that it was powerless to realize the Idea fully, and he therefore declared that the Idea, in giving existence to such a world, had become unfaithful to itself and had fallen away from itself, _Werke_ VI 413; VII 1, S. 23 f.; VII 2, S. 15 f.
Thus the way was prepared for pessimism, which in the manner of Buddhism regards being itself as the greatest sin, committed by the blind, alogical will, which is the _Urschuldige_, Schopenhauer, _Die Welt als W. u Vorst._⁶ I 193 f.; II 398 f.; von Hartmann, _Philos. d. Unbew._ II⁹ 198 f., 273 f., 295 f.
