## 8.

The distinction between the material and formal elements in sin does not, however, yet answer the question why God included sin in his decree and in its execution. The answer lies enclosed in God’s providence as it extends also over sin. Scripture repeatedly declares that God uses sin as a means: for the punishment of the wicked, Deut. 2:30; Josh. 11:20; Judg. 9:23, 24; John 12:40; Rom. 1:21–28; 2 Thess. 2:11, 12; for the salvation of his people, Gen. 45:5; 50:20; for the testing and chastisement of believers, Job 1:11, 12; 2 Sam. 24:1; 1 Cor. 10:13; 11:19; 2 Cor. 12:7; for the glorification of his name, Ex. 7:3; Prov. 16:4; Rom. 9:17; 11:33; etc. Precisely because God is the absolutely Holy and Almighty One, he can use sin as a means in his hand. Creatures cannot do so and at the slightest contact themselves become defiled and unclean. But God is so infinitely far from wickedness that he can make sin serviceable to his glorification as nothing but a will-less instrument. There are examples in abundance to prove that here too the proverb applies: _duo cum faciunt idem, non est idem_. God wills that Shimei curse David, that Satan afflict Job, that Jews and Gentiles deliver his holy child Jesus to death—and yet in all these unrighteous deeds the creatures stand guilty and God goes free. For even when he wills that evil exist, he never wills it otherwise than in a holy manner; he uses it but does not commit it. And for this reason he also permitted sin in his creation. He would not have tolerated it if he could not have governed it in an absolutely holy and sovereign manner. He would not have endured it if he were not God, the Holy and Almighty One. But because he is God, he did not fear its existence and its power. He willed it so that in it and over against it he might bring his divine virtues to light. Had he not granted it existence, there would always have been room for the thought that in all his virtues he was not exalted above a power whose possibility was given with creation itself. For every rational creature, as creature, as a finite, limited, mutable being, includes the possibility of apostasy. But because God is God, he did not fear the way of freedom, the actuality of sin, the eruption of unrighteousness, the power of Satan. And thus he always reigns over sin, both at its origin and in its development. He does not constrain it; he does not forcibly stop it; he does not crush it by his might; rather, he lets it come to its full development of power. He remains King and yet gives it free play in his kingdom; he grants it everything—his world, his creatures, even his Christ, for _mala sine bonis esse non possunt_. He permits it to make use of all that is his; he gives it opportunity to show what it can do, yet at the end to emerge from the arena as King of kings. For sin is of such a nature that it perishes through the freedom granted to it, that it dies of its own sickness, that it eats itself to death. At the summit of its power, through the cross alone, it is publicly displayed in its powerlessness, Col. 2:15. God therefore willed that sin should exist. _Quamvis ergo ea, quae mala sunt, in quantum mala sunt, non sint bona, tamen ut non solum bona sed etiam sint et mala, bonum est. Nam nisi hoc esset bonum, ut essent et mala, nullo modo esse sinerentur ab omnipotente bono, cui procul dubio quam facile est quod vult facere, tam facile est, quod non vult esse, non sinere. Hoc nisi credamus, periclitatur ipsum nostrae confessionis initium, qua nos in Deum omnipotentem credere confitemur_, Augustine, _Enchir._ 96. Because he knew that he could rule sin absolutely, _melius judicavit de malis bene facere quam mala nulla esse permittere_, ibid. 11, 27; _De civ._ XXII 1; _De Gen. ad litt._ II 9; _De Gen. ad Manich._ II 28. He intends and directs evil toward good and places it in the service of his glory. Augustine even employs all kinds of images to assign sin a place in the order of the whole. It has there the same significance as shadow in a painting, _De civ._ XI 23; solecisms and barbarisms in language, _De ord._ II 11; antitheses in a song, _De civ._ XI 18. God composed the _ordo seculorum_ as a _pulcherrimum carmen_ from certain antitheses in order to enhance the beauty and harmony of the whole, _De civ._ XI 18; XIV 11; _De Gen. ad Manich._ I 16; cf. Erigena, _De div. nat._ V 35; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I, qu. 48, art. 2; _C. Gent._ III 71; Leibniz in Pichler, _Theol. des Leibniz_ I 264 f.; and also above, p. 51. These images indeed contain some truth, but they easily give rise to misunderstanding. They make sin appear far too necessary and precisely in its place within the whole of things; they sacrifice the particular to the universal and therefore offer neither reconciliation nor comfort to the one who struggles with sin or is bowed down under suffering. But this is true: also and precisely in the government of sin God’s virtues come gloriously to revelation. The riches of God’s grace, the depth of his compassion, the immutability of his faithfulness, the inviolability of his justice, the glory of his wisdom and power have come to light all the more clearly through sin. When the human being broke the covenant of works, he put in its place the so much better covenant of grace. When Adam had fallen, he gave Christ as the Lord from heaven. This, now, is divine greatness: so to govern sin that contrary to its nature and striving it nevertheless becomes serviceable to the honor of his name. And therefore the sin that is in the world is so far from robbing us of faith in God, in his love and power, that, properly considered, it rather confirms and strengthens us in that faith. _Si malum est, Deus est. Non enim esset malum, sublato ordine boni, cujus privatio sit malum; hic autem ordo non esset, si Deus non esset_, Thomas, _C. Gent._ III 71.
