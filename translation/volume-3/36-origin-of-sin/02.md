## 2.

The tradition of such a fall also occurs among other peoples. The Avesta relates that Ahriman’s first evil deed consisted in producing a great serpent, Dahaka, with three heads, three mouths, six eyes, and a thousand powers, in order through it to corrupt the world. Ahriman, who is himself also called the serpent, the liar, and the deceiver of mortals, disturbed peace through it, destroyed paradise, sowed doubt and unbelief in human hearts, and brought sickness and suffering upon the earth. For Jima, the noble ruler in the golden age, was disobedient to God; he fell into sin through pride and self-exaltation, Ahuramazda abandoned him, and he was killed. The Babylonian-Assyrian tradition too stands in an unmistakable connection with the biblical narrative. There the serpent is called Tiamat and by its temptation brings the human being to the fall, but on that account is also cursed and opposed by Merodach; cf. Fischer, _Heidenthum u. Offenbarung_, 1878, pp. 137 f., 208 f.; Delitzsch on Gen. 3:1. Otherwise, just as the essence of sin, so too its origin is unknown to the pagans. Even the Jews, who acknowledged the fall and the temptation by Satan, Wis. 2:24, and therefore often called him the ancient serpent, sometimes taught that Satan was created on the sixth day together with Eve; that, incited by sensual desire, he attempted to seduce the human being; and that even before the fall the human being received alongside the יצר הטוב a יצר הרע, an inclination to evil, in order to overcome it and thus make his works truly meritorious, Weber, _System_ 210 f., 242 f. Elsewhere in the pagan world the origin of sin was therefore sought not in the will of rational creatures but in the essence of things. The fall is unknown. Confucianism is a shallow rationalism and moralism that regarded the human being as good by nature and sought the way of salvation in a virtuous life in conformity with the world order, Saussaye, _Religionsgesch._ I 249. According to Buddhism, Atman or Brahman, the divine substance, is the sole reality; the world of phenomena is only a dream, has illusion, Maya, as its principle, and is in continual becoming and change. Suffering and pain are therefore universal, for everything is subject to transience, birth, old age, and death; and the cause of that suffering is to be sought in desires, in the desire for being, in the will to be. Deliverance thus consists in the extinction of consciousness or even in the annihilation of being, nirvana, ibid., 411 f. Parsism traced evil back to an originally evil spirit, Ahriman, who stands over against the supreme God, Ahuramazda, possesses a kingdom of darkness of his own, corrupts God’s creation, but is subordinate to Ahuramazda and will one day be defeated by him, ibid., II 34 f. In their legends of an _aurea aetas_, Prometheus, and Pandora, the Greeks and Romans indeed possess something that can recall the biblical narratives, but originally they knew no evil spirits standing over against the good and ascribed all kinds of evil desires and misdeeds to the gods. Neither had the human race fallen all at once but had gradually degenerated, and the human will still possessed the power to live virtuously, to remain within bounds, and thus to overcome sin, which was essentially ὑβρις, ibid., 191; cf. G. Baur, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1848, p. 320. Philosophy usually adopted the same standpoint. According to Socrates, the cause and essence of sin lie solely in ignorance; no one is voluntarily evil, that is, unhappy. Whoever therefore knows the good is good and acts well; nothing other than development is needed to bring the human being, who is good by nature, to the practice of virtue, Xenophon, _Mem._ III 9, 4 sq.; IV 6, 6. Plato and Aristotle indeed saw the inadequacy of this view. Reason was by no means always able to control the passions; sin was rooted more deeply in human nature than to be overcome by knowledge alone. Plato even arrived at an entirely different doctrine concerning the origin of sin and sought it in a fall of preexistent souls. But both nevertheless maintained free will and remained of the opinion that virtue lies within our power. External fate may be determined, but virtue is ἀδεσποτος and depends solely on the human will, ἐφ’ ἡμιν δε και ἡ ἀρετη, ὁμοιως δε και ἡ κακια, Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II⁴ 852; III³ 588. From its pantheistic and deterministic standpoint the Stoa could not seek the cause of sin in the human will and therefore attempted to incorporate physical and moral evil into the order and beauty of the whole. It was impossible even for the Deity to keep human nature free from every defect. Sin is just as necessary as diseases and disasters and is good insofar as it serves the good and brings it to revelation, Zeller, ibid., IV³ 175 f. Yet the Stoa too knew no other way to overcome sin and practice virtue than the human will, ibid., 167 f. And finally, in Cicero, Seneca, Plotinus, and others the thought repeatedly returned that sin was an act of the will and could also be nullified again by the will, ibid., 667, 717, 722; V³ 585. Outside the sphere of special revelation, therefore, sin was always either deistically explained from the human will and understood as a pure act of will, or pantheistically derived from the essence of things and understood as something necessary in the order of the whole.
