## 10.

No precise determination is possible concerning the time of the fall.
For Manichaeism and pantheism the question has no significance either.
Sin is then eternal; it is rooted in an evil being or in God himself and coincides with the existence of the finite.
There is no distinction between creation and fall; creation is itself a falling away of God from himself as pure being.
According to the theosophists, the fall of the angels took place in the time lying between Gen. 1:1 and 2.
The formlessness, emptiness, and darkness of the earth cannot have been created by God, who is the God of life and light; they already presuppose a fall and a curse that followed it.
The angels first dwelt upon this earth; it was their οἰκητηριον, Jude 6; this is also evident from the fact that Satan is still called the ruler of the world, that he wishes to wrest it from the human being, who later received it from God as an inheritance, that in the temptation he wished to cede it to Christ in exchange for an act of worship, that he still dwells in the air, and that the world lies in the Evil One.
The heaven and earth, however, which were created in Gen. 1:1 and assigned to the angels, were of an entirely different nature from those prepared later in the six days, Gen. 1:3 f.
They were a spiritual, immaterial realm of light.
The physical, material earth that came into existence in the six days already presupposes the fall of the angels, as do the formlessness, emptiness, and darkness of Gen. 1:2.
Matter is in itself impure and selfish and therefore cannot have been produced directly by God.
_Nur ein ungeheures Verbrechen, weniger ein Abfall als eine Empörung, konnte diese materielle Offenbarung als Krisis, Hemmungs- und Wiederherstellungsanstalt veranlassen, und nur die Fortdauer dieses Verbrechens macht den Fortbestand oder die Forterzeugung dieser Materie begreiflich_ (Baader); cf. Joh. Claassen, _Jakob Böhme, Sein Leben u. seine theos. Werke_ II, 1885, S. 127 f.; Id., _Franz von Baader, Leben u. theos. Werke_ II, 1887, S. 157 f.; Keerl, _Der Mensch das Ebenbild Gottes_ I 166 f.; and many others, Hamberger, Schubert, K. v. Raumer, R. Wagner, Kurtz, Delitzsch, etc.; cf. Reusch, _Bibel u. Natur_⁴, 1876, S. 88.
But this theory, however attractive, has no adequate ground in Holy Scripture.
Gen. 1:2 does not say that the earth _became_ formless and empty but that it _was_ so; not a single word says that this formlessness and emptiness was a devastation that followed an ordered state; and much less is it said that the fall of the angels had taken place before that time and was the cause of that formlessness.
Further, it cannot be seen what connection could exist between the fall of the angels—supposing that this had already taken place before Gen. 1:2—and the formlessness of the earth.
To establish such a connection, one must resort to all kinds of Gnostic ideas; one must then teach that the angels are in a certain sense corporeal beings and received the original earth as their dwelling place, just as, according to some, they now still dwell upon the fixed stars; that the first earth, which was created in Gen. 1:1, was essentially different from the one prepared in the six days and consisted of a finer substance; and that the coarse matter of which it now consists, although created by God, nevertheless presupposes the fall and is something ungodly and by nature impure and selfish—all opinions borrowed not from Scripture but from Gnosticism.

There is just as little ground for the opinion that the fall of the human being already took place before Gen. 3, whether in the preexistent state of souls or in Gen. 2 at and before the creation of the woman.
This was the doctrine of Pythagoras and Plato: souls first existed in the τοπος ὑπερουρανιος, from which they were driven by a fall and, as punishment, confined in earthly bodies as in prisons; Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II 819 f.
Origen adopted this view in order to explain by it the inequality among creatures; vol. II 442.
Theosophy frequently also connected with it the opinion that the human being was first created androgynous and that the creation of the woman was already proof of a fall that had previously occurred; vol. II 548; and further M. Vitringa II 265.
Related to this is Kant’s doctrine, which speaks of an intelligible act as the cause of the radical _Hang zum Bösen_ and of the _angeborne Schuld_, _Religion_, ed. Rosenkranz, 34, 42, 44 f.; and likewise Schelling, _Werke_ I 7, S. 385; Müller, _Sünde_ II⁵ 99 f.; Steffens, Renouvier, Sécrétan, Dr. Edw. Beecher, and others.
But all these assertions lack any theological and also any philosophical ground.
First, they imply the preexistence of souls, which is unacceptable for various reasons; vol. II 565.
Further, the fall narrated in Gen. 3 is robbed of its character and significance; it ceases to be a fall and becomes merely the appearance of something that took place long ago; in connection with this, the temporal, empirical freedom of the human being loses all its value; the soul alone has fallen and has been placed in the body as punishment.
This doctrine is also contrary to the organic coherence of the human race.
Every human being determines his own fate: _das Wesen des Menschen ist wesentlich seine eigene That_ (Schelling).
It is then astonishing that all human beings individually, without a single exception, determine themselves for evil, and that only the first human being, although fallen, nevertheless received a test whether he might perhaps still wish to remain standing and restore his fall.
And finally, it is clear that humanity, thus dissolved into an aggregate of individuals, can have neither a common head in Adam nor in Christ.
There is no common fall, and thus there is also no common restoration; everyone falls for himself, and everyone must therefore also raise himself up; despite the radical _Böse_, Kant therefore inferred the _du kannst_ from the _du sollst_.

We must therefore remain with the data of Scripture, however few they may be.
The time of the fall of the angels is not mentioned at all.
In view of the ἀπ’ ἀρχης, John 8:44, many theologians were of the opinion that the angels, though not in the very moment of their creation itself, _in primo instanti_, nevertheless immediately thereafter, _in secundo instanti_, were through their first act of will either confirmed in the good or fell into sin; Augustine, _de civ._ XI 13; Thomas, _S. Th._ I qu. 62 art. 5; qu. 63 art. 5 and 6.
Others assumed that a short time elapsed after their creation and that their fall then took place either before the creation of heaven and earth in Gen. 1:1, Episcopius, _Inst. Theol._ IV 3, 1; or within the six days of creation, Coccejus on John 8:44; or, in view of Gen. 1:31, only after the completion of the whole work of creation, Voetius, _Disp._ I 919, 920; Turretinus, _Theol. El._ IX 5; M. Vitringa II 261.
Just as little can be said with certainty concerning the time of the human being’s fall.
Some speak of years after his creation; others think, because Genesis passes immediately from the account of creation to that of the fall and also on the basis of Gen. 4:1, that the fall of the human being took place only a few days after, or even on the same day as, his creation; Marck, _Hist. Parad._ III 7; Moor, _Comm._ IV 166; M. Vitringa II 261; Zöckler, _Urstand des Menschen_, 35 f.
These determinations of time are also of lesser importance.
What is indeed important is this: according to Scripture, the fall is essentially distinct from creation itself.
Sin is a phenomenon whose possibility was indeed given in the creation of finite, mutable beings but whose actuality could be brought into existence only by the will of the creature.
It is a power that does not belong to the essence of creation, that was not originally present, that came through disobedience and transgression, that unlawfully intruded into creation, and that ought not to be there.
It exists, and its existence is no accident; indeed, in view of God’s counsel, which included it and assigned it a place, it may to a certain extent and in a certain sense be said that it had to exist.
But it nevertheless always had to exist as something that _ought_ not to exist and that has no right to existence.
