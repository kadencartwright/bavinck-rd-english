## 3.

Both these conceptions repeatedly returned throughout the Christian centuries.
The first was revived by Pelagius; cf. Augustine, _c. duas ep. Pel._ and _c. Jul. Pelag._; and then found ever wider acceptance in the weakened form of Semi-Pelagianism.
After the Reformation it was taken up again by Socinianism, Fock, _Der Socin._, 484 f., 653 f.; gained considerable influence through Remonstrantism, Episcopius, _Inst. Theol._ IV 3 c. 6, 7; IV 5 c. 1, 2; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II 24; III 2 sq.; and then became the prevailing view of that time in the deism of Locke, Tindal, Rousseau, and others and in the rationalism of Wegscheider, _Inst._ § 99, 112; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ II 17 f.
Today it has once again acquired significance in the theology of Ritschl and his school; Ritschl, _Rechtf. u. Vers._, ² II 241–246; III 304–357; Kaftan, _Wesen der christl. Relig._, 1881, S. 246 f.; _Dogmatik_, 1897, § 34, 40; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, 319 f.; Siebeck, _Lehrb. der Religionsphilos._, 436 f.
However much it is presented in all kinds of modifications, the fundamental thought is nevertheless always this: sin is not rooted in a nature, is neither a habit nor a state, but is always an act of the will.
The image of God in the human being then consisted chiefly or exclusively in dominion; insofar as a _status integritatis_ is accepted, it consisted especially in childlike innocence, in the freedom of indifference, in the possibility of choosing good or evil.
The fall itself, if still acknowledged as a historical fact, loses its dreadful significance and becomes an event quite like that which takes place at every moment in human life when evil is chosen instead of good.
The consequences of the fall are therefore also slight.
Children are born in the same state in which Adam lived before his disobedience; freedom of the will, that is, the image of God, has remained; at most, a certain inclination toward sin is transmitted from one human being to another, but such an inclination is not properly the consequence of Adam’s first sin but of all the sins of all our ancestors; neither is it itself sin, but it first becomes sin when the free will yields to that inclination; there is thus a distinction between sin (pollution, sinful inclination) and guilt (evil deed, intentional, voluntary transgression); only the latter needs atonement and forgiveness, while the former—the inclination toward sin—and unconscious, involuntary yielding are properly not sin but rather ignorance, which brings no guilt.
And just as sin and guilt must be distinguished, so too must sin and suffering; there is much suffering that exists independently of sin and would also exist even if there were no sin; death is essentially not a consequence of sin but belongs to the human being by nature; spiritual and eternal death was not at all a punishment for the first sin; at most, that punishment consists in the _moriendi necessitas_, which in Adam’s case, if he had remained standing, would have been prevented by a miracle, or in the manner of dying, which without sin would have been less painful and less premature.

It is understandable that in the long run this conception of sin can satisfy neither mind nor heart.
It calls forth too many and too serious objections.
It does not reckon with the seriousness and power of sin as everyone learns to know it in daily experience; it proceeds from an atomistic conception of the human race and does not explain how all without distinction are subjected to sin; it fails to recognize the priestly and royal office of Christ and is content with the teacher and prophet of Nazareth; it restricts the operation of sin and consequently the dominion of redemption to the religious-ethical sphere and sets the whole of culture apart from Christianity.
Among its proponents themselves, the need for a different, deeper conception repeatedly awakens: thus in Semi-Pelagianism over against Pelagius, in Volkelius, _de vera religione_ II c. 6, over against Socinianism, in Wegscheider § 116 over against rationalism, and in Kaftan, Nitzsch, Schultz, and others over against Ritschl; cf. James Orr, _The Ritschlian Theology and the evang. faith_, London, 1897, p. 213; G. Ecke, _Die Theol. Schule Albr. Ritschl’s und die evang. Kirche der Gegenwart_, Berlin, 1897, I.
The more deeply sin is conceived, the less it becomes something accidental and arbitrary, and the more it increases in power and significance, not only for religious and ethical life but also for intellectual and aesthetic life, for physical life and the whole of cosmic life.
If, however, one asks about the origin of sin on the basis of this insight, different answers once again present themselves.
Not all go equally far.
Some derive it from human nature, others from the cosmos, and still others from God.
