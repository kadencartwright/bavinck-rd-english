## 7.

Still, not everything has been said with the statement that God is not the cause of sin.
Scripture, which keeps God far removed from all wickedness, on the other hand declares as decisively as possible that his counsel and government also extend over sin; see the passages already cited earlier, vol. II, pp. 315, 369, and above, p. 30 f.
God is not the author of sin, but sin nevertheless does not occur outside his knowledge, his will, and his power; how, then, is God’s relation to sin to be conceived?
Some, in order to keep God free from all sin, deprived him even of omniscience, vol. II, p. 161.
Others judged that sin did not occur outside God’s knowledge but did occur outside his will, and they contented themselves with the concept of _permissio_.
God foreknew sin but did not will it; he only permitted it and did not prevent it.
Thus spoke the church fathers, Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ IV, c. 12; Origen, _De princ._ III 2, 7; John of Damascus, _De fide orthod._ II 29; etc.; cf. Suicerus, s.v. προνοια and συγχωρησις.
The Pelagians acquiesced in this usage and this solution, cf. Augustine, _C. Julianum Pelag._ V, c. 3; as did many Roman Catholic theologians, Trent VI, c. 6; Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et stat. pecc._ II 16; Petavius, _De Deo_ VI, c. 6, 5; the Remonstrants, Arminius, _Op._ 644 sq., 694 sq.; Episcopius, _Inst. theol._ IV, sect. 4, c. 10; Limborch, _Theol. Christ._ II 29; the Lutherans, Gerhard, _Loc._ VI, c. 9; Quenstedt, _Theol._ I 533; Hollaz, _Ex. theol._ 449; Buddeus, _Inst. theol._ 560; Bretschneider, _Dogm._ I 506; and many more recent theologians, Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 265; Shedd, _Dogm. Theol._ I 419, 444; etc.
Now advocates of this view often acknowledged that _permissio_ was no lack of knowledge and power in God and that it did not make him an idle spectator of sin; yet _permissio_ was always described as an _actus negativus_, as a _suspensio impedimenti_, as neither a positive willing nor a positive not-willing of sin but as a _non velle impedire_.
It is clear that this conception not only provides no solution but is also ambiguous and evades the real issue.
The decisive question is this: suppose that such a more or less negative act of divine _permissio_ precedes in a particular case, does sin then follow or not; does it then still rest in the choice of the human being’s free will or not; can he then just as readily omit it as commit it?
If the decision then still rests with the human being’s free will, Pelagius is right; the government of sin has in fact been taken entirely from God, and he is at most an _otiosus peccatorum spectator_.
If, by contrast, God’s _permissio_ is of such a nature that the human being, when placed in those circumstances, must commit the sin not through compulsion but by virtue of the ordinances that apply especially to the moral life, then right is on Augustine’s side, however one may judge the word _permissio_.
By posing the problem in this way, Augustine had already seen that permission could not be purely negative but must be an act of God’s will.
_Non fit aliquid nisi omnipotens fieri velit, vel sinendo ut fiat vel ipse faciendo._
God does all that pleases him; he does not will something without doing it, but what he wills, that he does; and what occurs never occurs outside his will.
_Miro et ineffabili modo non fit praeter ejus voluntatem, quod etiam contra ejus fit voluntatem, quia non fieret, si non sineret (nec utique nolens sinit sed volens) nec sinere bonus fieri male nisi omnipotens et de malo facere posset bene_, _Ench._ 95–100; _De trin._ III 4 sq.; _De civ._ XIV 11; _De gr. et lib. arb._ 20, 21.
Many scholastic and Augustinian theologians still spoke in the same spirit; although they too used the word permission, it was nevertheless understood as _velle sinere_ or _velle permittere mala fieri_, Lombard, _Sent._ I 46; Thomas, _S. Theol._ I 19, art. 9; _C. Gent._ I 95; II 25; commentary on _Sent._ I 46; Hugh of St. Victor, _S. Sent._ I, c. 13; _De Sacr._ I 4, cc. 4–15.

In substance the Reformed held no other conviction; hence a certain Livinus de Meyer rightly said, _ovum ovo non esse similius quam doctrinam Calvinianam Thomisticae_, cited in Daalman, _Summa S. Thomae_ II 308.
They had, however, learned by experience that the word _permissio_ was used in a very ambiguous sense and was abused to conceal Pelagianism.
They were therefore not favorably disposed toward the word.
In itself they had so little objection to it that in fact they all used it again, cf. Ebrard, _Dogm._ § 265.
But in their conviction _permissio_ was then no pure negation, no _mera cessatio voluntatis_ flowing from _ignorantia_ or _impotentia_ or _negligentia_, but a positive act of God, a _volitio efficax_, yet not _efficiens_ or _producens_ but _deficiens_, upon which, according to the nature of the moral life, sin must follow, Zwingli, _Op._ III 170; IV, _De provid._, c. 5; Calvin, _Inst._ I 17, 11; 18, 1, 2; II 4, 2–4; III 23, 4, 8, 9; Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 315, 387, 399; II 347; III 426; Zanchius, _Op._ II 269; Martyr, _Loci C._, p. 58; Gomarus, _De provid. Dei_, c. 11; Twisse, _De permissione_, _Op._ I 544–668; Maccovius, _Loci C._, p. 206; Alting, _Theol. El. nova_, p. 316; _Ex. v. h. Ontw. v. Tol._ VI 277; M. Vitringa II 196; etc.
It is true that in the heat of controversy the Reformed sometimes used _dicta duriora_, cf., for example, Calvin, _Inst._ III 23, 7; Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 319, 360, 401; Zanchius, _Op._ V 2.
And Roman Catholics, Bellarmine, _De amiss. gr. et statu pecc._ II, c. 3 sq.; Petavius, _De Deo_ VI, c. 5; X, c. 8; Möhler, _Symb._ §§ 2–4; Socinians, _Cat. Racov._ X 16; Remonstrants, _Apol. Conf._, cc. 2 and 6; Episcopius, _Op._ I 375 sq.; and Lutherans, Gerhard, _Loc._ VI, c. 10; Quenstedt, _Theol._ II 97, did not fail to make use of these expressions and repeatedly accused the Reformed of making God the author of sin.
But first, all these _dicta duriora_ are still milder than those that sometimes occur in Holy Scripture, for example, Ex. 7:3; 2 Sam. 16:10; 24:1; Mal. 1:3; Luke 2:34; Rom. 9:17, 18; 2 Thess. 2:11; etc.; further, all such harsh expressions have at all times been charged against Paul by the Judaizers, against Augustine by the Pelagians, against Gottschalk by Hincmar, and against the Jansenists by the Jesuits; next, they were always avoided by the Reformed in their confessions, and Maccovius was admonished about them at the Synod of Dort, _Archief v. Kerk. Gesch._ III, 1831, p. 632; moreover, they were omitted or else elucidated and explained by most Reformed theologians, Voetius, _Disp._ I 1119–1137; Maresius, _Syst. Theol._ IV 18; Turretin, _Theol. El._ VI, qq. 7, 8; Trigland, _Kerk. Gesch._ IV 673 f.; V 694; idem, _Antapologia_, cc. 8–10; Chamier, _Panstr. Cath._ II, book 3; Moor II 487; etc.; and finally, their significance and intention are fully transparent to everyone willing to understand them from their connection with the whole of Reformed doctrine.
The matter is simply this: _permissio_ understood in a negative sense offers not the slightest solution to the problem of God’s relation to sin, by no means removes the objection that God is its author, and in fact withdraws the whole of sin from God’s providential government.
For whoever can prevent an evil and yet silently watches it happen is as guilty as the one who commits the evil himself, Beza, _Tract. Theol._ I 315.
Moreover, even if God has merely and solely permitted sin, there must nevertheless be a reason why he did not will to prevent it.
That reason cannot lie in God in a lack of knowledge or power; it must therefore lie in his will.
Thus _permissio_ is nevertheless again an act of his will; he _willed_ to permit sin; and this willing to permit can be understood in no other way than that sin then actually occurs, not through God but through the creature.

Indeed, when Christian theology discussed God’s government over sin, it never stopped at this _permissio_.
For if both Scripture and Christian thought forbade placing sin wholly or partly outside God’s will and providence, the only remaining solution was to distinguish the _manner_ of God’s government over good and evil.
And in fact, although in a certain sense it may also be said that God willed sin, that is, that he willed sin to exist, he nevertheless willed evil in an entirely different manner than he willed the good; in the good he delights, but evil he hates with divine hatred.
In order that this difference in God’s government over good and evil may come to light, it must first be pointed out that God and the human being are never separated but are always distinct, cf. above, p. 28.
Faith is a gift; God causes the human being to believe, yet _formaliter_ it is not God but the human being who believes.
This applies much more strongly to the sinful act.
_Materialiter_ it must certainly be ascribed to God, but _formaliter_ it remains to the human being’s account.
When a murderer kills someone, all the deliberation and power he needs for this come from God, but the act, considered formally, is his and not God’s.
Indeed, the fact of killing, considered purely in itself, is not yet sin, for the same thing often takes place in war and on the scaffold.
What makes the killing sin is not the _materia_, the substrate, but the _forma_, that is, the _vitiositas_, the ανομια; not the _substantia_ but the _accidens_ in the act.
It has been objected that this distinction, even if correct, in fact yields nothing, because it places the formal aspect of the act, that is, precisely what is sinful in sin, outside God’s government, Episcopius, _Op._ I 180; Quenstedt, _Theol._ II 101.
This observation is correct only in part; it contains truth not in general but only in this special case concerning sin.
In the case of faith, no one will conclude from the fact that the human being is _formaliter_ its subject that it falls outside God’s providence.
But it is true that the situation with faith is altogether different from that with sin.
Faith is an absolute gift and excludes all merit; sin, by contrast, is the human being’s act and brings guilt with it.
Therefore sin must here be set not over against faith, which is now granted by God out of grace, but over against the good that the human being would have done if he had remained standing.
That good would _materialiter_ have been entirely God’s work; _formaliter_, however, it would have had the human being as its subject and would have brought him, not of itself but according to the _foedus operum_, a claim to reward.
Just as that good would not thereby have been withdrawn from God’s government, so sin is not placed outside his providence because, in the formal sense, its subject is not God but the human being.
But there is more.
In the case of the good, God’s providence must be conceived in such a way that he himself works within the subject through his Spirit and positively enables it to do good.
In the case of sin it cannot and may not be represented in this way.
Sin is ἀνομια, _deformitas_, and therefore has God not as its _causa efficiens_ but at most as its _causa deficiens_.
Light cannot of itself produce darkness; darkness arises only when the light is removed.
God is therefore at most the negative cause, the _causa per accidens_, of sin; its actual, positive cause must be sought in the human being.
Because sin, however, is only _forma_ and no _substantia_, the fact that it is _formaliter_ the act of the human being does not place it in any respect outside God’s providence.
He works in it, but in a manner that corresponds entirely to the nature of sin.
Just as in his government he directs all things in accordance with their own nature, so too in the moral domain he maintains the ordinances that he has particularly established for it.
Sin too arises and develops according to fixed law, not according to the laws of nature or logic but according to those implanted in the ethical life and still operative even in its devastation.
Disease, decomposition, and death are the antipodes of health, development, and life, but no less than these are governed from beginning to end by fixed laws.
And thus there is also a law of sin that determines its entire history in the human being and humanity.
And precisely this law-governed character of sin proves that God also reigns royally in and over it.
A human being who sins does not make himself free and independent of God; on the contrary, whereas he was a son, he becomes a slave.
Whoever commits sin is a servant of sin.
