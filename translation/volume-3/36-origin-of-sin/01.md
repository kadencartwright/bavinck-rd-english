# § 36. The Origin of Sin

## 1.

God’s providence, as the government of sin, far transcends all our thought and comprehension and at the same time celebrates its highest triumph as such.
No sooner had God created the world good and perfect than sin intruded into it.
The mystery of being is made still more incomprehensible by the mystery of evil.
At almost the same moment that creatures come forth pure and glorious from the hand of their Maker, they are robbed of all their splendor and stand corrupt and unclean before his holy countenance.
Sin has devastated the whole creation, changing its righteousness into guilt, its holiness into impurity, its glory into shame, its blessedness into misery, its harmony into disorder, its life into death, and its light into darkness.
Whence, then, this evil, and what is the origin of sin?
Scripture vindicates God and offers a continuous theodicy when it declares and maintains that in no case is God the cause of sin.
For he is righteous and holy and far from wickedness, Deut. 32:4; Job 34:10; Ps. 92:16; Isa. 6:3; Hab. 1:13; a light without darkness, 1 John 1:5; tempting no one, James 1:13; the abundant fountain of all that is good and clean and pure, Ps. 36:10; James 1:17; he forbids sin in his law, Ex. 20, and in the conscience of every human being, Rom. 2:14, 15; takes no pleasure in wickedness, Ps. 5:5, but hates it and is wrathful against it, Ps. 45:8; Rom. 1:18; he judges and expiates it in Christ, Rom. 3:24–26; cleanses his people from it through forgiveness and sanctification, 1 Cor. 1:30; and wills to punish it both temporally and eternally, Rom. 1:18; 2:8.
For the origin of sin, Scripture always points us to the creature.
But for that reason God’s government of sin, including its origin, is not excluded.
It is God himself who created the _possibility_ of sin.
He not only formed the human being in such a way that he could fall.
He also planted the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, confronted the human being through the probationary command with the choice between good and evil, and permitted the temptation by the serpent.
It was his will to walk with the human being along the perilous path of freedom rather than to elevate him at once above the possibility of sin.
The tree of the knowledge of good and evil is undoubtedly so called because by eating from it the human being would obtain a knowledge of good and evil that he had not possessed until then, that was forbidden to him, and that he was not permitted to acquire.
The question is what is to be understood by this knowledge of good and evil.
The usual interpretation is that by eating from the tree the human being would acquire an experiential knowledge of good and evil; but it has rightly been objected that this knowledge of good and evil would make the human being like God, Gen. 3:5, whereas God does not and cannot have empirical knowledge of evil; further, that by eating from the tree the human being precisely lost the experiential knowledge of good; and finally, that Gen. 3:22a must then be understood as irony, which is already unacceptable in itself and decidedly conflicts with verse 22b.
Others have therefore thought that Gen. 3 narrated the human being’s development from the animal state to self-consciousness and reason and have consequently seen in the fall the first venture of reason, the beginning of moral life, the origin of culture, and the happiest event in the history of humanity; so already in earlier times some Ophites, who regarded the serpent as an incarnation of the Logos, and later Kant, _Mutmassl. Anfang der Menschengesch._, 1786; Schiller, _Ueber die erste Menschengesellschaft_, 1790; Hegel, _Werke_ VII 1, S. 14 f.; IX 390 f.; XI 194; Strauss, _Gl._ II 29; cf. Bretschneider, _Syst. Entw._ § 89.
This conception, however, conflicts so strongly with the intent of the narrative that today it is almost universally abandoned.
For it would presuppose that God created the human being in a state of childlike, even animal innocence and had intended always to keep him in it; but knowledge, including moral knowledge, was already granted to the human being at his creation, as is proved by creation in God’s image, the naming of the animals, and the reception and understanding of the probationary command; and the knowledge the human being acquired through his fall was of an entirely different kind, one that God had forbidden and that made him worthy of all manner of punishment.
Gen. 3 narrates no _Riesenfortschritt_ but a fall of the human being, Wellhausen, _Gesch. Israels_, 1878, S. 344 f.; Rütschi, _Gesch. u. Krit. der k. Leben v. d. urspr. Vollkommenheit_, Leiden, 1881, S. 8; Smend, _Altt. Relig._ 120; Marti, _Gesch. d. israel. Relig._, 1897, S. 179; Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 151 f.
These therefore advocate the view that by the knowledge of good and evil is meant not the very first intellectual or moral knowledge but, with an appeal to 2 Sam. 19:35, 36, _die intellectuelle Welterkenntniss, die metaphysische Erkenntniss der Dinge in ihrem Zusammenhange, ihrem Werth oder Unwerth, ihrem Nutzen oder Schaden für den Menschen_, in other words, wisdom, the art of mastering the world, which would in fact make the human being independent and like God and deprive God of dominion.
This view, however, is burdened by the same objections as the preceding one; only the manner in which Marti develops it contains a pointer toward the correct interpretation.
For in Gen. 3 the primary issue is not the content of the knowledge that the human being would acquire through disobedience but the manner in which he would acquire it, cf. Kuyper, _Heraut_ 950, 951.
The nature of the knowledge of good and evil intended here is clearly described by the fact that through it the human being would become like God, Gen. 3:5, 22.
By eating from the tree, he would detach himself from God, judge and determine for himself what was good and what was evil, set his insight and his wisdom over against the wisdom of God, and thus make himself like God.
Instead of living and acting in dependence upon God and in submission to his law, by eating from the tree and transgressing the command the human being would stand on his own feet, choose for himself the way he wished to go, and seek his own happiness for himself.
When the human being fell, he therefore received what he desired: he made himself like God, independent, knowing good and evil by his own insight and judgment; Gen. 3:22 is terrible earnestness.
But this emancipation from God did not and cannot lead to true happiness.
Therefore in the probationary command God forbids this drive for freedom, this craving for independence.
But the human being succumbed to the test and voluntarily and willfully took his own path.

He had probably been in the state of innocence for only a short time when he was tempted from without and brought to the fall by a serpent that was more crafty (עָרוּם, LXX φρονιμος, _prudens_; cf. Matt. 10:16; 2 Cor. 11:3) than all the beasts of the field.
The serpent addressed itself not to the man but to the woman, who had not herself received the prohibition against eating from the tree directly from God but through her husband and was therefore more receptive to reasoning and doubt.
First of all the serpent therefore tried to awaken doubt concerning God’s command in the woman’s heart and for this purpose also represented it as having been given by God out of harshness and selfishness.
In the way she repeats and expands the command, the woman clearly shows that this command of God had come to her consciousness as a sharp boundary and a restrictive provision.
After doubt had been awakened and the burdensomeness of the command brought to consciousness, the serpent proceeded to sow both unbelief and pride in the woman’s prepared mind; it now decisively denied that transgressing that command would result in death and intimated that God had given the command only out of selfishness; if the human being ate from the tree, instead of dying he would become like God and receive a perfect, divine knowledge.
The serpent’s assurance and the high expectation it aroused caused the woman to look at the tree; and the longer she looked, the more she was enticed by its fruit.
The lust of the eyes, the lust of the flesh, and the pride of life made the temptation irresistible; finally she took of the fruit and ate, and she also gave some to her husband with her, and he ate.
The serpent’s speech has led many to think that this narrative was an allegory, or at least that the serpent was not a real animal but a name and image for concupiscence, Philo, Clement of Alexandria, _Strom._ III 14, 17; or for sexual desire, Schopenhauer, _Die Welt als W. u. V._⁶ II 654, 666; or for erring reason, Bunsen in his _Bibelwerk_; or also for Satan, Cajetan, Eugubinus, Junius, Rivetus, Amyraldus, Vitringa father and son, Venema, and others; cf. Marck, _Hist. Parad._ III 5, 5; M. Vitringa II 256; and also J. P. Val d’Eremas, _The serpent of Eden, a philol. and crit. essay on the text of Gen. 3 and its various interpretations_, London, 1888.
But this interpretation is not tenable; in Gen. 3:1 the serpent is reckoned among the animals; the punishment in verses 14, 15 presupposes a real serpent; and in 2 Cor. 3:11 Paul is of the same opinion.
The mythical conception that arose later and found acceptance among many likewise conflicts with the intent of the narrative, with the whole context in which it occurs, and with the consistent teaching of Scripture; moreover, the mythical interpretations also diverge greatly among themselves, Hengstenberg, _Christol._ I² 5; Köhler, _Bibl. Gesch._ I 6; Delitzsch on Gen. 3:1.
The serpent’s speech must therefore be explained in another way; but not, with Josephus, _Ant._ I 1, 4, from the narrator’s opinion that before the fall animals had the gift of language, for he has just related that the human being is essentially distinguished from the animals, gave them names, and found no helper among them; but undoubtedly from the operation of a spiritual, superterrestrial power.
Of what nature that power was, not a word is said in the narrative itself; Gen. 3 confines itself to the visible facts, describing but not explaining.
Many have indeed been of the opinion that Gen. 3 relates nothing other than the origin of the enmity between human being and animal.
But apart from the fact that this interpretation is unsatisfying because of its shallowness, it conflicts with what is related in Gen. 2 about the relation between human being and animal and does not tell us how and why the serpent appeared over against the human being as a seductive power.
Hence many today return again to the old exegesis, even if only because it is upheld in the apocryphal literature of the Old Testament, Clemen, _Chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 158 f.
Further, it is understandable that Gen. 3 makes no mention of the spiritual background of the events.
Only gradually, with the advancing light of revelation, is the depth of the darkness unveiled.
Having begun in apparent innocence, sin becomes known in its nature and power only in the course of history.
The deviation from the right way is slight and scarcely perceptible at the beginning, but when continued it leads in an entirely wrong direction and to a completely opposite outcome.
This also explains why Scripture, in both the Old and New Testaments, looks back comparatively so seldom to the narrative of the fall; the principal passages that come into consideration here are Job 31:33; Prov. 3:18; Isa. 43:27; Hos. 6:7; Ezek. 28:13–15; Rom. 5:12 f.; 8:20; 1 Cor. 15:21 f., 42 f.; 2 Cor. 11:3; 1 Tim. 2:14; Rev. 2:7; 22:2; cf. Krabbe, _Die Lehre v. d. Sünde und vom Tode_, Hamburg, 1836, S. 83–100; Hofmann, _Schriftbeweis_ I 364 f.; Kurtz, _Gesch. d. A. Bundes_ I², 1853, S. 69; and for the citations in the apocryphal literature, Clemen, at the place cited, pp. 169 f., 173.
Only Paul brings the significance of Adam to light over against the person of Christ.
Thus too only gradually in the history of revelation does the spiritual power become known that conceals itself behind the appearance and temptation of the serpent.
Then it is gradually unveiled that in the struggle with evil here on earth a battle of spirits is also involved and that humanity and the world are the spoil for which the battle is waged between God and Satan, between heaven and hell.

The whole power of sin here on earth stands in connection with a kingdom of darkness in the world of spirits.
There too a fall has taken place.
Jesus himself says in John 8:44 that the devil is a murderer of human beings ἀπ’ ἀρχης, that is, from the beginning of humanity’s existence; that he οὐχ ἑστηκεν in the truth, did not take his stand and therefore does not stand, because there is no truth in him; and that when he speaks a lie, he speaks ἐκ των ἰδιων.
Likewise 1 John 3:8 teaches that he sins from the beginning; in 1 Tim. 3:6 Paul warns the novice against conceit, lest he fall εἰς κριμα του διαβολου, into the same judgment that struck the devil; and Jude speaks in verse 6 of ἀγγελους τους μη τηρησαντας την ἑαυτων ἀρχην ἀλλα ἀπολιποντας το ἰδιον οἰκητηριον, that is, of angels who did not preserve their principle, origin, or even dominion and abandoned the dwelling place assigned to them.
This clearly implies that many angels were not content with the state in which God had placed them.
Pride took possession of them and caused them to strive for another, higher rank.
Sin first broke out in the world of spirits; it arose in the hearts of beings of whom we possess only slight knowledge and under circumstances almost entirely unknown to us.
But on the ground of Holy Scripture this much is certain: sin began not on earth but in heaven, at the foot of God’s throne, in his immediate presence, and the fall of the angels took place before that of the human being.
Scripture is silent about whether a connection exists between that fall of the angels and the creation of the human being; neither does it say what moved the fallen angels to tempt the human being.
But for whatever reason, Satan is ὁ σατανας, ὁ πειραζων, ὁ διαβολος of the human race, ἀνθρωποκτονος, Matt. 4:3; John 8:44; Eph. 6:11; 1 Thess. 3:5; 2 Tim. 2:26; ὁ δρακων ὁ μεγας, ὁ ὀφις ὁ ἀρχαιος, Rev. 12:9, 14, 15; 20:2.
Thus he came to Christ, the second Adam, and thus he also came to the first human being.
That he did not come to him directly and personally but made use of a serpent can indeed be explained by the fact that he hoped to succeed better if the temptation occurred through a being that was known to the human being as good.
The serpent’s speech must undoubtedly have seemed strange to the woman, but precisely this strangeness strengthened the temptation; even an animal, by rejecting God’s command, attained to higher perfection.
Furthermore, Scripture teaches us that unclean spirits too can do superhuman things and can temporarily take possession of bodies and organs of speech, Matt. 8:28 f.; Mark 5:7 f.; Luke 8:28 f.; Acts 19:15.
The temptation by Satan resulted in the fall of the human being.
Scripture seeks the origin of sin solely in the will of the rational creature.
