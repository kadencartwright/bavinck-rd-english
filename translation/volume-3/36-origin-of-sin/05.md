## 5.

After the origin of being, the origin of evil is the greatest riddle of life and the heaviest cross for the intellect.
The question ποθεν το κακον has occupied human thought in every age and still waits in vain for an answer that satisfies better than that of Holy Scripture.
Insofar as philosophy has taught anything of significance in this regard, taken as a whole it is a powerful proof of the truth of Holy Scripture: _that this world cannot be explained without a fall_.
All great thinkers, even if they did not know Gen. 3 or rejected it as a myth, have despite themselves paid silent or express homage to this simple account.
And insofar as philosophy sought a solution to the problem in another way, it lost the trail and went miserably astray.
This applies first of all to the Pelagian explanation of sin, against which the many objections already mentioned briefly above on p. 44 will be discussed more fully later in connection with the nature and propagation of sin.
But it applies further also to all those systems that trace evil not to an act of the creature’s will but to the being or nature of the human being, the world, or God.

In the first place, sin cannot be derived from the human being’s sensuous nature.
For then sin would always and everywhere bear a sensuous, carnal character; this, however, is by no means always the case; there are also spiritual sins, sins with a demonic character, such as pride, envy, hatred, and enmity against God, which are more hidden but by no means lesser in degree than carnal sins; and these are not explained by sensuousness, just as the existence of fallen angels is impossible from this standpoint.
If sins proceeded from the human being’s sensuous nature, one would also expect them to be strongest and most frequent in the earliest years of life; and that the more the spirit developed, the more powerfully it would rule over them and finally overcome them altogether.
But experience teaches something entirely different.
As the human being matures, sin, including sensuous sin, becomes more powerful over him; it is often not the child but the youth and the man who is a slave to his lusts and passions; and the development of the spirit is frequently so little able to restrain sin that it instead supplies the means for seeking satisfaction of the desires to an even greater degree and in more refined ways.
And even when sensuous sins have lost their dominion later in life, they still remain secretly in the heart as desires or make way for others that, although more spiritual in nature, are no less dreadful.
If this explanation of sin from sensuousness is seriously intended, it must therefore lead to seeking redemption in suppression of the flesh; but precisely the history of asceticism is best able to cure once and for all the error that sin can be overcome in this way.
The human heart accompanies one even into the monastery, and from that heart proceed all kinds of sins and unrighteousness.
Finally, this theory wrongly attempts to maintain itself by appealing to the notion ‎‏בָּשׂר‏‎ and σαρξ in Holy Scripture, particularly in Paul.
This word designates first of all the matter, the substance, of the human body, 1 Cor. 15:39; then the body itself organized from that matter, in contrast with πνευμα, νους, καρδια, Rom. 2:25; 2 Cor. 7:5; Col. 2:5; further, more in the Old Testament sense, the human being as an earthly, weak, frail, perishable being, Gen. 6:3; 18:27; Job 4:17–19; 15:14, 15; 25:4–6; Ps. 78:39; 103:14; Isa. 40:6; Jer. 17:5; Rom. 3:20; 1 Cor. 1:29; Gal. 2:16; and finally, in Paul, the sinful direction of the human being’s life.
Thus he speaks of σαρκικος, ἐν σαρκι, κατα σαρκα εἰναι, ζην, περιπατειν, of σαρξ ἁμαρτιας, φρονημα της σαρκος, Rom. 3:7; 7:14; 8:3 f.; 1 Cor. 3:3; 2 Cor. 10:2, 3, etc.
In this sense σαρξ forms a contrast with πνευμα, though not with the human πνευμα, which after all is itself also sinful and needs sanctification, Rom. 12:1, 2; 1 Cor. 7:34; 2 Cor. 7:1; Eph. 4:23; 1 Thess. 5:23, but with the πνευμα ἁγιον or θεου, Rom. 8:2, 9, 11, which renews the human πνευμα, Rom. 7:6; 8:14; Gal. 5:18, also sanctifies the body and places it in the service of righteousness, Rom. 6:13, 19; 12:1; 1 Cor. 6:13, 15, 19, 20, and thus places within the human being a καινος ἀνθρωπος over against the old, sinful direction of life, the σαρξ, of the παλαιος ἀνθρωπος, Rom. 7:5 f.; 8:1 f.; Gal. 5:13–25; Eph. 2:3, 11; Col. 2:4.
Some have thought that according to this conception the flesh is not only the seat and organ but also the source and origin of sin: Baur, Holsten, Lüdemann, Zeller, Pfleiderer, _Der Paulinismus_², 1890, S. 60 f.; Holtzmann, _Neut. Theol._, 1897, II 13 f.; Clemen, _Die chr. Lehre v. d. Sünde_ I 188 f.; Matthes, _Theol. Tĳdsch._, 1890, pp. 225–239.
But this cannot be maintained in the face of, or reconciled with, these undeniable facts: that Paul clearly derives sin from the serpent’s temptation and Adam’s transgression, Rom. 5:12; 2 Cor. 11:3; that he speaks of a defilement of flesh and spirit and demands purification with respect to both, 2 Cor. 7:1; that among the fruits of the flesh he also names all kinds of spiritual sins, such as idolatry, strife, wrath, and even heresy, Gal. 5:19 f.; that he designates enmity against God as φρονημα της σαρκος, Rom. 3:7; that he accepts the existence of evil spirits, which nevertheless have no σαρξ at all, Eph. 6:12; that he acknowledges Christ, although γενομενος ἐκ γυναικος, Gal. 4:4, and descended from Israel το κατα σαρκα, Rom. 9:5, as nevertheless without any sin, 2 Cor. 5:21; that he calls the body a temple of God and claims all its members for the service of righteousness, Rom. 6:13, 19; 12:1; 1 Cor. 6:13–20; that he teaches a resurrection of the bodies of the dead, 1 Cor. 15, and opposes asceticism in principle, Col. 2:16; 1 Thess. 4:4.
The advocates of the opinion that Paul regards the flesh as the principle of sin therefore often retreat halfway and say that the flesh is not itself sin and does not of itself bring sin with it, but does incite and tempt to sin; Clemen 204; Holtzmann II 38.
Others have therefore judged that when Paul uses the word σαρξ in an ethical sense, he entirely loses sight of its original meaning; Neander, _Gesch. der Pflanzung u. Leitung der christl. Kirche_⁵, 1862, S. 508 f.; Tholuck, _Stud. u. Krit._, 1855, S. 477 f.; Weiss, _Bibl. Theol._³, 1880, § 68; Wendt, _Die Begriffe Fleisch u. Geist_, 1878; cf. _Theol. Stud._ van de la Saussaye c. s., 1878, pp. 361 f.; Nitzsch, _Ev. Dogm._, 315 f.
This, however, is already improbable in itself and fails to do justice to the connection that Scripture repeatedly makes between the earthly, weak, perishable nature of the human being and his sin.
There is undoubtedly an intimate connection between the two; the human being’s sensuous nature is neither sin itself nor the source or principle of sin, yet it is sin’s dwelling place, Rom. 7:17, 18, and the organ of its dominion over us, Rom. 6:12.
The human being is not mere spirit, but is earthly from the earth and became a living soul, 1 Cor. 15:45 f.; he thereby stands in relation to the cosmos and always has the body as his instrument and as the organ of his action, Rom. 6:13; 8:13.
This sensuous nature gives human sin a distinctive character, different from the sin of angels, both in its origin and in its nature.
Temptations come to him from without through the lust of the eyes, the lust of the flesh, and the pride of life.
And it is the human being’s sensuous nature that gives his sin this character: that he makes his belly his god, that he minds the things below, that he seeks himself and lives for himself, and that he honors the creature above the Creator, Rom. 1:21 f.; Phil. 2:4, 21; 3:19; Col. 3:2, etc.
Σαρξ designates the sinful direction of life of the human being who in soul and body turns away from God and toward the creature.
