## 6.

The explanation of sin from the sensuous nature of the human being cannot, however, as noted above, stop here but must come to seek its cause in matter or in the finitude and limitation of the creature, and so further in an eternal, independent power alongside God or in a dark nature or blind will within the divine being itself. This opinion concerning the origin of sin commends itself above the preceding one by its deeper insight into the power and dominion of sin. Its eye is open not only to sin’s ethical and anthropological but also to its cosmic and theological significance. It takes seriously the unmistakable truth that a power as dreadful as sin cannot have arisen by chance, outside God’s will and counsel. It finds support in the entire present condition of the world, physical as well as ethical. Everywhere, in nature and history, there are sharp, deep oppositions that appear necessary for life and development. Heaven and earth, light and darkness, day and night, summer and winter, storm and calm, war and peace, labor and rest, prosperity and adversity, love and hate, joy and pain, health and sickness, life and death, truth and falsehood, sin and virtue—they are the opposing factors from which all that exists is composed and without which no progress or advance appears possible. What storms are in nature, wars and revolutions in society, peasants and slaves in a drama, solecisms and barbarisms in language, antitheses in a discourse, dissonances in music, and shadow in a painting, sin is in the world. See these and similar images already in Plato, the Stoa, Plotinus; cf. Zeller, _Philos. d. Gr._ II 765, 929; IV 173; V 548–562; and then later also in Augustine, Erigena, Leibniz, and others. Every activity also presupposes an obstacle. A dove might indeed think that it could fly better in a vacuum, but the resistance of the air is precisely necessary for its flight (Kant); and so a human being too may think that he could live better without sin, but sin is precisely necessary for his moral perfection. The law of contradiction is the fundamental law of all that exists, _der Quellbronn des ewigen Lebens. Was zum Handeln treibt, ja zwingt, ist allein der Widerspruch. Ohne Widerspruch also wäre keine Bewegung, kein Leben, kein Fortschritt, sondern ewiger Stillstand, ein Todesschlummer aller Kräfte_, Schelling, _Werke_ I 8, pp. 219, 321. What would a life without sin be? It would be an existence without content, an empty abstraction, without opportunity for struggle and victory, for conflict and reconciliation; without material for drama and song, for science and art. Dante could therefore paint his hell with colors borrowed from this world, but this earth supplies no data for painting a heaven (Schopenhauer). Advocates of this opinion concerning the origin of sin even gladly appeal to many passages of Holy Scripture that speak of a necessity of sins and calamities, Matt. 18:7; Luke 24:26; John 9:3; 1 Cor. 11:19; 2 Tim. 2:20; to the doctrine of Augustine and Calvin, who extend God’s counsel and providence also over sin; and to the familiar words in the Easter Vigil of the _Missale Romanum_: _o certe necessarium Adae peccatum, quod Christi morte deletum est! o felix culpa, quae talem ac tantum meruit habere Redemptorem!_

There is so much that is true in this representation that it need not surprise us that it has fascinated minds in every age. Sin is not accidental or arbitrary; it is included in the counsel of God. It is so interwoven with our whole existence that we can form no conception of a holy life, a sinless history. Against its will it is made serviceable by God Almighty to the revelation of his virtues and the honor of his name. Yet despite all the truth concealed in this representation, which will later come still more clearly to light, it cannot and may not be accepted. In the first place, it robs sin of its ethical character. Sin is certainly not solely and not always an act of will, as Pelagianism says, but is very definitely also a condition, a nature of the will; yet it never occurs wholly apart from the will. _Omne peccatum est voluntarium_, understood in Augustine’s sense, is undoubtedly correct. Here, however, in a Gnostic and theosophical manner, sin is paralleled and identified with the physical phenomena of darkness, sickness, death, and so forth; it is derived from the flesh, matter, the essence of creatures, the nature of God, and thus made into a substance or a necessary quality of the existence of things. Thereby sin is robbed of its ethical character and reduced to a physical phenomenon. Second, according to this representation sin becomes eternal and invincible. For because it is not ethical but physical in nature, it necessarily belongs to everything that exists, both God and the world, and is indispensable to the existence of everything. Not only is the good necessary for evil, but conversely evil is also necessary for the good. Evil here is not subordinate to the good and to being; it is itself a being and itself a good, without which even the good cannot exist. The human being who strives for deliverance from sin would cherish a godless desire and labor toward his own destruction. A world without sin would be impossible; a _status gloriae_ nothing but a dream. Third, sin thereby ceases to be an antithesis. It becomes a lower, lesser degree of the good, as good in its place as the good itself. It becomes a moment in life and history always destined to disappear and yet never disappearing; a not-yet-being of what the creature ought to be and yet never becomes or can become; a pure negation that actually has no reality but exists only in our thought. _Bonum et malum quod attinet, nihil etiam positivum in rebus, in se consideratis, indicant, nec aliud sunt praeter cogitandi modos seu notiones, quas formamus ex eo, quod res ad invicem comparamus_, Spinoza, _Eth._, preface; cf. _Ep._ 32 and 34; _Cog. metaph._ I 6, 7; and further Hegel, _Werke_ VIII 180 f.; Strauss, _Gl._ II 365–384; Schleiermacher, _Chr. Gl._ § 81; Paulsen, _Syst. d. Ethik_ I 251 f.; Scholten, _L. H. K._ II 34 f., 422, 580. Fourth, from this standpoint God must become the author of sin. Parsism and Manichaeism still recoiled from this, placed the kingdom of light and the kingdom of darkness directly over against one another, and placed an eternal, divine being at the head of each. The God of nature is entirely different from the God of the good, the moral power that asserts itself in conscience. But Gnostic philosophy and theosophy took the opposites up into the one Absolute. In order to become person and spirit, God himself must bear and eternally overcome a dark nature within himself. He himself arrives at his divine existence through a struggle, a process, whether before and outside the world or in and through it. _An sich_ he is βυθος ἀγνωστος, dark nature, blind will, and as such the Creator of matter. _Damit des Böse nicht wäre, müsste Gott selbst nicht seyn_, Schelling, _Werke_ I 7, p. 403. Against this not only Holy Scripture testifies, but the moral consciousness in all human beings also revolts. Sin may be whatever it is, but this one thing is certain: God is the Righteous and Holy One, who prohibits it in his law, testifies against it in the conscience, and visits it with punishments and judgments. Sin is neither rational nor lawful; it is ἀνομια. It is not necessary for the existence of creatures, much less for the existence of God. The good is necessary so that even evil could exist, but the good does not need evil, holiness does not need sin, truth does not need falsehood, God does not need Satan. If sin nevertheless repeatedly serves to bring the good to fuller revelation and to glorify God’s virtues, this happens not with and through its will but against it, through the wisdom and omnipotence of God. Contrary to its nature, sin is then compelled to serve the honor of God and the coming of his kingdom. Thus evil often pays homage to the good; thus falsehood is overtaken by the truth; thus Satan, in order to deceive, must often appear as an angel of light. But all this is due not to sin but to the omnipotence of God, who can bring forth good from evil, light from darkness, and life from death. Finally, this whole false representation avenges itself in a dreadful manner in the practice of life. If philosophy proclaims in so many words, “God bears the blame for everything; the human being goes free,” libertinism and pessimism do not long delay in practice. Libertinism regards sin as an illusion and this illusion as the only sin; erases every boundary between good and evil; falsifies all moral concepts or, with Nietzsche, melts them down and coins them anew; and under the slogan of the emancipation of the flesh glorifies bestiality as genius. Pessimism, blind to sin and thinking only of suffering, casts the blame for all that suffering upon the alogical act of an absolute will and seeks deliverance from suffering in the annihilation of what exists. Judged by its outcome, even so-called independent philosophy is guided by the striving proper to every human being to justify himself and accuse God of injustice. Cf. Müller, _Sünde_ I⁵ 374 f.; Weizsäcker, “Zu der Lehre v. Wesen der Sünde,” _Jahrb. f. d. Theol._, 1856, pp. 131–195; Kahnis, _Dogm._ I 478 f.; Vilmar, _Theol. Moral_ I 143 f.; Dorner, _Chr. Gl._ II 114 f.; Orr, _Chr. View of God and the World_, p. 193.
